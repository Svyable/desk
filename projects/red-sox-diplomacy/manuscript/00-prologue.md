# Prologue: The Diplomat in the Red Sox Cap

The cap was not part of the uniform.

Nicholas Burns wore it anyway.

At the State Department, the flags, titles, readouts, and order of precedence belonged to the institution. The Red Sox cap belonged to Burns. He was the official at the microphone and a Bostonian who had carried the team with him through decades of government service.

That mattered less than a treaty and more than nothing.

A diplomat speaks for a state but arrives as a person, with a hometown, loyalties older than the job, habits that survive it, and stories that can make an official less abstract. The distinction matters because the person is never the policy. A baseball cap cannot negotiate an arms-control agreement, hold an alliance together, or decide what Washington means to say.

Burns entered government near the end of the Cold War and spent more than three decades inside American diplomacy: at the White House and State Department, in Moscow, at NATO, in negotiations with India and Iran, and finally in Beijing. The assignments changed. So did the balance of power around them. Again and again, policy had to pass through a human being before another human being could answer it.

Governments do not literally speak. Alliances do not remember who kept a promise. Embassies do not hear hesitation in a sentence. People do those things on behalf of institutions, under instructions they did not write alone and amid histories they did not choose.

That is the problem this book follows.

The Red Sox recur because they recurred in Burns's life: as inheritance, local language, private allegiance, public signal, and occasionally a way to talk about rivalry. The metaphor has a hard limit. Baseball has rules both sides accept, umpires, standings, a schedule, and the promise of another game. Diplomacy sometimes begins because those guarantees are missing.

In Athens, historical memory complicated the messages an embassy wanted to send. At NATO, allies could fight bitterly over Iraq without ceasing to be allies. Negotiations with India required years of work across governments and institutions before a civil-nuclear agreement could be signed. Talks with Iran showed that adversaries can negotiate without becoming less adversarial. In Beijing, communication itself could become part of the contest.

None of those episodes needs baseball to matter. The cap is useful because it keeps the scale human.

The diplomat works in the middle: between a government and another government, between policy and the people who receive it, between instructions from home and facts encountered abroad. Burns spent a career there.

The Red Sox came along.
