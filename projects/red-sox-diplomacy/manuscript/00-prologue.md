# Prologue: The Diplomat in the Red Sox Cap

The cap was not part of the uniform.

Nicholas Burns wore it anyway.

At the State Department, where a room can be arranged down to flags, precedence, and the exact nouns permitted in a readout, the cap made a different claim. Burns was the official at the microphone. He was also a Bostonian who had spent decades carrying the Red Sox with him through government service.

That mattered less than a treaty and more than nothing.

A diplomat speaks for a state but arrives as a person. The person has an accent, a hometown, loyalties that predate the job, habits that survive it, and stories that can make an official legible before the first hard question. Burns understood that. He also understood the limit. A baseball cap could open a conversation. It could not negotiate an arms-control agreement, hold an alliance together, or decide what Washington meant to say.

The distinction runs through his career.

He entered government near the end of the Cold War and spent more than three decades inside American diplomacy: at the White House and State Department, in Moscow, at NATO, in negotiations with India and Iran, and finally in Beijing. The assignments changed. So did the balance of power around them. The work kept returning to a stubborn fact: institutions act through people.

Governments do not literally speak. Alliances do not remember who kept a promise. Embassies do not hear hesitation in a sentence. People do those things on behalf of institutions, under instructions they did not write alone and amid histories they did not choose.

This is a book about that problem.

It is not a manual for turning baseball into foreign policy. The Red Sox recur because they recurred in Burns's life: as inheritance, local language, private allegiance, public signal, and occasionally a useful way to explain rivalry to people who knew enough baseball to argue back. The metaphor works until it doesn't. Baseball has rules both sides accept, umpires, standings, a schedule, and the promise of another game. Diplomacy sometimes exists because none of those guarantees can be assumed.

Burns's career offers a way into the difference. In Athens, historical memory complicated the messages an embassy wanted to send. At NATO, allies could fight bitterly over Iraq without ceasing to be allies. Negotiations with India required years of work across governments and institutions before a civil-nuclear agreement could be signed. Talks with Iran showed that adversaries can negotiate without becoming less adversarial. In Beijing, communication itself could become part of the contest.

None of those episodes needs baseball to matter. The cap is useful because it keeps the scale human.

The diplomat is always in the middle: between a government and another government, between policy and the people who receive it, between instructions from home and facts encountered abroad. Burns spent a career there.

The Red Sox came along.
