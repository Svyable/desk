# The Pigeon Computer

Working subtitle: **B. F. Skinner, Project Pigeon, and the Weapon Guidance System That Was Alive**

The first scene is not a joke. A pigeon is sitting inside a missile nose cone, looking at a target image through a window, pecking to keep the target centered. There are three birds because redundancy matters. The United States actually paid B. F. Skinner to pursue this during World War II, and the work later continued at the Naval Research Laboratory under ORCON, short for “organic control.”

That is enough for a good oddity. The book gets better if the birds are treated as engineering rather than comic relief. In the 1940s, visual target recognition was a brutally hard machine problem. A pigeon already arrived with a compact visual system, low power consumption, pattern recognition, trainability, and a body that had been debugging itself for millions of years. Skinner's proposal was to use the animal as a component inside a feedback loop before electronics could cheaply do the same job.

The real subject is procurement and technological timing. A component can work and still lose. A system can be empirically impressive and institutionally impossible. The pigeon was not competing against stupidity; it was competing against rapidly improving radar, television guidance, electronics, conventional assumptions about reliability, and the reputational cost of asking admirals to put birds in missiles.

A possible spine: the targeting problem; Skinner before the war; the first bird apparatus; how pecking became steering; three-bird voting; stress and repeatability; Project Pigeon; cancellation; ORCON; what the tests did and did not prove; animal vision before computer vision; biological reliability; procurement embarrassment; the ethics of making a living organism a disposable component; modern biohybrid systems; and the larger question of how many “crackpot” ideas are simply technologies born before their supporting infrastructure.

Evidence boundary: Project Pigeon, the physical nose-cone prototype, Skinner's own account, and the later ORCON continuation are documented. Claims about battlefield deployment are not. Popular retellings often compress prototype concepts into an operational weapon; the book should not.
