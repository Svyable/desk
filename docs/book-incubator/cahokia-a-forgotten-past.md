# Cahokia: A Forgotten Past

Working subtitle: **The Indigenous Metropolis America Learned Not to See**

Cahokia is not interesting because it was a “mystery civilization.” That phrase repeats the mistake the book should investigate. Cahokia was an Indigenous metropolis in the Mississippi floodplain east of present-day St. Louis. At its height around the eleventh and twelfth centuries, the core city held roughly 10,000–20,000 people, while estimates for the wider Greater Cahokia complex run much higher. Nearly 120 earthen mounds once organized the landscape. Monks Mound alone rises about thirty metres and covers several hectares. The place had planned plazas, neighborhoods, monumental construction, palisades, specialized craft production, long-distance exchange, and repeated large-scale public works.

The strange fact is not that such a place existed. The strange fact is how effectively the dominant American historical imagination learned to live beside it without treating it as part of the history of cities.

This book should make “forgotten” an accusation that has to survive scrutiny. Cahokia was not forgotten by everyone, and the people who built it did not vanish into metaphysics. Archaeology increasingly describes the city as a multiethnic, migratory place. Strontium-isotope studies of human teeth indicate that a substantial fraction of sampled individuals grew up outside the American Bottom, with people arriving from multiple regions. Later dispersal likewise belongs to the histories of Native peoples across the Midwest, Southeast, Plains, and Mississippi Valley, even where a simple one-to-one genealogy between Cahokia and a modern nation cannot responsibly be claimed. The disappearance is therefore partly demographic and political, but also historiographic: a society can leave a city while later institutions erase the category of city from the story.

The deeper book is about urbanism without the paperwork later historians were trained to privilege. Cahokia left no alphabetic civic archive that tells us who ordered a plaza enlarged or how tribute, kinship, migration, ritual authority, agricultural surplus, warfare, and household politics fit together. Instead we have mound stratigraphy, postholes, ceramics, botanical remains, isotopes, burials, landscape geometry, destroyed mounds, old excavation records, descendant consultation, and the increasingly awkward history of archaeology itself. The absence of writing does not make the society unknowable. It changes the kind of evidence that can speak.

One chapter should begin with the so-called “Big Bang” around A.D. 1050, when population and monumental construction accelerated with unusual speed. Another should stay with immigration long enough to make Cahokia feel like a city of arrivals rather than an isolated tribe. Another should reconstruct the Grand Plaza as deliberate earthmoving rather than empty ceremonial space. Monks Mound should be treated as labor, soil, drainage, repair, staging, authority, and repeated redesign—not simply as a big pile of dirt. Woodhenge belongs in a chapter about calendrical alignment without turning astronomy into mysticism.

Mound 72 deserves a book-length argument inside the book. Older interpretations famously centered a powerful male “Birdman” burial and used it to support particular models of chiefly authority and cosmology. Bioarchaeological reanalysis later identified male/female pairings, numerous women, and at least one child in contexts previously interpreted through a heavily male political frame. That correction is not an embarrassing footnote. It shows archaeology manufacturing a past, then having to reopen its own evidence when better osteology and archival reconstruction make the old story unstable.

The decline chapters should resist the word *collapse* until the evidence earns it. Cahokia’s population fell, monumental construction slowed, palisades appeared and were rebuilt, and by the fourteenth century the urban center had been largely abandoned. Drought, flooding, ecological pressure, political fragmentation, violence, disease, food systems, factionalism, and changes in regional networks have all entered the argument. The book should not choose a cinematic single cause because readers want one. The better subject is how cities can disperse while cultures, people, practices, and relationships continue elsewhere.

Then comes the second disappearance. Farmers leveled mounds. St. Louis consumed much of its own mound complex. Roads, rail lines, extraction, private ownership, collecting, museum transfer, and archaeological excavation changed what survived and where it survived. Cahokia became easier to describe as a ruined prehistoric site than as evidence that large-scale Indigenous urbanism belongs inside the ordinary history of North America. The modern preservation story, UNESCO designation, NAGPRA work, repatriation, and consultation with descendant communities should therefore be part of the main narrative rather than an ethical appendix.

A possible chapter spine: the view from Monks Mound; the American Bottom before Cahokia; the 1050 transformation; immigration and isotope geography; maize and surplus without agricultural determinism; the Grand Plaza; building Monks Mound; neighborhoods and difference; Woodhenge and time; Mound 72 and the archaeology that had to correct itself; trade without calling every exotic object commerce; palisades and insecurity; the long decline argument; leaving without vanishing; the destruction of the St. Louis mound landscape; nineteenth- and twentieth-century mound mythology; museums, remains, NAGPRA and descendant authority; and the final question—what else becomes invisible when a nation decides civilization arrived with its own ancestors.

Evidence boundary: the scale, chronology, monumental earthworks, planned spaces, Mississippian context, rapid eleventh-century growth, substantial immigration, long-distance interaction, population decline and eventual abandonment of the urban core are supported by archaeology. Exact population estimates vary. “Cahokian” was not necessarily a single ethnicity. There is no responsible basis for importing lost-race, Old World colony, Atlantis, giants, or other pseudohistorical explanations. The causes of decline remain contested and likely multicausal. Connections to living Native nations should be developed through current consultation and scholarship, not invented genealogies. The phrase “forgotten past” refers primarily to settler-American historical memory, not to a claim that Indigenous descendants forgot their own histories.
