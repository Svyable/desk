# The Copper Sky

Working subtitle: **Project West Ford and the Day America Tried to Build a Radio Reflector Around the Earth**

In 1963 the United States released a huge number of hair-thin copper dipoles into orbit. The purpose was communications. Before reliable satellite relays had fully matured, military planners worried that undersea cables could be cut and the ionosphere could be unreliable. Project West Ford proposed an artificial scattering layer: a belt of tiny resonant wires in space that radio signals could bounce from.

This is one of those projects that becomes stranger when the bureaucratic language is read slowly. A presidential report to Congress described placing roughly fifty pounds of copper filaments into orbit to test passive communications reflection. NASA's later orbital-debris history describes the first failed deployment and the second, partially successful mission that released on the order of hundreds of millions of tiny copper wires. Communications experiments worked. Astronomers and other scientists were furious about the precedent.

The book is not “the government put needles in space.” The better subject is how early spaceflight forced humanity to discover that orbit is an environment rather than an empty dumping ground. West Ford sits before modern space-debris law, before today's crowded low-Earth-orbit arguments, and before society had a mature vocabulary for planetary commons. The experiment was technically elegant precisely because it treated the sky as infrastructure.

A possible spine: the vulnerability of Cold War communications; radar and ionospheric propagation; why a dipole only needs to be tiny; Lincoln Laboratory; the first launch; the failure to disperse; the second release; the belt closing around Earth; actual communication tests; astronomers' objections; international reaction; orbital lifetime; what debris remained; how satellites made the concept obsolete; the birth of space environmentalism; later megaconstellations; passive infrastructure versus active machines; and the uncomfortable fact that technological success can create governance failure.

Evidence boundary: the launches, purpose, physical dipoles, and successful communication tests are established. Claims that West Ford created a durable permanent artificial ionosphere are wrong. Many individual dipoles decayed; some associated dispenser debris persisted much longer.
