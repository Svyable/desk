# Cincinnati Dose

Working subtitle: Total-Body Irradiation, Cancer Patients, and the Pentagon's Human Data Problem

## Thesis

Cincinnati's total-body irradiation studies sit in an ethically difficult overlap: physicians treating patients with advanced cancer, military agencies interested in radiation effects relevant to nuclear war, and subjects whose consent and understanding became the center of later controversy. The book should reconstruct the overlap without pretending that military funding automatically proves military experimentation or that therapeutic intent settles the ethics.

## Evidence posture

Use ACHRE, Department of Defense and university records, medical publications, consent documents where available, litigation and settlement records, and congressional material. Separate clinical rationale, sponsor interest, actual protocol design, and later interpretation.

## Narrative engine

Begin with the machine and a patient facing late-stage cancer. Then widen to Cold War casualty modeling, radiation medicine, funding, dose, cognitive and physiological measurements, consent, institutional review, public exposure, and litigation.

## Research targets

ACHRE chapters and records; University of Cincinnati archives; DoD contract records; contemporaneous oncology/radiobiology papers; federal hearings; court records.

## Defeat conditions

Do not describe all irradiations as nontherapeutic unless protocol-specific evidence supports it. Keep retrospective testimony distinct from contemporaneous documentation.
