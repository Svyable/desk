# Whitecoat

Working subtitle: Fort Detrick, Conscientious Objectors, and the Problem of Voluntary Infection

## Thesis

Operation Whitecoat used Seventh-day Adventist servicemen, many of them conscientious objectors, as volunteers in infectious-disease research at Fort Detrick. Unlike many notorious human-experimentation histories, Whitecoat forces a harder question: when subjects volunteer, consent procedures are comparatively elaborate, and the research produces useful medical knowledge, how much does the military structure surrounding the choice still matter?

## Evidence posture

Use Army records, published medical studies, Adventist archives, National Academies histories, oral histories, and human-subject regulations. Preserve positive participant testimony alongside criticism. Do not retrofit the moral template of Edgewood or Holmesburg onto a program with materially different recruitment and consent practices.

## Narrative engine

Follow one experimental challenge from recruitment through quarantine, inoculation, observation, treatment, and release, then widen into biological-warfare defense, conscientious objection, vaccine development, military medicine, and the ethics of choosing risk in uniform.

## Research targets

U.S. Army Medical Research Institute histories; National Academies material; Adventist archives; contemporaneous infectious-disease publications; DoD human-research policy; oral histories with clear provenance.

## Defeat conditions

Do not call infection coercive merely because the subjects were servicemen. Conversely, do not treat signed consent or favorable recollection as proof that institutional pressure was absent.
