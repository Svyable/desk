How business learned to price the person, not the product.

The working thesis is that a fixed, public, non-negotiated price is a nineteenth-century invention, not a law of nature — and that the twenty-first century's data infrastructure is quietly dismantling it. From John Wanamaker's "one price" department stores through De Beers' engineered diamond expectations, airline yield management, Uber's surge algorithm, and the Federal Trade Commission's 2024–2025 discovery of a "surveillance pricing" industry already serving hundreds of retail clients, the book traces how sellers went from moving one public number with psychology to computing a separate number for each customer, often invisibly.

The book does not claim every price a reader sees today is individually computed — the evidence for that is thinner than the public conversation about it suggests, and the manuscript says so directly. Its narrower, better-supported claim is that the technical and legal apparatus for doing so now exists at scale, that documented cases already reach from SAT tutoring to rental housing, and that the resulting fight — over Delta's AI-assisted fares, over Ticketmaster's dynamic pricing, over Wendy's aborted "surge pricing" announcement — is a real argument about a real shift, even where individual companies' practices remain contested or unresolved.
