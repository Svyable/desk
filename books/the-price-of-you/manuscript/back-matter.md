# Back Matter

## Epilogue

None of the mechanisms in this book are secret in the sense that a conspiracy is secret. Every one of them left a paper trail: a Senate letter, a Federal Trade Commission report, a jury verdict, a court filing, a company's own earnings call, an economist's survey from forty years ago that a fast-food chain rediscovered the hard way in three days. The story here is not that firms are hiding what they do. It is that the technical and legal apparatus for treating each of us as a distinct pricing problem now exists, is being sold at meaningful scale, and has outpaced the ordinary shopper's ability to tell a demand-based price from a person-based one by looking at a receipt.

That gap will not close because a regulation requires a disclosure label, though the label may help. It closes, if it closes at all, the way most information asymmetries have ever closed in the history of commerce: slowly, unevenly, through litigation, embarrassment, competition, and the accumulated stubbornness of customers who compare notes. Wanamaker's one price took decades to become the default. Whatever replaces it, or however it is contained, will likely take about as long.
