# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

For most of the history of retail commerce, a price was a single public fact: one number, chalked on a board or printed on a tag, that applied to whoever walked in the door. That fact was itself a nineteenth-century invention — most buying and selling before it was haggled, one transaction at a time. Fixed pricing was sold to the public as a form of honesty: the same price for the banker and the bricklayer.

*The Price of You* argues that this shared public price is quietly disappearing, and that most of our intuitions about fairness, trust, and markets were built for a world that assumed it would always exist. Over the twentieth century, sellers learned to move that single price with psychology — anchoring, charm pricing, loss leaders, engineered scarcity. Late in the twentieth century, industries with perishable inventory (airline seats, hotel rooms) learned to change the *public* price by time and channel, a practice called revenue management. In the twenty-first century, the infrastructure of online commerce — cookies, location data, browsing history, purchase history, real-time bidding — has made it technically possible to compute a *different* price for each individual shopper, often without telling them. Regulators now call this "surveillance pricing." It is no longer speculative or rare; the Federal Trade Commission's own 2024–2025 inquiry found the practice already embedded in a two-hundred-fifty-client industry serving supermarkets alone.

The strong version of this thesis — that companies now charge each of us our exact maximum willingness to pay, perfectly, all the time — is not well supported and the book should not claim it. The defensible version is narrower and more durable: the technical and legal infrastructure that would make individualized pricing routine now exists; it is being adopted unevenly and often clumsily; and the public reacts to the *idea* of it with an intensity that outruns the evidence of how far it has actually gone. That gap — between what companies can now do, what they are actually doing, and what the public believes they are doing — is itself the story.

## Core empirical tests

1. **The history of the single price.** When and why did a fixed, public, non-negotiated price become the norm in Western retail, and what did it replace?
2. **Psychological price engineering.** How much can a single public price be moved by framing, anchors, digits, and scarcity claims, and how solid is the evidence behind the most famous examples?
3. **Revenue management.** How did airlines, and later other perishable-inventory industries, learn to legally vary the *public* price by time, channel, and remaining capacity — and where is the line between that and discrimination by individual?
4. **Personalization infrastructure.** What data plumbing (cookies, device fingerprinting, loyalty-app telemetry, real-time bidding, third-party pricing vendors) actually exists to price an individual differently today, and who sells it?
5. **Documented cases.** Which individualized or geographic pricing practices have actually been caught, admitted, litigated, or studied, as opposed to merely feared?
6. **Algorithmic coordination.** When software recommends a price to many competitors using shared nonpublic data, does that behave like classical collusion even without an explicit agreement, and what have courts and regulators said?
7. **Fairness psychology.** Why does the public treat some price changes (surge pricing, itemized fees) as intuitively outrageous and others (off-season discounts, senior discounts) as unremarkable, even when the economic logic is similar?
8. **Policy response.** What have the FTC, state legislatures, and the European Union actually done about disclosure, junk fees, and personalized pricing, as of the most recent available action?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should look for evidence against the strong thesis.

- Dynamic pricing that varies by *aggregate* supply and demand (an airline seat, a hotel room, a rideshare during a storm) is not the same thing as pricing an *individual* differently from another individual seeing the identical product at the identical moment; conflating the two overstates the case. Delta's own 2025 statement to Congress explicitly denied setting individualized fares from personal data and described its practice as demand-based, not person-based; the book must present that denial fairly rather than assume it is false.
- Geographic and channel price variation has legitimate, non-discriminatory drivers — local competition, delivery cost, rent, labor cost, local tax — and the Wall Street Journal's own 2012 Staples investigation found that distance to a rival's store, not income or race, explained most of the variation it measured.
- Loss-leader pricing (Costco's hot dog) and charm pricing are voluntary, transparent-once-you-look, and do not depend on tracking anyone individually; they belong to the older psychological-pricing tradition, not the personalization one, and the book should not blur that line.
- Loyalty programs are a real, mutually-understood exchange (data and repeat business for rewards) that predates modern personalization by decades and gives customers a visible, optable-into deal rather than a hidden one.
- Regulators, courts, and companies themselves are actively contesting these practices; the RealPage case ended in a settlement, the Ticketmaster case is still in its remedies phase, and the FTC's personalized-pricing policy is a proposed statement, not a final rule as of this writing. The book should mark these as live, not settled.

## Evidence lanes

### I. The single public price and its psychology
Wanamaker and the department-store fixed-price movement; charm pricing and the left-digit effect (Anderson & Simester); anchoring, decoys, and engineered scarcity (De Beers); loss leaders (Costco); the theory of willingness to pay and price discrimination.

### II. Revenue management and the legal channel-and-time economy
American Airlines, Crandall, SABRE/DINAMO, and 1980s yield management; the spread of dynamic and variable pricing into sports tickets and hotels; Uber's 2011–2012 surge-pricing launch and backlash; the Ticketmaster/Live Nation dynamic-pricing controversies and the DOJ's 2024 monopoly suit through the April 2026 jury verdict.

### III. Personalization infrastructure and documented cases
Amazon's 2000 DVD price test; the 2012 Staples.com geographic-pricing investigation; the 2015 ProPublica/Princeton Review zip-code investigation; the FTC's 2024 6(b) surveillance-pricing study and January 2025 report; the RealPage algorithmic rent-pricing case (DOJ, August 2024, and its resolution); Delta's 2024–2025 AI-pricing rollout and the Senate response.

### IV. Fairness psychology and public reaction
Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler's 1986 fairness-as-entitlement research and the snow-shovel scenario; Wendy's February 2024 "surge pricing" backlash as a live case study; the psychology of why personalized and surge pricing register as a moral violation rather than an economic adjustment.

### V. Historical counter-case: suppressing the public price
The WWII Office of Price Administration, rationing, and the black markets that price controls generate, as the opposite failure mode from personalization — useful for showing that a single administered price is not automatically fairer than a flexible one.

### VI. Policy response
The FTC's December 2024 junk-fees final rule (effective May 2025); the FTC's August 2026 proposed enforcement policy statement on personalized-pricing disclosure; the EU's Digital Services Act, Digital Markets Act, and the emerging Digital Fairness Act / Consumer Rights Directive personalization-disclosure requirements.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary sources: court filings and DOJ/FTC press releases and reports, company statements and SEC filings, peer-reviewed studies, and first-party investigative reporting (ProPublica, WSJ) with a clear, checkable methodology.
- Record the date a source was accessed, since regulatory and litigation status here changes quickly (this manuscript was drafted in September 2026, with the Ticketmaster case in its remedies phase and the FTC's personalized-pricing policy statement still a proposal).
- Distinguish an allegation in a complaint from a finding of fact, a settlement from an admission, and a proposed rule from a final one.
- Do not invent a quotation. Where a widely repeated quotation (e.g., the Costco hot dog exchange) exists in only one or two original tellings, cite that telling and note that it rests on one person's account.
- Preserve genuine uncertainty about how widespread individualized (as opposed to aggregate-dynamic) pricing actually is; the evidence base is thinner than the public conversation about it suggests, and the book should say so.
