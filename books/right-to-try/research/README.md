# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Right To Try* asks what changes when medicine becomes so personalized, biologically complex, or early in development that a patient's realistic treatment options are determined not only by diagnosis but by regulatory pathway and geography.

The easy version of the argument is that regulators are slow, desperate patients should be free to assume risk, and jurisdictions with fewer constraints become laboratories of progress. That version is too simple and often factually wrong. Montana now has a deliberately constructed state framework for licensed experimental-treatment centers. Mexico regulates clinical research, health establishments, new molecules, organs, tissues and cells through federal law and COFEPRIS, even though enforcement capacity, legal ambiguity and a medical-tourism market can create larger practical gaps between written rules and what is sold.

The stronger thesis is this:

> Personalized medicine is creating treatments whose unit of development may be a patient, a mutation, a tumor, a bacterial isolate or a manufactured cell lot. Regulation built for repeatable mass-market products strains when the product itself changes from patient to patient. The resulting pressure is increasingly expressed as expanded access, right-to-try laws, experimental-treatment jurisdictions and cross-border medical travel. The question is not whether to regulate these therapies, but how to make access fast enough to matter while preserving the evidence, manufacturing discipline and independent judgment that keep desperation from becoming a business model.

The book treats speed and oversight as design variables rather than ideological opposites. The strongest counterexamples to deregulation-first arguments are cases in which regulators moved very quickly: single-patient expanded-access INDs, emergency phage access, milasen, and the patient-specific base-editing therapy developed for KJ. Those examples do not prove the existing system is sufficient. They show that the relevant policy frontier is more interesting than a binary choice between FDA approval and lawless experimentation.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Geography:** Which therapies are actually available because a patient crosses a state or national border, and which are merely marketed that way?
2. **State versus federal authority:** What can a state such as Montana lawfully authorize in the practice of medicine when federal law still governs unapproved drugs, biologics, devices and interstate commerce?
3. **Mexico's real framework:** Where does Mexican law require COFEPRIS authorization, ethics review or facility licensing, and where do enforcement gaps or ambiguous categories create practical room for experimental clinics?
4. **Personalization:** At what point does a therapy stop behaving like a conventional mass-manufactured drug and become a family of individualized products?
5. **Platform evidence:** When can safety, manufacturing and mechanistic evidence be reused across many individualized treatments without pretending that each patient-specific product is identical?
6. **Compassionate access:** How often can existing expanded-access systems move quickly enough for rapidly progressive disease, and what are their bottlenecks?
7. **Evidence loss:** Do right-to-try and medical-tourism pathways produce usable data, or do they privatize both risk and information?
8. **Manufacturing:** How much of modern therapeutic safety is created not by the molecule's idea but by process control, identity testing, sterility, potency, release criteria and chain of custody?
9. **Consent:** What does informed consent mean when the patient is dying, evidence is sparse, payment is substantial and the provider may also be the seller?
10. **Access and inequality:** Who receives experimental medicine when insurance does not pay, manufacturers may charge costs, and travel itself becomes a prerequisite?
11. **Commercial incentives:** When does charging for experimental treatment fund learning, and when does it reward the sale of hope before efficacy is known?
12. **Regulatory learning:** Which access models generate evidence that makes the next patient's decision better?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should preserve evidence that cuts against its preferred argument.

- Federal oversight may be faster than its reputation. FDA states that it allows approximately 99% of expanded-access requests to proceed, and emergency requests can move extremely quickly.
- State experimentation may reveal useful institutional designs that federal policy later copies. Montana's licensing, independent-review, adverse-event and access requirements should be evaluated on their actual operation rather than dismissed because they challenge the traditional division of authority.
- Mexico should not be described as unregulated. COFEPRIS authorizes human clinical-research protocols, maintains a national clinical-trial registry, licenses relevant health establishments and has taken enforcement action against unauthorized stem-cell clinics.
- A treatment's lack of approval does not mean it is ineffective. Approval status is an evidence-and-regulatory category, not a biological verdict.
- Approval itself does not eliminate uncertainty. Accelerated approvals, autologous cell therapies, gene therapies and rare-disease products can enter routine care with limited long-term evidence and post-market obligations.
- Randomized controlled trials are not always feasible for one-person or ultra-rare therapies. Requiring evidence appropriate for a common chronic disease can make a bespoke therapy impossible without making the resulting decision more scientific.
- Compassionate-use anecdotes can identify signals and rescue individuals even when they cannot establish general efficacy.
- Medical tourism is not always irrational. Patients may travel for therapies that are approved elsewhere, for legitimate trials, for lower-cost conventional care or for specialist expertise. The book should distinguish those cases from commercial unproven interventions.
- Manufacturing standardization can be excessive if it prevents safe patient-specific variation; too little standardization can make every dose an uncontrolled experiment.
- The most permissive jurisdiction may not become the most innovative if reputable manufacturers, insurers, investigators and hospitals avoid unresolved liability and federal exposure.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Right to Try, expanded access and the regulatory stack

Separate four things that are often collapsed in public discussion: ordinary clinical trials; FDA expanded access or compassionate use; the federal Right to Try Act; and state right-to-try or experimental-treatment laws. Track eligibility, sponsor consent, IRB involvement, FDA involvement, charging rules, data reporting and liability separately.

### II. Montana as a live federalism experiment

Trace Montana's 2015 right-to-try law, its 2023 expansion beyond a terminal-illness-only frame, Senate Bill 535 in 2025 and the 2026 implementing rules for experimental treatment centers. Record the statutory definition of experimental treatment, facility licensing, independent experimental-treatment review, adverse-event reporting, informed consent and the requirement that centers devote a portion of net profit to access for qualifying Montana residents. Keep the unresolved federal questions visible: the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the Public Health Service Act, biologics licensing, IND requirements, interstate commerce, patent exposure and federal preemption.

### III. Mexico as regulated system plus enforcement gap

Use COFEPRIS and Secretaría de Salud materials as primary sources for clinical-trial authorization, new-molecule review, facility licensing, the national trial registry and enforcement. Then use academic literature on stem-cell tourism and cross-border care to examine how unauthorized or weakly evidenced interventions can still be sold. The central distinction is between what Mexican law permits and what the market sometimes manages to offer.

### IV. N-of-1 genetic medicine

Use milasen as the foundational modern case of a patient-customized antisense oligonucleotide and the 2025 KJ base-editing treatment as evidence that a one-patient genomic intervention can move from diagnosis to treatment on a radically compressed timeline. Study FDA's 2021 individualized-ASO guidances and 2026 plausible-mechanism framework as attempts to turn one-off rescue into a reusable regulatory architecture.

### V. Personalized cancer and immune therapies

Follow individualized neoantigen vaccines, autologous CAR-T, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and T-cell-receptor therapies. Distinguish therapies that are patient-specific in manufacturing from therapies that are genetically matched but standardized as a commercial product.

### VI. Personalized antimicrobials

Phage therapy makes personalization visible because the treatment can be selected against the patient's bacterial isolate. Expanded-access cases and compassionate-use cohorts show both the appeal and the evidentiary problem: the sickest patients, changing phage cocktails, concurrent antibiotics, limited controls and rapidly evolving manufacturing.

### VII. Cells, tissues, microbiota and organs

Compare products that sit near category boundaries. Human cells and tissues can be regulated differently depending on manipulation and homologous use. Fecal microbiota transplantation has operated under limited FDA enforcement discretion while standardized microbiota products have received biologics approval. Xenotransplantation began in living recipients through highly exceptional access before conventional trials. Each case shows that regulatory categories are part of the technology's economics.

### VIII. Manufacturing as the hidden regulator

For advanced therapies, chemistry, manufacturing and controls are not paperwork after the science. They determine whether the material administered to the patient is the material described by the hypothesis. Track identity, purity, potency, sterility, off-target analysis, vector or nanoparticle characterization, comparability, release testing, chain of identity and process deviations.

### IX. Markets in hope

Study who pays for experimental access, what manufacturers are permitted to charge, how clinics price uncertainty, how adverse-event care is financed and how travel creates selection by wealth. Distinguish cost recovery from profit-seeking and examine whether a learning obligation should attach to paid experimental treatment.

### X. A learning-access architecture

Test a policy alternative in which faster access is exchanged for mandatory independent review, public registration, standardized adverse-event reporting, outcome capture, manufacturing traceability, transparent pricing and a defined mechanism for updating what is known. The aim is not a new slogan but a system in which every exceptional treatment makes the next exceptional treatment less exceptional.

## Source discipline

- Prefer statutes, administrative rules, regulator guidance, approval documents, trial registries, peer-reviewed case reports and original clinical studies.
- Treat law-firm analysis and journalism as interpretation, not as the source of the legal rule.
- Never describe Mexico as a regulatory vacuum. When discussing weak enforcement, unauthorized clinics or legal ambiguity, say exactly which layer is weak or unclear.
- Never state that Montana law overrides FDA authority. Describe the state authorization and the unresolved or potentially conflicting federal layer separately.
- Distinguish Phase 1 completion from evidence of efficacy. A Phase 1 study can establish useful early safety information without showing that a treatment works.
- Distinguish an individualized product from a personalized selection rule. A bespoke ASO sequence is not the same kind of personalization as selecting a commercial therapy with a biomarker.
- Distinguish compassionate treatment outcomes from efficacy claims. A dramatic recovery can matter clinically while remaining causally uncertain.
- Record whether a cited source describes approved clinical use, a formal trial, expanded access, Right to Try, enforcement discretion, or a commercial intervention outside those pathways.
- Do not provide readers with instructions for obtaining unapproved therapies or evading regulators. The book is about institutions, evidence and incentives, not a consumer guide to experimental treatment.
- For every major claim, keep a note titled **What would make this false?**
