# Mexico clinical-research regulation — 2026 evidence note

Current evidence cutoff: August 31, 2026.

This note supports Chapters 4 and 18 of *Right To Try*. Its purpose is narrow: establish what current COFEPRIS materials say about Mexico's formal clinical-research authorization and transparency system, and separate those facts from harder empirical questions about enforcement capacity and the commercial market for experimental treatment.

## Primary-source floor

### COFEPRIS — clinical-trial authorization process

**Updated:** August 26, 2026  
https://www.gob.mx/cofepris/acciones-y-programas/proceso-de-autorizacion-de-ensayos-clinicos-y-sus-enmiendas-o-modificaciones

COFEPRIS states that evaluation and authorization of human-research protocols and their amendments are core activities intended to protect participants, preserve scientific quality and provide timely access to new therapeutic alternatives. The agency directs applicants to the formal protocol-authorization and amendment procedures and states that submissions must comply with applicable Mexican and international clinical-research requirements.

For the manuscript, this is direct primary-source evidence against describing Mexico as a jurisdiction where human clinical research simply lacks federal authorization requirements.

### COFEPRIS — public clinical-trial information and RNEC

**Updated:** August 26, 2026  
https://www.gob.mx/cofepris/acciones-y-programas/informacion-publica-de-ensayos-clinicos

COFEPRIS describes the Registro Nacional de Ensayos Clínicos (RNEC) as a digital platform intended to promote transparency and public access to technical information about clinical trials authorized in Mexico. The agency states that RNEC has existed since 2013 and that RNEC 2.0 was launched in December 2023 together with the DIGIPRiS online-regulation platform.

The current public page separates records for trials authorized from 2024 onward in RNEC 2.0 from the 2013–2023 legacy registry and also publishes lists of clinical-trial authorization applications, including 2026 lists.

This supports a practical verification rule used in the manuscript: if a commercial provider describes an intervention as an authorized Mexican clinical study, that claim should have an independently checkable regulatory or registry anchor where the relevant public system exposes one.

### COFEPRIS — 2026 interinstitutional clinical-research reforms

**Published:** January 16, 2026  
https://www.gob.mx/cofepris/es/articulos/mexico-fortalece-la-coordinacion-interinstitucional-para-agilizar-y-robustecer-la-investigacion-clinica

COFEPRIS reported work with the Secretaría de Salud, the coordinating commission for national health institutes and high-specialty hospitals, and CONBIOÉTICA on implementation of NOM-262-SSA1-2024 on Good Clinical Practices.

The agency announcement identified three specific initiatives relevant to the book:

- implementation work around the new Good Clinical Practice standard;
- a pilot intended to authorize priority clinical-research protocols within **up to 45 days**; and
- strengthened review of informed-consent formats and research-ethics committees.

This is useful evidence for a more precise claim than “Mexico is permissive.” The regulator was publicly presenting speed, ethics review and formal clinical-research quality as objectives to be improved together.

**Important limitation:** an agency announcement of a 45-day priority-review pilot is not evidence that all clinical trials are reviewed within 45 days, or that the pilot achieved a particular median review time. Treat it as evidence of the announced program and regulatory direction unless outcome data are separately verified.

### COFEPRIS and CONBIOÉTICA — implementation training after legal reform

**Published:** July 24, 2026  
https://www.gob.mx/cofepris/es/articulos/cofepris-y-conbioetica-realizaron-capacitacion-para-agilizar-el-ingreso-evaluacion-y-autorizacion-de-protocolos-de-investigacion-clinica

COFEPRIS and CONBIOÉTICA reported training involving research and ethics committees, public health institutions, industry groups and other participants on reforms to the Ley General de Salud concerning clinical studies. The stated aim was to help implement the new provisions and facilitate submission, evaluation and authorization of clinical-research protocols.

This supports the manuscript's argument that Mexico's formal system is evolving toward more structured and potentially faster legitimate development rather than competing solely through regulatory gaps.

### COFEPRIS — current clinical-trial portal

**Updated:** July 8, 2026  
https://www.gob.mx/cofepris/acciones-y-programas/ensayos-clinicos-protocolos-de-investigacion-en-seres-humanos-397098

The current COFEPRIS clinical-trial portal describes clinical studies as research in volunteers intended to evaluate safety, efficacy, tolerability and quality of investigational products before commercial availability. It links the authorization process, public protocol information, RNEC and other oversight resources.

The agency's current materials also distinguish procedural modalities for medicines, biologics and biotechnology products; bioequivalence studies; and “new resources” including materials, grafts, transplantation, prostheses and physical, chemical or surgical procedures in humans.

That procedural detail matters for *Right To Try* because “experimental medicine” is not one regulatory category. Different technologies can enter different formal pathways even inside the same national system.

## What these sources establish

The primary sources support the following manuscript propositions:

1. Mexico has a federal clinical-research authorization system administered through COFEPRIS.
2. Authorized clinical trials can have public regulatory/registry records through RNEC and RNEC 2.0.
3. Mexico has been actively revising and operationalizing clinical-research governance in 2026, including Good Clinical Practice implementation, ethics/consent work and faster-review initiatives.
4. A market in treatments available to foreign patients can coexist with a formal regulatory system. Availability by itself does not establish regulatory approval, trial authorization or legal compliance.
5. Regulatory competition can occur through **capability and speed inside a formal pathway**, not only through weak rules or non-enforcement.

## What these sources do not establish

These materials do **not** by themselves establish:

- how consistently rules are enforced across all Mexican clinics, regions or modalities;
- the real-world median time required to authorize a clinical trial;
- that the announced 45-day pilot met its target in every or any particular case;
- the regulatory status of a specific commercial intervention unless that product, procedure, facility or protocol is checked directly;
- that a clinic's facility license constitutes authorization or evidence for every intervention it sells;
- that an RNEC listing establishes efficacy; or
- that unauthorized experimental-treatment markets have disappeared.

COFEPRIS pages are also agency self-descriptions. They are the correct primary source for the agency's formal processes and announced initiatives, but they should be paired with enforcement records, independent scholarship, court or administrative material, and product-specific records when the manuscript makes claims about implementation rather than formal structure.

## Chapter-level inference

The strongest defensible inference remains the one the book already favors:

> Mexico is more analytically useful as a regulated system with variable implementation, evolving capacity and a cross-border commercial market than as a “regulatory vacuum.”

The 2026 evidence adds a second point:

> A jurisdiction can respond to demand for faster experimental medicine by making the legitimate research pathway faster and more legible. That is a different form of regulatory competition from simply tolerating a gray market.

Future revisions should test whether the 2026 reforms actually changed authorization times, registry completeness, ethics-review quality, enforcement and the behavior of clinics serving foreign patients. The existence of a reform program should not be mistaken for proof of its success.
