# Federal Right to Try reporting — evidence note

Current-law cutoff: August 31, 2026.

This note supports Chapter 16, **The Data We Lose**. It separates what manufacturers must report to FDA from what FDA publishes publicly.

## Primary sources

### 21 U.S.C. § 360bbb-0a — Right to Try statute

https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/360bbb-0a

The statute creates two connected rules that matter for the chapter.

First, subsection (c) generally prevents FDA from using a clinical outcome associated with Right to Try to delay or adversely affect ordinary drug or biologic review unless FDA determines that use of the outcome is critical to determining safety or the sponsor asks FDA to use it. A safety-critical determination requires written notice and a public-health justification and may not be delegated below the relevant FDA center director.

Second, subsection (d) requires the manufacturer or sponsor to submit an annual summary covering use under Right to Try. The statutory summary includes the number of doses supplied, the number of patients treated, the uses for which the drug was made available, and known serious adverse events. FDA must also post an annual public summary organized around whether Right to Try clinical outcomes were used under the safety exception, used at the sponsor's request, or not used in marketing review.

**Interpretive caution:** the statute intentionally limits how adverse Right to Try outcomes can affect development. A zero in the public table's “used” columns does not establish that the underlying observations were scientifically worthless or ignored internally by a sponsor.

### FDA — Right to Try

https://www.fda.gov/patients/learn-about-expanded-access-and-other-treatment-options/right-try

FDA states that it does not review or approve individual Right to Try requests and that its role includes receiving and posting specified information. The agency's current reporting instructions say mandatory annual reporting includes:

- eligible investigational drug name;
- IND number or numbers;
- number of doses supplied;
- number of patients treated;
- disease or condition the drug is intended to treat; and
- serious-adverse-event information.

Reports are due annually by March 31. FDA uses Form FDA 5023, with different submission routes for CDER- and CBER-regulated products.

### FDA — Right to Try Annual Reporting Summary

https://www.fda.gov/patients/learn-about-expanded-access-and-other-treatment-options/right-try-annual-reporting-summary

FDA's public consolidated table was current through calendar year 2025 as of this note.

| Reporting period | Outcomes used by FDA under safety exception | Outcomes used at sponsor request | Outcomes not used in marketing review |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2018–2022 | 0 | 0 | 12 |
| 2023 | 0 | 0 | 4 |
| 2024 | 0 | 0 | 5 |
| 2025 | 0 | 0 | 6 |

The units in the public table are **eligible drugs and biological products**, not patients.

The public table does **not** expose the number of patients treated even though manufacturers and sponsors are required to submit patient counts to FDA.

Do not add 12 + 4 + 5 + 6 and describe the result as a count of unique products. The same product can be represented in multiple annual reporting periods.

The defensible manuscript claim is narrower: through the 2025 reporting period, FDA's public consolidated table reported zero products for which Right to Try clinical outcomes were used through the statutory safety exception and zero for which a sponsor requested use of those outcomes; every product count shown for those periods appeared in the “not used” category.

## What this evidence can establish

- Federal Right to Try is not literally data-free; regulator-facing annual reporting exists.
- The regulator-facing report is more granular than the public consolidated table because it includes patient counts, doses, intended uses, and known serious adverse events.
- The public annual summary is structured around whether clinical outcomes entered marketing review, not around reconstructing a public patient-level or case-level denominator.
- Public reporting can therefore satisfy a statutory transparency requirement while remaining insufficient to answer basic public questions such as how many patients were treated in total.

## What this evidence cannot establish

- It cannot establish how many unique patients have ever received Right to Try treatment from the public consolidated table alone.
- It cannot establish that Right to Try cases generated no useful private or clinical learning.
- It cannot establish that an outcome was irrelevant to a sponsor merely because the public table says it was not used in FDA marketing review.
- It cannot establish a causal comparison between Right to Try and expanded access.

The chapter should preserve these distinctions. The useful argument is about the architecture of public learning, not an unsupported claim that no one learned anything from a Right to Try case.
