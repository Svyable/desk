# Chapter Briefs and Falsification Notes

## 1 — The Border of Permission

**Job:** Establish the book's central object: the border at which the same patient, diagnosis and treatment become legally, practically or commercially different because the jurisdiction changes. Open with the contrast between a formal regulatory pathway and a marketed cross-border intervention. Introduce Montana's experimental-treatment-center framework and Mexico's medical-tourism reputation without flattening either into caricature.

**Evidence:** Montana SB 535 and final rules; FDA expanded access; COFEPRIS clinical-trial and facility-licensing materials; literature on stem-cell tourism.

**What would make this false?** If geography is mostly incidental because manufacturers and federal rules determine access everywhere, the book must reduce the importance it assigns to jurisdictional arbitrage.

## 2 — What Right to Try Actually Means

**Job:** Untangle ordinary trials, expanded access, emergency INDs, federal Right to Try, state right-to-try laws and commercial unapproved treatment. Explain why the phrase “right to try” promises more than the statutes deliver: no law can force a manufacturer to supply a product, create a specialist, manufacture a one-off dose or make an insurer pay.

**Evidence:** FDA Right to Try page; FDA expanded-access overview; FDA expanded-access forms and 99% proceed statistic; federal statute and state-law examples.

**What would make this false?** If Right to Try has become a large, routinely used pathway that materially outperforms expanded access on speed or access, the chapter should not describe it primarily as a political-symbolic intervention.

## 3 — Montana's Experiment

**Job:** Treat Montana as a serious institutional experiment. Trace the 2015 law, 2023 expansion, 2025 SB 535 and 2026 rules. Explain licensed experimental treatment centers, Phase 1 threshold, independent review, adverse-event monitoring, informed consent, health-freedom/access requirement and state noninterference. Then place federal questions beside, not beneath, the state regime.

**Evidence:** Montana Code §§ 50-5-250, 50-5-251, 50-12-102 through 110; SB 535 enrolled bill; ARM experimental-treatment-center rules; DPHHS implementation materials; Goodwin and Duane Morris analyses for unresolved federal issues.

**What would make this false?** If federal agencies formally bless the Montana model or courts clearly preempt it, the chapter's description of legal uncertainty must be updated.

## 4 — Mexico Is Not a Regulatory Vacuum

**Job:** Correct the lazy story that patients travel to Mexico because “anything goes.” Show a federal regulator, authorized-trial process, national registry, ethics requirements, facility licensing and enforcement actions. Then ask why an international market in weakly evidenced or unauthorized interventions can nevertheless exist: enforcement capacity, category ambiguity, private-sector opacity, advertising and cross-border demand.

**Evidence:** COFEPRIS clinical-trial process; RNEC; 2025 ICH guidance adoption; 2026 NOM-262 implementation work; health-establishment licensing; 2015 stem-cell-clinic suspension; stem-cell tourism literature.

**What would make this false?** If current enforcement data show that the commercial gray market has largely disappeared, historical tourism examples should not be presented as the contemporary norm.

## 5 — One Patient, One Drug

**Job:** Tell the milasen story as the moment the drug-development unit visibly shrank toward one person. Explain diagnosis, splice defect, ASO design, preclinical work, manufacturing, expanded-access IND and the distinction between a drug that is customized and a platform that may eventually be standardized.

**Evidence:** Kim et al., NEJM 2019; FDA individualized-ASO administrative, nonclinical, CMC and clinical guidances; rare-disease guidance collection.

**What would make this false?** If individualized ASOs prove too expensive, slow, unsafe or biologically narrow to generalize beyond exceptional cases, the chapter should frame milasen as an important demonstration rather than the start of a broad therapeutic category.

## 6 — Editing KJ

**Job:** Use the 2025 patient-specific base-editing treatment for CPS1 deficiency to show how far the platform idea has moved. Separate the reusable machinery from the patient-specific guide and target. Emphasize the compressed diagnosis-to-treatment timeline, regulatory collaboration and the limits of inference from one patient.

**Evidence:** Musunuru et al., NEJM 2025; NIH release; CHOP follow-up; FDA 2026 individualized-therapy framework.

**What would make this false?** If later follow-up shows major delayed toxicity or little durable benefit, the case remains important for regulatory speed but not as evidence of therapeutic success.

## 7 — The Vaccine Made From Your Tumor

**Job:** Explore a different personalization problem: the product is manufactured from information unique to the patient's tumor. Follow neoantigen identification, vaccine manufacture and the logistical race against recurrence. Compare this with autologous cell therapies in which each lot begins with the patient's own tissue.

**Evidence:** Rojas et al., Nature 2023 pancreatic cancer individualized mRNA vaccine; current clinical-trial records for autogene cevumeran and V940; FDA materials on autologous cellular products.

**What would make this false?** If individualized cancer vaccines fail to outperform standardized immunotherapy or prove too slow for routine oncology, the manufacturing model may remain technically impressive without becoming a major clinical category.

## 8 — The Virus That Fits the Bacterium

**Job:** Make phage therapy the book's clearest example of biological matching at the bedside. The patient's bacterial isolate can determine which phage or cocktail is useful. Tell the Tom Patterson case cautiously, then widen to compassionate-use cohorts and the difficulty of turning rescue cases into general evidence.

**Evidence:** Schooley et al. 2017 case report; UC San Diego/IPATH materials; FDA-NIAID phage workshop; compassionate-use cohorts for M. abscessus and customized phages.

**What would make this false?** If standardized phage mixtures, engineered broad-host phages or conventional antibiotics make isolate-level matching unnecessary, the personalization thesis should narrow.

## 9 — Cells at the Edge

**Job:** Show how the word “cell” hides multiple regulatory worlds. Compare minimally manipulated homologous-use HCT/Ps, autologous cell therapies that require full biologics oversight, CAR-T, TIL therapy and commercial clinics that market “stem cells” for broad conditions. Explain why manufacturing and intended use matter more than the marketing label.

**Evidence:** FDA HCT/P guidance; regenerative-medicine framework; CAR-T guidance; Amtagvi approval; Tecelra approval; FDA warning letters; COFEPRIS cell/regenerative-medicine materials.

**What would make this false?** If courts or regulators substantially redraw the HCT/P boundary, update the category analysis rather than treating current guidance as permanent.

## 10 — The Organ Before Approval

**Job:** Use living-recipient pig-organ xenotransplantation to explore a modality too large to fit comfortably into the drug metaphor. The intervention includes the animal source, genomic edits, pathogen controls, surgery, immunosuppression and lifelong surveillance. Ask what “product approval” means when the therapy is a supply chain plus a biological system.

**Evidence:** NEJM pig-kidney reports; FDA xenotransplantation guidance and public materials; institutional reports from authorized cases and trials.

**What would make this false?** If xenotransplantation rapidly standardizes into conventional approved products, treat the early expanded-access era as transitional rather than intrinsically exceptional.

## 11 — The Microbiome Exception

**Job:** Follow fecal microbiota transplantation from physician practice under FDA enforcement discretion toward standardized approved microbiota products. This is a clean case of the same biological idea occupying different regulatory categories depending on processing, indication and productization.

**Evidence:** FDA 2022 enforcement-discretion guidance; safety alerts; Rebyota 2022 approval; Vowst 2023 approval.

**What would make this false?** If enforcement discretion is withdrawn or the remaining practice converges on approved products, update the chapter's description of the exception.

## 12 — When Manufacturing Becomes Medicine

**Job:** Move the reader from scientific idea to release testing. For a mass-market pill, manufacturing is often invisible. For a one-patient ASO, gene editor, phage cocktail or autologous cell lot, manufacturing may determine whether treatment can happen at all. Explain CMC as a clinical discipline.

**Evidence:** FDA individualized-ASO CMC guidance; CAR-T CMC guidance; gene-therapy manufacturing guidance; published personalized-treatment protocols.

**What would make this false?** If platform manufacturing becomes sufficiently automated and validated that lot-specific uncertainty collapses, the bottleneck may shift away from CMC toward diagnosis, delivery or reimbursement.

## 13 — The Platform and the Patient

**Job:** Develop the book's central regulatory design problem: what evidence can be inherited from the platform and what must be regenerated for the patient-specific component? Compare ASO backbone chemistry, lipid nanoparticles, editing enzymes, guide RNAs, vaccine manufacturing and phage banks. Introduce FDA's plausible-mechanism framework as a move toward platform-level accumulation of evidence.

**Evidence:** FDA 2026 individualized-therapy framework; 2021 ASO guidances; KJ case; peer-reviewed platform discussions.

**What would make this false?** If supposedly minor patient-specific changes produce highly nonlocal safety or efficacy differences, platform inheritance should be treated more conservatively.

## 14 — The Price of a Chance

**Job:** Follow money through experimental treatment. Right-to-try laws may allow cost recovery; Montana permits payment arrangements; travel adds lodging and caregiver costs; insurers may exclude the experimental intervention and sometimes related complications. Ask whether payment changes the ethical character of compassionate treatment.

**Evidence:** Montana statutes and consent rules; FDA charging guidance; insurance/legal literature; published cost analyses for cell and gene therapies where relevant.

**What would make this false?** If broad public or private coverage emerges for experimental access, the chapter's emphasis on wealth selection should be reduced.

## 15 — Consent Under Desperation

**Job:** Treat consent as a market design problem, not a signed form. A patient's willingness to accept risk can be completely rational while still being vulnerable to distorted probabilities, provider conflicts, loss framing and therapeutic misconception. Distinguish autonomy from the seller's right to make unsupported efficacy claims.

**Evidence:** statutory informed-consent requirements; bioethics literature on expanded access and therapeutic misconception; ISSCR guidance on unproven interventions; adverse-event cases from unauthorized cell therapies.

**What would make this false?** If high-quality empirical work shows that patients in paid experimental programs consistently understand uncertainty and alternatives as well as trial participants, claims about systematic consent weakness should be narrowed.

## 16 — The Data We Lose

**Job:** Ask what the scientific system learns from exceptional access. Clinical trials are designed to create interpretable evidence. Compassionate use prioritizes treatment. Paid cross-border care may create neither public data nor structured follow-up. Show how losing denominator, comparator, protocol and adverse-event information can make thousands of interventions add up to very little knowledge.

**Evidence:** FDA reporting requirements; published right-to-try critiques and data; compassionate-use case series; trial-registration systems; COFEPRIS RNEC.

**What would make this false?** If state programs or private networks publish complete, standardized outcomes and safety data, they may become genuine learning systems rather than evidence sinks.

## 17 — The Federalism Trap

**Job:** Explain why U.S. medicine divides authority awkwardly: states regulate clinicians and facilities; the federal government regulates drugs, biologics, devices and interstate commerce. Montana therefore can create a facility and professional-law framework without necessarily eliminating federal product-law exposure. Avoid predicting litigation outcomes.

**Evidence:** Montana law; FD&C Act structure; Public Health Service Act; FDA Right to Try; contemporary legal analyses; any future federal statements or litigation.

**What would make this false?** A definitive federal statute, agency policy or court decision could resolve the ambiguity in either direction.

## 18 — The Medical-Tourism Machine

**Job:** Follow the full commercial system that forms when access is geographically uneven: digital advertising, patient communities, brokers, destination clinics, package pricing, testimonials, financing and aftercare shifted back to home systems. Keep legitimate medical travel separate from unproven-intervention tourism.

**Evidence:** academic reviews of stem-cell tourism; regulator enforcement; marketing studies; cross-border-care literature; documented patient-harm cases.

**What would make this false?** If most experimental cross-border care moves into registered trials or authorized pathways, the “machine” framing should become historical.

## 19 — A Better Edge

**Job:** Build a concrete alternative: faster access tied to learning obligations. Possible components include public registration, independent review, standardized consent, transparent pricing, manufacturing traceability, minimum evidence gates, common adverse-event definitions, outcome reporting, remote follow-up, stopping rules and a mechanism for regulator learning across variants.

**Evidence:** best elements of FDA expanded access, Montana's facility rules, trial registries, platform-guidance proposals, adaptive regulatory programs and post-market surveillance systems.

**What would make this false?** If the added obligations slow access until the model recreates the bottlenecks it was meant to solve, simplify it. The goal is not to build a second FDA around every patient.

## 20 — Right to Try, Right to Know

**Job:** Resolve the moral argument without pretending it has a clean answer. A dying or severely ill patient can have a strong claim to choose uncertainty. The next patient has a claim that today's choice not disappear into private anecdote. The seller has obligations different from the patient's autonomy. End on a right-to-know principle: exceptional access should leave behind usable knowledge.

**Evidence:** synthesis of the book; no new factual dependence unless needed for the closing case.

**What would make this false?** If evidence capture itself materially deters manufacturers or clinicians from offering compassionate access, the balance between learning obligations and access must be reconsidered rather than treated as costless.
