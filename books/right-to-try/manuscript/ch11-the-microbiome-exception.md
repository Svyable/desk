# The Microbiome Exception

For years, one of the strangest experimental products in American medicine could be made in a bathroom.

Fecal microbiota transplantation sounds less like advanced biotechnology than the return of a very old medicine. The basic idea is direct: transfer intestinal microbial communities from a donor into a patient whose own microbiome has been disrupted, most famously in recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection.

The clinical effect can be dramatic.

Patients with recurrent C. difficile can become trapped in a cycle of antibiotics, temporary improvement, and relapse. Restoring a healthier microbial ecosystem can break that cycle. By the time formal regulatory categories caught up, physicians and patients already had compelling evidence that something medically important was happening.

The problem was deciding what the something was.

Is stool a drug?

Is it tissue?

Is the therapeutic agent a community of organisms, their metabolites, the ecological relationships among them, or all of the above?

How does a regulator define the active ingredient of an ecosystem?

The FDA treated fecal microbiota for transplantation as a biological drug requiring an investigational new drug application, but it also exercised enforcement discretion for certain uses in C. difficile infection not responding to standard therapies when specified conditions, including informed consent, were met.

That temporary accommodation lasted long enough to become its own clinical institution.

FMT occupied an unusual middle ground. It was not simply approved commercial medicine. It was not confined to ordinary clinical trials. Physicians could provide it under a limited federal policy because the clinical need and accumulating evidence made strict enforcement of the IND requirement difficult to justify in every case.

This was regulation by declared restraint.

The agency kept legal authority while choosing not to enforce it fully under defined conditions.

That is a different mechanism from Right to Try.

No legislature created a patient right. No state declared the intervention free of federal oversight. The regulator itself acknowledged that a rigid application of the ordinary investigational framework could obstruct care and announced a narrower practical boundary.

Enforcement discretion is an awkward tool because it creates access through something close to a promise not to prosecute.

It can also be remarkably useful when a field is changing faster than the categories around it.

FMT demonstrates both sides.

The biological material is variable. Donors differ. Microbial communities differ. Collection, screening, processing, storage, and administration methods differ. A treatment assembled from one donor on one day is not chemically identical to a treatment assembled from another donor a week later.

The variation made conventional product standardization difficult.

It also created real safety risks.

Pathogens can be transmitted. Donor screening can miss organisms that are unknown, newly important, or not included in the panel. Antibiotic-resistant bacteria can travel with the transplant. The same ecological complexity that makes FMT therapeutically interesting makes it difficult to reduce to a list of known components.

Safety alerts followed.

The lesson was not that FMT should never have been used before a standardized product existed. For recurrent C. difficile, the clinical need and apparent benefit were substantial. The lesson was that biological plausibility and strong efficacy signals do not erase manufacturing and donor-control problems.

The stool is part of the treatment.

So is the screening system.

That becomes obvious when one asks what makes a donor acceptable. Medical history matters. Recent antibiotics matter. Infectious exposures matter. Laboratory testing matters. The emergence of new pathogens can change the screening protocol. A donor who was acceptable under yesterday's risk model may not be acceptable under today's.

The product is partly a surveillance system.

This resembles xenotransplantation more than the physical materials suggest. Both involve transfer of complex living biological material from another organism. Both create infectious-risk questions that cannot be answered by characterizing one active molecule. Both require source control.

FMT also shows how an experimental category can move toward conventional productization.

In 2022 the FDA approved Rebyota, a rectally administered microbiota-based product for prevention of recurrent C. difficile infection in defined patients. In 2023 it approved Vowst, an orally administered microbiota product.

The field did not move from “unregulated poop” to “regulated medicine” in one step.

It moved through overlapping forms: physician-provided FMT, enforcement discretion, clinical research, donor-bank systems, manufacturing controls, and eventually approved standardized products.

That path is important because it shows what the edge of approval can become.

Exceptional access need not be a permanent alternative to regulation. It can be a bridge while a field learns which parts need standardization.

This bridge can fail in two directions.

One failure is premature closure. Regulators can insist on a product model before enough is known to define one, making useful medical practice inaccessible while commercial development catches up.

The other failure is permanent exception. Clinicians and businesses can become comfortable operating under a looser category and resist standardization even after the evidence and technology make it possible.

The microbiome forced that negotiation.

It also complicates the idea that personalized medicine must always mean matching a product to a person's genome.

A microbial ecosystem is personalized in a different sense. The relevant state of the patient includes thousands of microbial species and strains, antibiotic history, diet, immunity, gastrointestinal anatomy, and recent exposures. The intervention attempts to alter an ecosystem rather than correct a single mutation.

That makes prediction difficult.

Why does one patient respond and another not? Which organisms engraft? Is diversity itself important? Are particular functions more important than particular species? Can a defined consortium reproduce the effect of donor-derived material? Do bacteriophages in the donor material matter? Do metabolites carry part of the therapeutic function?

The more science learns, the less obvious it becomes that stool itself is the final technology.

The crude intervention can reveal the therapeutic principle before the refined product exists.

Medicine has seen this pattern before.

Early vaccines were crude compared with modern recombinant products. Blood transfusion preceded modern blood banking. Organ transplantation preceded the immunology and preservation systems that made it routine. A useful intervention can arrive before institutions understand which components must be controlled.

That history should make regulators humble.

It should not make them passive.

The role of regulation during the crude phase is to identify the dimensions of variation most likely to harm patients and build standards around them without pretending the underlying science is already complete.

FMT required donor screening, informed consent, pathogen precautions, and clinical monitoring before anyone could fully define the microbial mechanism.

Personalized gene therapies may require standardized manufacturing and off-target analysis before the full relationship between sequence and outcome is understood.

Phage therapy may require quality-controlled banks and susceptibility testing before the optimal cocktail rules are known.

The pattern is repeated uncertainty with nonnegotiable floors.

This is where experimental-treatment laws need more nuance than a Phase 1 threshold can provide.

What would Phase 1 mean for a biological ecosystem?

A specific manufactured microbiota product can complete clinical phases. Physician-provided FMT is not one stable product. A donor-derived preparation can vary from administration to administration. A state law built around a conventional investigational drug may fit the commercial microbiota product and fit the practice-based intervention poorly.

Regulatory categories can therefore determine which version of a scientific idea gets financed.

If a standardized commercial product has a clear approval path while decentralized physician-prepared material sits in legal uncertainty, investment will flow toward the standardized product. That can improve quality and make trials possible. It can also increase price and concentrate control.

The patient sees two ways of altering the microbiome.

The market sees two different assets.

One can be patented or protected through manufacturing know-how, branded, approved, reimbursed, and distributed. The other can be performed by clinicians using donor material and protocols that are harder to monopolize.

Regulation can create scarcity around one form without changing the underlying biology.

This is not inherently bad. Standardization can be worth paying for. A product with validated manufacturing, consistent dose, traceability, and post-market surveillance may be safer and easier to use than ad hoc preparation.

But the economic consequence should be visible.

When experimental access is restricted, society is not only reducing risk. It may be selecting a business model.

The same dynamic matters in Montana.

A licensed experimental treatment center could become a place where modalities that sit awkwardly between medical practice and regulated product are offered under state law. The center might reduce the pressure to force every intervention immediately into a conventional commercial form.

That could preserve useful experimentation.

It could also attract therapies specifically because their sponsors prefer an experimental market to the cost of approval.

The microbiome provides a test for that incentive.

Once approved microbiota products exist, an experimental provider should have to explain why its alternative remains outside ordinary approval or standard care. Perhaps the product addresses a different disease. Perhaps the patient's condition falls outside the approved indication. Perhaps the therapy uses a meaningfully different mechanism. Perhaps a clinical trial is the right route.

“Personalized” should not become a permanent exemption from comparison with available approved alternatives.

Right-to-try frameworks usually require that approved options be exhausted or unsuitable in some way because the moral justification for uncertainty weakens when a known effective treatment exists.

That principle becomes especially important when the experimental option is commercially attractive.

A clinic should not be able to turn patient preference into an excuse to bypass a safer, effective standard merely because the experimental product is more profitable.

The patient can still choose risk.

The provider still owes a truthful comparison.

FMT also highlights the problem of informed consent under uncertain infectious risk.

A patient can understand that an intervention might fail. It is harder to explain the possibility that the biological material contains a hazard no current test detects. This is uncertainty in the strict sense: not a known risk with a percentage, but a category of unknowns created by biological complexity.

Consent documents often respond by becoming longer.

Length is not comprehension.

A more honest explanation may be simpler: this material comes from another person's intestinal microbial community; screening reduces known infectious risks but cannot eliminate all risks; the biological contents are complex and not fully characterized; approved alternatives or products may exist depending on the indication.

The goal is not to frighten the patient into refusal.

It is to describe the uncertainty at the level where it actually lives.

That principle should guide experimental medicine generally.

A personalized gene editor should not bury the uncertainty inside fifty pages of molecular detail if the central unknown is whether a sequence-specific off-target effect has been missed. A stem-cell clinic should not use twenty citations about regenerative biology to obscure the absence of controlled evidence for its product. A phage provider should not describe a century of phage research instead of the susceptibility of this patient's bacterium to this preparation.

Good consent compresses complexity without laundering it.

The microbiome is a particularly good teacher because no one can plausibly pretend to have complete mechanistic knowledge.

The field advanced anyway.

It advanced through a mixture of clinical observation, controlled studies, enforcement discretion, safety alerts, better screening, manufacturing innovation, and product approval. The sequence was messy. It was also productive.

That may be what good regulation looks like at an emerging frontier.

Not one perfect rule chosen in advance.

A series of adjustments that become stricter where harm is discovered and more permissive where experience justifies confidence.

The danger is that exceptional pathways are often designed as static political victories. A right is enacted. A threshold is defined. The statute remains while the science changes.

The microbiome suggests a different model.

Regulatory flexibility should have a memory.

When safety signals appear, requirements should tighten around the failure mode. When manufacturing becomes standardized, evidence from the platform should be reusable. When approved alternatives emerge, the justification for looser exception should change. When a product moves from crude biological material to a defined consortium, the regulatory framework should move with it.

The donor-bank phase deserves special attention because it shows how infrastructure can become a quasi-product before a regulator formally calls it one.

A stool bank can recruit donors, screen them, process material under common procedures, store preparations and distribute them to many clinical sites. The patient may never meet the donor. The treating physician may not know the detailed microbial composition of the dose. Standardization moves upstream into the bank.

That architecture solves some problems and creates others.

Centralized screening can be more consistent than every hospital maintaining a small local donor program. A bank can update testing protocols quickly, retain records and investigate adverse events across recipients. It can also become a single point through which a screening failure reaches many patients.

The value of centralization therefore depends on traceability.

If a new pathogen concern appears, the system should be able to identify which donor samples were collected during which period, which lots came from those donations, where the lots went, and which patients received them.

The same logic applies to any biological bank.

Phage banks need phage identity and host-range records.

Cell banks need provenance and characterization.

Xenotransplant source herds need lineage.

The microbiome simply makes the principle unusually visible because the starting material is so biologically dense.

Donor effects create an additional evidence problem.

If outcomes differ among preparations, is the difference random, patient-driven, procedural, or attributable to the donor community? A field that treats every donor preparation as interchangeable can miss meaningful heterogeneity. A field that treats every dose as completely unique can never aggregate evidence.

The right level of sameness has to be discovered empirically.

That discovery can be part of the regulatory process rather than a prerequisite for it.

A registry that links donor source, processing method and patient outcome can reveal whether certain variables matter enough to become future specifications. The rule can become more precise because treatment occurred under conditions that preserved the variation instead of hiding it.

This is regulation learning from practice.

Defined microbial consortia take the next step.

Instead of transferring a broad donor-derived ecosystem, developers can attempt to identify and manufacture a narrower set of organisms or spores with desired functions. The product becomes easier to characterize in some ways. It may become harder in others because the therapeutic effect of the crude ecosystem may have depended on interactions the defined product does not reproduce.

Refinement is not automatically improvement.

The history of biotechnology contains many attempts to isolate the active element of a complex biological phenomenon. Sometimes the purified mechanism works better. Sometimes the surrounding system was doing more than anyone realized.

This is why the coexistence of practice-based FMT and commercial microbiota products can be scientifically useful for a period.

They are not merely competitors.

They are different hypotheses about where the therapeutic value resides.

A mature evidence system should be able to compare them without assuming that the most productized intervention is necessarily the most effective or that the least processed intervention is inherently more natural and therefore better.

The economic pressure cuts both ways.

A company developing a defined product needs a pathway to recoup the costs of trials, manufacturing and distribution. A physician-based practice can have lower development costs but may impose more local quality-control burden. An insurer may prefer the predictability of an approved product. A patient may prefer the cost or accessibility of a physician-provided intervention.

Regulation determines which of these costs are visible to whom.

An approved product can look expensive because its price includes a commercial development system. A decentralized intervention can look cheap because some of its quality and surveillance work is absorbed by hospitals, donor programs or the absence of formal evidence generation.

Comparing sticker prices alone can therefore mislead.

The fair comparison includes the infrastructure required to know what happened.

Safety alerts provide the clearest demonstration of why that infrastructure matters.

When a transmissible organism is discovered after recipients have been treated, the system has to work backward. It needs donor records, testing history, lot information, recipient identification and clinical follow-up. A field that treats each administration as a private act of medical practice can struggle to reconstruct the network after the fact.

This is exactly the kind of problem that exceptional-treatment markets can create if they grow without shared traceability.

The absence of a conventional product does not mean the absence of a supply chain.

Once a supply chain exists, someone has to be able to audit it.

The microbiome therefore adds another item to the book's definition of a credible edge: recallability.

If new information appears tomorrow, can the system identify yesterday's exposed patients?

A personalized treatment is often described as the opposite of mass production, but a platform can distribute one hidden error across many personalized cases. The shared reagent, donor bank, algorithm, cell-processing step or laboratory assay may be the real common exposure.

Personalization can obscure common-mode risk.

Regulation has to look for the shared layer.

That is why the move from crude FMT toward standardized microbiota products is not simply a story about more control.

It is a story about discovering where common risk lives and deciding which parts of a variable biological intervention can be made repeatable enough to monitor.

An experimental system that cannot learn from its own exceptions becomes merely permissive.

An approval system that cannot absorb the lessons of exceptional care becomes merely slow.

FMT lived between those systems long enough to show why both need each other.
