# When Manufacturing Becomes Medicine

The treatment can be conceptually correct and still be dangerous because somebody cleaned the equipment badly.

This is not the glamorous failure mode of experimental medicine.

No one raises money around it. Families do not travel for it. Scientists do not give keynote lectures about correct labels, endotoxin limits, sterility assays, chain of identity, freezer temperatures, release specifications, or whether the potency test was validated on the same process used to make the patient's dose.

Yet these details are often the difference between a scientific idea and a medicine.

The pharmaceutical industry calls much of this work chemistry, manufacturing, and controls: CMC. The phrase sounds administrative. For personalized therapies, it is clinical care performed upstream.

A physician can understand the patient's disease perfectly. A molecular biologist can design the correct sequence. An animal model can show the intended effect. None of that guarantees that the material administered to the patient has the right identity, purity, potency, stability, or sterility.

Manufacturing is where the hypothesis becomes physical.

Traditional drugs conceal this because industrial production is so good at sameness.

A bottle of tablets feels like a commodity. The pills are expected to contain the labeled drug within a narrow range. The patient does not meet the chemists, process engineers, quality-control analysts, validation specialists, microbiologists, or inspectors whose work makes that expectation boring.

Personalized medicine makes the factory visible because the lot can be the patient.

An autologous cell therapy begins with biological material collected from one person. A manufacturing failure may eliminate that person's only dose. A one-patient antisense drug can be synthesized in a batch with no commercial inventory behind it. A patient-specific gene editor can use a guide sequence that has never entered the process before. A neoantigen vaccine may have a manufacturing window defined by the patient's recurrence risk. A phage cocktail may need to be changed because the infection evolves.

The economics of quality control change when there is no millionth unit.

In mass manufacturing, the cost of validating a process can be spread across enormous output. In one-person manufacturing, every fixed requirement lands heavily on one case.

That creates the central temptation of experimental access.

If the patient is dying and the batch is unique, perhaps some requirements can be relaxed.

Sometimes they can.

The problem is deciding which ones.

There is a category of evidence that protects against uncertainty about benefit. That evidence can often be reduced rationally when the patient has no good alternatives and the population is too small for conventional trials.

There is another category that protects against uncertainty about what was administered.

That floor is harder to negotiate.

A patient can say, “I understand that this gene editor may not improve my disease.”

A patient cannot meaningfully say, “I accept that nobody knows whether this vial contains the intended material,” when the provider could have tested identity.

The first is therapeutic risk.

The second is preventable ignorance.

Experimental medicine becomes safer when institutions learn to tell the difference.

Sterility is the obvious example. A contaminated intravenous product can cause catastrophic infection regardless of whether the therapeutic mechanism is brilliant. Endotoxin contamination can provoke serious reactions. Mislabeling an autologous cell product can expose a patient to someone else's cells. A potency assay that does not correlate with biological function can release a product that is chemically present but therapeutically inert.

These are not abstract regulatory concerns.

They are ways patients get hurt.

The hard cases arise because quality itself is not binary.

Every additional test consumes time and material. Some assays take days. Some require so much sample that performing them on a tiny batch leaves less product for the patient. Some results arrive after the treatment window has closed. Some specifications were designed for mass-market drugs and may not predict risk in a new modality.

Quality systems can become scientifically obsolete too.

A good regulator should therefore be willing to ask the same question about manufacturing rules that it asks about clinical evidence: what uncertainty does this requirement reduce, and is that reduction worth the delay or cost in this case?

The answer will differ by modality.

For individualized antisense oligonucleotides, much of the chemistry can be standardized. Synthesis methods, backbone modifications, purification processes, analytical techniques, container systems, and release testing can be reused. The sequence changes. That sequence can influence impurities, off-target binding, stability, and other characteristics, but the manufacturing platform can carry a large amount of prior knowledge.

For autologous cells, the starting material itself changes every time.

The patient's cells may be exhausted, diseased, old, recently exposed to chemotherapy, or collected under difficult clinical conditions. The process can be standardized while the input resists standardization. The manufacturer therefore needs in-process controls capable of recognizing when the lot is drifting away from a useful state.

For gene editing, the delivery system may be stable while the guide changes. The manufacturing question includes not only whether the guide was synthesized correctly but whether the final combination behaves as expected and whether sequence-specific impurities or functional changes matter.

For phages, the biological agent can replicate. The same property that makes the virus therapeutically useful complicates production. Bacterial hosts, purification, residual endotoxin, genomic characterization, and stability all matter.

For xenotransplantation, manufacturing extends into animal husbandry.

The source herd is part of quality control.

This diversity is why a single political rule such as “Phase 1 completed” cannot do the work of a manufacturing framework.

Phase 1 says something about prior human exposure to an investigational treatment. It does not tell a clinician whether today's personalized batch passed release.

A permissive state can lower the legal threshold for administering unapproved therapy and still require strict manufacturing discipline.

In fact, it probably has to.

The more a jurisdiction loosens proof-of-efficacy requirements, the more important product-quality requirements become because the patient is already accepting unusually high uncertainty on one dimension. Allowing avoidable uncertainty on every dimension would compound risk beyond what consent can reasonably absorb.

This is one of the most promising features of the Montana model if it develops in the right direction.

A licensed experimental treatment center gives the state an institutional object to inspect. Instead of policing experimental therapy only through after-the-fact professional discipline, the state can ask what policies, facilities, quality systems, review mechanisms, and reporting structures a center has before treatment begins.

The challenge is expertise.

A state health department that knows how to inspect a hospital is not automatically equipped to evaluate a bespoke oligonucleotide manufacturing chain or a cell-processing facility. The most consequential quality work may occur outside the treatment center entirely, at a contract manufacturer or academic laboratory in another state.

Facility regulation can create the appearance of control while the risky process sits somewhere else.

That is why advanced therapy does not map neatly onto geographic jurisdiction.

The treatment center is the last mile.

The product may have crossed many legal and physical borders before arrival.

Consider a personalized gene-editing program in a state experimental center. The patient's sequencing could be performed by a national laboratory. Computational design could run in a cloud environment. The guide RNA could be synthesized by a specialist manufacturer in another state. Lipid nanoparticle components could come from several suppliers. Final fill-and-finish might occur at a contract facility. Release testing could be split among laboratories. The material would then be shipped to the center.

Which regulator sees the whole chain?

Federal product regulation is partly designed to answer that question by attaching obligations to the investigational product and sponsor across sites.

A purely state-local model can struggle if it regulates only the endpoint.

This does not mean states have no role.

They can require centers to demonstrate provenance. Where was the product made? Under what quality system? What were the release criteria? Who reviewed deviations? Was chain of custody maintained? Does the center have the competence to store and administer the product as specified? Can it respond to a recall or safety alert?

The center need not become the manufacturer to be accountable for knowing who the manufacturer is.

That sounds elementary until one looks at markets for unproven treatments.

Patients are often given broad descriptions—“autologous stem cells,” “exosomes,” “peptides,” “immune cells,” “personalized vaccine”—without the kind of product-specific documentation that would be routine in regulated development.

The less standardized the treatment, the more specific the record should be.

Instead, commercial uncertainty can produce the opposite.

The name becomes vague because vagueness makes comparison difficult.

Manufacturing transparency is therefore a form of consumer protection.

A patient considering an experimental intervention does not need to audit every batch record personally. The patient needs confidence that someone independent and competent can.

That is the value of regulatory infrastructure when it works.

It converts invisible technical details into a credible assurance without requiring every patient to become a process engineer.

This is also why medical tourism creates a difficult information problem.

A treatment administered abroad may have been manufactured to excellent standards. Many countries have sophisticated pharmaceutical and biologics industries. Nationality tells us little about quality.

The problem arises when the patient's home clinicians cannot verify the product.

If a complication appears, what lot was given? What components did it contain? Were there known deviations? Did other patients from the same batch experience similar events? Is there a contact capable of supplying the information quickly?

A cross-border experimental market without interoperable records can turn manufacturing uncertainty into clinical uncertainty after the patient returns home.

This is avoidable.

A standardized treatment record could travel with the patient much like an implant card or vaccination record. It need not expose proprietary manufacturing secrets. It could identify the product or personalized construct, manufacturer, lot or batch, date of manufacture, release status, administration details, known safety information, and contact point for adverse-event follow-up.

The more experimental the intervention, the stronger the argument for such a record.

The commercial objection is that transparency can reveal too much.

Companies legitimately protect trade secrets. A personalized-treatment platform may derive much of its value from manufacturing know-how. Requiring public disclosure of every process parameter could destroy incentives to invest.

But traceability is not the same as full disclosure.

Airlines do not publish blueprints for jet engines to track which engine is on which aircraft. Pharmaceutical companies protect processes while regulators inspect facilities and lots. A patient can know who made the product and what regulatory category it occupies without receiving the recipe.

The goal is accountable opacity.

Not everyone needs to see everything.

Someone trustworthy needs the authority to see enough.

This becomes especially important when the provider and manufacturer are vertically integrated. A clinic that designs, manufactures, administers, evaluates, and sells the intervention can move quickly. It can also become its own closed evidentiary loop.

The same institution defines the product, judges the quality, explains the evidence, chooses the patient, records the outcome, and receives the payment.

That is too much epistemic power for a highly uncertain therapy unless independent checks are strong.

A state experimental-treatment review board can provide one check. External manufacturing audits can provide another. Mandatory adverse-event reporting provides another. Public registration can expose whether outcomes disappear.

The purpose is not to slow the integrated model until it resembles a large pharmaceutical company.

It is to break the places where one incentive can control the entire story.

Manufacturing also changes the economics of right-to-try access.

Federal expanded-access rules allow charging under defined conditions, generally oriented toward recovery of certain costs rather than ordinary commercial profit. State frameworks may create different payment possibilities. For personalized products, the cost can be substantial because the patient is funding a development and manufacturing run rather than buying marginal inventory.

This makes price hard to interpret.

A $250,000 charge could represent exploitative pricing for a weak intervention.

It could also reflect genuine costs of custom design, synthesis, quality testing, specialist labor, and inpatient care.

The amount alone does not tell us which.

Cost transparency matters because the experimental market lacks ordinary price discipline. There may be no competing product. Insurance may not pay. The patient has little bargaining leverage. Urgency weakens the ability to shop.

A serious experimental-treatment center should therefore be able to separate categories of cost.

Design.

Manufacturing.

Testing.

Clinical administration.

Hospital services.

Follow-up.

Contingency for complications.

Platform development should not be invisibly financed by one desperate family unless the arrangement is explicit.

This is not only a fairness issue.

It affects evidence.

When the price is high, clinics have a stronger incentive to keep the treatment commercially experimental. Approval can bring reimbursement, but it can also bring stricter evidence standards and price negotiation. A profitable bespoke-treatment business may prefer a world in which every patient is a unique exception and therefore no aggregate efficacy claim must ever be defended.

Manufacturing can become the justification for permanent uniqueness.

Each batch is different, so comparisons are impossible.

That claim should be resisted.

Manufacturing variation can be measured. Process parameters can be recorded. Product attributes can be compared. Clinical outcomes can be linked to those variables. Unique lots can still belong to a common learning system.

CAR-T already proves this at scale.

Every autologous lot begins with a different patient. The field nevertheless studies manufacturing success rates, cell phenotypes, potency, toxicities, response, durability, and process improvements across populations.

Personalization is compatible with industrial learning.

The hardest manufacturing decision often appears when the batch is almost good enough.

A specification is missed narrowly. A potency assay lands below the predefined threshold. A sterility result is not yet final. A cell count is lower than planned. An impurity is slightly above the target. The patient has already received conditioning therapy or is deteriorating quickly. There may be no second batch.

Now quality and medicine collide directly.

In ordinary commercial production, a failed lot is rejected and replaced. In a one-patient program, replacement may not exist. The decision becomes whether to administer material that does not meet the original plan because the alternative is no treatment.

That is not a loophole to be normalized.

It is a foreseeable category that needs governance before the crisis arrives.

Who can authorize a release under deviation? What data must support the decision? Which specifications are absolute because they protect against unacceptable hazards, and which can be reconsidered because they are conservative proxies? Does the patient receive a fresh consent discussion describing the deviation? Is the event reported so future specifications can improve?

A center that answers these questions only after the batch fails will make the decision under maximum emotional pressure.

The better system precommits.

This is another form of stopping rule, except the question is whether to start.

Some release criteria should be nearly inviolable. Identity cannot become optional because the patient is sick. A credible sign of dangerous contamination cannot be wished away. Other criteria can be scientifically contestable, particularly early in a new platform when the relationship between an assay threshold and clinical performance is not yet well established.

The distinction belongs in the quality system rather than in bedside improvisation.

Small-batch manufacturing also changes how assays should be designed.

A test that consumes ten percent of a commercial batch is trivial when thousands of doses remain. The same test can be prohibitive when the entire patient-specific lot is tiny. Developers may need low-volume analytical methods, non-destructive assays, validated in-process measurements or retained samples planned from the beginning.

The analytical platform can become as important as the production platform.

This creates another opportunity for evidence inheritance. If a particular assay has been shown across many related personalized products to predict a meaningful quality attribute, later variants can rely on it with more confidence. If an assay repeatedly fails to distinguish successful from unsuccessful lots, insisting on it forever adds cost without adding knowledge.

Quality regulation should be capable of learning too.

Distributed manufacturing will make the problem harder.

One vision of personalized medicine puts sophisticated manufacturing close to the patient. Hospital-based facilities, regional centers or modular production units could shorten shipping and turnaround. The opposite vision concentrates manufacturing in a few expert facilities that achieve better consistency and scale.

Each model moves risk.

Centralization creates transport dependence and capacity bottlenecks but can concentrate expertise. Distribution reduces some logistics while multiplying the number of sites that must reproduce a validated process. A digital recipe is not enough. Equipment calibration, raw materials, environmental controls, operator training and local deviations can all change the output.

A platform that claims to be distributed therefore has to prove that the process transfers.

This is where regulatory federalism can meet manufacturing in a useful way. States may license facilities and clinicians, but a national standard can preserve comparability across sites. The goal should not be fifty different definitions of a correctly manufactured personalized therapy.

The therapeutic design can vary.

The quality language should travel.

Supply-chain fragility matters even for one-person products.

A specialized reagent can be unavailable. A contract laboratory can close. A freezer failure can destroy material. A courier delay can thaw a product. A software outage can interrupt batch records. A small manufacturer can depend on one employee who knows a critical manual step.

These look like operational details until the patient has no substitute dose.

Redundancy becomes a clinical property.

A serious platform should know which suppliers and steps can fail the patient, which alternatives are qualified, and what can be recovered inside the treatment window.

That is especially important for experimental centers selling access as speed. A legal pathway can be fast while a fragile supply chain remains slow.

Recall systems deserve the same attention.

The word recall sounds strange for a personalized product that may already have been administered. But new information can emerge after treatment: a reagent contamination, an analytical error, a software bug that affected sequence design, a problem with a source cell line, a pathogen detected in a biological bank.

The system must be able to identify which patients were exposed to the common component.

This is one of the paradoxes of personalization. The final products differ, yet a single shared upstream error can link them all.

Without component-level traceability, a platform can look like a set of isolated one-patient treatments precisely when regulators most need to see the common exposure.

Manufacturing history is therefore not merely documentation of how one lot was made.

It is a map of relationships among lots.

That map is what lets later knowledge travel backward.

The future of experimental medicine depends on making that compatibility routine before approval rather than only after it.

A patient-specific treatment may never have a millionth unit.

It still deserves a manufacturing history.

That history is how one person's experiment stops being only one person's risk.
