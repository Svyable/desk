# The Organ Before Approval

An organ does not fit in a vial, but regulators still need to decide what it is.

Xenotransplantation makes that problem impossible to hide.

The modern experiment is usually described simply: transplant an organ from a genetically modified pig into a human being. The sentence sounds like a surgical event. In reality, the intervention is a biological supply chain stretching backward through animal breeding, genome editing, pathogen surveillance, housing conditions, tissue characterization, immunology, organ procurement, surgery, anti-rejection therapy, and long-term monitoring.

The kidney is only the most visible component.

This is why xenotransplantation belongs in a book about personalized medicines even though the organ itself is not necessarily designed for one patient's mutation.

The modality sits at the far end of the same regulatory transition. The finished treatment cannot be understood as a standardized chemical product. It is an integrated system whose components vary, whose risks can persist for years, and whose consequences can extend beyond the recipient.

That last feature changes the ethics.

If an experimental small-molecule drug harms the patient, the primary risk is individual. A xenotransplant can carry theoretical infectious risks involving animal pathogens that adapt to humans. The probability can be low and still matter because the consequence might not stop with the person who consented.

Autonomy reaches a boundary when risk becomes communicable.

Xenotransplantation therefore reveals why a broad statement such as “a dying patient should be allowed to take any risk” cannot govern experimental medicine by itself.

Some risks belong partly to other people.

This does not make xenotransplantation reckless. It explains why the field developed unusually elaborate expectations around source animals, pathogen screening, records, recipient monitoring, and long-term surveillance. The animal is not merely a donor. It is part of a controlled manufacturing environment.

That is a strange phrase until one considers what modern xenotransplantation demands.

Pigs can be genetically modified to remove or alter molecules that trigger human immune responses and to add human genes intended to improve compatibility. The animals can be raised under controlled conditions designed to limit infectious risk. Their genomes, health status, and lineage matter. The organ itself has to be evaluated. The recipient undergoes major surgery and receives an immunosuppressive regimen whose choices affect both graft survival and infection.

A failure can originate anywhere in the stack.

Was the organ rejected because the genetic edits were insufficient? Did a clotting problem emerge from cross-species biology? Did the immunosuppression fail? Did the patient's condition make the organ unusually difficult to support? Was there an infection? Did an animal-line characteristic matter? Did surgical technique matter?

The intervention is too integrated for the simple product metaphor.

That makes early human use especially dependent on institutional competence.

In recent years, teams in the United States have moved from experiments in deceased human recipients toward living patients receiving gene-edited pig organs under highly exceptional circumstances and into formal clinical-trial development. Published reports documented early pig-kidney xenotransplantation in living recipients. The cases generated intense attention because organ shortage makes the potential benefit enormous.

By 2026, the trial transition is no longer hypothetical.

The EXPAND study, registered as NCT06878560, is a multicenter open-label study of a ten-gene-edited porcine kidney in people with end-stage renal disease. It began in October 2025 and lists an estimated enrollment of fifty participants. Its entry criteria are designed around patients who are either ineligible for a conventional human kidney transplant or face a sufficiently poor probability of receiving one before death or continued deterioration. The study's initial post-transplant period is measured in months.

Its follow-up is measured in a lifetime.

After the first twenty-four weeks, the protocol continues to track participant survival, xenokidney survival, and screening for zoonotic infections for as long as the recipient remains alive.

A second program makes the same point with a different biological design. The EXTEND study, NCT07224763, is structured around a GGTA1-knockout porcine kidney combined with thymic tissue. It also plans roughly fifty participants drawn from end-stage renal disease populations with poor access to ordinary transplantation. Its long-term follow-up extends for the lifetime of recipients who retain the graft, or for a defined period after nephrectomy if the organ must be removed.

The existence of two trial architectures is itself informative.

“Pig kidney” is not one product class in the ordinary sense. One program can use a set of ten genomic alterations. Another can pair a more limited edit with a thymic-tissue strategy. Source animals, immune engineering, immunosuppression, monitoring, and surgical protocols can differ. The field is not merely deciding whether xenotransplantation works. It is comparing systems for making xenotransplantation work.

That makes a premature headline especially dangerous.

A successful transplant under one edit set, one source-animal program, one immunosuppressive regimen, and one center's protocol does not validate every treatment marketed under the word xenotransplantation. The unit of evidence is narrower than the public label.

Kidney transplantation already contains a cruel arithmetic.

There are not enough human organs for everyone who could benefit. Patients can spend years on dialysis while waiting. Some become too sick to transplant. Some die. A renewable supply of compatible organs would not merely improve one procedure. It could reorganize the entire economics and ethics of transplantation.

That possibility changes the risk tolerance.

The baseline is not a healthy volunteer receiving a speculative intervention. It may be a patient with severe organ failure whose existing options are dialysis, uncertain waiting, or death.

Again, severity matters without becoming a blank check.

The early living-recipient xenotransplant cases have often occurred through exceptional regulatory routes rather than ordinary product approval. That is exactly what expanded-access systems are designed to permit: treatment before general approval when circumstances justify it and adequate safeguards can be constructed.

The cases are useful counterexamples to the belief that frontier medicine can move only by leaving the FDA behind.

Few interventions are more novel than putting a gene-edited pig organ into a living person.

It happened under federal oversight.

The move into registered trials strengthens that point rather than weakening it. Exceptional access created information and experience. Trials create a structure for accumulating it across participants. The two are not rival moral systems. They are different stages and tools in a development process.

The relevant question is whether such oversight can scale from exceptional cases into a development pathway fast enough to address the organ shortage without losing the safeguards that make the experiment socially acceptable.

A state right-to-try framework would face unusual limits here.

A Montana experimental treatment center could in principle become interested in xenotransplantation if a treatment fits the state's statutory criteria and facility requirements. But the actual modality depends on a source-animal program, biologic controls, surgery, transplant expertise, infectious-disease surveillance, and federal product law. The treatment is not something a state can easily localize by allowing a physician to act.

The source animal may be raised elsewhere. The organ may cross state lines. The genetic modification platform may be federally regulated. The immunosuppressive drugs are part of a clinical protocol. The recipient's long-term monitoring can involve public-health considerations.

Xenotransplantation therefore demonstrates where state-level permission becomes a relatively small part of the system.

The harder the modality is to contain geographically, the less useful pure jurisdictional arbitrage becomes.

A patient can travel to another country for a surgery. A national border cannot separate that patient from delayed consequences after returning home. A pathogen-monitoring obligation is only useful if records follow the recipient. A graft that fails months later creates evidence relevant to the source animals and future recipients wherever they live.

The system must remain longitudinal.

The formal trials make that requirement visible in a way a rescue story cannot.

The headline event is the transplant. The regulatory object is the recipient-organ relationship over time.

A twenty-four-week endpoint can tell investigators whether the patient and graft survived an early period. It cannot settle the questions that motivated lifelong surveillance. A zoonotic concern could emerge later. Chronic rejection can unfold on a different clock from perioperative failure. A company can change ownership. A center can stop participating. The source-animal platform can evolve while earlier recipients are still living with prior generations.

A study that follows recipients for life is admitting something important about the therapy.

The treatment has no clean administrative ending.

That requirement makes xenotransplantation different from many forms of medical tourism.

A patient can buy a dental implant abroad and, if all goes well, never contact the clinic again. A xenotransplant recipient cannot responsibly be treated as a one-time customer. The intervention creates a durable relationship among patient, transplant center, source-animal provider, regulators, laboratories, and future public-health surveillance.

Any jurisdiction that tried to compete by offering xenotransplants with minimal follow-up would undermine the premise of the field.

The most permissive credible system may therefore be the one with the most demanding aftercare.

This inversion appears repeatedly in advanced therapy.

A jurisdiction can reduce barriers before treatment only if it has enough infrastructure after treatment to absorb the uncertainty it has allowed.

Gene therapy needs long-term follow-up for some risks. Engineered cells may require surveillance. Xenotransplantation can require extensive monitoring for graft function and infection. A fast front door can create a decades-long back end.

Right-to-try policy tends to focus on the front door.

The patient is blocked today. The law opens access today. The emotional and political urgency belongs to the decision point.

The cost arrives later.

Who monitors the patient in year three? Who pays for specialized assays? What happens if the company that supplied the organ or therapeutic platform fails? Who owns the biological samples? If a novel infectious concern appears, can the recipient refuse further testing? Can family members be asked to participate in surveillance? What records have to survive for decades?

These questions can sound bureaucratic until the treatment exists.

Then they become clinical care.

The organ also makes manufacturing visible in a new way.

A pig intended for xenotransplantation is not a commodity animal that happens to have useful kidneys. The genetics and husbandry are part of product control. A change in breeding line can be a manufacturing change. A pathogen detected in the herd can be a lot-level problem. A new set of genomic edits can create a materially different product generation.

Comparability—the regulatory problem of showing that a manufacturing change has not altered the product in clinically important ways—becomes biological lineage.

This is another reason the rhetoric of “natural” treatments can be misleading.

An organ is natural in one sense and an engineered biomedical product in another.

So are many cell and microbiome therapies.

The more biological the intervention becomes, the more important process control can be because the material itself cannot be reduced to a single chemical structure.

The xenotransplant field is also a lesson in why early access and trials are not competitors.

Exceptional living-recipient cases can answer questions that no animal study can fully resolve. They can expose unforeseen physiological problems, generate evidence about immunosuppression, and show whether the organ can support human life for meaningful periods. But the field cannot remain a sequence of carefully chosen rescue stories.

At some point, the intervention has to move into studies capable of comparing outcomes systematically.

The current kidney studies make that transition concrete while also exposing its limits.

Fifty participants is enormous compared with one heroic case and tiny compared with the population that could eventually need kidney replacement. Open-label transplantation cannot hide which organ a participant received. Patient selection is necessarily unusual. Long-term data will accumulate slowly because the relevant outcomes are long-term.

Formal development does not make the evidence problem disappear.

It gives the evidence problem a shape.

That transition changes patient selection.

A compassionate-use case may involve a patient whose options are unusually poor and whose circumstances justify exceptional risk. A clinical trial may seek participants whose condition is stable enough to produce interpretable evidence. The people most desperate for access can be the least informative participants for the questions regulators need answered before broader approval.

This is one of the hardest ethical tensions in experimental medicine.

The patient with the strongest moral claim to try may not be the patient whose outcome best predicts what will happen in ordinary use.

Expanded access and right-to-try pathways can coexist with trials partly because they serve different purposes.

Treatment pathways prioritize the individual.

Trials prioritize learning, even while they must still protect and potentially benefit participants.

Problems arise when a commercial market makes treatment access so attractive that patients stop enrolling in trials. If everyone can simply buy the experimental therapy, the sponsor may lose the controlled evidence needed for approval. The short-term expansion of access can slow long-term access for everyone.

That concern is often raised by manufacturers deciding whether to provide investigational drugs outside trials.

In xenotransplantation, the scarcity of organs and specialized centers may limit the problem. In other modalities, especially treatments that can be sold to cash-paying patients, the conflict can become substantial.

A well-designed experimental-treatment regime therefore needs some concept of development preservation.

It should not allow a lucrative exceptional-access market to substitute indefinitely for the work required to establish safety and efficacy.

Montana's law includes treatments that remain under investigation and treatments with certain documented safety records. The commercial incentives around the second category deserve careful observation. If a treatment can generate revenue through state-licensed experimental care without ever completing development for ordinary approval, the pathway may create an equilibrium in which uncertainty becomes permanent.

That could be rational for the seller.

Approval is expensive. Trials can fail. A market of desperate patients may tolerate higher prices and lower evidentiary certainty than insurers or regulators would after approval. If the experimental label protects the business from having to prove benefit, there may be little incentive to leave the edge.

Xenotransplantation is unlikely to thrive under that model because transplant centers, public-health concerns, and complex supply chains push toward formal development.

But its structure makes the incentive problem easier to see.

The purpose of exceptional access should be to cross a temporary evidentiary gap, not to build a permanent business on the gap itself.

That principle does not require every intervention to become a mass-market approved product. Some one-person treatments may never have a population large enough for conventional approval. Some therapies may remain individualized platform programs. The endpoint can be a mature regulatory category rather than a blockbuster label.

What cannot become permanent is the excuse that nothing more can be learned.

Every recipient of a xenotransplant creates information too valuable to lose.

The source-animal genetics are known. The immunosuppressive regimen is known. Graft function can be measured. Rejection can be characterized. Pathogens can be monitored. Tissue can be studied. The cases are small in number, but the data are unusually rich.

This is the opposite of the testimonial model.

A patient story remains important, but it is embedded inside a technical record capable of changing the next case.

There is another reason this record has to outlive the immediate transplant team.

Source animals create genealogies.

If multiple organs come from related animals or from the same engineered line, a safety signal in one recipient may be relevant to people transplanted elsewhere. A genetic change introduced to reduce rejection may interact with coagulation, infection or organ physiology in ways that become visible only after several recipients. A pathogen concern may lead investigators back through the source herd, archived samples and prior recipients.

The unit of surveillance can therefore be larger than one patient and smaller than an entire product class.

It may be a lineage.

That is a regulatory object without a simple analogue in ordinary pharmacy.

The archive matters as much as the active treatment.

Which animal produced the organ?

Which edit set did it carry?

Which facility housed it?

What screening was performed and when?

Which biological samples remain available if a later concern emerges?

Which other recipients are linked to the same lineage?

These are questions a functioning system should be able to answer years later, including if the original company has been acquired, renamed or dissolved.

That creates a continuity obligation beyond ordinary commercial incentives.

A startup may have a shorter life than a xenotransplant recipient.

If long-term surveillance depends entirely on the continued existence of the original sponsor, the system has designed a predictable failure mode.

Regulators and transplant programs need durable repositories, transferable records and contingency plans for stewardship when companies change.

The same problem will appear in other advanced therapies, but xenotransplantation makes it especially vivid because the surveillance horizon can be long and the externality can extend beyond the treated person.

Public-health risk also complicates the right to withdraw.

In research ethics, a participant generally has a strong right to stop participating. In ordinary medical care, a patient can decline follow-up. Xenotransplantation can create circumstances in which surveillance was part of the social bargain that made treatment acceptable in the first place.

That does not erase bodily autonomy.

It does mean consent has to be unusually explicit before the organ is implanted.

A patient should understand that the treatment may create obligations that are difficult to reverse. Those obligations can include specimen collection, health monitoring and notification of certain events. Family or close contacts may become relevant if an infectious signal emerges.

The ethical mistake would be to describe the transplant as a private risk at the beginning and reveal its collective dimensions only afterward.

The economic model should reflect the same reality.

The price of the organ is not the price of the therapy.

There is source-animal infrastructure, screening, organ procurement, surgery, immunosuppression, intensive inpatient care, specialized testing and years of follow-up. A patient who gains access through an exceptional pathway can create downstream costs for insurers, hospitals and public programs even when the investigational organ itself is supplied without charge.

This is not an argument against access.

It is a reminder that experimental treatment can externalize cost as well as risk.

A right-to-try framework focused only on whether a patient may pay for an experimental intervention can miss the much larger system required to make the intervention medically responsible.

If xenotransplantation eventually becomes routine, this infrastructure may be normalized into transplantation medicine. Source herds may operate under mature standards. Centers may know how to select recipients. Surveillance may be integrated into registries. Insurers may have established payment rules. The intervention may stop looking exceptional even though every organ remains biological and every recipient remains different.

That transition would strengthen, not weaken, the book's larger argument.

The goal of early access is not to preserve the romance of being first.

It is to build the boring systems that make being fiftieth safer, cheaper and more predictable.

A frontier succeeds when it stops feeling like a frontier.

That is the standard experimental medicine should move toward even when conventional trials are impossible.

The scale of the intervention should not determine whether learning occurs.

A two-gram oligonucleotide and a pig kidney could hardly be more different as physical objects.

They are becoming similar as regulatory problems.

Each may be made possible by a reusable platform. Each may contain patient- or donor-specific variation. Each can require exceptional access before general approval. Each depends on manufacturing controls that the patient cannot inspect. Each creates knowledge that should travel farther than the treatment itself.

And each shows why the most important question at the border of permission is not simply whether the patient can enter.

It is what follows them through.
