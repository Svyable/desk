# Right to Try, Right to Know

A patient can be out of time before medicine is out of ideas.

That is the fact beneath every right-to-try argument.

The disease is progressing now. The child is losing abilities now. The infection is spreading now. The cancer has returned now. The organ is failing now. The ordinary system speaks in timelines measured in protocols, cohorts, review cycles, manufacturing runs and accumulated evidence. The patient experiences time as the next scan, the next seizure, the next admission, the next laboratory result that moves in the wrong direction.

It is easy, from outside that clock, to become philosophical about uncertainty.

Inside it, uncertainty is already the environment.

The patient is not choosing between risk and safety. Usually the patient is choosing among risks whose shapes differ. The approved treatment may have stopped working. The clinical trial may require travel, randomization or eligibility criteria the patient cannot satisfy. An experimental therapy may have little human evidence. Doing nothing may have the strongest evidence of all and the worst outcome.

Autonomy matters most when the options are bad.

A serious account of experimental medicine should therefore begin by granting something that regulation's defenders sometimes concede too reluctantly: a competent patient facing severe disease can have a strong moral claim to choose an uncertain intervention.

That claim does not depend on the intervention being wise in retrospect.

Rights would be thin things if they protected only choices that later proved correct.

A patient can understand that a therapy may not work and still prefer the possibility it represents. A parent can understand that a one-patient genetic treatment contains unknowns and still conclude that the known course of the disease is worse. A person with terminal cancer can decide that preserving eligibility for a future trial matters less than trying a therapy available now. Someone with a multidrug-resistant infection can accept risks that would be irrational for a person with an uncomplicated infection.

The regulator does not inhabit the patient's utility function.

Neither does the physician.

Neither does the ethicist.

The difficulty begins when this truth is stretched into a second proposition that does not follow from it: because the patient may choose uncertainty, everyone else may be uncertain about everything.

That is where the right to know begins.

The patient should know what is known.

The clinician should know what was administered.

The manufacturer should know what was made.

The regulator should know when serious harm occurs.

The next patient should be able to know that the treatment has been tried before.

The public should know when a provider has transformed exceptional treatment into a repeated business.

These claims do not compete with autonomy in the simple way that prohibition does.

They make autonomy more real.

Choice without information can become a ceremonial freedom: the patient signs, the provider treats, the invoice clears, and the language of consent turns a radical imbalance of knowledge into a private transaction.

The signature is not the freedom.

The quality of the choice is.

This book began at a border because borders make hidden structures visible.

Move a patient from one jurisdiction to another and suddenly the rules that had seemed like properties of medicine reveal themselves as institutions. The disease is the same. The molecule is the same. The cells are the same. The tumor sequence is the same. The bacterium is the same. What changes is permission, enforcement, professional authority, facility licensing, payment, liability, data capture and the willingness of institutions to participate.

Montana makes this visible inside the United States.

Its experimental-treatment-center framework is an unusually direct attempt to create a state-level home for treatment beyond ordinary approval. The state has not merely passed a symbolic right-to-try law and stopped. It has created a licensing structure, implementing rules, review and monitoring obligations, and an access mechanism tied to the economics of licensed centers.

That makes Montana more interesting than the slogan attached to it.

It is building an institution.

The institution may work. It may fail. It may be narrowed by federal law. It may provoke accommodation. It may become a destination for treatments that could also have moved through expanded access. It may attract therapies that sponsors would not otherwise provide. It may discover that the hardest constraint is not legal permission at all but manufacturing, insurance, hospital capability or company willingness.

The important point is that those outcomes can be observed.

Montana should be judged by what happens, not by whether one begins politically sympathetic or hostile to right to try.

Mexico makes a different structure visible.

The simplistic version of medical tourism imagines a regulated country next to an unregulated one. The real picture is harder. Mexico has clinical-trial authorization, ethics structures, health-establishment regulation, a national trial registry and enforcement. It also has a large private medical economy, international patients, variable enforcement capacity, ambiguous categories and providers whose marketing can outrun evidence.

That combination is not exotic.

It is what health regulation looks like when written rules meet a commercial world larger than any regulator's field of view.

The border then becomes informational.

The patient may know that a clinic is in Mexico without knowing whether the intervention is part of an authorized clinical protocol, an approved product, a permitted medical practice, a discretionary category, or an unauthorized commercial experiment. The website may use scientific vocabulary that does not answer the regulatory question. The physician at home may know less about the product than the patient believes the clinic knows. After treatment, complications can cross the border more easily than records do.

This is why "regulated" and "unregulated" are inadequate categories.

The useful question is whether the pathway is accountable.

Accountability is not the same as caution.

A system can be extremely cautious and badly accountable if nobody can explain why it takes so long or who owns the decision. A system can permit very risky treatment and be highly accountable if the risk is explicit, the product is traceable, the decision is independently reviewed, the outcomes are recorded and the rules for continuing change with accumulating evidence.

The opposite is also possible: a provider can advertise safety while operating inside an information void.

The future of personalized medicine will make these distinctions harder to avoid.

The drug-development system was built around repetition.

A compound is manufactured to a specification. It is tested in groups. Dose and safety information accumulate. Later trials ask whether benefit exceeds harm for a defined population. Approval attaches to a product, indication and labeling structure. Manufacturing scales so that the unit given to one patient is meant to be materially comparable with the unit given to the next.

Personalized modalities pull on each part of that model.

Milasen was designed for one patient.

KJ's gene-editing treatment used a broader technical platform but a patient-specific target.

Neoantigen vaccines can be manufactured from the mutational information in one person's tumor.

Phages may be selected against the organism infecting a particular patient.

Autologous cell therapies begin with biological material from the person who will receive the product.

Xenotransplantation turns the "product" into an organ, an engineered animal source, pathogen controls, surgery, immunosuppression and long follow-up.

The microbiome has shown how one biological idea can move among medical practice, enforcement discretion and standardized products as the field matures.

These are not versions of one regulatory problem.

They share one pressure.

The unit that repeats is moving.

Sometimes what repeats is chemistry while sequence changes.

Sometimes manufacturing repeats while cells change.

Sometimes the bank repeats while the selected biological member changes.

Sometimes the delivery machinery repeats while the genetic target changes.

Sometimes almost nothing in the final therapeutic object can be standardized, but the process by which it is selected and made can.

Regulation has to find the repeatable layer because that is where knowledge can accumulate.

This is why the most important development in personalized medicine may not be a single approval.

It may be the maturation of platform thinking.

Platform thinking asks a practical question: what does the next patient get to inherit from the last one?

If the answer is nothing, personalized medicine remains artisanal in the worst sense. Every case repeats the same institutional invention. Every custom therapy is expensive not only because it is custom, but because the system has forgotten how it made the previous custom therapy.

If the answer is everything, the system becomes reckless. Patient-specific changes can matter. Targets can create new biology. Small manufacturing changes can alter products. Delivery can behave differently in different tissues. A platform is not a magic certificate that turns novelty into familiarity.

The useful answer is conditional inheritance.

Carry forward what has a defensible reason to carry forward.

Regenerate what is materially new.

Record enough that the distinction can improve over time.

That last requirement is the one most likely to be lost in a politics organized around permission.

Permission is dramatic.

Data is clerical.

A family fighting for access can mobilize attention. A lawmaker can sponsor a bill. A clinic can announce a new program. A company can agree to manufacture a dose. A regulator can authorize an emergency request.

Twelve months later, the follow-up form has no constituency.

This is where the next patient's right enters.

Today's patient is not merely a beneficiary of past evidence.

Every treated patient also sits somewhere in the evidence stream future medicine will use.

That does not turn a sick person into a research subject against their will. Treatment remains treatment. The clinician's first obligation is not to science as an abstraction but to the patient in front of them.

Yet when the intervention itself is experimental, pretending that nothing can be learned from treatment is wasteful in a more serious sense than lost research opportunity.

It makes future consent worse.

Suppose ten people receive a treatment and no outcomes are assembled.

The eleventh patient is told that evidence is limited.

Technically true.

Institutionally false.

There has been experience. The system has failed to convert experience into evidence.

The uncertainty offered to the eleventh patient is partly manufactured by neglect.

This distinction should become central to the ethics of experimental care.

There is uncertainty nobody can yet eliminate.

And there is uncertainty somebody chose not to reduce.

A novel gene edit may have an unknowable long-term risk because not enough time has passed.

That is irreducible for now.

A provider may not know whether five earlier patients were hospitalized because nobody followed them after they went home.

That is different.

A phage therapy may work unpredictably because bacterial evolution and host biology are complicated.

That is scientific uncertainty.

A clinic may not know which preparation a prior patient received because batch records are poor.

That is organizational failure.

A tumor vaccine may not generate the expected immune response despite careful design.

That is biological uncertainty.

A company may not disclose that manufacturing fails in a large fraction of attempted patients.

That is commercial opacity.

The right to know is the claim that we should stop mixing these together.

Patients who choose experimental treatment are not asking for negligence.

They are asking for access before certainty.

Those are not the same thing.

This distinction also clarifies the role of the seller.

Patients and providers do not enter the transaction with symmetrical moral obligations.

The patient can hope.

The seller has to describe evidence.

The patient can choose a very small chance.

The seller cannot turn a very small or unknown chance into an implied probability through testimonials and adjectives.

The patient can accept financial loss.

The seller should disclose the financial terms before urgency makes them harder to refuse.

The patient can decide that a plausible mechanism is enough.

The seller should not describe mechanism as demonstrated clinical benefit.

The patient can change their mind.

The provider should preserve that possibility as long as medically feasible.

This is why strong autonomy rules can coexist with strong truth-in-marketing rules.

They protect different things.

One protects the patient's authority over the body.

The other protects the information environment in which that authority is exercised.

The same logic applies to regulators.

Regulators are often criticized for acting as if they own risk.

They do not.

Adults accept risk throughout medicine and life. Surgery can be dangerous. Chemotherapy can be toxic. Pregnancy can be dangerous. People refuse treatment. People enroll in early trials. People choose options physicians would not choose for themselves.

The legitimate regulatory role is not to make every risk decision on behalf of the patient.

It is to manage information problems, product quality, externalities and commercial incentives that individual consent cannot solve well.

The regulator can inspect manufacturing because the patient cannot.

The regulator can aggregate adverse events because the patient sees only one case.

The regulator can demand truthful labeling because the seller controls the presentation.

The regulator can intervene when a risk extends beyond the consenting person.

The regulator can establish a floor below which "experimental" becomes an excuse for not knowing what the product is.

Seen this way, experimental access need not be a retreat from regulation.

It can be a test of whether regulation knows what work only regulation can do.

The same is true of states.

American federalism can produce a strange mismatch in which states license the people and places practicing medicine while federal law governs the products moving through that practice. This can look incoherent because it is the result of institutions built for overlapping purposes rather than one clean theory.

But the overlap can be productive if it becomes a laboratory for process rather than a competition to erase the other layer.

A state can test how quickly centers can review requests.

It can test consent formats.

It can test patient follow-up.

It can require transparent outcomes.

It can test whether an access fund broadens participation.

It can publish operational data.

It can make facility quality visible.

None of those experiments requires pretending federal product law does not exist.

Likewise, the federal government can adapt its own pathways when state experimentation reveals useful design.

That is the better version of regulatory competition.

The worse version is a race in which jurisdictions compete by making ignorance cheaper.

The phrase "right to try" contains another ambiguity worth preserving.

Whose right?

The patient's, obviously.

But a statute cannot compel every actor necessary to make treatment exist.

A manufacturer may refuse.

A physician may refuse.

A hospital may refuse.

An insurer may refuse to pay.

A laboratory may lack capacity.

A company may decide that one exceptional case creates too much risk for a development program.

A custom therapy may be technically possible but impossible to manufacture within the patient's clinical window.

A right that depends on voluntary cooperation is not meaningless.

It is incomplete.

This explains why many access debates feel unsatisfying even after laws change.

The legal barrier was only one barrier.

Personalized medicine makes the rest harder to ignore because the treatment may have to be created rather than released from inventory.

The real right is therefore closer to a right to seek.

A right to ask.

A right to have the request evaluated through a tractable process.

A right not to have arbitrary institutional delay consume the clinical window.

A right to know why the answer was no.

A right to know what evidence supports yes.

That is less rhetorically powerful than a right to try.

It is more operationally honest.

The economic problem remains.

A personalized therapy can be scientifically possible and socially inaccessible.

The first few treatments often carry the cost of inventing a platform, building a manufacturing route, designing assays, validating release tests, assembling regulatory knowledge and training clinicians. If each patient must finance that infrastructure as though it belongs only to them, access will select for wealth, philanthropy, institutional prestige or unusually compelling stories.

That would produce a peculiar future.

Medicine becomes more biologically personalized while access becomes more socially selective.

The learning system proposed in the previous chapter is partly an economic response.

When evidence and process can be inherited, cost should eventually be inherited too.

The next patient should benefit from the fact that the laboratory has done this before.

The next hospital should not recreate every document.

The next regulator should not ask a platform question already answered unless something relevant changed.

The next manufacturer should know where failures occurred.

The next payer should have a clearer picture of what is experimental about the therapy and what is established infrastructure.

Personalization does not have to mean bespoke bureaucracy.

If we get the architecture right, the system can become more individualized at the therapeutic layer and more standardized everywhere around it.

That is the paradox worth aiming for.

Standardize the process so the medicine can vary.

Standardize the data so the patient can be unique.

Standardize the truth obligations so the risk decision can remain personal.

There will still be hard cases.

A patient will request a therapy whose mechanism is too weak to justify exposure.

A family will find a clinic willing to sell something a major medical center refuses to administer.

A promising one-patient result will generate more confidence than it deserves.

A treatment will be delayed by a safety question and the patient will deteriorate during the delay.

A regulator will be blamed for a death after denying access.

A provider will be blamed for a death after granting it.

A treatment that looked reckless will work.

A treatment that looked rational will cause harm.

No framework removes tragedy from severe illness.

This is another reason to distrust arguments that promise a morally clean system.

The choice is not between a world in which experimental treatment harms people and one in which it does not.

The choice is among systems that distribute different harms, delays, opportunities and information.

The best one will still produce painful outcomes.

Its claim to legitimacy will come from whether those outcomes were faced honestly and whether they changed what happened next.

There is a useful image in manufacturing.

A batch record is boring when everything goes well.

It lists materials, steps, times, operators, tests and deviations. It exists so that the product administered today is not a mystery tomorrow. If something goes wrong, the record lets people reconstruct what happened.

Experimental medicine needs a batch record for decisions.

Why did we believe this might work?

What was actually made?

What did the patient understand?

What happened after treatment?

What changed in our judgment?

Who should know before the next patient is treated?

That record does not need to be public in every detail. Privacy matters. Proprietary manufacturing information can matter. A child with a unique mutation can be identifiable even in supposedly de-identified data. Commercial incentives matter because companies will not develop platforms if every trade secret becomes a public artifact.

The right to know is not a demand that all information be open.

It is a demand that necessary information exist, move to the people who need it, and not vanish merely because treatment occurred outside a conventional trial.

That is enough to change the moral character of exceptional access.

A private gamble can become part of a public learning system without ceasing to be a private medical decision.

The first patient takes a risk no later patient can fully repay.

The least we can do is make sure the later patient inherits something.

This is what the most impressive examples in the book already suggest.

Milasen was not produced by a world without regulation. It emerged from a collaboration willing to adapt regulation to a one-person therapy.

KJ's treatment did not prove that gene editing should be freed from oversight. It showed what can happen when a platform, a clinical team, scientists, manufacturers and regulators compress their work around one child's clock.

Personalized phage cases often depend on emergency coordination, laboratory matching and regulatory permission rather than simple evasion.

Approved autologous cell therapies show that patient-specific manufacturing can become regulated infrastructure.

Microbiome products show that a field can move from discretionary practice toward standardized products as evidence and manufacturing mature.

Xenotransplantation shows why some uncertainty demands more collective oversight, not less, because the externalities do not belong solely to the recipient.

These cases do not point toward a single permissive threshold.

They point toward regulatory resolution that matches the technology.

A century from now, the most primitive thing about early personalized medicine may look like our insistence that every therapy fit categories built around identical products for large groups.

Future systems may treat a therapeutic platform more like an operating system: validated core components, controlled interfaces, versioned changes, patient-specific modules and continuous surveillance.

That analogy will be imperfect, as all software analogies to biology are. Bodies are not deterministic machines, and small changes can have effects that do not respect our architectural diagrams.

But the institutional direction is plausible.

We will get better at distinguishing the stable layer from the variable one.

We will get better at manufacturing one-off products under repeatable quality systems.

We will get better at matching treatments to molecular causes, immune targets, pathogens and patient-derived cells.

As that happens, the approval border will become less like a finish line and more like a set of nested confidence levels.

Some components will be mature.

Some will be new.

Some risks will be known.

Some will be inferred.

Some outcomes will be measurable quickly.

Others will take years.

The law will have to learn to speak that grammar.

The public argument will have to learn it too.

"Approved" will remain important, but it will not answer every question about a patient-specific system.

"Experimental" will remain important, but it should stop functioning as one undifferentiated warning label.

"Right to try" will remain politically powerful, but it should be paired with a less dramatic phrase.

Right to know.

Know what the therapy is.

Know what has been tried.

Know what has happened.

Know what is only theory.

Know who is selling it.

Know what it costs.

Know who will care for you afterward.

Know whether your experience will disappear.

For the patient, this is the information needed to choose.

For the next patient, it is the inheritance needed to choose better.

The border of permission will not disappear.

There will always be a first use, an incomplete dataset, a jurisdiction willing to move earlier, a regulator willing to wait longer, a clinician willing to attempt something another clinician would decline. There will always be patients whose disease clocks outrun the institutions built to protect them.

The humane response is not to pretend we can abolish uncertainty.

It is to stop wasting it.

If we are going to let patients stand at the edge of what medicine knows, the edge should move after they have been there.
