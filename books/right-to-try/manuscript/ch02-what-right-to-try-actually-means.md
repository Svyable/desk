# What Right to Try Actually Means

The phrase sounds like a door.

Right to try.

Three words imply that somebody has been standing between a patient and a treatment, and that the law can move aside. The image is politically efficient because it contains a villain, a victim, and a remedy before anyone has had to describe the supply chain.

The supply chain is where the argument changes.

Suppose a patient has amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. The disease is progressing. Approved options have been discussed. A biotechnology company has an investigational drug in development. The patient has read about it, understands that the evidence is incomplete, and is willing to accept risks that would be unreasonable for someone with a mild condition.

What, exactly, would a legal right to try need to provide?

The first answer is permission: the government should not prohibit the patient and physician from using the investigational treatment merely because it has not received ordinary marketing approval.

But permission alone leaves most of the problem untouched.

The manufacturer has to agree. It may have only enough drug for a clinical trial. It may worry that a serious adverse event in a much sicker patient will complicate development. It may have no distribution system outside research sites. It may be a small company with ten employees and one batch of material whose next financing round depends on hitting trial milestones. It may believe, sincerely, that the patient is too fragile to receive the drug safely. It may not know how to charge for the product, or may not be allowed to profit from doing so under the relevant pathway.

A physician has to agree too. The physician becomes responsible for treating a patient with a product whose dosing, toxicities, or interactions may be incompletely understood. A hospital or clinic may need to approve the administration. A pharmacy may need to store the drug. Laboratory monitoring may be required. A specialist may need to be available if an immune reaction, arrhythmia, liver injury, cytokine release syndrome, infection, bleeding event, or other foreseeable complication occurs.

The right to try is therefore not a right in the ordinary consumer sense.

It is closer to a right to ask without one particular regulatory barrier.

That distinction explains much of the history of the American movement.

Long before Congress enacted a federal Right to Try law in 2018, the Food and Drug Administration already permitted expanded access to investigational drugs. The system grew from a basic clinical reality: trials are designed to answer research questions, but some patients cannot enroll and still have compelling reasons to seek an investigational product. They may fail eligibility criteria. The trial may be too far away. Enrollment may have closed. Their disease may be so rare that no suitable trial exists. A patient can be medically appropriate for an investigational therapy without fitting the protocol intended to generate generalizable evidence.

Expanded access creates a legal bridge.

For an individual patient, a licensed physician submits a request, the manufacturer permits use of the product, and the FDA evaluates whether the potential benefit justifies the potential risk in light of the disease and available alternatives, and whether access would interfere with clinical development. Institutional review has a role. Safety information flows back into the regulatory system. In emergencies, the process can be compressed dramatically.

The bureaucracy became a target partly because its existence was visible and the other barriers were not.

A family can write an angry letter to the FDA. It is harder to compel a manufacturer whose reasons for refusal may be buried inside inventory forecasts, legal advice, trial design, or cash runway. Regulators became the face of a system in which they were often not the actor saying no.

The FDA eventually simplified the individual-patient process. Its current materials emphasize that emergency requests can be authorized rapidly and that approximately 99 percent of expanded-access requests are allowed to proceed. The figure complicates a simple story in which regulators routinely prevent doctors from treating desperate patients.

It does not prove that the system is easy.

The denominator matters. A request that reaches the FDA has already survived several filters. A physician has agreed to do the work. A manufacturer has agreed to provide the product. Institutional machinery exists. Patients who cannot persuade a sponsor never become an FDA request. A high approval rate at the final gate can coexist with substantial friction before the gate.

This is one reason Right to Try retained political force even after expanded access improved.

State laws appeared first. By 2018, dozens of states had adopted versions of a right-to-try policy. Congress then created a federal route for certain patients diagnosed with life-threatening disease who had exhausted approved treatment options and could not participate in a clinical trial involving the eligible investigational drug. The product must have completed a Phase 1 trial and remain under active development, among other conditions. Written informed consent is required.

Most important for the political theory, the federal pathway can operate without the same FDA authorization step required for expanded access.

Most important for the practical theory, it still cannot force the company to provide the drug.

The distinction between these two theories runs through every access debate.

The political theory begins with bodily autonomy. If the government permits a competent adult to refuse lifesaving treatment, accept a risky surgery, or choose among approved options, why should the same adult be prevented from choosing a promising but unapproved medicine when death or irreversible decline is already likely?

The practical theory asks what additional institution must cooperate for that choice to become real.

The patient cannot choose a drug that has not been manufactured. The physician cannot administer a gene therapy without appropriate facilities. A company cannot supply every compassionate request if doing so would consume material intended for a pivotal trial. A treatment that requires custom production cannot be summoned by statute.

Right to Try therefore exposes an unusual property of medical autonomy: it is technologically dependent.

We tend to imagine autonomy as a sphere around the body. The patient chooses what enters it. But advanced therapy often requires a long chain of other people's affirmative work. A personalized antisense drug can require genomic diagnosis, sequence design, toxicology studies, synthesis, analytical testing, formulation, pharmacy procedures, and clinical monitoring. A CAR-T product requires cell collection, shipping, manufacturing, release, return shipping, lymphodepleting chemotherapy, infusion, and management of potentially severe toxicities. A personalized phage treatment may require isolating the pathogen, screening a library of phages, expanding suitable candidates, purifying them, testing them, and obtaining a legal route to administer them.

The patient's freedom sits at the end of an industrial process.

That makes the language of rights awkward.

A right against government interference is different from a right to another person's labor, inventory, intellectual property, hospital bed, or manufacturing capacity. The first can be created with a prohibition: the state shall not stop you. The second requires allocation.

Right-to-try statutes mostly create the first kind.

Patients often need the second.

The mismatch becomes especially severe in rare disease. A common investigational cancer drug may already exist in multiple trial sites and have established manufacturing. An individualized genetic treatment may not exist until somebody decides that one patient is worth building it for. By the time a family asks for permission, the central question may not be whether it is legal to administer the treatment. It may be whether any institution is willing to turn a diagnosis into a development program.

Milasen made this visible.

The drug was not sitting on a shelf behind a regulatory lock. Researchers had to identify a unique genetic problem, design an antisense oligonucleotide intended to correct it, test the candidate, manufacture material, and create a single-patient clinical plan. The permission mattered. So did everything required to make permission meaningful.

The 2025 treatment of KJ with patient-specific base editing made the same point with a more complex modality. The relevant right, if we wanted to describe one, was not merely the right to receive a vial. It was the right to inhabit an ecosystem capable of recognizing that one infant's mutation might be addressable, organizing experts, making a therapy, assessing enough risk to proceed, and moving faster than the disease.

No legislature can guarantee that ecosystem.

It can make the ecosystem easier or harder to build.

This is where the Montana experiment departs from the earlier right-to-try model. Rather than focusing only on an individual patient's exemption, the state has created a licensed facility category around experimental treatment. The institutional bet is different. If a jurisdiction creates places where experimental care can be organized, reviewed, delivered, monitored, and paid for, perhaps practical access will emerge around the legal permission.

That is more ambitious than telling regulators to stand aside.

It is also riskier.

An exemption used rarely for a patient with no good alternatives does not automatically create a market. A licensed center can. Once an institution has rent, staff, capital equipment, marketing costs, investors, and revenue targets, experimental treatment is no longer only an act of compassion. It becomes a line of business.

The moral assumptions change when a seller enters the room.

A patient may rationally accept a ten percent chance of a severe adverse event for a one percent chance of meaningful benefit. The problem is that neither number may be known. If the person describing the odds is also being paid $100,000 to administer the treatment, ordinary informed consent has to carry an extraordinary conflict.

This is not unique to experimental medicine. Surgeons are paid to operate. Oncologists administer drugs within systems that create financial incentives. Fertility clinics sell procedures with uncertain outcomes. Fee-for-service medicine is full of imperfectly aligned interests.

Experimental treatment adds a special ingredient: uncertainty can make exaggeration difficult to disprove.

If a therapy fails, the disease was severe. If a patient improves, the treatment receives credit. If outcomes are not systematically collected, the clinic can remember the successes and forget the failures. Testimonials become a kind of private trial in which the denominator is permanently hidden.

A good access pathway has to resist that tendency.

Expanded access does so imperfectly but explicitly. It exists within the investigational regulatory system. The product has a sponsor. The FDA knows the use occurred. Serious safety information can matter to the broader development program. Institutional review is not designed to prove efficacy, but it creates a second set of eyes.

Federal Right to Try reduces some of that architecture. Its advocates consider that a feature. Critics consider it a loss of useful safeguards without much gain because the manufacturer remains the decisive gatekeeper.

Both sides can point to something real.

A patient with days to live does not benefit from a process optimized for administrative elegance. A manufacturer trying to determine whether an experimental drug actually works can be harmed if uncontrolled use creates noisy safety signals or diverts scarce material. A regulator can impose unnecessary delay. A regulator can also detect a dosing problem that an enthusiastic team missed. An institutional review board can be slow and formulaic. It can also identify a conflict a patient in crisis has little reason to notice.

The useful question is not whether friction is good.

It is what each piece of friction buys.

A signature that nobody reads buys little. A second manufacturing release test may prevent a contaminated or misidentified dose. A month of committee scheduling may be indefensible for a rapidly progressive disease. A day spent checking off-target edits may be the difference between a plausible experiment and negligence. A public registry may take an hour to complete and save future patients from believing a treatment has never been tried.

The politics of Right to Try rarely works at that resolution because “reduce low-value friction” is not a movement.

The slogan instead invites a binary choice: regulation or freedom.

Personalized medicine makes the binary impossible to maintain.

The more bespoke a treatment becomes, the more difficult it is for the patient to evaluate alone. A pill sold for a common indication may have years of public evidence. A one-off editor has none. The patient can decide how much uncertainty to tolerate, but cannot personally perform sequencing quality control, sterility testing, off-target analysis, vector characterization, or potency assays. Autonomy depends on trusted intermediaries precisely when the treatment is most individualized.

This creates a paradox.

The therapies that make personal choice most morally compelling can make collective oversight most technically important.

If the intervention is a familiar approved drug used off label, the physician and patient can reason from an enormous evidence base. If the intervention is a new molecule never used in humans, personal willingness tells us almost nothing about whether the product in the syringe is what it claims to be. If the intervention is genetically tailored, the patient may have more reason than anyone else to want it and less ability than anyone else to independently verify its manufacture.

A serious right-to-try system therefore has to distinguish two freedoms.

One is freedom from a paternalistic veto: the government should not require a terminal or catastrophically ill patient to value uncertain survival the way a regulator values population-level evidence.

The other is freedom from fraud, hidden contamination, fabricated efficacy claims, undisclosed conflicts, and experiments that vanish without producing knowledge.

Removing the first kind of constraint can strengthen autonomy.

Removing the second can destroy it.

A choice based on false information is not made more autonomous because nobody regulated the seller.

This is why debates about experimental medicine cannot end with consent.

Consent is necessary, but it is downstream. Before a patient can consent intelligently, someone has to describe what is known. That description depends on data. The data depends on prior patients. The prior patients depend on reporting. Reporting depends on institutions. Institutions depend on rules or norms strong enough to resist the temptation to turn every uncertain outcome into a marketing story.

The right to try, taken seriously, begins to imply a right to know.

What is the product? Who manufactured it? Has this exact version been given to anyone before? What happened? Which risks come from the platform and which are specific to this variant? What alternatives exist? Is the clinician paid by the manufacturer or clinic? What happens if the treatment causes an expensive complication? Will the clinic report the outcome? Who will follow the patient six months later? If the patient travels home, who owns the handoff?

These are not bureaucratic decorations around autonomy.

They are the information architecture that makes autonomy possible.

The strongest version of the right-to-try argument does not require pretending that every desperate choice is wise. It says something narrower and more defensible: when the baseline is severe enough, society should be willing to tolerate more uncertainty, and its institutions should be capable of moving at the speed that severity demands.

The strongest answer from regulation should not be that rules are rules.

It should be that oversight can move quickly enough to remain relevant.

Recent individualized therapies suggest that this is possible. The FDA did not need to declare an entire category approved before a single patient could be treated. Investigators did not need to pretend that one patient's outcome would settle efficacy for a population. They used exceptional pathways, platform knowledge, preclinical work, manufacturing controls, and case-specific judgment.

This is not a victory for bureaucracy over freedom.

It is evidence that the argument has been framed too crudely.

There are at least four distinct ways an experimental treatment can reach a patient in the United States: inside a trial, through expanded access, through federal Right to Try, or through a state-created pathway whose interaction with federal law may be contested. Add international travel and the practical menu becomes wider still.

Those routes differ in review, reporting, legal authority, manufacturer obligations, data capture, and liability. They should not be discussed as interchangeable versions of “experimental.”

The distinction matters before anyone reaches the bedside because the pathways can change sponsor behavior.

A company deciding whether to offer expanded access does not look only at the patient. It looks at the development program. How much drug exists? How far is the pivotal trial from enrollment? Could broader access make recruitment harder? Does the company have staff to support physicians outside trial sites? What safety monitoring can it realistically perform? Will the use occur under the same formulation and dose being studied? Is there a compassionate-use policy already in place?

A well-designed public policy should make those company decisions more predictable rather than pretending they do not exist.

One useful obligation is transparency about availability. Sponsors cannot be compelled to provide every investigational product, but patients and physicians should not have to discover policy one email at a time. A public statement can say whether expanded access or Right to Try is considered, which products are covered, how requests are evaluated, and how long a response should take.

That does not make the answer yes.

It turns the answer into a process.

This matters because silence consumes clinical time. A patient can spend weeks contacting a company that never intended to supply outside a trial. A physician can repeat the same request another physician made yesterday. A small sponsor can be overwhelmed by compassionate-use inquiries after a promising news story and respond inconsistently because nobody designed the intake system before attention arrived.

The burden of exceptional access can therefore be reduced by standardizing refusal as much as approval.

A rapid, reasoned no can be more useful than an indefinite maybe.

Reasons also create policy evidence. If manufacturers decline mostly because inventory is scarce, the access problem is production. If they decline because hospitals lack capability, the problem is infrastructure. If they decline because they fear regulatory consequences from adverse events, the regulator can address that concern directly. If requests are denied because patients are eligible for nearby trials, then trial access rather than right-to-try law may be the more relevant reform.

Without reasons, every denial is politically interpreted as obstruction.

With reasons, the bottleneck can be named.

The physician's burden deserves the same scrutiny.

Expanded-access processes have become simpler, but one-patient use still asks a treating doctor to take on unfamiliar responsibilities. The physician may have no research coordinator. Community oncologists and neurologists may be less comfortable with investigational paperwork than major academic centers. A patient can therefore have a willing manufacturer and still fail to find a clinician able to carry the case.

This is a geographic inequality hidden inside a formally national right.

Patients near major research hospitals have more institutional machinery around them. Patients in rural or underserved regions may have to travel even when the drug itself can be shipped.

A mature access system could provide shared support: template protocols, centralized regulatory assistance, remote specialist consultation, standardized safety reporting, and clear pathways for local hospitals. The goal would not be to turn every physician into an investigator. It would be to make exceptional treatment possible outside the handful of centers that already know how to navigate it.

State experimental-treatment centers are one answer to that coordination problem because they concentrate experience.

They can also concentrate incentives.

A center whose business is experimental treatment will be better at saying yes than a hospital that encounters one request a year. That competence is valuable. The danger is that saying yes becomes the product.

The system therefore needs a visible rate of no.

How often did independent reviewers decline treatment because the evidence was too weak, manufacturing inadequate, the patient too medically fragile, or an ordinary trial preferable? How often did centers advise a patient to pursue standard care instead? How often did a manufacturer refuse even after the center was willing?

Those numbers would tell us whether the institution is a treatment center or merely an access retailer.

Liability is another part of what the right actually buys.

Right-to-try laws often contain liability protections or limitations designed to prevent fear of lawsuits from defeating access. The intuition is understandable. A physician or manufacturer may hesitate to participate if an experimental treatment's known uncertainty creates ordinary negligence exposure simply because the outcome is bad.

But immunity can be overread the same way permission can.

Accepting therapeutic uncertainty should not excuse negligent administration, misrepresentation, manufacturing failure, or failure to disclose a known conflict. The law has to preserve a difference between harm that arose from a risk the patient knowingly chose and harm that arose because somebody failed at a duty the patient could not perform personally.

That is the same line running through the entire book.

The patient can own the gamble.

Professionals still own competence.

A patient does not need a theory of federalism while confronting a fatal diagnosis.

The institutions do.

If we want permission to become meaningful access rather than a symbolic right, we have to follow the treatment backward from the bedside. Who can make it? Who will supply it? Who will review it? Who will pay for it? Who will record what happens?

Only then do we know what the right actually buys.
