# A Better Edge

The most useful question at the end of an argument about experimental medicine is not whether the gate should be open or closed.

It is what should happen at the gate.

A closed gate can be cruel. A patient with a rapidly progressive disease may not have time for the ordinary sequence of trial enrollment, data maturation, regulatory review, manufacturing scale-up, reimbursement negotiation and commercial launch. For an ultra-rare mutation, there may never be a conventional commercial launch at all. The patient may be the market.

An open gate can be cruel too. Desperation is valuable inventory for anyone willing to sell hope without learning from the sale. Remove every friction and the system does not become neutral. It becomes a market in which the provider knows more about the product, the patient has more to lose, and the evidence can disappear the moment the invoice is paid.

The choice between those two gates has distorted the politics of experimental treatment for years.

One side imagines bureaucracy standing between a patient and a medicine. The other imagines regulation as the thing standing between a patient and exploitation. Both pictures contain enough truth to survive political repetition. Neither is a good design specification.

The better problem is narrower.

How do we make the edge of medicine faster without making it forgetful?

The answer begins by separating two kinds of friction that are usually bundled together.

Some friction exists because an institution has accumulated procedures. Forms are duplicated. Reviews happen sequentially when they could happen in parallel. A physician does not know whom to call. A manufacturer has no standard route for a one-patient request. A hospital's legal department sees an unfamiliar treatment and starts from zero. An insurer needs a code that does not exist. A regulator asks for information that another division already has. None of this uncertainty improves the therapy. It is coordination cost.

Other friction exists because somebody has to know what is going into the patient, why it might work, whether it is contaminated, how much will be given, what happened after the last patient received something similar, who will notice if the patient's condition worsens, and whether the seller has quietly changed the product while keeping the same name.

That friction is not bureaucracy in the pejorative sense.

It is memory.

The task is to remove the first kind without deleting the second.

That sounds obvious until a real program has to decide what belongs in each category.

Consider a personalized antisense oligonucleotide. The sequence may be new. The target may be unique to one child. But the backbone chemistry may have been used before. The manufacturing process may be familiar. The toxicology of the class may be partly understood. The route of administration may be established. The laboratory may already know how to test identity and purity. A system that treats every new sequence as if chemistry itself has been reinvented will be too slow. A system that treats the new sequence as irrelevant because the platform is familiar may miss a sequence-specific hazard.

The reasonable answer is not one universal threshold.

It is inheritance with exceptions.

Evidence should travel when the reason for believing it travels is explicit.

That principle is already visible in the evolution of individualized-therapy regulation. The important move is away from asking whether every bespoke product can complete the full evidentiary journey of a mass-market drug and toward asking what parts of the journey belong to the platform. If the delivery vehicle is the same, perhaps some manufacturing and nonclinical knowledge can be inherited. If the target changes, perhaps target-specific analysis has to be regenerated. If a manufacturing step changes, the inherited confidence may shrink.

This is what a learning-access system would formalize.

It would not promise that every patient receives an experimental treatment. It would create a tractable route by which a clinician, manufacturer and patient can discover what must be known before treatment and what must be recorded afterward.

The route can be surprisingly small.

Before treatment, the system needs a public identity.

Not the patient's identity. The intervention's.

Every exceptional-access treatment should have a registration record that exists before the treatment is administered. It need not resemble a hundred-page clinical-trial protocol. It should answer a few basic questions in a form another physician can find later: what disease is being treated, what intervention is being used, what evidence supports the mechanism, which component is patient-specific, where the product was manufactured, what pathway authorized or permitted the treatment, and what outcomes will be followed.

This single step changes more than it appears to.

A registered intervention becomes countable.

Countability is one of the quiet achievements of clinical-trial registration. Before public registries became normal, it was easier for studies to disappear, change endpoints without notice, or exist only inside a sponsor's files. Registration did not solve selective publication, but it made absence more legible. You could at least know that something had been attempted.

Exceptional access needs the same primitive.

At present, the scientific record of one-patient medicine is shaped partly by publication luck. A spectacular success becomes a paper. A catastrophic failure may become an adverse-event report or a lawsuit. An ambiguous course may remain in a chart. A treatment that does nothing often has no narrative energy at all.

That is precisely the treatment future patients need to know about.

The right registry would not demand a publication.

It would demand a trace.

The second requirement is independent review proportionate to the risk.

Independent does not have to mean slow.

The history of institutional review contains enough procedural excess to make skeptics suspicious of the phrase. But the function matters. Someone who is not being paid for the treatment should look at the proposed intervention and the consent process before the patient receives it.

For a one-patient gene edit, that review should be technically serious. For an investigational product that has already been given to hundreds of patients under a mature protocol, the review may be narrower. Proportionality matters because a system that subjects every request to the maximum imaginable process will convert oversight into denial by delay.

The independent reviewer should ask questions the commercial relationship is bad at asking.

Is the biological rationale coherent?

Is the evidence being described honestly?

Are alternatives being presented in the same frame as the experimental intervention?

Are manufacturing uncertainties disclosed?

Is the proposed dose supported by anything beyond optimism?

Does the provider have a plan for predictable complications?

Is there a reason this treatment should be given now rather than after one more piece of information becomes available?

None of those questions guarantees safety.

They make recklessness harder to confuse with autonomy.

The third requirement is standardized consent.

Standardized does not mean generic.

A twenty-page form full of institutional disclaimers can technically disclose everything and communicate almost nothing. The useful part of standardization is forcing certain facts to appear in the same place, in ordinary language, every time.

What is approved, if anything, about this intervention?

What is known from humans?

What is known only from animals, cells or mechanistic reasoning?

How many people have received this exact product or a materially similar one?

What happened to them?

What are the plausible serious harms?

What does the provider not know?

What is the total price expected to be, including ordinary clinical care if complications occur?

Who is being paid?

What data will be collected and where will it go?

Will the patient be informed if later information changes the risk assessment?

A patient should not need to reverse-engineer the epistemic status of a therapy from adjectives.

"Innovative," "advanced," "regenerative," "personalized" and "precision" are not evidence grades.

A better consent document would make the grade visible.

The fourth requirement is price transparency before commitment.

This seems peripheral to a medical-regulation argument until one notices how money changes the information environment.

A clinical trial generally does not sell the investigational product to the participant as an ordinary commercial service. A paid experimental-treatment program can. Once revenue depends on enrollment, the provider acquires a reason to emphasize possibility and minimize ambiguity. That does not make the provider dishonest. It creates a conflict that should be designed around rather than wished away.

The patient should know the treatment price, the estimated ancillary-care price, the refund policy if manufacturing fails, the financial relationships among physician, facility and manufacturer, and whether follow-up care is included.

For personalized products this is especially important because manufacturing can fail before administration.

Cells do not expand.

A tumor sample is inadequate.

A phage cannot be matched.

A sequence does not pass a quality test.

A custom batch is contaminated.

An organ is not suitable.

The commercial contract should say who bears that risk.

The fifth requirement is manufacturing traceability.

The temptation in right-to-try politics is to place all moral weight on the patient's willingness to accept risk. But consent to biological uncertainty is not consent to manufacturing opacity.

Those are different categories.

A patient may rationally choose an intervention whose efficacy is unknown. That does not mean the identity of the administered material should be unknown. A patient may accept a novel cell therapy with incomplete clinical evidence. That does not mean sterility, chain of custody or dose should become optional. A patient may choose a phage cocktail assembled under time pressure. That does not mean nobody should know which phages were actually in the bag.

The edge should preserve uncertainty where the science is uncertain and eliminate uncertainty where competent manufacturing can eliminate it.

That is a useful regulatory test because it distinguishes courageous treatment from careless production.

The sixth requirement is a common adverse-event language.

If every program describes harm differently, the combined record becomes noise.

One clinic records "temporary discomfort."

Another records hypotension requiring treatment.

A third records an infusion reaction.

A fourth records nothing because the patient went to an emergency department in another state after flying home.

The patient experience may be similar. The data system sees four different worlds.

Exceptional-access programs do not need to duplicate every data field of a pivotal trial. They do need a minimum common vocabulary for serious adverse events, hospitalization, intensive-care admission, disability, major procedure, disease progression and death, with enough timing information to permit later interpretation.

The point is not to prove causality from one patient.

The point is to stop making causality impossible to investigate across patients.

The seventh requirement is outcome follow-up that survives geography.

The weakest point in medical tourism is often the flight home.

A patient receives the intervention in one jurisdiction and the consequences become someone else's clinical problem. A complication may be treated by a physician who has never heard of the product. The original provider may receive an email summary or nothing. The regulator overseeing the treatment site may never learn what happened. The home clinician may not know how to report the event.

Domestic state-level experimentation can reproduce the same failure.

A patient can travel to Montana, receive an experimental treatment and return to Florida, New York or California. If the treatment center's learning system ends at the state border, the country has built medical tourism without passports.

Remote follow-up should therefore be part of the treatment, not an optional research extra.

The provider should know before administration who will monitor the patient, at what intervals, what laboratory or imaging data will be collected, how adverse events will be escalated, and how the home clinician can obtain technical information.

This is ordinary care coordination treated as a regulatory asset.

The eighth requirement is stopping rules.

Autonomy is often discussed patient by patient, while evidence accumulates across patients.

Suppose a provider gives a similar experimental cell product to ten people. Each individual consented. After the first several cases, a pattern of serious complications begins to emerge. Does the eleventh patient's consent erase the relevance of the first ten?

Of course not.

A learning-access system should specify the conditions under which new treatment pauses automatically pending review.

The threshold may differ by modality. A death after a xenotransplant does not carry the same causal meaning as a death in a population already expected to have very high short-term mortality. A severe unexpected immune reaction after a personalized gene-editing infusion may demand more immediate scrutiny. The point is not one formula. It is precommitment.

People are poor at inventing stopping rules after they become financially, emotionally or professionally invested in continuing.

Trials learned this through data-monitoring practices.

Exceptional access should borrow the logic without importing every layer of trial administration.

The ninth requirement is regulator reciprocity.

This is where jurisdiction matters most.

If Montana learns something useful about an intervention, that knowledge should not remain a Montana fact. If a Mexican authorized trial produces safety information relevant to an American patient, the border should not make the information scientifically foreign. If an FDA expanded-access case identifies a manufacturing problem, a state experimental-treatment center using the same product should be able to know about it.

The biological system is global.

The data architecture remains provincial.

A better edge would make verified safety signals, manufacturing corrections and treatment outcomes portable across pathways.

That does not require one global regulator.

It requires common identifiers and a norm that exceptional-access programs are part of a shared evidence system.

The tenth requirement is a sunset for ignorance.

This may be the most important.

There should be a point at which a provider can no longer sell the same experimental intervention while claiming not to know whether it works.

The exact point will vary. Rare diseases may generate patients slowly. Highly personalized therapies may never produce a large homogeneous cohort. Some conditions fluctuate too much for simple outcome interpretation. But repeated commercial use creates an obligation to aggregate.

If fifty patients have paid for materially similar treatment and the provider still cannot say what happened to those fifty, the uncertainty is no longer purely scientific.

It is organizational.

The organization has chosen not to learn.

A learning-access regime should escalate evidence obligations with repetition. The first few treatments may look like structured exceptional access. As numbers grow, the provider should face stronger expectations for protocol consistency, prospective endpoints, external analysis and perhaps transition into a formal trial or product-development pathway.

This solves one of the strangest loopholes in experimental medicine.

A therapy can remain "too experimental" to support strong efficacy claims while being apparently mature enough to sell to patient after patient.

That asymmetry is good for the seller and bad for knowledge.

The system should not allow experimental status to become permanent commercial insulation.

There is an objection to all of this.

Each requirement sounds reasonable alone. Together they can become another bureaucracy.

That objection should be taken seriously because regulatory systems accrete.

A registration record becomes a ten-page application.

Independent review becomes a monthly committee.

Standardized consent becomes forty signatures.

Outcome reporting becomes a data contract that requires a research coordinator.

Remote follow-up becomes a telemedicine licensing problem.

A small hospital leaves the program because the compliance cost exceeds its capacity.

Soon the "faster" pathway takes six months.

This is not hypothetical in the broad sense. Institutions often respond to risk by adding layers whose individual logic is defensible and cumulative effect is paralysis.

A better edge therefore needs a design constraint: the minimum viable learning system.

The default package should be small enough to complete quickly and structured enough to scale automatically.

A registry entry can be generated from the treatment request rather than entered twice.

Manufacturing identifiers can be drawn from batch records already required for competent production.

Consent fields can populate the follow-up record.

Adverse events can use existing clinical terminology.

Outcome collection can rely on a short core dataset plus modality-specific modules.

Review can be asynchronous when urgency requires it.

The same digital record can travel among facility, physician, manufacturer and regulator with role-based access.

The design question is not how many forms demonstrate seriousness.

It is how little new work is required to make the treatment scientifically legible.

This is where modern information systems should matter.

Medicine has spent decades digitizing billing more successfully than evidence.

A patient can cross a state line and generate exquisitely standardized claims data about what was charged while generating almost no standardized public knowledge about whether the experimental intervention helped.

That is not a technical limitation.

It is an institutional choice.

The edge can be redesigned around the opposite priority.

Imagine a patient with an ultra-rare genetic disease for whom a personalized therapy is technically feasible.

The treating team opens one exceptional-access record.

The system imports the diagnosis and molecular finding.

The manufacturer identifies the platform and the patient-specific component.

Existing platform evidence is linked rather than rewritten.

A risk module flags what has changed from prior variants.

An independent reviewer sees the same packet.

The consent document is generated from the actual evidence state, including prior human exposures.

The batch identifier attaches automatically after release testing.

The patient receives treatment.

Follow-up prompts go to the treatment center and home clinician.

Serious events trigger predefined notification.

At three, six and twelve months, a small set of outcomes enters the shared record.

The next treating team can see the accumulated experience without seeing the prior patient's identity.

Nothing about this picture guarantees a good outcome.

That is not its promise.

Its promise is that speed compounds into knowledge instead of anecdotes.

The same architecture works differently for phages.

The patient-specific element may be the bacterial isolate and the selected cocktail. The reusable layer may be the characterized phage bank, purification process and administration experience. Matching data should be recorded. The system can learn which phages are repeatedly useful against which organisms and resistance patterns.

For neoantigen vaccines, the variable layer may include tumor mutations and selected antigens while manufacturing and delivery sit on a common platform. The system can learn where prediction fails, how often manufacturing meets the clinical window, and which immune responses correlate with outcome.

For autologous cells, the product is inherently patient-specific but process controls can be shared. Failed manufacturing attempts matter as much as successful infusions because feasibility is part of the therapy's real performance.

For xenotransplantation, the learning obligation is much heavier because the intervention implicates public-health surveillance as well as recipient outcomes.

The framework should flex with the externality.

That word matters.

Much of right-to-try rhetoric is built around an individual transaction: my body, my disease, my risk.

Many experimental treatments genuinely fit that moral frame. If an adult with a terminal illness chooses a poorly proven drug after understanding the uncertainty, the state needs a strong reason to interfere.

But not every consequence stays inside the body that consented.

A transmissible pathogen risk in xenotransplantation does not.

A fraudulent commercial market does not; it changes what future patients are told.

A hidden manufacturing failure does not if the same process is reused.

A lost adverse event does not if another patient is about to receive the same intervention.

The edge should become more demanding as externalities grow.

This offers a way out of the false choice between paternalism and laissez-faire.

The patient controls the willingness to face personal uncertainty.

The system controls whether avoidable uncertainty is imposed on other people.

That division will never be perfectly clean, but it is more useful than asking whether experimental treatment is simply a right.

Rights describe claims.

Systems decide whether those claims can be exercised without destroying the information everyone else needs.

Montana's experiment is valuable partly because it can make this concrete.

A licensed experimental treatment center has the opportunity to show that state-level flexibility can coexist with data capture, independent review, monitoring and access obligations. If the centers become transparent learning institutions, they may demonstrate a form of domestic regulatory competition worth copying.

If they become opaque commercial destinations that report little beyond compliance minimums, they will demonstrate something else.

The important part is that the experiment itself should be evaluated.

How many patients were treated?

For what conditions?

Using what classes of intervention?

How many were Montana residents and how many traveled from elsewhere?

What did patients pay?

How often did manufacturing fail before treatment?

How many serious adverse events occurred?

How complete was follow-up?

How often did providers stop using an intervention because accumulating evidence changed the judgment?

Did the access requirement meaningfully reduce wealth selection?

Did companies use the state pathway for therapies they were unwilling to provide through federal expanded access, and if so, why?

Did the federal government tolerate, challenge or accommodate the model?

Those are empirical questions.

The political debate will be tempted to answer them in advance.

Supporters will call success the existence of the centers.

Critics will call failure the existence of risk.

Neither is enough.

The measure should be whether the system gives seriously ill patients earlier access while producing information reliable enough to improve the next decision.

Mexico presents the same design challenge from another direction.

Its importance is not that it offers a place where regulation disappears. It is that written regulation, enforcement capacity, clinical excellence, private commerce and cross-border demand can coexist unevenly. That is normal for large health systems. The question is whether a patient can tell which pathway they are entering before they travel.

A learning-access model would make authorized experimental care easier to distinguish from commercial opacity.

A registered trial has an identity.

A carefully governed medical-innovation program has an identity.

A compassionate-use case has an identity.

A product authorized in another jurisdiction has an identity.

A clinic selling a treatment that cannot explain its regulatory status, manufacturing provenance or prior outcomes would also acquire an identity: the absence of those things.

Transparency cannot eliminate bad medicine.

It can make bad ambiguity more expensive.

There is a larger economic benefit to this design.

Personalized medicine will remain artificially expensive if every patient begins at zero.

The laboratory repeats the search.

The hospital repeats the legal analysis.

The regulator repeats the platform review.

The manufacturer repeats documentation.

The clinician repeats the consent explanation.

The payer repeats the argument over what category the treatment belongs to.

Some repetition is unavoidable because the patient is different.

Some is a failure to accumulate institutional capital.

A learning-access system turns prior exceptional cases into infrastructure.

The fiftieth personalized therapy should not require the same amount of institutional invention as the first.

That does not mean it should receive less scrutiny in total.

It means scrutiny should move toward what is actually new.

This is the deepest promise of platform regulation.

It is not deregulation.

It is more precise regulation.

The old system often asks whether a product is approved.

The next system will increasingly have to ask which parts of this treatment have been validated, which parts are new, what evidence can be inherited, and what must be learned from this patient.

That is a more complicated sentence.

It is also closer to the biology.

The edge of medicine will remain uncomfortable because the underlying moral problem cannot be engineered away.

Some patients will take treatments that fail.

Some will be harmed.

Some will receive a therapy just before stronger evidence shows it was a mistake.

Others will receive something years before ordinary approval would have made it available and will live because somebody accepted uncertainty early.

No regulatory architecture can sort those people in advance.

The best architecture can do something less dramatic and more useful.

It can make the uncertainty explicit.

It can make preventable quality failures harder.

It can separate a clinical gamble from a commercial deception.

It can preserve what happened.

And it can make the next gamble a little less blind.

That is a better edge.
