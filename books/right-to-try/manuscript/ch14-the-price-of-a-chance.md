# The Price of a Chance

Experimental treatment does not become free because it is uncertain.

It can become more expensive.

A conventional drug reaches the patient after development costs have been spread across a market, manufacturing has been scaled, distribution exists, insurers have decided whether and how to cover it, and hospitals know roughly what infrastructure the treatment requires. None of those systems guarantees affordability. Some approved cell and gene therapies are among the most expensive treatments in medicine. But a market structure exists around them.

An individualized experimental treatment can ask one patient to pay while the market is still being invented.

The charge may include scientific work that ordinary patients never see: sequencing, design, custom synthesis, small-batch manufacturing, analytical testing, nonclinical studies, regulatory preparation, specialized pharmacy handling, inpatient monitoring, and follow-up. Travel can add flights, hotels, local transportation, childcare, unpaid leave, and a second household maintained near a treatment center.

The price of the intervention is only the visible invoice.

The price of the chance is larger.

This matters because right-to-try debates often present access as though regulation were the scarce good. Remove the legal barrier and the patient reaches the medicine.

Money can remain a harder barrier than law.

A statute can say that an eligible patient may receive an investigational treatment from a willing provider. It cannot make a custom gene-editing program inexpensive. It cannot compel an insurer to cover a therapy without evidence sufficient for the insurer's policies. It cannot guarantee that a hospital will absorb uncompensated monitoring or manage complications without billing the patient.

The legal right can become a wealth test.

That possibility is particularly sharp in jurisdictions that build an industry around experimental care.

A clinic has to survive. Staff must be paid. Laboratories and clean rooms are expensive. Insurance is expensive. Personalized manufacturing can be brutally expensive. If ordinary reimbursement systems do not pay, the center has three broad options: philanthropy, cross-subsidy, or cash-paying patients.

Cash is the fastest.

It also changes the patient population.

The people who can travel for experimental treatment are not a random sample of people with the disease. They have financial resources, or families capable of raising money, or social networks capable of turning a diagnosis into a public campaign. They may be younger, more educated, more medically connected, or simply luckier in the attention their story receives.

The resulting outcomes can look better even before the treatment does anything.

Selection is not only a statistical nuisance.

It is part of the ethics.

A state may believe it has expanded medical freedom while expanding it most for people who can buy uncertainty.

Montana's requirement that licensed experimental treatment centers allocate two percent of net annual profits toward access for qualifying Montana residents is an attempt, however limited, to confront that problem. The mechanism recognizes that a state hosting an experimental-treatment industry may owe something to patients who live there and cannot pay market prices.

The rule is more interesting than the percentage.

It treats experimental medicine as an industry with distributive obligations.

The same principle could be designed many ways. A center might provide a fixed proportion of treatments at reduced cost. A state could create a fund supported by licensing fees or industry assessments. Manufacturers could offer need-based access. Philanthropy could subsidize custom development. Public insurance could cover defined categories of exceptional treatment when evidence meets a threshold below ordinary approval but above speculation.

Every mechanism creates new problems.

Means testing can be intrusive. A free-treatment requirement can incentivize centers to select the least expensive qualifying cases. A state fund can be captured politically. Insurance coverage can inflate prices. Philanthropy can favor emotionally compelling diseases. Mandates can drive providers to another jurisdiction.

There is no neutral allocation rule once scarcity exists.

The ordinary approval system hides some allocation inside population evidence. A drug is approved for a class of patients, and coverage decisions then operate across that class. Experimental medicine returns allocation to individual transactions.

Who gets the one manufacturing slot?

Who receives the company's limited investigational inventory?

Whose compassionate-use request does a small team process?

Which child's mutation becomes the foundation-funded project?

Which family can pay for the trip?

These are rationing decisions even when nobody uses the word.

Manufacturers have tried to create policies for compassionate access because ad hoc decisions are both unfair and operationally dangerous. A public policy can state which products are available, under what conditions, and why a company may decline requests. Some companies use external advisory mechanisms for difficult cases.

Right to Try does not eliminate those decisions.

It can move them from the regulator to the manufacturer.

That may be desirable if the regulator was adding little. It also makes a private company the gatekeeper of a morally charged resource.

The company has legitimate interests that do not align perfectly with the patient.

An early-stage sponsor may have enough drug for fifty people. Forty-five slots are needed for a trial designed to determine whether the treatment works. Five patients request access outside the trial. If the company supplies all five, it may have no reserve for manufacturing loss. If it supplies none, patients may die without trying the drug. If one outside patient has a severe adverse event because that patient is much sicker than trial participants, regulators and investigators may have to interpret a safety signal that complicates the program.

The company is not necessarily selfish when it says no.

It can still be wrong.

No allocation rule removes moral tragedy from scarcity.

Charging adds another layer because price can become a selection mechanism disguised as cost recovery.

Under federal investigational pathways, rules constrain what sponsors may charge in certain contexts and generally distinguish recovery of specified costs from ordinary commercial profit. The principle is that investigational status should not become a license to sell an unapproved drug as though efficacy were established.

State experimental-treatment frameworks can alter the economic environment.

A licensed center built as a business expects revenue. The Montana access requirement's use of net annual profits makes that explicit. Profit is contemplated.

This is not automatically a defect.

Profit finances infrastructure. Pharmaceutical companies develop medicines because future returns can justify present risk. Specialized manufacturers need customers. Clinicians need to be paid. A system that requires experimental treatment to be charitable will remain small and depend on the generosity of institutions already wealthy enough to subsidize it.

The question is what profit is paying for.

A provider can earn money for doing difficult, skilled work under uncertainty.

A provider should not earn money because uncertainty makes the patient's probability estimates impossible to challenge.

This is where price transparency becomes part of informed consent.

A patient should know which costs are fixed and which are contingent. Does the quoted price include manufacturing failure? Is the deposit refundable if the product cannot be made? Does it include hospital care for expected toxicities? What happens if a serious complication requires an intensive-care stay at another hospital? Is follow-up included? Who pays for repeat imaging, sequencing, laboratory monitoring, or treatment of adverse events after the patient returns home?

The commercial answer can affect the clinical decision.

A family that has raised $200,000 publicly can feel pressure to proceed because the money exists for that purpose. A patient who has already flown to another country may feel unable to walk away after a physician expresses new doubts. A nonrefundable manufacturing payment creates a sunk cost before treatment.

Desperation is not the only force that can distort consent.

Investment can too.

The more a family has committed, the harder refusal becomes.

Experimental-treatment programs should therefore separate irreversible financial commitments from clinical decision points whenever possible. The patient should not have to pay the full cost before independent review is complete. Major new information should reopen the decision without a financial penalty engineered to keep the patient on course.

This is not how many consumer markets operate.

Medicine is not an ordinary consumer market when the seller knows more, the buyer is ill, and the product's benefit is uncertain.

Medical tourism adds another set of financial asymmetries.

The headline price abroad may be lower than American care. That can be genuinely valuable. Cross-border surgery and dentistry can save patients substantial money for competent treatment.

Experimental care can invert the comparison.

A treatment unavailable at home has no ordinary domestic price. The foreign clinic is not competing against another seller offering an equivalent service under equivalent evidence standards. It is pricing access to possibility.

The scarcity is narrative as much as physical.

“You cannot get this in the United States” can justify a premium even when the reason is not that the treatment is proven and suppressed, but that it remains unproven.

This is why legal exclusivity can become a marketing asset.

Montana could encounter the same dynamic. If its experimental treatment centers become the only visible domestic locations offering certain therapies under state law, geography itself can create pricing power.

The state may welcome the resulting medical tourism as economic activity.

Patients will need protection from mistaking price for evidence.

High cost can make an intervention feel advanced. Custom manufacturing can genuinely cost more. Neither fact establishes benefit.

The reverse is also true.

A cheap experimental therapy is not necessarily less risky. Low cost can mean an efficient platform, a subsidized program, or weak manufacturing. Price is an unusually poor signal of medical quality at the frontier.

A better pricing system would make uncertainty legible.

One approach is itemized cost recovery plus a disclosed development margin. Another is outcome-contingent payment, though that creates difficult questions about what counts as success and can incentivize providers to select patients most likely to improve. A third is pooled financing: many payers fund a platform or rare-disease network so no one family bears the full custom-development cost.

Insurance could eventually play a larger role.

Insurers are often portrayed as enemies of experimental access because they refuse to cover unapproved treatment. The refusal has an understandable rationale. Insurance works by pooling predictable categories of risk. Paying six figures for interventions without reliable evidence can invite enormous spending on products that do not help.

But a binary approved/not-approved coverage rule may become increasingly mismatched to personalized medicine.

Some one-person therapies may never obtain conventional mass-market approval. A platform could have substantial evidence while each variant remains technically investigational. If insurers refuse the category entirely, individualized treatment becomes private luxury medicine.

Coverage policy may need intermediate evidence tiers.

A payer could reimburse treatment when the platform meets defined manufacturing and safety standards, the mechanism is well supported, independent review approves the case, no adequate alternative exists, and outcomes are reported to a registry. Payment could be conditional on evidence capture rather than ordinary approval status.

That model would make insurers participants in learning.

It also creates risks. Payers could use complex criteria to deny almost every case. Providers could game eligibility. Political pressure could force coverage of fashionable but weak treatments. Registry requirements could become administratively heavy.

Still, the alternative is not neutral.

The alternative is rationing by cash.

The cost problem extends beyond treatment to complication care.

A patient can accept an experimental therapy at a private clinic and experience a serious adverse event days later. Emergency departments do not usually turn away the patient because the original intervention was elective or unapproved. The public or insured health system can inherit the cost.

This creates a form of moral hazard.

The experimental provider captures revenue from the treatment while another institution absorbs part of the downside.

A responsible payment system should anticipate this.

Centers could be required to carry complication insurance, maintain reserves, or establish transfer agreements with hospitals. Patients should be told clearly whether ordinary insurance is expected to cover complications related to an investigational treatment. The answer can vary by policy and jurisdiction; ambiguity should not be discovered in an intensive-care unit.

Cross-border care makes the externality more severe because the treatment provider may be beyond the practical reach of the patient's insurer or home hospital.

The receiving clinician cannot undo the transaction.

This is one reason cheap access abroad can become expensive care at home.

The ledger is distributed across systems that do not share the revenue.

None of this means patients should be prevented from spending their own money on experimental treatment.

It means the transaction should be judged by a higher standard than ordinary consumption because the seller controls information the buyer cannot independently verify and because costs can spill onto others.

A right to spend is not a right to be misled.

The most defensible experimental markets will therefore make price boring.

Not necessarily low.

Legible.

The patient should know what the money buys, which costs reflect custom work, which costs support the platform, what happens if manufacturing fails, what happens if the patient becomes ineligible, and who pays for foreseeable aftercare.

That transparency can also improve the science.

If centers report treatment costs alongside outcomes, policymakers can learn which modalities are becoming scalable. A $500,000 one-off program that falls to $80,000 after ten related patients reveals platform learning. A clinic whose price rises while evidence remains unchanged reveals something else.

Cost curves can become evidence of institutional maturity.

The first personalized treatments will often be expensive because the system is being built around the patient.

The promise of a platform is that the next patient should not pay to rebuild the same system.

If prices never fall as experience accumulates, the platform may be learning less than it claims—or keeping the learning to itself.

That last possibility deserves its own accounting.

A platform can become cheaper to operate without becoming cheaper to buy.

The tenth custom sequence may use the same design pipeline, the same release assays and the same regulatory templates as the first. Manufacturing yield may improve. Failure rates may fall. Staff may become faster. None of those savings has to reach the patient if the provider faces little competition and demand remains desperate.

Learning curves and price curves can separate.

That is ordinary business behavior in a market with pricing power. It becomes ethically sharper when patients financed part of the learning through earlier paid experimental use.

A serious access regime could therefore require some form of cost reporting without dictating a price. Centers could report broad categories of direct manufacturing cost, clinical cost, platform overhead and margin to the regulator while protecting legitimate trade secrets. The purpose would not be to determine the morally correct price of hope.

It would be to make clear whether repeated use is becoming operationally cheaper and whether the public access mechanism is growing with the business.

Montana's profit-based access rule creates a natural reason to care about that accounting. A requirement calculated from net profit is only as meaningful as the definition and verification of the underlying financial figures. If experimental centers become vertically integrated with manufacturers, intellectual-property companies, management entities or affiliated clinics, value can move among entities before it appears as center profit.

That is not evidence of evasion by itself.

It is a familiar reason regulators define related-party transactions carefully when a public obligation depends on reported profitability.

The same issue appears in cross-border treatment networks. The clinic may be one entity, the manufacturer another, the referral company another, and the marketing operation somewhere else. The patient sees one package price while the economic value is split across a chain.

Follow the money and the treatment can look less like one medical act and more like a vertically coordinated industry.

Intermediaries deserve attention because they can make experimental medicine discoverable.

Patient brokers, medical-tourism facilitators, online communities, foundations, consultants and physician referral networks can help families navigate genuinely difficult systems. They can locate trials, translate records and coordinate travel. They can also be paid for steering patients toward particular providers.

A referral fee can distort the apparent independence of advice.

If a person recommending an experimental clinic receives compensation when the patient enrolls, the patient should know. The same should apply to social-media testimonials, patient ambassadors and physicians with financial interests in the treatment company.

Disclosure does not eliminate the conflict.

It lets the patient price the recommendation correctly.

Crowdfunding creates a parallel market in attention.

Two patients with similar diseases can face radically different financing outcomes because one has a large social network, a photogenic story, media access or a campaign that fits the grammar of online generosity. The platform may be nominally open to anyone who can pay while the actual allocation mechanism rewards narratability.

That is not the same as wealth, though the two often overlap.

It is social capital converted into medical access.

The result can distort research priorities too. Highly visible individual cases can attract philanthropic funding toward technically tractable personalized treatments while less visible diseases remain neglected. This can be rational at the project level and uneven at the population level.

The answer is not to suppress individual fundraising.

It is to build pooled infrastructure that does not require every family to become a media company before scientists can act.

Platform funds are one possibility. A rare-disease network could finance common manufacturing, toxicology and regulatory resources across many variants. Public agencies or philanthropy could underwrite the reusable layer while treatment-specific costs remain narrower. Insurers could contribute once a platform reaches an evidence threshold. Companies could receive payment for the individualized product without charging each family for the entire institutional invention.

This is what amortization means morally.

The first patient should not be the only balance sheet available.

Outcome-based payment sounds attractive in this context because it appears to align incentives. Pay more if the treatment works. Pay less if it does not.

The difficulty is defining work.

A cancer patient may live longer than expected while receiving several therapies. A rare-disease treatment may stabilize function rather than improve it. A metabolic edit may reduce crises without normalizing every biomarker. A cell therapy may produce remission followed by relapse two years later. Who chooses the horizon and the comparator?

The more personalized the treatment, the more room the contract has to become a second argument about evidence.

Outcomes-based contracts can still be useful. They should not be mistaken for a substitute for a good evidence system.

There is also a perverse incentive in charging only for success: providers may avoid the sickest or most uncertain patients because those cases threaten payment. A financing model designed to reduce patient risk can quietly reduce access for precisely the people right-to-try laws were meant to help.

Every payment rule selects patients somehow.

The useful design makes the selection visible.

The right to try is morally compelling because time is scarce.

The price of a chance matters because money is scarce too.

A system that opens one gate and ignores the other has not created equal freedom.
