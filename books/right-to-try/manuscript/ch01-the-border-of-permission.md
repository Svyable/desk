# The Border of Permission

The most important line in experimental medicine is often not drawn through a laboratory.

It is drawn on a map.

The patient can remain the same. The diagnosis can remain the same. The proposed intervention can remain the same. The risk does not change when a car crosses a state line or a plane crosses an international border. Yet the legal description of what may happen next can change abruptly.

That is strange enough in ordinary medicine. It becomes harder to ignore when the treatment itself is unusual: a drug that has completed only an early clinical study, a personalized genetic intervention, a cell product manufactured from the patient, or a procedure that belongs to a regulatory category still being argued over.

A patient in Colorado may hear that a treatment is not available outside a clinical trial. A patient who travels north into Montana may encounter a state statute and a licensed experimental treatment center designed around a broader permission to treat. A patient in Texas may be told that a cell intervention is not approved by the Food and Drug Administration. The same patient may find a clinic in Mexico advertising something with a similar name, perhaps with a different manufacturing process, different evidence, and a different relationship to Mexican law than the advertisement makes clear.

The medicine did not become safer somewhere over the Rio Grande. Nor did the FDA stop existing at the Montana border.

What changed was the institutional path between a person's willingness to accept risk and a clinician's ability to act on it.

That path has become a medical technology in its own right.

For most patients, regulation is invisible. A prescription arrives in a familiar bottle. An implant has already passed through years of testing. An antibiotic's label contains the accumulated decisions of manufacturers, investigators, statisticians, regulators, hospitals, insurers, and professional societies. The patient may dislike the bureaucracy around medicine without seeing how much of the finished product consists of bureaucracy that has been converted into trust.

The vial does not carry a little inspector inside it. The pill does not explain that a factory was audited, that potency was measured, that impurities had limits, that adverse events were reported, that lots had release criteria, that somebody compared a proposed benefit with a known risk. Those activities disappear into the ordinary quality of the thing.

Experimental medicine makes them visible again because the ordinary assurances are incomplete.

When a drug is unapproved, what exactly does the patient know? Perhaps it completed a Phase 1 trial. That phrase is often repeated as though it were a threshold between dangerous and safe. It is not. Phase 1 is an early stage of human study that can provide information about tolerability, dosing, pharmacology, or safety signals, depending on the program. It is valuable information. It is not a certificate that serious risks have been exhausted, much less proof that the treatment works.

If the treatment is personalized, even the phrase “the drug completed Phase 1” can become slippery. Which part completed Phase 1? A chemical backbone? A delivery vehicle? A related sequence? A platform for editing DNA? A manufacturing process? The exact material administered to one patient may never have existed before that patient's diagnosis.

This is where permission begins to separate from approval.

Approval is a conclusion, however provisional, that a regulator has enough evidence for a product to be marketed for a defined use under defined conditions. Permission can be narrower. A regulator may allow one patient to receive an investigational product without approving it for sale. A hospital may permit a physician to proceed after an ethics review. A state may create a framework within its regulation of facilities and medical practice. A national regulator may authorize a clinical research protocol that is not commercial approval. A surgeon may obtain permission for an exceptional use that would be inappropriate as routine care.

The distinctions matter because patients live in the narrow permissions long before the public sees an approval announcement.

The United States already has a system for this. Expanded access, often called compassionate use, allows patients with serious or immediately life-threatening diseases to seek investigational products outside ordinary clinical trials when criteria are met and the manufacturer agrees. The Food and Drug Administration can authorize individual-patient access, including emergencies. The agency says it allows the overwhelming majority of completed requests to proceed. A manufacturer still has to provide the product. A physician still has to manage the treatment. An institutional review board has a role. Safety reporting does not disappear.

That system is more permissive than many political arguments about the FDA suggest.

It is also more burdensome than its defenders sometimes admit.

Permission from a regulator does not manufacture a dose. It does not make a small biotechnology company divert scarce material from a trial. It does not guarantee a hospital will accept the liability or the administrative work. It does not create an expert clinician in the patient's city. It does not make an insurer pay. For a treatment that must be designed from scratch, a rapid regulatory answer is only one step in a chain whose slowest link may be chemistry, vector production, sequencing, release testing, or a sponsor's willingness to proceed.

The federal Right to Try Act was born from dissatisfaction with that chain. It created a route for certain patients with life-threatening disease to use eligible investigational drugs that have completed Phase 1 without going through FDA's expanded-access authorization in the same way. State right-to-try laws had already spread across the country. Their political appeal was obvious. A dying person should not have to ask a distant bureaucracy for permission to take a risk that belongs to the patient.

The sentence has force because it identifies a real asymmetry. Regulators are rewarded for preventing unsafe products from reaching the public and criticized when they fail. The individual patient may be living with a disease for which the known outcome is already unacceptable. A risk that looks excessive from the perspective of population regulation can look different from the perspective of a person running out of time.

But the right described in the slogan has always been incomplete.

No statute can guarantee that a manufacturer will say yes. No declaration of autonomy creates supply. A patient may have a legal right to receive a treatment from a willing provider without having a practical route to obtain the treatment at all. The hardest problems in personalized medicine are often upstream of the final permission: finding the mutation, designing the intervention, manufacturing it to an acceptable standard, deciding on a dose, creating assays, and persuading a team that the expected risk is not absurd.

Montana has now moved the experiment further.

The state had a right-to-try law before 2025. What changed with Senate Bill 535 was the creation of a more concrete institution: the experimental treatment center. Under Montana law and rules adopted in 2026, such centers are licensed by the state. The statute defines experimental treatment broadly enough to include an investigational drug, biologic, device, or other treatment that has completed Phase 1 and remains unapproved, subject to additional conditions. The licensing law calls for operational standards, oversight, inspections, data collection, outcome monitoring, and adverse-event reporting. It also imposes an unusual access requirement: a licensed center must allocate a portion of its net annual profits toward access for qualifying Montana residents or a designated support account.

This is not deregulation in the ordinary sense.

A state that charges an application fee, issues a facility license, requires policies, creates review obligations, monitors outcomes, and specifies an access contribution has regulated something. The novelty lies in what it has chosen to regulate toward. Instead of saying that unapproved treatment belongs almost entirely inside the federal investigational system, Montana has built a state-level institutional home for certain experimental care.

That decision raises a question the state cannot answer by itself.

The United States does not have one sovereign medical regulator. States license physicians, nurses, hospitals, pharmacies, and many other health facilities. They define standards of professional practice and much of malpractice law. The federal government, meanwhile, regulates drugs, biologics, devices, labeling, clinical investigations, manufacturing, and interstate commerce under statutes whose reach does not evaporate because a state prefers a different policy.

The division usually works because the layers are complementary. A state medical license permits a physician to practice. It does not authorize the physician to manufacture and commercially distribute an unapproved biologic. An FDA approval permits a company to market a drug under federal law. It does not grant a surgeon a state license.

Montana makes the seam visible because it has created a positive state permission where federal restrictions may still apply.

Legal analysts disagree about how far the experiment can go. The question is not merely philosophical. If an experimental treatment is made in Montana from inputs sourced elsewhere, does federal interstate-commerce authority attach? If a biologic remains subject to federal licensing requirements, what can a state facility license change? If a product has an active investigational new drug application, can it move into a state pathway without implicating the sponsor's federal obligations? If a clinic compounds, manufactures, imports, or distributes something, which federal rules still follow the material?

A patient sees one treatment. The law may see five regulated acts.

That complexity is part of why jurisdictional experiments are so appealing. They turn an abstract debate about the speed of medical regulation into a place a patient can point to. They also risk producing a category error: assuming that because a state has made conduct permissible under one body of law, every other body of law has been displaced.

The same category error appears internationally in a rougher form.

Mexico is often described in American conversations as a destination for treatments that “aren't allowed here.” Sometimes that statement is directionally true in practice. A clinic may offer an intervention that an American clinic would not lawfully market as routine treatment. But the reason can be misdescribed. Mexico has a national health law, a federal health regulator, research authorization procedures, establishment licensing, and enforcement powers. It maintains a public registry of authorized clinical trials. COFEPRIS has suspended clinics for offering stem-cell interventions without authorized research protocols or adequate scientific support.

The existence of those rules did not prevent a cross-border market from developing.

That is more revealing than the fantasy of a lawless country.

Written regulation and practical regulation are different objects. A regulator can have broad authority and limited inspection capacity. A legal category can be clear in a statute and contested in a clinic. An intervention can be marketed with language chosen specifically to avoid the category that would attract the strictest rule. Enforcement can be uneven across regions. Patients can arrive from another country carrying money, urgency, and little ability to evaluate what happened after they return home.

A commercial market can grow in the gaps without any legislature deciding that it should.

Medical tourism makes those gaps harder to police because the transaction is distributed. Advertising occurs on social media in one country. The patient community may live on a private messaging platform. Financing happens through crowdfunding. A broker arranges travel. The procedure occurs at a licensed hospital but the specific intervention may have a different regulatory status than the patient assumes. Follow-up is performed by a physician back home who did not recommend the treatment. If a complication appears months later, the clinic may never learn about it.

Every participant can describe only the piece it touched.

The patient experiences the whole system.

That is why the border of permission cannot be reduced to a map of laws. It is a map of institutions strong enough to make a law matter.

For a treatment at the edge of approval, there are at least six separate questions.

The first is whether someone can legally possess or manufacture it. The second is whether a clinician can legally administer it. The third is whether an institution will allow the clinician to do so. The fourth is whether a manufacturer will supply it. The fifth is whether someone will pay for the treatment and the consequences around it. The sixth is whether what happens to the patient will become knowledge that anyone else can use.

A permissive answer to one question can coexist with restrictive answers to all the others.

Consider a hypothetical genetic treatment for an infant with an ultra-rare metabolic disease. A research team can identify the pathogenic variant. A related editing platform has already been studied. The patient-specific guide can be designed quickly. The family is willing to accept substantial risk.

The legal permission to treat is only one bottleneck.

The team still has to determine whether the edit might hit important off-target sequences. Someone has to manufacture the components. Someone has to test sterility and potency. The delivery system has to reach the right cells. A hospital pharmacy has to receive and control the material. Clinicians have to decide on dose and monitoring. An ethics body may have to review the plan. The family has to understand that success in cells or animals does not establish benefit in their child. The sponsor or investigators have to decide what adverse event would stop treatment or prevent a second dose.

If a state legislature removed every state legal barrier tomorrow, none of those questions would solve themselves.

If a federal regulator insisted on a conventional multiyear development program, the child might die before the questions were answered.

That is the actual tension.

Medicine needs institutions capable of distinguishing between delay that produces knowledge and delay that merely preserves process. It also needs to distinguish between speed created by learning and speed created by refusing to look.

Those can feel identical to a patient until something goes wrong.

The history of experimental treatment contains examples of both. Early access has rescued people, generated hypotheses, and sometimes opened entire fields. Uncontrolled interventions have blinded patients, caused infections, produced tumors, emptied family savings, and left behind records too poor to explain what happened. Regulators have sometimes been too slow. Markets have sometimes been far too fast.

The moral vocabulary becomes unreliable when it assigns virtue by institutional role.

A regulator can be compassionate. A clinic can be cautious. A patient can be sophisticated. A manufacturer can refuse access for defensible reasons. An activist can identify a genuine bureaucratic absurdity. A hospital can hide risk aversion behind procedure. A seller can use the language of freedom to make claims the evidence does not support.

What matters is the design of the pathway and the incentives inside it.

The strongest experimental-access system would not ask only whether the patient is allowed to say yes. It would ask what must be true before that yes deserves institutional cooperation.

Is there a plausible mechanism? Is there enough evidence about the platform to justify exposing a human being to the patient-specific variant? Is the material what the provider says it is? Has somebody independent of the seller reviewed the case? Is the price disclosed before the patient travels? Are adverse events reported? Will outcomes be followed long enough to catch delayed harms? Will the next patient be able to learn that the first patient existed?

These are forms of friction.

Some friction is the enemy of timely care. Some friction is the infrastructure of trust.

The political difficulty is that both look like delay from the outside.

Personalized medicine intensifies the problem because the evidentiary clock can be faster than the disease clock in one place and slower in another. A conventional approval program can spread fixed development costs across a large future market. A one-person treatment cannot. A manufacturer of a blockbuster can run trials because millions of potential customers justify the expense. A laboratory designing an oligonucleotide for one child has no comparable market to amortize the work. The pathway therefore has to borrow evidence from prior molecules, prior manufacturing runs, prior platforms, and mechanistic understanding.

That borrowing is where the frontier of regulation is moving.

The question is no longer simply, “Has this drug been approved?”

It is increasingly, “Which parts of this intervention have we seen before, which parts are new, and how much uncertainty travels with the difference?”

That is a more technical question than the politics of Right to Try usually permits. It is also the only question capable of scaling personalized medicine beyond heroic one-off cases.

A jurisdiction can make itself attractive by answering the permission question more generously. Montana has done that deliberately. A country can become attractive because its practical market appears more permissive than the patient's home system, whether or not its formal law is actually weak. Mexico has sometimes occupied that role in American medical travel.

Neither geography solves the scientific problem.

At best, a jurisdiction can create a better place to confront it.

At worst, it can convert uncertainty into a product before anyone has learned enough to price the risk.

The border of permission is therefore not the edge between freedom and regulation.

It is the place where a society decides what kind of uncertainty it is willing to institutionalize.
