# Right To Try

**Personalized Medicine at the Border of Permission**

Sven Hardy Benson

A medicine can be real before it is approved.

That sentence is easy to misunderstand. It can mean that a molecule has been synthesized, that a cell product has been manufactured, that a virus has been matched to a bacterium, that a gene editor has corrected the intended sequence in a laboratory, or that a patient has already received the intervention under a narrow legal pathway. None of those facts means the treatment works. None means it is safe enough for ordinary use. None means the next patient should receive it.

But none means the treatment is imaginary either.

Modern medicine increasingly occupies this uncomfortable middle. We know how to make things that the old categories did not anticipate very well: an antisense oligonucleotide designed for one child's mutation; a base-editing therapy whose guide sequence is chosen for one infant; a vaccine manufactured from the mutations found in one tumor; a bacteriophage cocktail assembled against one resistant infection; immune cells removed from a patient, expanded or engineered, and returned as a treatment; a gene-edited pig kidney whose relevant “product” includes the animal, the edits, the pathogen controls, the surgical team, the immunosuppression, and years of surveillance afterward.

These are not all the same kind of medicine. Their risks differ, their evidence differs, and their regulatory histories differ. What they share is a growing mismatch between a twentieth-century image of a drug and the thing being treated as one.

The familiar drug is repeatable. A company discovers a compound, manufactures standardized lots, enrolls enough patients to learn something about safety and efficacy, submits evidence to a regulator, and—if the evidence is persuasive—sells essentially the same product to many people. The model is imperfect, expensive, sometimes slow, and capable of approving bad bets or delaying good ones. But its basic unit is legible: one product, many patients.

Personalized medicine can reverse the geometry.

One platform may produce many related products. One patient's mutation may determine the sequence. One tumor may determine the antigens. One bacterial isolate may determine the phages. One patient's own cells may become the manufacturing input. The recurring object may no longer be the finished medicine. It may be the process that designs and makes the medicine.

That creates a problem of permission.

If every meaningful variation is treated as a wholly new drug, a therapy for one person can be forced through evidentiary machinery designed for thousands. If every variation is waved through because the platform seems familiar, a small sequence change or manufacturing deviation can carry risks that nobody has actually measured. The scientific problem is difficult enough. The legal one arrives on top of it.

And then geography enters.

A patient in the United States can encounter several overlapping worlds. There are ordinary clinical trials. There is the Food and Drug Administration's expanded-access system, including individual-patient and emergency pathways. There is the federal Right to Try Act. There are state right-to-try laws. And now, in Montana, there is a state-created framework for licensed experimental treatment centers that reaches further than the older terminal-illness model and deliberately creates a place for certain interventions that have passed a Phase 1 threshold but remain unapproved.

The Montana experiment is not a declaration that federal law has disappeared at the state line. It is more interesting than that. States regulate medical practice and health facilities. The federal government regulates drugs, biologics, medical devices, and important parts of interstate commerce. Montana has used its authority to build a new state permission structure in territory where federal authority still exists. What happens when a clinic complies with the state and the product remains an unapproved drug or biologic under federal law is not a question a slogan can settle.

South of the border, another slogan becomes tempting.

Americans have traveled to Mexico for medical care for generations: dentistry, surgery, prescription drugs, fertility treatment, cancer care, bariatric procedures, and, more controversially, interventions sold as stem-cell, regenerative, immune, or other experimental therapies. In the American imagination, “Mexico” can become shorthand for medicine beyond regulation.

It is a bad shorthand.

Mexico has a federal health regulator, COFEPRIS. It authorizes human clinical-research protocols, maintains a national clinical-trial registry, licenses categories of health establishments, evaluates new molecules, adopts good-clinical-practice standards, and has taken enforcement action against clinics offering unauthorized stem-cell interventions. The existence of a market for unproven treatment does not prove the absence of law. It may instead reveal something less tidy: imperfect enforcement, contested categories, aggressive marketing, uneven institutional capacity, cross-border demand, and patients willing to pay before evidence has caught up.

That distinction matters because the moral argument changes depending on what we are actually describing.

There is a meaningful difference between a dying patient asking for a treatment that has shown enough early evidence to justify a supervised exception and a clinic selling an intervention whose contents, manufacturing, or claimed benefits cannot be independently established. There is a difference between a regulator authorizing a one-patient experiment within days and a seller using the language of innovation to avoid producing evidence. There is a difference between autonomy and advertising.

The title of this book comes from a political phrase, but the book is not an argument for or against Right to Try as a brand. It is about the deeper right that the phrase points toward and the obligations that come with it.

A person facing a lethal or severely disabling disease can rationally prefer a dangerous uncertainty to a known decline. That preference does not require ignorance, magical thinking, or contempt for science. For some patients the baseline is already catastrophic. A regulator looking at population-level evidence and a patient looking at the next six months may be solving different optimization problems.

The patient's claim is powerful.

It is not the only claim in the room.

The next patient has an interest too. If today's exceptional treatment produces an adverse event, a response, a manufacturing failure, a dosing lesson, or nothing at all, that information can change what tomorrow's patient should be told. When experimental treatment occurs inside a good clinical trial, a system exists for capturing some of that knowledge. Expanded access retains reporting and oversight. Outside structured systems, the information can dissolve into a testimonial, a private chart, a nondisclosure agreement, or a family story that nobody can interpret.

A market can absorb thousands of acts of experimentation and still learn almost nothing.

That is the problem this book keeps returning to.

The choice is often presented as access versus evidence. The opposition is too convenient. Access can be designed to produce evidence. Evidence requirements can be designed to preserve access. Manufacturing standards can be platform-based rather than blindly product-by-product. Independent review can be fast. Adverse events can be reported without requiring a full randomized trial. Prices can be transparent. Outcomes can be registered. The fact that a therapy is exceptional does not mean the learning around it has to be private.

Some of the most striking recent examples came not from regulatory escape but from regulatory compression.

Milasen, an antisense drug designed for a single child with Batten disease, moved from a genetic insight to an individualized treatment under a single-patient pathway and helped force regulators to think about how a one-person drug should be evaluated. In 2025, physicians and scientists reported treating an infant known as KJ with a personalized base-editing therapy for carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 deficiency. The timeline from diagnosis to dosing was measured in months, not the decade commonly associated with conventional drug development. The treatment still required design review, manufacturing work, preclinical evidence, clinical judgment, and regulatory authorization. Speed did not arise because nobody was watching. It arose because many people watched the same problem at once and agreed that the ordinary sequence could be compressed.

Those cases should make both sides of the usual debate uneasy.

They weaken the claim that meaningful oversight necessarily makes bespoke medicine impossible. They also weaken the claim that the only route to speed is to find a jurisdiction willing to look away.

The real frontier is institutional design.

What should remain constant when the treatment changes patient by patient? What can be learned once about a delivery system, chemical backbone, editing enzyme, manufacturing process, or release assay and carried forward? What must be rechecked for every new sequence? Who should be able to say no when a patient is willing to say yes? Who pays? Who owns the resulting data? What happens when the person selling the treatment is also the person describing its chances? How much evidence should be required before a state licenses a center to administer an unapproved intervention? What happens when state permission meets federal prohibition or uncertainty? What happens when a patient crosses a national border and the aftercare returns home?

These are not hypothetical questions waiting for a future technology. They are already embedded in therapies at the edge of medicine.

The chapters ahead move from law to laboratories and back again. Montana supplies one experiment in federalism. Mexico supplies a study in the gap between regulatory text and international perception. Personalized antisense drugs and gene editing show what happens when the product approaches a population of one. Cancer vaccines, phages, cell therapies, microbiome products, and xenotransplantation show different ways that manufacturing and biological variability can collide with categories built around standardized commodities. The later chapters follow money, consent, data, and medical tourism because no access system can be understood only by reading its statute.

The aim is not to tell sick people where to go for an unapproved treatment. A book cannot know enough about an individual case to do that responsibly, and the existence of a legal pathway is not evidence that a particular intervention is wise. The aim is to understand why more patients are finding themselves at the border of permission, why some borders move faster than others, and what we should demand from institutions that invite people to cross them.

A right to try is a claim about the present.

Medicine also owes something to the future.
