# Back Matter

*Right To Try* is a book about institutions at the edge of medical evidence. It is not medical advice, legal advice, or a directory of experimental treatments. The examples in the manuscript are included to examine how access, manufacturing, evidence, regulation, payment and patient autonomy interact when a therapy is investigational, highly personalized, unusually early, or available through a narrow jurisdictional pathway.

The legal and regulatory landscape described here is unusually changeable. Montana's experimental-treatment-center framework is new. Federal-state questions around investigational products can change through agency policy, enforcement, legislation and litigation. Mexico's clinical-research rules, health-establishment requirements and enforcement priorities also evolve. Readers making real medical, legal or investment decisions should consult current primary sources and qualified professionals rather than treating a trade book as a current rulebook.

The manuscript deliberately avoids treating "experimental" as a synonym for "bad" or "innovative" as a synonym for "good." Many therapies now considered ordinary entered medicine through periods of uncertainty. Some interventions that once appeared promising failed when tested carefully. Others succeeded only after manufacturing, patient selection, dosing, delivery or supportive care improved. A dramatic rescue case can be clinically important without proving general efficacy. A failed individual treatment can be tragic without disproving an entire platform. The recurring question is what can reasonably be inferred from the evidence that actually exists.

That is why the research trail matters.

The working research files for this book live beside the manuscript in the Sven Hardy Benson’s Desk. The research brief states the thesis under test and the main empirical questions. The chapter briefs identify what each chapter is trying to establish and include a "What would make this false?" note so that the argument has an explicit route for revision. The source ledger favors primary statutes, regulator guidance, approval records, trial registries and original clinical reports, with secondary legal or academic analysis used where interpretation, history or commercialization patterns require it.

Several distinctions should remain intact in any later revision.

Federal Right to Try is not FDA expanded access. Neither creates a drug, forces a manufacturer to provide it, obligates a physician or hospital to administer it, or guarantees insurance coverage. State laws can regulate clinicians and facilities without automatically displacing federal product law. A Phase 1 threshold means a product has crossed an early evidentiary threshold; it does not mean that safety or efficacy has been established. A therapy offered in Mexico is not, by virtue of geography, unregulated. A personalized product is not necessarily experimental: approved autologous cell therapies already show that patient-specific manufacturing can exist inside a conventional approval framework. Conversely, a treatment described as "regenerative," "precision," "advanced" or "personalized" does not acquire evidentiary strength from the adjective.

The manuscript also uses "platform" in a deliberately practical sense. A platform is not a regulatory exemption and not a claim that patient-specific changes are harmless. It is the repeatable layer from which evidence, manufacturing controls, toxicology, delivery experience or operational knowledge may sometimes be inherited. The regulatory problem is to identify which knowledge genuinely travels across variants and which must be regenerated for the new patient-specific component.

The cases of milasen and the patient-specific base-editing treatment for KJ are central because they demonstrate that individualized medicine can sometimes move quickly through intense collaboration rather than through the absence of oversight. They should not be read as evidence that every rare disease is technically addressable, that one-person therapies will become cheap automatically, or that later safety findings cannot change the interpretation of an early success. Long-term follow-up matters especially for modalities whose consequences may unfold over years.

Phage therapy, individualized cancer vaccines, autologous cellular therapies, microbiome interventions and xenotransplantation appear in the same book because each destabilizes a different assumption about what a medical product is. They are not interchangeable technologies. Their risk structures differ. Their manufacturing problems differ. Their externalities differ. Their appropriate evidentiary pathways may differ. The common thread is the pressure they place on systems designed around stable products given repeatedly to defined populations.

The policy proposal in the closing chapters is therefore intentionally modest in one respect. It does not offer one new approval category for all personalized medicine. It proposes a design principle for exceptional access: earlier treatment should leave behind usable knowledge. Registration, proportionate independent review, manufacturing traceability, understandable consent, transparent financial terms, common adverse-event definitions, portable follow-up, stopping rules and escalating evidence obligations are presented as components that can be combined according to risk rather than as a second full-scale drug-approval bureaucracy.

That proposal has a falsification condition too. If the learning obligations become so expensive or slow that manufacturers and clinicians stop offering reasonable compassionate access, the design has failed. Information capture has costs. Privacy matters. Trade secrets matter. Small hospitals have limited staff. Ultra-rare diseases generate tiny cohorts. Patient-specific therapies can make anonymity difficult. Any serious implementation has to minimize duplicate work and collect only information capable of changing later decisions.

The economic question remains open. Personalized medicine can distribute fixed platform costs across future patients, or it can create a world in which each desperately ill family is asked to finance a bespoke development program. Which future emerges will depend on manufacturing automation, reusable regulatory evidence, public and private reimbursement, philanthropic infrastructure, intellectual property, competition and the number of patients who can share a platform even when they cannot share the exact therapy. The manuscript argues that learning should reduce the next patient's friction. Whether institutions actually pass those savings forward is an empirical question for later editions.

The strongest claim of the book is simpler.

A patient's willingness to accept uncertainty should not be treated as evidence that a treatment works. It should also not be treated as permission for preventable ignorance.

At the edge of medicine, autonomy and evidence do not have to be enemies. A patient can have a serious claim to try before certainty while future patients have a serious claim that today's experiment not vanish into private anecdote. The seller, physician, manufacturer, facility and regulator each hold pieces of information the patient cannot produce alone. A credible experimental-access system is one that makes those pieces meet.

That is the standard by which the examples in this book should be revisited as the field changes.

If Montana's centers produce transparent outcomes, learn from adverse events and broaden access, say so. If they become opaque commercial destinations, say so. If federal law resolves the current ambiguity, remove the ambiguity from the text. If Mexico's enforcement environment changes, update the medical-tourism chapters. If individualized therapies become routine, revise the language of exception. If a promising modality fails, preserve the failure rather than protecting the thesis. If platform inheritance proves less reliable than hoped, narrow the platform argument.

The edge should move when the evidence moves.

So should the book.
