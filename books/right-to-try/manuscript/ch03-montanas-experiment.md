# Montana's Experiment

A new medical institution can begin as a line in a statute.

Before the building exists, before a patient walks through the door, before a physician orders anything, the institution is already partly real because the law has named it.

Montana did that with the experimental treatment center.

The phrase sounds generic enough to have existed for decades. In American health law it is not. The state created a specific licensed facility category, gave the Department of Public Health and Human Services responsibility for licensing it, attached operating requirements to it, and placed that facility inside a broadened version of Montana's right-to-try regime. By the summer of 2026, implementing rules had been adopted.

What happens next is uncertain.

That uncertainty is the reason to take Montana seriously.

Right-to-try legislation had already become common by the time Montana changed its approach. The first generation of laws usually focused on the patient's eligibility and the investigational product. They were legal exemptions built around an encounter: a sick person, a willing clinician, a willing manufacturer, and an experimental drug. They expressed a principle more clearly than they built an industry.

Montana's recent law is different because it creates infrastructure for the encounter.

A person may not simply declare an office an experimental treatment center. The state requires a license. The licensing statute specifies an application, identifies managers and supervisors, charges substantial initial and renewal fees, and requires the department to adopt operational standards, inspection rules, oversight mechanisms, safety standards, policies, and systems for data collection, quality assurance, outcome monitoring, and adverse-event reporting.

Those requirements complicate the word “permissive.”

Montana is more permissive about the category of treatment it wants to make possible, while being quite deliberate about regulating the facilities that provide it. The state has not said, in effect, “anything goes.” It has said that a particular class of experimental care should have a lawful institutional form under state law.

The distinction is easy to miss because American health policy tends to place regulation on a single imaginary slider. One end is strict. The other is free. Move toward access and one must be moving away from oversight.

Montana has attempted something else. It moves the eligibility boundary outward while constructing state oversight around the new territory.

Whether that construction is strong enough is an empirical question. Whether the state has authority to solve every federal legal problem is a constitutional and statutory question. But neither can be answered fairly by pretending the state simply repealed regulation.

The definition of experimental treatment is the first place to look.

Under the 2025 Montana Code, the category can include an investigational drug, biologic, device, or other treatment that has successfully completed Phase 1 of a clinical trial but has not been approved for general use by the FDA. The statute then requires one of two additional conditions: the treatment remains under investigation in an FDA-approved clinical trial, or it has a demonstrated safety record through documented clinical evidence from a qualified medical institution as defined by department rule.

That language deserves slow reading.

A Phase 1 threshold sounds like a strong filter to a lay reader because numbered phases imply a staircase. But development programs vary. Phase 1 can involve healthy volunteers or patients, single or multiple doses, dose escalation, pharmacokinetics, initial safety observations, or other early objectives. Some advanced therapies begin in very small groups because the condition is rare or because healthy-volunteer exposure would make no sense. “Completed Phase 1” does not mean the major safety questions have been answered. It means the intervention has crossed an early human-evidence threshold.

The second condition matters for another reason. Montana's law contemplates experimental treatment that may no longer be inside an ongoing FDA-approved trial if there is a documented safety record from a qualified medical institution under state rules.

That is where the state experiment becomes more than a copy of federal Right to Try.

The law is trying to create a route for treatments whose development status might otherwise leave them stranded between categories. A therapy can be biologically plausible, have some human exposure, and still be commercially unattractive or abandoned by a sponsor. A rare-disease program can fail to justify a conventional development budget. An intervention can accumulate safety experience without reaching marketing approval.

From the patient's perspective, abandonment and danger are not the same thing.

From the regulator's perspective, neither are abandonment and evidence.

A treatment can be discontinued because it did not work, because it caused adverse events, because the sponsor ran out of money, because a competitor became better, because manufacturing was too difficult, because a trial design failed, or because the market was too small. A state pathway built partly around historical safety evidence must distinguish among those reasons.

That makes the review process more important than the headline eligibility rule.

Montana's framework includes independent experimental-treatment review. The details matter because a center has a built-in incentive to treat. It exists for that purpose. A treating physician may believe strongly in the intervention. A patient may have traveled a long distance and committed substantial money. The moment of decision is therefore crowded with sunk costs before the first dose is administered.

Independent review is supposed to introduce someone whose institutional interest is not identical to the seller's.

This does not make the review infallible. Institutional review boards have been criticized for inconsistency, delay, excessive paperwork, and a tendency to protect institutions as much as participants. An experimental-treatment review body can inherit the same flaws. Independence can be formal rather than real. Expertise can be thin in a genuinely novel modality. A committee can become a rubber stamp if its business depends on repeat customers.

But the problem it addresses is real.

The patient and the provider do not enter an experimental transaction with symmetrical information. The provider may know the literature, the manufacturing history, the adverse-event reports, and the uncertainties in the proposed mechanism. The patient knows the disease from the inside. Both forms of knowledge matter. Only one party is usually selling the intervention.

A review mechanism creates an opportunity to ask questions that consent alone cannot answer.

Does the evidence cited by the clinic actually concern the same product? Is a trial described as successful because no dose-limiting toxicity occurred in six patients, or because there was evidence of benefit? Has the manufacturer changed the process since the cited study? Are there delayed risks that the proposed follow-up is unlikely to capture? Is the patient's condition similar enough to the studied population for the inference to make sense? Does the price create pressure to oversell uncertainty?

Montana's rules are interesting because the state appears to understand that a market for experimental care needs more than permission.

The access provision reinforces that conclusion. Licensed experimental treatment centers must allocate two percent of net annual profits toward experimental treatment and health care for qualifying Montana residents, either by providing free experimental treatment of equivalent value or contributing to a specified support account.

The number itself may prove too small, cumbersome, or symbolic. A profitable center can satisfy the rule while still serving mostly wealthy visitors. An unprofitable center has no net profit to allocate. The accounting definition of net profit can become its own policy problem. Yet the presence of the requirement tells us something about the legislature's theory of the institution.

This was not conceived only as a private refuge for out-of-state medical tourists.

The state is trying to claim a local dividend from an industry it hopes to host.

That makes Montana part medical policy, part economic-development experiment, and part federalism challenge.

The economic-development element should not be treated as corruption by default. States routinely build policy around desired industries. They create university research parks, tax incentives, hospital districts, energy corridors, insurance markets, and professional licensing regimes. If a state believes personalized medicine will become a major field, it can rationally try to attract clinicians, laboratories, manufacturing, and capital before the category is mature.

The danger is that economic development changes what failure means.

A regulator responsible only for safety can close a bad clinic and call the intervention a failure. A state that has publicly branded itself as a destination for medical innovation may acquire a reputational interest in the industry's success. The more jobs, investment, and political identity become attached to experimental treatment, the harder it can be to admit that a particular center should not exist.

This problem is not unique to Montana. Financial regulators oversee banks that employ thousands of people in their jurisdictions. Aviation regulators understand the economic importance of airlines and manufacturers. States regulate hospitals that are among the largest employers in rural communities. Industry and oversight rarely occupy separate planets.

Experimental medicine simply compresses the conflict.

The patients are vulnerable, the products are uncertain, and the evidence base can be too small for ordinary statistical confidence.

The federal question sits above all of this.

American law assigns states enormous authority over the practice of medicine. A state decides who may practice as a physician. It licenses hospitals and many other facilities. It can define professional misconduct, scope of practice, consent requirements, and much of the civil-liability environment.

The federal government has a different but overlapping role. The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and the Public Health Service Act create federal authority over drugs, devices, biologics, manufacturing, clinical investigations, marketing, and related conduct. Congress has also created federal pathways for expanded access and Right to Try.

Montana cannot erase those statutes by renaming a clinic.

The unresolved issue is what conduct its framework can protect under state law without colliding with conduct federal law still prohibits or regulates.

This is why careful descriptions of the Montana experiment use words like “may,” “could,” and “unresolved.” Those are not evasions. They are the accurate vocabulary for a system whose legal boundary has not yet been fully tested.

Imagine a treatment manufactured entirely inside Montana from materials already lawfully present in the state and administered by a Montana-licensed physician to a patient inside a state-licensed experimental treatment center. The state may consider the conduct permissible under its framework.

Now alter one fact at a time.

The active biological material crosses a state line. The treatment is manufactured under an active federal investigational new drug application. The sponsor ships it from California. The clinic imports a component. The intervention is a biologic for which federal law requires a license to introduce it into interstate commerce. The manufacturer makes claims on a national website. A medical device travels from another state. A patient arrives after responding to an advertisement seen in New York.

The simple picture becomes a web of federal contacts.

Modern medicine is unusually difficult to localize.

Even a product physically made in one state can depend on reagents, sequencing, software, laboratory services, intellectual property, equipment, and financing that crossed state lines. The scientific collaboration itself is interstate. A personalized treatment may be designed by one laboratory, manufactured by another, tested by a third, and administered by a fourth.

The more advanced the modality, the less plausible the image of an isolated state medical market becomes.

That does not mean Montana's law is meaningless.

Federal law is not self-executing in the broad political sense. Agencies choose priorities. Congress can amend statutes. Courts interpret conflicts. A state experiment can create facts on the ground that change the national debate. Marijuana policy is the obvious modern example of states creating legal regimes in the presence of federal prohibition and forcing a long, uneven renegotiation of enforcement. Medicine is not marijuana, and analogy can mislead. But federalism often evolves through periods in which state policy and federal text do not align neatly.

Montana may be trying to create such a period for experimental medicine.

The consequences depend heavily on who enters the market.

If reputable academic centers, experienced investigators, GMP-capable manufacturers, and specialists use the state pathway for therapies with substantial platform evidence but awkward conventional development economics, Montana could generate evidence that the existing federal architecture is unnecessarily rigid in specific cases. Its centers could become laboratories not only for treatments but for regulation.

If the market is dominated by clinics selling expensive interventions on the basis of thin evidence, permissive definitions, and testimonials, the experiment will teach a different lesson.

The statute cannot decide in advance which ecology will emerge.

Institutions around the statute can influence it.

Licensing standards determine the cost of entry. Review requirements determine how easily a weak treatment receives internal approval. Advertising rules shape what clinics can promise. Data obligations determine whether poor outcomes become visible. Enforcement determines whether the license means anything after issuance. Professional discipline determines whether clinicians can externalize the risk to patients and local hospitals. Insurance rules determine who pays when complications occur.

The most important data from Montana may therefore have nothing to do with a dramatic cure.

How many centers apply? Who owns them? Which modalities do they offer? Where are the products manufactured? How many patients are Montana residents? What do centers charge? How often does an independent review body reject a proposed treatment? How many serious adverse events occur? Are outcomes reported publicly in a form that can be interpreted? Do federal agencies intervene? Do hospitals outside the centers end up providing uncompensated complication care? Do sponsors with active FDA development programs participate or avoid the pathway?

Those facts will tell us what kind of institution the law created.

A single successful patient will not.

This is difficult because experimental medicine attracts stories more easily than denominators. If one person with an aggressive cancer improves after receiving an unapproved therapy in Montana, the case may be medically important. It may also be impossible to attribute. Was the response caused by the treatment, prior therapy, concurrent therapy, selection, spontaneous variation, or a diagnostic mistake? How many other patients received the same intervention without benefit?

A regulatory experiment deserves the same skepticism we would apply to a clinical one.

We need to know what would have happened otherwise.

Montana's defenders can reasonably say that conventional development often gives no satisfactory answer for ultra-rare or rapidly fatal disease. Its critics can reasonably say that difficulty generating evidence is not a reason to stop trying to generate it. The state will matter if it finds a way to combine access with learning better than the alternatives.

The standards for success should therefore be broader than patient volume.

A center that treats many people and learns little may be commercially successful while being scientifically poor. A center that treats few patients but documents manufacturing, outcomes, adverse events, and failed hypotheses could contribute disproportionately to the field. A state that attracts revenue by being the place where uncertain treatments can be sold cheaply has created one kind of market. A state that makes exceptional access conditional on high-quality evidence capture has created another.

The law contains pieces of the second model.

Outcome monitoring and adverse-event reporting matter. Independent review matters. Facility licensing matters. The access contribution matters. Yet none guarantees that the resulting information will become publicly useful or comparable across centers.

That gap is where the next generation of policy could begin.

Imagine that every experimental treatment center had to register each intervention before administration using a standardized public record stripped of unnecessary personal identifiers. The record would identify the modality, manufacturer, relevant prior human evidence, treatment rationale, price range, review decision, and planned follow-up. Serious adverse events would be reported in a standardized format. Outcomes would be recorded at defined intervals. The center could protect trade secrets without hiding whether a treatment had been tried twenty times with no apparent benefit.

Such a system would not turn experimental care into a randomized trial.

It would turn repeated private experiments into a visible learning process.

The objection is obvious: every additional reporting obligation adds friction and cost. If the system becomes too demanding, only large institutions can participate and the state recreates the federal burdens it was trying to escape.

That is the design problem Montana has chosen to enter.

A permissive jurisdiction does not succeed by having fewer pages of rules.

It succeeds if its rules remove low-value delay while preserving the information and manufacturing discipline that justify asking strangers to trust experimental medicine.

The story of Montana will not be settled by the text of Senate Bill 535 or the first set of administrative rules. Those documents create the conditions of the experiment. The actual experiment begins when patients arrive.

For the first time, a state has made an explicit institutional wager that experimental treatment can be treated as a licensed field of care rather than only as an exception to ordinary drug development.

That wager may produce a model, a warning, or both.
