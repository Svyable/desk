# The Data We Lose

A thousand treatments can produce less knowledge than fifty.

The difference is not the number of patients.

It is whether anyone can tell what happened.

Clinical trials are expensive partly because they convert medical events into interpretable evidence. They define eligibility, intervention, dose, endpoints, follow-up, and analysis before the outcome is known. They create a denominator. They try to distinguish a treatment effect from the natural course of disease, concurrent care, selection, and chance.

Experimental access has a different priority.

The patient is not primarily there to answer a research question. The patient is there because treatment may be worth trying despite incomplete evidence.

That difference is ethically important.

It is also the source of a preventable scientific loss.

An exceptional treatment can be clinically justified without being structured as a trial. It does not follow that the resulting information should vanish.

Too often, it does.

A patient receives an investigational intervention through compassionate use. The treating physician records the course in a chart. A serious adverse event may be reported. If the case is dramatic, it may become a publication. If nothing obvious happens, the details can remain local.

A patient pays for an experimental treatment at a private clinic. The clinic records enough information to provide care and bill the patient. Outcomes may be collected inconsistently. Follow-up declines after the patient goes home. The clinic remembers the people who send updates and loses the people who do not.

A patient travels internationally. The destination clinic has one record. The home oncologist has another. The patient's family keeps PDFs on a laptop. A complication appears in an American hospital, where the clinicians may not know exactly what was administered abroad.

No one holds the whole case.

Multiply that by years and a field can accumulate activity without accumulating evidence.

This is one of the hidden costs of regulatory arbitrage.

The public debate focuses on whether patients gain access.

The less visible question is whether medicine loses the learning that stricter pathways were designed to capture.

A trial can fail and still teach something.

A commercial treatment can succeed as a business and teach almost nothing.

That asymmetry matters because experimental markets can outgrow formal studies. If a therapy becomes widely available for cash in a permissive jurisdiction, patients may prefer to buy it rather than risk randomization or wait for a trial. The provider gains revenue and patients gain access, but the system can lose the comparative evidence needed to determine whether the intervention works.

The result can be a stable market in uncertainty.

Nobody has an incentive to resolve the uncertainty because the uncertainty is part of what permits the market to exist.

This is not a conspiracy.

It can emerge from individually rational behavior.

The patient wants treatment now.

The clinic wants to provide treatment.

The manufacturer wants revenue.

The state wants medical investment.

The home physician wants to preserve the therapeutic relationship even after disagreeing with the decision.

The insurer wants to avoid paying for unproven care.

No participant is tasked with turning all of the resulting experiences into a dataset.

The missing institution is a learning obligation.

Expanded access contains a limited version of one. The FDA remains connected to the investigational product. Safety reporting matters. Sponsors can learn from adverse events. The pathway is not designed to establish efficacy, and the data can be difficult to interpret, but the use does not occur entirely outside the regulatory memory of the product.

Federal Right to Try weakens some of that connection by design.

That does not mean federal Right to Try creates no record.

Congress required manufacturers or sponsors to submit an annual summary of use. The statute calls for the number of doses supplied, the number of patients treated, the uses for which the drug was made available, and known serious adverse events. FDA's implementing rule and reporting form operationalize that obligation.

The information exists at the regulator-facing layer.

The public layer is much thinner.

FDA's consolidated annual Right to Try report does not publish the number of patients treated. Instead it reports the number of eligible drugs and biological products in three categories tied to a separate statutory rule governing how Right to Try outcomes may affect ordinary approval review.

That rule is protective by design. FDA generally may not use a clinical outcome from Right to Try to delay or adversely affect review unless the agency determines that using the outcome is critical to evaluating safety, or the sponsor asks the agency to use it.

The annual table therefore asks an unusual question about learning: did these outcomes enter the approval system strongly enough to be used?

Through calendar year 2025, the published answer was strikingly consistent.

For the combined 2018–2022 reporting period, FDA listed twelve eligible drugs or biological products in the category for which Right to Try clinical outcomes were not used in marketing review. It listed four in that category for 2023, five for 2024, and six for 2025. In every reporting period, the other two columns were zero: no listed product for which FDA determined the Right to Try outcome was critical to a safety determination, and none for which the sponsor requested that the outcome be used.

Those annual figures should not be added and described as twenty-seven unique products. A product can appear in more than one reporting year.

The zeroes also do not prove the cases produced useless information.

A sponsor may have learned internally. A treating physician may have changed practice. A serious adverse event may have influenced decisions without fitting the statutory categories in the public table. Some products may never have reached a marketing application during the period. Congress deliberately made it difficult for an adverse Right to Try outcome to damage a development program unless the safety significance justified that use.

Still, the public record reveals a real design choice.

The government asks manufacturers for patient counts and serious adverse events, then publishes a consolidated summary from which the public cannot reconstruct the patient denominator and cannot see case-level outcomes.

That is not an absence of reporting.

It is a granularity gap.

The distinction matters for this book because a system can satisfy a reporting statute while remaining a weak learning system for everyone outside the sponsor and regulator.

A future patient asking the most elementary question—how many people have actually received treatment under the federal Right to Try pathway?—cannot answer it from the consolidated FDA table, even though manufacturers are required to report that number to the agency.

This is what data loss can look like in a mature bureaucracy.

The data is not necessarily missing.

The useful connection between the data and the public question is missing.

State experimental-treatment systems create an opportunity to rebuild it differently.

Montana's licensing statute explicitly calls for data collection, quality assurance, outcome monitoring, and adverse-event reporting in experimental treatment centers. That is one of the most consequential parts of the law because it acknowledges that exceptional care can be a source of structured information.

The value will depend on implementation.

“Collect outcomes” can mean almost anything.

A center can record whether the patient was alive at discharge and technically satisfy an internal policy. A serious learning system has to decide what outcome matters for each modality, when it should be measured, how loss to follow-up is handled, how adverse events are classified, and what becomes visible outside the center.

Standardization is the difference between records and data.

Suppose five experimental treatment centers administer related personalized cancer vaccines. One records radiographic response at twelve weeks. Another records physician impression. A third records circulating tumor DNA. A fourth records only serious adverse events. A fifth sends satisfaction surveys.

All five can claim to monitor outcomes.

None can easily learn from the others.

The same problem appears across countries.

Mexico's national clinical-trial registry makes authorized research visible. But commercial interventions outside formal trials can escape the structure that makes comparison possible. The United States has ClinicalTrials.gov and multiple adverse-event systems, but paid experimental care that falls outside conventional research can remain surprisingly opaque.

A global market for experimental treatment needs a global minimum vocabulary.

Not a single approval standard.

A data standard.

For every exceptional treatment, a core record could identify the disease, relevant molecular or biological features, regulatory pathway, manufacturer, treatment date, dose or procedure, concurrent therapies, major adverse events, prespecified clinical outcomes, and follow-up duration. Personalized products would add variant-specific information without necessarily disclosing proprietary details.

The goal would not be to pretend observational data is randomized evidence.

The goal would be to stop throwing observation away.

This distinction matters because registries are often oversold.

A large registry can still mislead. Patients who receive experimental therapy differ from those who do not. Disease severity influences both treatment choice and outcome. Wealth influences access. Clinicians select patients. Follow-up is incomplete. Outcome measures can be subjective. Multiple therapies are given together.

Statistical adjustment cannot manufacture a randomized trial out of every observational dataset.

But a biased dataset is still usually more useful than a collection of anecdotes if the bias is visible.

The denominator matters again.

If a center treated two hundred patients and followed one hundred fifty for a year, that fact is interpretable. If it publishes twenty success stories and says nothing about the rest, it is not.

Loss to follow-up should itself be reported as an outcome of the system.

This is especially important for medical tourism because the business model naturally produces missing data. Patients travel in for treatment and leave. The clinic has to spend money to follow people who are no longer local and may have no reason to respond.

A center that bases its claims on long-term benefit therefore has a duty to build long-term follow-up into the price and logistics of treatment.

Otherwise the claim is being supported by data the business model is designed not to collect.

Technology can reduce the burden.

Patients can report outcomes remotely. Home laboratories can draw blood. Imaging can be performed locally and transferred. Wearable devices can capture some physiological measures. Electronic health records can exchange selected data. Patient-reported outcomes can be standardized.

None of these tools solves the central governance question.

Who is responsible for asking?

A voluntary registry often produces participation from the most conscientious centers and leaves the weakest invisible. A mandatory registry can become a compliance burden so heavy that small legitimate programs avoid participation or move elsewhere.

The useful requirement has to be small enough to survive.

This is why the minimal dataset should be genuinely minimal.

Regulators and researchers have a habit of asking for everything because data feels free after the infrastructure exists. Every additional field can reduce completion quality. A ten-page form filled badly is worse than a one-page form filled reliably.

The required core should answer the questions future patients actually need.

Was this treatment tried in someone like me?

What exactly was given?

What happened in the short term?

What happened later?

What serious harm occurred?

Did the patient receive other treatments that make interpretation difficult?

How many patients disappeared from follow-up?

More sophisticated research can build on that foundation.

The existence of a public record also changes marketing.

If a clinic says it has treated five hundred patients, a registry can show whether five hundred records exist. If the clinic claims unusually high success, outsiders can compare the claim with standardized outcomes. If a serious adverse event occurs, patients can learn that the event happened without relying on the provider's website.

Transparency creates reputational discipline before legal enforcement arrives.

This could be one of the most useful features of a state experimental-treatment market.

Montana does not need to prove that every therapy offered in its centers works.

It could prove that every therapy leaves a trace.

That would make the state valuable to regulators rather than merely adversarial to them.

Federal agencies could learn from outcomes generated under the state pathway. Researchers could identify signals worth studying. Manufacturers could detect problems across variants. Patients could compare centers. The state could see whether its industry is improving or repeating the same uncertain treatments indefinitely.

A state that wants to be a laboratory should publish the lab notebook.

The commercial objection will be privacy and trade secrets.

Both are real.

Rare-disease patients can be re-identifiable from combinations of diagnosis, age, and mutation. A one-person therapy can make anonymity almost impossible because everyone in the field knows who the patient is. Companies can lose proprietary information if detailed sequences or manufacturing processes are disclosed.

A learning system therefore needs layered access.

The public does not need the patient's name or exact genome. Regulators may need more detail than the public. Qualified researchers may be able to use coded data under agreements. The patient can decide whether additional biological samples or identifiable data are shared beyond mandatory safety reporting.

Privacy should shape the data architecture.

It should not become an excuse for no architecture.

The other objection is causality.

If experimental-access data cannot prove efficacy, why require it?

Because medicine learns more than efficacy.

Safety signals can emerge from uncontrolled use. Manufacturing failures can be counted. Dosing experience can accumulate. Biomarker trajectories can generate hypotheses. Natural history can be documented. Rare adverse events can connect cases across institutions. A lack of apparent benefit across many patients can weaken implausibly optimistic claims even without a randomized comparator.

Evidence comes in layers.

A trial is one layer.

A high-quality registry is another.

A mechanistic case report is another.

Pharmacovigilance is another.

The mistake is not using imperfect evidence.

It is forgetting what kind of evidence it is.

This matters especially for individualized therapies because the future evidence base may never look like a conventional large trial.

Imagine a platform that treats fifty different pathogenic variants across twenty ultra-rare diseases. No exact therapy is given to more than three people. A randomized trial of each variant is impossible. Yet across the platform, investigators can accumulate information about delivery, dosing, immune reactions, manufacturing, off-target effects, target engagement, and disease-specific outcomes.

The dataset becomes a matrix rather than a single trial.

Regulation will need methods capable of interpreting that matrix without pretending the rows are identical.

This is a statistical problem and an institutional one.

The statistical methods will improve only if the data exists.

Exceptional-access systems therefore have a duty to preserve raw material for future interpretation even when today's models cannot extract a definitive answer.

The patient may not benefit from that future analysis.

This is where the ethics becomes uncomfortable.

A person who pays for an experimental treatment should not be told that data contribution is the price of being allowed to try, as though access were a favor exchanged for research participation. The patient is seeking care.

At the same time, a provider who repeatedly sells uncertain treatment has obligations beyond the individual transaction because the provider's claims to future patients will inevitably draw on prior experience.

The obligation can attach to the provider rather than the patient.

The center must make good-faith efforts to follow outcomes, report what it knows, and state what it does not know. The patient can refuse optional research procedures while basic safety and treatment records remain part of ordinary professional accountability.

This is a cleaner ethical line.

It also changes the right-to-try debate.

The patient's freedom does not have to purchase society's ignorance.

A person can be free to accept uncertainty while the institution is required to remember the consequences.

That is the bargain experimental medicine has not yet made consistently.

A useful registry would also record why treatment occurred outside a trial.

That field sounds bureaucratic until one tries to interpret outcomes later.

Was the patient ineligible because of age, organ function or prior therapy?

Was there no trial within travel range?

Was the disease progressing too quickly to wait?

Did the sponsor decline trial enrollment but agree to expanded access?

Did the patient refuse randomization?

Did a state pathway permit treatment that a federal route did not practically enable?

These reasons describe selection.

Without them, the exceptional-access population can look like a mysterious cohort whose outcomes are compared naively with trial participants who were different from the beginning.

Selection bias is not merely a statistical nuisance here.

It is part of the policy story.

If wealthy patients disproportionately use paid experimental programs because they can travel and self-finance, their outcomes may reflect better baseline resources, stronger caregiver support or access to high-end medical centers. If the sickest patients receive compassionate use only after exhausting every alternative, mortality can look worse than in the trial even if the product helps.

A registry that ignores why people entered the pathway can produce evidence that punishes compassionate access or flatters commercial access.

The denominator needs context.

Timing creates another distortion.

A patient may receive an experimental therapy at a point when the disease is changing rapidly. Imaging performed four weeks earlier can be obsolete by treatment day. A biomarker may be moving. Steroids, antibiotics, surgery or bridging therapy can alter the baseline.

Trials manage this problem with protocol windows and scheduled assessments.

Exceptional treatment needs a lighter version of the same discipline.

The record should capture a clinically meaningful baseline close enough to administration that later change can be interpreted.

Otherwise a center can report improvement from a baseline chosen when the patient was sicker or report failure without noting that disease progressed substantially before the product was ready.

Manufacturing time itself may become an outcome.

For individualized therapies, a technically successful product that routinely arrives after the patient's clinical window is not a successful platform in practice. How long did design take? How long did manufacturing take? How often did production fail? How many patients deteriorated before administration?

Those cases are easy to erase because no treatment occurred.

They belong in the denominator.

This is one of the most important data lessons from personalized medicine.

The patient who never receives the custom therapy can be as informative about feasibility as the patient who does.

A platform that advertises outcomes only among successfully manufactured and treated patients can hide a major part of its real performance.

The same applies to phage matching when no active phage is found, to cell therapy when cells fail to expand, and to neoantigen vaccines when manufacturing misses the postoperative window.

Failure before treatment is still treatment-system failure.

Negative results need a home too.

Publication has always favored novelty and success. Exceptional medicine intensifies that bias because a one-patient failure may seem unpublishable while a one-patient rescue becomes international news. If the evidence system relies on journals to preserve experience, failure will disappear structurally.

Registries can correct this without asking physicians to write papers about every case.

A record can say that the treatment was administered, target engagement was not observed, disease progressed and no unexpected toxicity occurred.

That may be scientifically modest.

Across twenty similar records, it can become decisive.

The absence of effect is often exactly what commercial testimonial markets fail to preserve.

Data governance therefore becomes a form of consumer protection.

A provider making efficacy claims from its own experience should have to reconcile those claims with the complete treated denominator. If the center says that seventy percent of patients "improve," the outcome definition should be stated in advance or at least described consistently. If the definition changed, the change should be visible. If patients who died or stopped responding are excluded, that should be visible too.

This is not exotic methodology.

It is the minimum needed to stop arithmetic from becoming marketing.

Payers may eventually become an important source of evidence because they see care across institutions.

An insurer can observe hospitalizations, procedures and many downstream costs after an experimental treatment even when the treating center loses contact. Claims data is clinically blunt and often delayed. It can still reveal whether patients are being admitted, undergoing intensive care or receiving expensive rescue treatment after traveling for an intervention.

Public programs can see similar patterns.

The difficulty is linking those events to the experimental product without exposing patient privacy or creating punitive coverage policies that deter people from seeking necessary care after a complication.

A learning system should never give patients a reason to hide an experimental treatment from their home clinicians.

That would make both care and data worse.

Interoperability is therefore not a technical luxury.

The destination center should provide a structured treatment summary the patient can carry home: product identity, batch or variant identifier, dose, route, dates, relevant premedication, anticipated toxicities, emergency contact and reporting instructions.

The home health system does not need the entire regulatory dossier.

It needs enough to recognize a complication and return information to the treating program.

This is where a modest data standard can produce direct clinical value before any research analysis occurs.

The record improves the next emergency-room visit.

Later, aggregation improves the next patient.

Governance has to survive institutional incentives.

A registry controlled entirely by providers may underreport embarrassing outcomes. A registry controlled only by regulators may become slow, punitive or inaccessible to patients. A commercial data vendor may create a valuable dataset and then place the evidence behind a paywall. A patient advocacy group may have trust but limited technical resources.

The most credible structure may be layered: mandatory core reporting to a durable public or quasi-public system, detailed confidential data available to regulators, and additional research modules operated by qualified networks.

The architecture matters less than the principle that no one seller should own the only longitudinal record of its own uncertain product.

Independent memory is a form of oversight.

Clinical trials remember because they were built to learn.

Routine medicine remembers imperfectly through records, claims, registries, and surveillance because it was built to treat at scale.

Experimental markets can fall between both systems: too individualized for routine data and too commercial to behave like research.

That gap is becoming more expensive as the treatments become more powerful.

The data we lose today is the uncertainty the next patient has to buy again.
