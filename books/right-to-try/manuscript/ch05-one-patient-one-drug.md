# One Patient, One Drug

The old economics of drug development begins with repetition.

A company spends money once to discover and test a product, then manufactures it many times. The first pill can cost a fortune. The millionth should not.

Rare disease breaks that arithmetic.

A mutation found in one child can be medically devastating and commercially irrelevant at the same time. The scientific question may be unusually precise: one change in DNA disrupts one transcript in a way a particular chemistry could plausibly correct. The market question can be brutal: who spends years and millions of dollars to create a drug for one person?

Milasen forced those questions into the same room.

The patient was a young girl with a form of Batten disease, a progressive neurodegenerative disorder. Her clinical decline pointed toward a serious genetic condition, but the genetic answer was not initially straightforward. Researchers eventually identified an unusual mutation affecting the MFSD8 gene. The mutation created abnormal splicing—a problem in the way genetic instructions were processed into RNA.

That mattered because the defect suggested a class of intervention already familiar to molecular medicine.

Antisense oligonucleotides are short synthetic strands of nucleic-acid chemistry designed to bind RNA. Depending on their design, they can alter splicing, reduce production of a harmful transcript, or change other aspects of RNA behavior. The concept was not invented for one child. By the time milasen was contemplated, antisense drugs existed as a technological category and some had received regulatory approval for other diseases.

The sequence was new.

That distinction became the entire project.

The investigators did not need to invent nucleic-acid chemistry from the beginning. They did not need to discover that an oligonucleotide could be manufactured. They did not need to establish the general existence of intrathecal administration. They did need to decide whether a sequence designed for this child's mutation could alter the specific splicing defect, whether it could be manufactured to an acceptable standard, what nonclinical information was needed, how to dose it, and whether the expected risk justified treating a patient whose disease was already advancing.

The drug was named milasen after the patient.

That naming detail is emotionally powerful and scientifically clarifying. Conventional drug names are meant to detach the product from any one person. Milasen could not pretend to have that distance. Its target was an individual genetic event. The size of the intended market was one.

The case is often described as a triumph of personalized medicine. It was also an accounting crisis for the regulatory state.

How do you regulate a medicine that cannot be tested in another patient with the same mutation because no such patient may exist?

The normal sequence of development distributes uncertainty across stages. Early laboratory and animal work precedes initial human exposure. Phase 1 provides early information. Later trials investigate efficacy and continue to characterize risk. Manufacturing matures. The sponsor accumulates enough evidence to make a marketing application. Each stage can fail.

A one-patient drug collapses the sequence.

The first therapeutic use may also be the only therapeutic use. There is no population large enough for a conventional randomized trial. There may not be a second patient for dose escalation. The child who needs the medicine is also the person from whom the field will learn whether it can be given at all.

This is not a reason to abandon evidence.

It changes the kind of evidence that can exist.

For milasen, investigators used the mutation's molecular mechanism, cell studies, animal toxicology, experience with related antisense chemistry, analytical testing, and the patient's clinical circumstances to build a case for treatment. The FDA authorized the intervention under an individual-patient expanded-access investigational new drug application.

The process took roughly a year from identification of the relevant mutation to administration of the personalized drug—a remarkable compression compared with conventional development and an eternity if measured against a child's neurological decline.

The case became important partly because the treatment did not fit a comfortable category of success.

The published report described changes in seizure duration and frequency, among other observations, but the therapy did not reverse the underlying devastation of a progressive neurodegenerative disease. The evidence came from one person receiving a treatment in a setting where disease progression, supportive care, dosing changes, and measurement all complicate causal interpretation.

Milasen was not a randomized proof that personalized antisense therapy works.

It was proof that a personalized drug could be designed, manufactured, reviewed, and administered on a clinically relevant timeline.

That may be the more important first result.

In technological revolutions, feasibility often precedes scalability by years. The first demonstration does not tell us whether the system is affordable, repeatable, or equitable. It tells us which impossibility has disappeared.

Milasen removed one impossibility.

A drug for one patient no longer had to remain a thought experiment.

The next problem was institutional memory.

If every future patient-specific oligonucleotide were treated as though no antisense drug had ever existed, the field would never scale. The same basic chemistry would be re-litigated. Manufacturing questions already studied across a platform would be asked from zero. Nonclinical testing would become so extensive that the disease outran development. The cost of treating the second patient could remain almost as high as the first.

But if regulators assumed that every oligonucleotide with a familiar backbone behaved identically, they would erase the reason sequence specificity matters in the first place.

A different sequence can bind somewhere unintended. A different target can change biology in unexpected ways. A different dose or distribution can create different toxicity. Even if the synthesis platform is shared, the molecule is not merely a label change.

The challenge is to separate inherited evidence from patient-specific uncertainty.

The FDA's subsequent guidance for individualized antisense oligonucleotide products is one attempt to do that. The agency issued recommendations addressing administrative procedure, nonclinical testing, chemistry and manufacturing, and clinical considerations for individualized ASOs intended for severe or life-threatening diseases.

The existence of separate guidance is itself evidence that the old product model was under strain.

Regulators were beginning to treat “individualized ASO” as a repeatable category even when the finished sequence was not repeatable across patients.

This is a subtle change in what counts as the product.

For a traditional medicine, the product is the molecule in the container.

For individualized antisense therapy, the more durable regulatory object may be a stack: a known chemical architecture, a sequence-design process, a manufacturing method, analytical release criteria, a toxicology strategy, a dosing logic, and a patient-specific sequence at the top.

Some parts of the stack can accumulate evidence across patients.

Others cannot.

The same pattern appears throughout personalized medicine. The guide RNA in a CRISPR editor can change while the editing enzyme and delivery system remain similar. A neoantigen vaccine can contain different tumor-derived targets while the manufacturing platform remains stable. A phage cocktail can change composition while the laboratory's purification and release methods remain constant. A patient's engineered cells are unique as a lot while the production process follows a validated protocol.

The finished intervention varies. The factory of decisions can become standardized.

This distinction is where personalized medicine can become more than heroic medicine.

Heroic medicine is built around exceptional people doing exceptional work for an exceptional patient. It can be morally admirable and structurally fragile. The right scientist has to hear about the case. The right laboratory has to have spare capacity. Donors or foundations may have to fund work that no insurer covers. Regulatory specialists must improvise. Manufacturers have to accept tiny orders. The patient's family may become a project manager for an entire biomedical supply chain.

A system cannot rely on heroism at population scale.

It needs boring infrastructure.

Sequencing has to identify actionable variants routinely. Candidate design has to be faster. Screening for off-target binding has to become standardized. Small-batch manufacturing has to be available without custom negotiations every time. Regulators need known templates. Hospitals need procedures for pharmacy handling, consent, administration, and follow-up. Outcome data have to flow into a shared evidence base.

Personalization becomes scalable when the personalized part is the smallest possible part of the system.

That sounds almost hostile to the idea of individualized medicine. It is actually what makes it possible.

A modern eyeglass prescription is personal because the measurement changes; nobody reinvents glassmaking for each customer. A blood transfusion is matched to the patient without redesigning the blood bank. Kidney allocation is individualized through compatibility while transplantation uses institutional routines developed across thousands of cases.

The same principle can apply to molecular medicine.

The question is what can safely become routine.

This is where the language of Right to Try becomes insufficient. Milasen did not need a law declaring that one child was free to take a risk. The family was already willing. The difficult part was assembling a credible development path around an intervention that had no conventional commercial future.

A permissive jurisdiction might reduce one legal obstacle.

It cannot create the platform evidence that makes the risk intelligible.

If a clinic in a permissive state offered to synthesize a novel antisense sequence for a child tomorrow, the important questions would not be whether the center was licensed to provide experimental treatment. They would include how the pathogenic mechanism was established, how the sequence was selected, what chemical modifications were used, what analytical methods confirmed identity and purity, what off-target risks were considered, what nonclinical studies were performed, how the starting dose was chosen, and what monitoring would detect harm.

The permit belongs near the end of that list.

A good regulatory system should make answering those questions easier with each case.

This is the deeper importance of the milasen story. The treatment generated not only a clinical outcome but a regulatory precedent and a conceptual template. It helped establish that the FDA was willing to consider individualized oligonucleotides under a pathway adapted to the realities of one-person development. The later guidance began turning the exception into a category.

That is how regulation can learn.

The usual caricature treats agencies as institutions that accumulate rules. A learning regulator accumulates reusable confidence.

If twenty individualized ASOs share a backbone chemistry and manufacturing process without producing a particular class of toxicity, that information should matter to the twenty-first. If a sequence-design algorithm repeatedly identifies off-target risks that later testing confirms, confidence in the process should rise. If a manufacturing method produces consistent purity across many one-patient batches, every batch still requires release testing, but the background uncertainty changes.

The same principle works in the other direction.

If a supposedly familiar platform produces an unexpected immune reaction when a certain sequence motif appears, that adverse event should affect future designs. If a delivery route causes a delayed toxicity across related products, no future patient-specific variant should be allowed to hide behind the word unique.

A platform is valuable because it lets evidence travel.

The ethical problem is deciding how far it should travel.

Too little inheritance makes personalized medicine impossibly slow. Too much turns analogy into evidence and exposes patients to risks that the platform cannot actually predict.

No statute can calculate that boundary in general.

It has to be built from modality-specific science.

Antisense oligonucleotides are unusually suitable for the discussion because the sequence can be changed while much of the chemical and manufacturing architecture remains related. Even there, the target tissue, administration route, patient biology, disease mechanism, and sequence-specific effects matter. A framework that works for ASOs may not transfer cleanly to viral gene therapy, cells, or whole organs.

This is another reason broad right-to-try laws can feel simultaneously liberating and scientifically thin.

They define eligibility at a high level: disease severity, prior treatment, development phase, consent. The clinically decisive questions live lower down in the modality.

What does Phase 1 mean for a family of personalized products?

If one related oligonucleotide completed a Phase 1 trial, has the patient-specific sequence “completed Phase 1”? If the chemistry is the same but the target is different, which evidence transfers? If the finished product has literally never been administered to another human, the phase language inherited from mass-market development begins to lose precision.

Montana's framework, federal Right to Try, and expanded access all ultimately encounter this problem as personalized products become more common.

A rule written around an “investigational drug” assumes the drug is stable enough to have a development history.

A platform medicine can have a development history while the final variant is new.

The FDA's 2026 move toward a framework for individualized therapies in ultra-rare disease reflects the same pressure. The agency described a pathway in which treatments sharing a plausible mechanism could benefit from accumulated evidence, recognizing that randomized trials may be impossible for populations measured in single digits.

The important word is not permissive.

It is accumulated.

Personalized medicine will depend on the ability to accumulate evidence without requiring identical products.

That changes what we should demand from exceptional access.

If an individualized therapy is administered under expanded access, a state experimental-treatment pathway, or another exception, the value of the case should not end with the patient. The manufacturing record, sequence-specific findings, dosing experience, adverse events, biomarkers, and clinical outcome can all contribute to platform knowledge if they are captured in a form that future teams can use.

Without that, every patient becomes the first patient again.

Natural history becomes unusually important when there is only one treated patient.

In a common disease, a trial can compare treated and untreated groups. In an ultra-rare progressive disorder, the best available comparator may be the patient's own prior trajectory, a small natural-history cohort, affected siblings, or patients with related variants. Each comparison can be informative and each can mislead.

A disease may progress unevenly. Seizures can fluctuate. Development can plateau and decline in ways that are difficult to model. Supportive care can improve. A patient can appear stable for a period even without disease modification. If the expected untreated course is poorly understood, a post-treatment change becomes hard to interpret.

This means natural-history work is not secondary research waiting for a drug.

It is part of therapeutic infrastructure.

Rare-disease networks can collect longitudinal measures before an individualized treatment exists. Families often experience that data collection as observation without action, especially when disease is worsening. Yet the record can later become the only way to judge whether a one-person intervention plausibly changed the course.

The faster custom treatments become, the more valuable it is to start measuring before a candidate exists.

That creates a tension in outcome selection.

The easiest thing to measure may not be what matters most to the family. A biomarker can change quickly while function continues to decline. A seizure count can improve while cognition does not. A molecular assay can show corrected splicing without proving that enough affected cells recovered to change the disease meaningfully.

One-patient evidence has no room to hide a bad endpoint inside a large sample.

The clinical plan should state in advance what changes would be considered encouraging, what would count as no meaningful effect, and what observations are too confounded to support a claim.

That precommitment does not convert the case into a randomized trial.

It reduces the temptation to redefine success after seeing the outcome.

Repeated dosing can turn one patient into a small longitudinal experiment, but even that needs caution. If dose increases are followed by improvement, the relationship can strengthen a causal argument. Disease fluctuation, concurrent treatment and maturation can still interfere. If the treatment's effect is expected to accumulate slowly, early dosing periods may not provide a clean baseline for later ones.

The N-of-1 label can therefore create false confidence if it suggests that every single-patient treatment is automatically a rigorous N-of-1 trial.

A formal N-of-1 design has structure. It may use repeated treatment periods, withdrawal, randomization or blinded comparison where feasible. Many irreversible or progressive-disease therapies cannot use those methods. Gene editing cannot be crossed over. A child with a degenerative condition cannot be returned to the untreated state after a biological change.

The evidence has to fit the intervention rather than borrowing the prestige of a design that cannot actually be used.

This strengthens the case for platform aggregation.

The clinical efficacy evidence for one sequence may remain weak forever. Safety, manufacturing and mechanistic evidence can become strong across a family of sequences. If multiple individualized ASOs show target engagement predicted by the same design framework and their toxicities behave in ways that correlate with known platform features, the field can learn even while each disease remains rare.

The evidentiary unit becomes partly the platform and partly the patient.

That duality is what conventional approval categories are learning to accommodate.

There is an uncomfortable commercial implication.

The people who pay to create one-person therapies may generate knowledge that lowers the cost of future therapies. A family foundation can finance the expensive first proof of concept. Later patients benefit from the regulatory templates and manufacturing infrastructure. A company can use data from related individualized products to build a scalable platform.

Who should pay for the first case?

The answer today is often whoever can.

Families raise money. Philanthropists fund academic programs. Institutions absorb costs. Small companies make strategic exceptions. Researchers donate time that would never appear in a reimbursement code. The model favors patients whose diagnoses attract scientific attention, whose families can organize, or whose mutations fit an existing platform.

This is not the equity model promised by genomic medicine.

A permissive state could make the inequality worse if it turns one-person drugs into a luxury service. A wealthy family might be able to pay for design, manufacturing, travel, and treatment while another child with an equally tractable mutation never enters the pipeline.

Or a permissive state could make the inequality better if it helps create shared facilities whose fixed costs are spread across many individualized programs and whose access obligations include local patients who cannot pay.

The law does not determine which future arrives.

Infrastructure and business model do.

Batching can change those economics in a way that sounds almost contradictory for one-person medicine.

The sequences can remain unique while the work is grouped. Several individualized ASOs can move through analytical testing, manufacturing scheduling, regulatory preparation and clinical-review cycles using common infrastructure. A laboratory can validate one method across a family rather than negotiating a new method for each molecule. A regulatory team can maintain a standing template instead of recreating the dossier.

The patients are not a batch biologically.

The institution can batch its overhead.

This is one route by which the second and tenth patient become cheaper without reducing patient-specific attention. It is also a reason specialized centers matter. A hospital treating one bespoke ASO every five years will repeatedly pay the startup cost. A network treating many related cases can amortize expertise.

Public policy can either help this concentration become shared infrastructure or allow it to become monopoly power.

If only one center knows how to navigate a particular personalized-treatment pathway, that center can become the practical gatekeeper even when the law is permissive. Standardized templates, transferable manufacturing methods and shared registries can make expertise portable enough that specialization does not become permanent dependence.

Milasen's legacy is therefore larger than one molecule.

It showed that the unit of medicine can become one patient without forcing the entire scientific system to become one-off. The chemistry was individualized. The knowledge around it did not have to be.

That distinction is the route out of the population-of-one paradox.

A treatment can belong to one patient.

The learning has to belong to more than one.
