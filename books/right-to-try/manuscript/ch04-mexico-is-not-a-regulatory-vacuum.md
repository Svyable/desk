# Mexico Is Not a Regulatory Vacuum

A clinic can be illegal and still be open.

That fact is obvious in almost every other area of life. An unlicensed contractor does not prove the city abolished building codes. An unauthorized investment scheme does not prove securities law disappeared. A restaurant can violate health rules in a country that has health inspectors.

Experimental medicine is often discussed differently.

When an American patient travels to Mexico for a treatment unavailable at home, the story is frequently compressed into a sentence: Mexico allows it.

Sometimes the sentence means the intervention is lawful under a Mexican category that differs from American law. Sometimes it means a physician can perform a procedure there that an American physician would not offer. Sometimes it means regulators have not stopped a clinic. Sometimes it means only that the clinic has a website and takes appointments.

Those are not the same fact.

Mexico has a federal system for regulating health products and clinical research. Its Federal Commission for the Protection against Sanitary Risks, COFEPRIS, authorizes human clinical-research protocols, maintains a national clinical-trial registry, evaluates new molecules, regulates categories of health establishments, and participates in the adoption of international good-clinical-practice standards. In 2015, the agency publicly suspended a Mexico City clinic that had offered stem-cell therapies without authorized research protocols and without human clinical evidence sufficient to support the claims being made.

A regulator cannot suspend an unauthorized clinic in a regulatory vacuum.

The more useful question is why the vacuum story persists.

Partly because American patients often encounter Mexican medicine through markets rather than institutions.

They do not begin with the Diario Oficial or a COFEPRIS guidance page. They begin with a search result, a patient forum, a video testimonial, an advertisement, a private Facebook group, a WhatsApp number, a podcast appearance, or a friend who says someone went to Tijuana and improved. The legal status of the intervention is filtered through whoever wants the patient to come.

The clinic may describe itself as licensed. That can be true without answering the relevant question. A facility can be licensed to provide health services without every product it administers having ordinary marketing authorization for every claimed indication. A physician can be licensed without a claimed research protocol being authorized. A hospital can be legitimate while a specific commercial offering sits in a gray or prohibited category.

“Licensed clinic” is not a complete regulatory sentence.

Neither is “clinical trial.”

Mexico's national clinical-trial registry exists precisely because authorized research can be identified. A trial has a protocol, sponsor, investigators, eligibility criteria, oversight, and a regulatory record. A patient paying a clinic for an experimental intervention may be participating in research, receiving an authorized product off label, receiving treatment under another lawful category, or buying something that should have been treated as research or an unapproved health product but was not.

The difference is not philosophical. It determines what evidence and accountability surround the patient.

A formal trial asks in advance what will be measured. It defines who receives the intervention. It records adverse events. It imposes a protocol that can be criticized. It creates a denominator: not just the patient who improved enough to give a testimonial, but the patients who did not.

A commercial intervention can be medically sophisticated and still fail this test.

If ten patients receive a treatment and two improve, the clinic may know something worth investigating. If only the two are visible to the world, the public learns the wrong lesson. If the clinic treats two hundred patients over five years but publishes nothing systematic, the market may have produced an enormous amount of human experimentation without producing a commensurate amount of human knowledge.

This is the hidden cost of regulatory arbitrage.

The immediate transaction is private. The lost evidence is public.

Mexico is especially useful for studying this problem because it combines a large, capable medical sector with proximity to the United States, lower prices for many services, major border cities, and an established culture of cross-border care. Millions of ordinary medical transactions have nothing to do with experimental treatment. Americans travel for dental work, bariatric surgery, fertility care, prescriptions, and elective procedures because cost and access differ.

Medical tourism is not a synonym for quackery.

That distinction matters because otherwise every cross-border patient can be dismissed and every clinic can hide behind the legitimacy of ordinary medical travel.

A person can rationally seek high-quality surgery in Mexico because the same procedure costs less. A patient can enter a properly authorized clinical trial there. A family can seek a specialist whose expertise happens to be in Monterrey or Mexico City. None of those cases belongs in a book about regulatory escape merely because a passport is involved.

The relevant cases are those in which geography changes access to an intervention whose evidence or authorization remains materially unsettled.

Stem-cell medicine became one of the clearest examples.

The phrase “stem cells” is almost useless without qualifiers. Hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation is an established part of medicine for defined diseases. Other cellular products are approved under specific regulatory pathways. At the same time, clinics around the world have marketed loosely characterized cell preparations for neurological disease, orthopedic conditions, autism, aging, spinal injury, autoimmune disease, and many other indications for which the evidence varies from preliminary to absent.

The shared vocabulary creates cover.

A legitimate field of science can make an unsupported intervention sound like an early version of an inevitable future therapy.

The patient hears that stem cells can become many cell types, that researchers are studying regeneration, that clinical trials exist, and that prestigious institutions publish work in the field. The clinic then offers “stem cell therapy.” The inference from the field to the product can happen without anyone making a single explicitly false statement.

That is why product identity matters.

What cells are being administered? Where did they come from? How were they processed? Were they expanded in culture? Are they autologous or donor-derived? What is their identity and viability? What contaminants were tested? What dose is given? By what route? What is the claimed mechanism? Which human evidence concerns this exact preparation for this exact condition?

Those questions sound technical because they are.

A regulatory category is partly a machine for forcing them to be answered before the marketing outruns the evidence.

COFEPRIS's 2015 suspension of a stem-cell clinic is instructive not because one enforcement action proves the system works perfectly, but because of the reasons the agency gave. The clinic lacked authorized scientific research protocols. It lacked human clinical studies supporting the therapeutic use being offered. The regulator also cited deficiencies related to information and to assuring cellular count and viability.

The enforcement language points directly at the distinction between an idea and a product.

“Mesenchymal cells may have therapeutic properties” is a scientific proposition.

“The cells in this syringe, processed this way, at this dose, given by this route, will benefit a patient with this disease” is a medical claim that depends on far more evidence.

A cross-border clinic can profit in the space between the two.

Why is that space durable if laws exist?

Enforcement is expensive.

A national regulator has to oversee food, medicines, medical devices, clinical research, health establishments, advertising, and other sanitary risks across a large country. Every inspection hour has an opportunity cost. Experimental clinics can be small, can change corporate names, can alter marketing language, can serve mostly foreign patients, and can distribute parts of the transaction across multiple entities.

Evidence is expensive too.

To prove that a clinic is making an impermissible claim or using a regulated product without required authorization, an agency may need records, samples, expert analysis, and a legally durable case. The clinic needs only enough ambiguity to continue operating until enforcement arrives.

International demand adds another asymmetry.

A treatment that is expensive but attractive to Americans can generate revenue at a scale unrelated to local epidemiology. The patients may leave the country days after treatment. Complications can appear at home and be treated by American hospitals. Long-term failures may never return to the Mexican clinic's records. The provider receives payment immediately while some of the clinical and financial consequences are exported.

This is not a criticism of Mexico so much as a property of cross-border markets.

The same structure can appear anywhere patients travel from a stricter, more expensive, or slower jurisdiction to a more permissive one. The destination captures revenue. The home system may capture aftercare. Neither automatically owns the complete data.

The internet has made the geography stranger.

Medical tourism used to require local reputation, brokers, or word of mouth. Now a clinic can build an international brand without a local patient ever seeing an advertisement. Search engines, social media, influencer testimonials, and disease-specific communities allow providers to target exactly the people most motivated to seek alternatives.

Personalized medicine fits this marketing environment unusually well.

The promise can be framed as the opposite of bureaucracy. Conventional medicine treats averages; we treat you. Conventional trials exclude patients; we customize. Regulators require population evidence; your disease is unique. The rhetoric contains a legitimate scientific insight: biological variation matters, and many modern therapies really are becoming more individualized.

The insight can also become a shield against falsification.

If every treatment is personalized, every failure can be explained as the wrong personalization and every success as proof of the method. Standardization begins to sound old-fashioned. Controls sound insensitive. A missing comparator is reframed as individualized care.

At that point personalization stops being a scientific strategy and becomes a way to avoid accumulating evidence.

The difference is whether the personalization itself is reproducible.

A serious personalized therapy has rules. A neoantigen vaccine is different for each patient, but the method for sequencing the tumor, selecting candidate antigens, manufacturing the vaccine, releasing the product, and monitoring immune response can be standardized. A patient-specific antisense oligonucleotide has a unique sequence, but the chemistry, analytical methods, toxicology logic, and dosing framework can be organized as a platform. A phage cocktail may differ by infection, but the methods for identifying bacterial susceptibility, purifying phages, testing sterility and endotoxin, and documenting outcomes can be repeated.

Personalization does not eliminate standards.

It moves the standards up a level.

This is where the comparison between Mexico and Montana becomes useful.

They are not equivalent jurisdictions. One is a sovereign country with a national regulator and an enormous health system. The other is an American state operating inside federal law. Their legal powers, institutions, economies, and patient populations differ radically.

Yet both illustrate the same pressure.

Patients and innovators search for places where a treatment can move from plausible to administered before conventional approval. A jurisdiction can respond by creating a formal pathway, tolerating ambiguity, failing to enforce existing rules consistently, or modernizing its approval system so the incentive to leave the formal pathway decreases.

Mexico in 2025 and 2026 has been doing some of the last thing as well.

COFEPRIS has publicized efforts to strengthen clinical-research regulation, adopt International Council for Harmonisation guidance, digitize and expose trial information, and accelerate parts of the authorization process. Those developments run directly against the popular image of a country competing by having no rules.

A regulator can compete on speed.

That may be the more important international story.

If countries believe advanced therapies, decentralized trials, cell and gene products, and precision medicine will be economically significant, they do not have to choose between strict approval and a gray market. They can try to become attractive places to conduct legitimate development: faster protocol review, clearer standards, interoperable data, capable ethics committees, predictable facility licensing, and credible enforcement against sellers who refuse to play by those rules.

The competition then shifts from “Who lets us do this?” to “Who can review this intelligently enough to let us do it sooner?”

That is a healthier race.

It is also harder.

Weak regulation is cheap in the short run. Capable regulation requires specialized reviewers who understand rapidly changing science. A regulator evaluating an individualized gene editor needs expertise in genomics, off-target analysis, delivery, manufacturing, immunology, and rare-disease clinical design. A regulator overseeing autologous cell products needs to understand process variability that cannot be eliminated by making a million identical tablets. A regulator evaluating a phage program confronts a biological product whose clinically relevant composition may need to change as bacteria evolve.

Flexibility without expertise becomes discretion.

Expertise without flexibility becomes delay.

The most attractive jurisdiction for responsible experimentation will have to build both.

Patients rarely have the luxury of grading regulatory systems. They confront a simpler question: can I get the treatment here?

That urgency gives clinics tremendous narrative power.

A provider can say that American medicine is too slow, that regulators suppress innovation, that foreign doctors have more freedom, that the treatment is personalized, that the patient has nothing to lose. Some of those statements can contain pieces of truth. Their combination can still be manipulative.

“Nobody else will treat you” is not evidence that this treatment will help.

“You have nothing to lose” is almost never literally true. A patient can lose money, time, eligibility for a trial, physical function, organ reserve, immune capacity, the ability to travel home easily, or the opportunity to spend the remaining time differently. A severe disease changes the acceptable risk. It does not reduce the risk to zero.

This is why the home physician's role becomes so uncomfortable.

A patient may return from an experimental clinic with new symptoms and little documentation. The local oncologist or neurologist can disapprove of the decision and still inherit the duty to manage the consequences. If the outside clinic does not share manufacturing information or treatment records promptly, the physician may not know what was administered well enough to interpret an adverse event.

Cross-border experimental care can therefore create an information externality.

The patient and clinic make the decision. The home health system needs the data.

A better international model would treat continuity as part of the intervention rather than an afterthought. Before treatment, the clinic would identify who will manage follow-up. The patient would receive a standardized record of what was administered, lot or manufacturing identifiers where relevant, known risks, monitoring recommendations, and a clear contact path for serious adverse events. Outcomes would be captured across borders with patient consent. A complication treated in Arizona would not remain invisible to a clinic in Baja California.

None of this requires pretending that Mexico and the United States will harmonize every product rule.

It requires acknowledging that the patient already crosses the systems whether the data does or not.

Verification can begin before travel with a surprisingly basic discipline: ask for identifiers that exist outside the clinic's own website.

If the intervention is described as an authorized clinical study, there should be a protocol or registry record capable of being located independently. If a product is described as approved, the approval should have a name, indication and responsible authority. If the provider says a facility holds a particular authorization, the authorization should be verifiable through the relevant public or official channel where one exists. If a physician claims a specialty or institutional affiliation, that too should be independently checkable.

None of these checks proves efficacy.

They establish that the clinic's factual claims have anchors outside its marketing system.

That is a meaningful threshold in a market where the same entity can otherwise manufacture the product, define the evidence, interpret the law and sell the conclusion.

A sophisticated clinic may pass every identity check and still offer a weak treatment. A clinic that refuses to provide basic identifiers should trigger a different kind of caution before anyone debates mechanism.

The burden should not fall entirely on families.

American physicians counseling patients about cross-border experimental care often lack a practical way to verify foreign regulatory status quickly. Regulators publish information for domestic legal purposes, not necessarily for a neurologist in Ohio trying to understand what a patient plans to receive in Tijuana. Translation, terminology and fragmented databases add friction.

Cross-border access would be safer if national regulators exposed machine-readable, searchable records of authorized trials, licensed facilities, enforcement actions and product status in forms foreign clinicians could use.

This is not harmonization of substantive law.

It is interoperability of public facts.

The destination country benefits too. A clinic serving foreign patients can damage the reputation of an entire national medical sector if bad outcomes abroad are reported as “treatment in Mexico” without distinguishing the provider's actual legal status. Better public verification protects legitimate Mexican hospitals and researchers from being grouped with businesses that merely share geography.

That reputational problem creates an enforcement incentive beyond individual consumer protection.

Countries competing for biotechnology investment and medical tourism need credible differentiation. A multinational sponsor considering a clinical program cares whether regulators are predictable, whether trial authorizations mean something, whether competitors can ignore the rules without consequence, and whether a safety scandal in a loosely supervised clinic will be associated with the jurisdiction's entire advanced-therapy sector.

Enforcement quality is therefore part of innovation policy.

A permissive gray market can attract short-term patient spending while deterring the kinds of companies that need stable rules for long-term development.

That tradeoff is easy to miss when every unconventional treatment is counted as innovation.

Domestic patients create another perspective.

An experimental-treatment industry oriented toward wealthy foreigners can absorb scarce clinical expertise, laboratory capacity and regulator attention. It can also create jobs, training and infrastructure that benefit the local system. Which effect dominates is an empirical question.

The presence of foreign demand does not make a treatment market exploitative by definition. It does mean policymakers should ask who the new capacity serves.

Does a specialized cell-processing facility increase access for Mexican patients or operate mostly as an export service delivered inside Mexico? Are local patients enrolled in legitimate trials? Are public hospitals participating in the resulting expertise? Do enforcement priorities differ when most customers leave the country?

These are distributive questions similar to the ones Montana will face if out-of-state patients become the main customers of experimental treatment centers.

Jurisdictional innovation can create a local industry whose benefits and clinical risks are not distributed to the same people.

The most dangerous thing about calling Mexico a regulatory vacuum is not that the phrase is unfair.

It is that it makes the wrong policy problem seem obvious.

If there are no rules, the solution is more rules.

If there are rules but commercial demand outpaces enforcement, the solution may involve enforcement capacity, better public registries, advertising oversight, cross-border data sharing, and clearer categories. If patients leave because formal research takes too long, the solution may involve faster legitimate pathways. If clinics exploit the word personalized to avoid product-level evidence, the solution may involve platform standards that preserve customization while making manufacturing and outcomes comparable.

Those are different interventions.

A country with a clinical-trial registry, a health regulator, facility licensing, new-molecule review, and enforcement authority is not outside the regulatory world.

It is inside the same argument as everyone else: how to keep scientific possibility from moving faster than institutional credibility without making institutional credibility so slow that possibility leaves.

The border does not separate regulated medicine from unregulated medicine.

It separates systems that fail in different ways.
