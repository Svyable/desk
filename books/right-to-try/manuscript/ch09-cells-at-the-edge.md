# Cells at the Edge

A cell can be a transplant, a tissue, a drug, a manufacturing input, or a patient.

Regulation depends on which description wins.

This is one reason cellular medicine has generated so much confusion at the border of approval. The object being regulated is alive. It may come from the same patient who receives it. It may be only lightly processed or extensively manipulated. It may perform the same basic function in the recipient that it performed in the donor, or be asked to do something biologically different. It may be expanded for weeks, genetically engineered, selected, frozen, thawed, shipped across the country, and infused back into the person from whom it originated.

“Stem cell therapy” does not tell us where in that landscape we are.

Neither does “autologous.”

The fact that cells came from your body does not make every use equivalent to ordinary medical practice. A surgeon removing a vein and using it as a vascular graft is doing something quite different from a laboratory culturing cells, altering their properties, and returning them as a treatment for a disease they did not previously address.

The regulatory system has tried to encode that difference through concepts such as minimal manipulation and homologous use.

The details are technical because the biological distinction is technical. Some human cells, tissues, and cellular and tissue-based products can fall under a lighter regulatory framework when they meet defined criteria. Products that are more than minimally manipulated or used for nonhomologous purposes may be regulated as drugs, devices, or biological products requiring premarket review.

That boundary has been fought over for years.

It matters economically because crossing it can transform the cost of a medical business.

If a clinic can treat a patient's own cells as part of the practice of medicine, it may be able to operate with relatively familiar state professional and facility regulation. If the same activity creates a biological drug under federal law, the clinic enters a world of investigational applications, manufacturing controls, clinical evidence, and marketing approval.

A classification can be worth millions of dollars.

This helps explain the persistence of clinics that market broad regenerative claims around autologous cells.

The sales pitch is intuitive. Your own cells. Your own healing. Minimal rejection. Personalized medicine. The intervention can sound less like a drug than like returning something that already belongs to you.

Biology is not obliged to respect the rhetoric.

Moving cells to a new anatomical location can create risks. Processing can change them. Contamination can occur. Cells can aggregate, migrate, provoke inflammation, or fail to behave as intended. A product prepared from a patient can still be manufactured badly.

Autologous does not mean harmless.

The contrast with approved cellular therapies makes this clearer.

CAR-T therapy is among the most personalized treatments in mainstream medicine. T cells are collected from the patient, genetically engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor, expanded, tested, and returned. The final lot belongs to one patient. Chain of identity is critical because a labeling error would not merely be a packaging problem; it could mean giving someone another person's engineered cells.

The product is bespoke at the lot level and standardized at the process level.

CAR-T is also heavily regulated.

That should disturb the easy assumption that personalized medicine and conventional approval are opposites.

Several CAR-T products have received FDA approval after clinical development. They are not experimental merely because each patient's cells are individually manufactured. The regulator evaluates the manufacturing process, product specifications, clinical evidence, and systems surrounding the treatment even though the actual cells differ from patient to patient.

This is a precedent hiding in plain sight.

Medicine already knows how to approve a platform that produces unique lots.

The experience is not cheap or frictionless. CAR-T manufacturing can fail. Patients can progress while waiting. Facilities need specialized capabilities. Cytokine release syndrome and neurological toxicities require expertise. The therapies are expensive, and access is unequal.

But the category proves something important.

Product sameness is not a prerequisite for regulatory approval.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy pushes the point differently. In 2024 the FDA approved lifileucel, marketed as Amtagvi, for certain patients with advanced melanoma. The treatment begins with tumor tissue removed from the patient. Lymphocytes that have infiltrated the tumor are expanded outside the body, then administered after preparative treatment.

Again the final cellular material varies by patient.

The approved object is the reproducible process and its clinical result across a population.

T-cell receptor therapies add another layer because the cells can be genetically modified to recognize defined tumor antigens in selected patients. The manufacturing is personal, while patient selection may depend on a shared antigen and HLA type.

Cellular medicine therefore contains multiple forms of personalization at once.

The source can be personal.

The manufacturing lot can be personal.

The target can be shared or personal.

The selection rule can be genomic.

The process can be standardized.

Public debate usually collapses all of this into the word cells.

Commercial clinics benefit from the collapse.

A provider can point to the undeniable success of bone-marrow transplantation, CAR-T, or other cell therapies and imply that a very different cell preparation belongs to the same arc of progress. The patient hears that cells are already curing cancer. The unanswered question is whether these cells, prepared this way, given by this route, for this disease, have evidence behind them.

The distinction is not pedantic.

It is the difference between a therapeutic class and a therapeutic product.

Insulin treats diabetes. That does not make every peptide a diabetes treatment. Antibodies can treat cancer. That does not make every antibody an oncology drug. Cell therapies can work. That does not make every cell therapy plausible.

Regulators have struggled to communicate this because the boundary is not visible to patients.

A clinic can look clean. A physician can be licensed. The treatment can be described in scientific language. The cells can genuinely come from the patient. None of those facts answers the federal product question or the efficacy question.

This is where a state like Montana enters a difficult space.

An experimental treatment center may be permitted under state law to provide certain unapproved interventions meeting the statutory requirements. A cellular intervention that has completed Phase 1 or otherwise fits the state's definition may be eligible under that state framework.

But the center's state license does not automatically transform a federally regulated biological product into ordinary medical practice.

The federal layer follows the product and conduct.

This overlap may matter more for cells than for some small-molecule drugs because manufacturing is inseparable from treatment. A center could partner with an outside manufacturer, process cells itself, or receive a product made under an investigational program. Each configuration can create different federal questions.

The most scientifically ambitious centers are likely to discover that regulatory flexibility is only useful if manufacturing is credible.

Cell products are unforgiving.

A vial of a chemically synthesized small molecule can often be characterized with extraordinary precision. A cellular product is a population. Cells can differ in phenotype, viability, activation state, differentiation, genetic characteristics, potency, and contamination risk. The process that creates the population matters enormously.

This makes potency testing especially difficult.

What measurement predicts that the cells will perform the intended function in a patient? Cell count alone may be inadequate. Surface markers can be informative but incomplete. Functional assays can take time. A release specification has to be fast enough that a living product remains usable.

The factory becomes part of the clinical hypothesis.

That fact gives cellular therapy a natural stopping point against pure deregulation.

A patient can consent to uncertainty about benefit. It is harder to argue that consent should substitute for basic certainty about identity, sterility, viability, or dose.

Those are not paternalistic judgments about how much risk a person should tolerate.

They are properties the provider is responsible for knowing.

This distinction can clarify a lot of right-to-try debate.

There are risks that belong to the patient's preference and risks that belong to professional competence.

Whether a ten percent chance of severe immune toxicity is worth a five percent chance of remission can be a patient-centered judgment when the estimates are credible enough to discuss.

Whether the infusion bag contains the intended patient's cells is not a preference.

Whether a bacterial contaminant should be accepted because the patient is desperate is not autonomy.

Manufacturing quality creates a floor beneath choice.

The same floor should exist internationally.

Mexico's health-establishment rules include categories relevant to tissue, cell, transplant, stem-cell, and regenerative-medicine activity. COFEPRIS has taken enforcement action against clinics that lacked authorized research support for cell treatments. That does not guarantee consistent enforcement or resolve every category. It does show that the choice is not between American regulation and Mexican freedom.

The real comparison concerns the quality and predictability of oversight.

A patient traveling abroad for a cellular intervention faces the same basic questions anywhere.

What cells are these?

Where were they collected?

How were they processed?

Were they expanded?

Were they genetically modified?

How was identity confirmed?

How was sterility tested?

What potency assay was used?

What disease is being treated?

What clinical evidence exists for this product and route?

What follow-up is planned?

The answers may reveal a legitimate research program, a carefully governed medical innovation, an approved therapy in the destination country, or a commercial intervention whose scientific description is mostly decorative.

The border cannot answer the questions for the patient.

A registry can help.

One of the strongest tools against category confusion is public verifiability. If a clinic says the treatment is part of a clinical trial, the trial should be findable. If it says regulators authorized the protocol, a corresponding record should exist where the jurisdiction provides one. If it cites a published study, the study should concern the same intervention rather than a broad field.

This is not perfect protection. A registered trial can be poorly designed. A publication can be weak. A legitimate product can fail.

But verifiability changes the transaction.

It makes claims falsifiable.

The commercial unproven-intervention market thrives on unfalsifiable proximity. The treatment sits near real science but not inside its accountability structures. It borrows the vocabulary of approved cell therapies, legitimate trials, academic stem-cell research, and personalized medicine while avoiding the requirement to show where the specific offering fits.

The way out is not to ban every unconventional cell treatment.

The field itself is too important and too dynamic for that. Early access can be appropriate. Medical innovation can occur before definitive trials. Some diseases are too rare for conventional evidence. Autologous processes create forms of variability that mass-market drug rules do not handle elegantly.

The way out is to require the provider to make the category explicit.

Approved product.

Authorized clinical trial.

Expanded access.

Right to Try.

State experimental treatment.

Enforcement-discretion category.

Unapproved commercial intervention.

Each description should carry known obligations and evidence expectations.

Patients should not have to infer regulatory status from the clinic's tone.

Cellular medicine is a useful stress test because it demonstrates both sides of personalized regulation at once.

On one side are therapies such as CAR-T and TIL that prove unique patient lots can live inside rigorous approval systems.

On the other are commercial stem-cell markets that prove the word personalized can be used to make weak evidence harder to see.

The dividing line is not whether the treatment comes from the patient.

It is whether a system can tell us what happened between removal and return.

That system includes manufacturing records, specifications, clinical evidence, adverse-event reporting, and long-term follow-up. For genetically modified cells it can include extended monitoring for delayed effects. For tumor-derived cells it includes the logistics of tissue collection and expansion. For any autologous product, it includes chain of identity from patient to factory and back again.

The operational difficulty of chain of identity is easy to underestimate because the phrase sounds administrative.

In a conventional pharmacy, a drug can often be replaced from inventory if a package is damaged. In an autologous therapy, the material may represent weeks of work beginning with a collection procedure that cannot simply be repeated on demand. The patient may have received lymphodepleting chemotherapy in preparation for infusion. Disease may have progressed while the cells were being manufactured. A labeling deviation can therefore be both a quality event and a clinical emergency.

This changes what competence means for the treatment center.

A hospital cannot treat the product as an unusually expensive bag delivered by courier. It needs systems for scheduling collection, verifying identity, receiving cryopreserved material, maintaining custody, coordinating preparative treatment, thawing or handling the product correctly, administering it within specification, and responding to modality-specific toxicities.

The site itself becomes part of the product's effective performance.

Two hospitals can receive material manufactured to the same specification and still produce different outcomes if supportive care, recognition of toxicity, timing, staffing or coordination differs.

This is another reason simple arguments about access can mislead.

Permission to use a cell product is not equivalent to capacity to use it well.

A state can authorize an experimental treatment center. It cannot legislate an experienced cellular-therapy team into existence overnight.

The same applies to commercial clinics.

A facility may have elegant rooms, physicians with legitimate licenses and laboratory partners that use scientific terminology. The harder questions concern systems that patients rarely see. Does the center investigate deviations? Does it track failed manufacturing attempts? Does it know whether potency measurements correlate with outcome? Does it have a process for product complaints? Can it recall or quarantine material? Does it participate in long-term surveillance? Can it trace every manipulated cell lot back through collection and processing?

These are mundane questions compared with whether stem cells can regenerate tissue or engineered T cells can eliminate a tumor.

Mundane questions are where advanced medicine often becomes real.

Comparability creates another problem as platforms evolve.

A cell-therapy manufacturer may change a reagent, a culture medium, a piece of equipment, a freezing process, an assay or a manufacturing site. In ordinary industrial language, these can sound like improvements around the edges. In a living product, a process change can alter the composition of the final cell population.

The manufacturer therefore has to ask whether the product after the change is meaningfully comparable with the product that generated the existing clinical evidence.

Personalized medicine does not eliminate this question.

It intensifies it because natural patient-to-patient variability already creates noise. If both the starting material and the manufacturing process change, it becomes difficult to know which difference matters.

A mature platform controls variation intentionally.

An immature commercial program can hide variation inside the claim that every treatment is individualized.

That is a dangerous use of personalization.

If every lot is expected to be different, quality failure can masquerade as customization.

The answer is not to demand identical cells. That is biologically unrealistic.

The answer is to define the dimensions along which variation is expected and the dimensions along which it is unacceptable.

Cell number may vary within a justified range.

Phenotype may have a defined distribution.

Viability may have a release threshold.

Sterility should not be optional.

Identity should not be approximate.

A critical genetic modification should be measured rather than assumed.

A platform becomes credible when it can describe variability instead of merely invoking it.

This distinction should shape experimental-treatment policy.

A center offering an early cellular product should be able to tell the patient not only that the treatment is unapproved, but which parts of the manufacturing process are established, which release criteria are being used, how often manufacturing has failed, and whether the process used today is materially the same as the process behind the human evidence being cited.

Those facts are consent facts.

They are also regulatory facts.

They expose a gap in the usual right-to-try frame, which focuses on whether the patient may receive the product while paying less attention to whether the product remains stable as an object of knowledge.

This matters when a provider treats many patients over time.

If the clinic quietly changes preparation methods while continuing to market one therapy name, the apparent outcome history may combine materially different products. Successes and failures become scientifically difficult to interpret. The business retains continuity while the evidence does not.

A learning system should version the treatment.

The patient does not need a software-style release number on the infusion bag. The record does need enough process information to distinguish meaningful changes.

That is how experience accumulates instead of dissolving into a brand.

These are forms of regulation that personalized medicine cannot simply outgrow.

It needs them more.

As therapies become harder for the patient to inspect, institutions must become better at making invisible quality visible.

A patient can look at a pill and learn almost nothing about how it was made.

A bag of living cells reveals even less.

The future of experimental access will therefore depend on a distinction that politics usually avoids.

Freedom to choose a treatment is one question.

The right to call something a treatment worth choosing is another.
