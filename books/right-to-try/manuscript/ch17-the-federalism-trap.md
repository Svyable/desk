# The Federalism Trap

A physician can be licensed by a state and still be unable to sell an unapproved drug.

That sentence contains most of the American argument about experimental treatment.

States govern the practice of medicine in ways that shape nearly every clinical encounter. They license physicians, nurses, hospitals, pharmacies, and many other health facilities. They define scopes of practice, professional discipline, consent rules, malpractice standards, and public-health duties. A patient experiences medicine mostly through institutions whose authority is rooted in state law.

The federal government governs another layer. Congress has given the Food and Drug Administration authority over drugs, biological products, medical devices, clinical investigations, manufacturing, labeling, and distribution. Federal statutes and regulations follow products through development and commerce in ways state medical licenses do not.

Usually the two layers cooperate so quietly that nobody notices the seam.

A state says the physician may practice.

The FDA says the drug may be marketed.

The hospital says the physician may administer it there.

The insurer says whether it will pay.

Professional guidelines say what peers consider reasonable care.

Liability law waits in the background if something goes wrong.

Experimental medicine makes the seam visible because the layers can point in different directions.

Montana has now done that deliberately.

The state has created licensed experimental treatment centers and expanded a state-law framework under which qualifying experimental treatments can be offered. The statute reflects a clear policy judgment: after a defined early human-evidence threshold and review, certain patients should have broader access to unapproved treatment than conventional federal development might otherwise provide.

The state can make that conduct lawful under state health law.

What it cannot do by declaration is erase federal statutes.

This is not a technicality invented by lawyers to frustrate dying patients. It is the structure of American government.

A state can legalize conduct that federal law still regulates or prohibits. The resulting conflict can persist for years. Federal agencies can choose enforcement priorities. Congress can create exemptions. Courts can decide whether federal law preempts state law. States can change their rules in response. Industries can form before the legal boundary settles.

The public often experiences these periods as hypocrisy.

How can something be legal and illegal at the same time?

The answer is that different sovereigns are asking different legal questions.

Montana can say a physician does not violate Montana law merely by providing a treatment in a state-licensed experimental treatment center under the state framework.

Federal law can still ask whether the product is an unapproved drug or biologic, whether an investigational new drug application is required, whether manufacturing complies with federal requirements, whether the product moved through interstate commerce, or whether promotion violates federal restrictions.

A clinic can satisfy one system and remain exposed to another.

This creates the federalism trap.

The more a state tries to make its experimental pathway practically useful, the more the pathway may depend on conduct that enters federal jurisdiction.

Modern advanced therapy is difficult to keep inside a state.

A personalized antisense drug may be designed in Boston, synthesized by a contract manufacturer in another state, tested by a specialty laboratory elsewhere, and shipped to Montana for administration. A gene-editing therapy may rely on reagents sourced internationally, cloud computation, licensed intellectual property, and a delivery component made by a different company. A cellular product may require leukapheresis in Montana and manufacturing in California before returning to the patient.

The treatment is interstate by construction.

Even if final manufacturing moved to Montana, the materials and equipment likely did not originate there. Federal authority over drugs and biologics does not depend only on where the physician stands at the moment of administration.

This is why analogies to state medical-marijuana policy are useful only up to a point.

Cannabis showed that states can create large regulated markets despite conflicting federal law and that federal enforcement discretion can allow those markets to persist. But advanced therapies are not agricultural products sold mostly within retail supply chains. They rely on nationally integrated biotechnology infrastructure, federally regulated manufacturers, institutional investors, patents, hospitals, research universities, and companies whose other products depend on good standing with federal agencies.

A biotechnology company with an active FDA program may be far less willing than a local dispensary to test the limits of federal enforcement.

Reputation becomes regulation.

The FDA may never need to bring an enforcement action if reputable sponsors refuse to participate in a pathway they believe could endanger their federal development programs.

This is one way legal uncertainty can defeat a permissive state law without a court case.

The state opens the door.

The supply chain declines to walk through.

That possibility should temper predictions that Montana will immediately become an American capital of cutting-edge experimental biotechnology.

The first entrants may be therapies whose federal status is already ambiguous, products from sponsors no longer pursuing approval, interventions manufactured entirely within a provider network willing to accept legal risk, or services framed as medical procedures rather than conventional distributed products.

Those are not necessarily the therapies with the strongest evidence.

Regulatory arbitrage selects for legal adaptability, not scientific quality.

That is a dangerous selection effect.

A serious state pathway should want the opposite. It should make participation easiest for the programs with the best manufacturing, strongest evidence, clearest monitoring, and most credible sponsors.

To do that, the federal seam needs to become more predictable.

One route is congressional action.

Congress could create a specific federal safe harbor for state-licensed experimental treatment programs meeting defined conditions. The conditions might include a minimum human-evidence threshold, independent review, manufacturing standards, registration, adverse-event reporting, and limits on promotion. States could innovate inside the safe harbor while federal law retained a floor.

That would turn conflict into cooperative federalism.

The federal government already uses similar structures elsewhere. Environmental law often sets national minimums while states implement programs. Insurance regulation combines state authority with federal requirements. Public-health systems routinely divide responsibilities among levels of government.

Experimental medicine could do the same.

The challenge is that Congress would have to agree on what the federal floor should be.

Right-to-try politics made agreement possible around a relatively narrow principle: some patients with life-threatening disease should be able to access eligible investigational drugs without the ordinary FDA expanded-access route.

Montana's model is broader and more institutional. A federal safe harbor for commercial experimental-treatment centers would raise harder questions about profit, facility standards, abandoned products, individualized platform variants, and interstate manufacturing.

Another route is agency policy.

The FDA could issue guidance describing how it will exercise enforcement discretion around state experimental-treatment programs or clarifying which activities remain squarely within federal requirements. That would not eliminate statutory limits, but it could reduce uncertainty enough for institutions to participate.

Agencies are often criticized for “regulation by guidance.”

In fast-moving technology, guidance can be the only tool fast enough to define an interim boundary before Congress acts.

The best guidance would not pretend the state laws do not exist.

It would identify the collision points.

Manufacturing.

Interstate shipment.

Products under active INDs.

Biologics requiring federal licensure.

Advertising and promotion.

Safety reporting.

Importation.

Personalized variants derived from platform products with prior human data.

The state and federal systems could then discover where their objectives overlap.

Both want patients to avoid contaminated products.

Both should want serious adverse events reported.

Both should want informed consent to be meaningful.

Both should want fraudulent efficacy claims controlled.

The real disagreement concerns how much evidence is needed before administration and who gets final authority over that threshold.

That is a narrower conflict than the rhetoric suggests.

States have a legitimate argument for experimentation because they are closer to the practice of medicine and can respond to local preferences. Federal regulation has a legitimate argument for uniformity because drugs and biologics do not respect state borders and because fragmented safety standards can create a race to the bottom.

Both arguments become stronger in personalized medicine.

The state argument strengthens because one-person cases do not fit uniform mass-market development well.

The federal argument strengthens because the manufacturing platforms are national and the evidence from rare cases is too scarce to fragment.

This is the trap in its purest form.

Personalization makes local flexibility more valuable and centralized learning more valuable at the same time.

A good federalism design would therefore decentralize treatment decisions while centralizing enough data and manufacturing oversight to learn across them.

The state could license centers and clinicians, define eligibility more broadly, and review individual cases.

The federal system could maintain platform-level manufacturing files, adverse-event aggregation, national registries, and rules for interstate movement.

Neither side would need to own the entire process.

This is not how current law is neatly arranged.

The distinction is a policy direction, not a description of a settled legal regime.

That caveat is important because experimental-medicine advocacy often outruns legal reality. A clinic can cite a state statute and imply that treatment is lawful full stop. A critic can cite federal product law and imply the state statute is meaningless. Both can be wrong because legality can depend on the exact conduct.

A patient should not be made the test case for that ambiguity without knowing it.

Legal risk belongs in informed consent when it can affect continuity of treatment.

If federal action could interrupt access after one dose of a multidose regimen, the patient should know. If a product might be seized or a manufacturer ordered to stop shipping, that is not merely the clinic's business risk. If the treatment depends on a state interpretation that has not been tested, the patient should not be told simply that the therapy is “legal in Montana.”

The more accurate statement may be that Montana law authorizes the center to provide qualifying experimental treatment under state law while federal requirements may still apply to the product and supply chain.

That sentence is less marketable.

It is more honest.

The legal uncertainty also changes which therapies a center can realistically build around.

Consider three programs that all appear, from the patient's perspective, to offer experimental treatment in Montana.

One center works with a sponsor whose investigational product is already moving through a conventional federal program. The sponsor has batch records, safety reporting, manufacturing contracts, and a development plan it expects the FDA eventually to judge. The state pathway may offer another route to a patient, but every decision is made in the shadow of the sponsor's national program. The sponsor has something valuable to lose if the state experiment creates a conflict.

A second center works with a product whose developer has largely abandoned conventional approval. Perhaps the commercial market was too small, financing disappeared, or later trials never materialized. That developer may be more willing to supply a state pathway because there is less federal development value at risk. But the reason access is easier may have nothing to do with the quality of the therapy.

A third center tries to internalize as much as possible. It diagnoses the patient, designs the intervention, contracts or performs manufacturing, treats the patient, and follows the outcome inside one network. That can reduce handoffs and make speed possible. It can also concentrate legal interpretation, scientific judgment, commercial incentive, and clinical care inside the same institution.

The state license is identical in none of these situations even if the plaque on the wall is.

The surrounding dependency graph determines the real freedom of action.

This is why legal analysis of experimental access has to follow transactions rather than labels.

Where did the active material come from?

Who owns it?

Who shipped it?

Who made the treatment decision?

Who made the manufacturing release decision?

Which entity advertised to the patient?

Which entity accepted payment?

Who is responsible if a second dose becomes unavailable?

A statute can create a category called an experimental treatment center. The conduct inside the category can still implicate several different legal regimes.

For patients, this complexity appears first as continuity risk.

A person may not care whether a dispute is formally about preemption, shipment, labeling, manufacturing, or promotion. The person cares whether the next infusion arrives on Thursday.

That practical translation matters.

Experimental centers should therefore distinguish between clinical uncertainty and pathway uncertainty. Clinical uncertainty concerns what the treatment will do. Pathway uncertainty concerns whether the institution will remain able to provide it.

The two can interact. A one-time intervention such as an irreversible gene edit creates a different continuity problem from a therapy requiring monthly dosing. A treatment made from the patient's collected cells creates different risks from a stocked small molecule. A customized product whose manufacturing begins only after payment can leave a patient exposed if a supplier withdraws halfway through the process.

If a center is operating near an unsettled jurisdictional line, the consent discussion should reach the practical consequences rather than merely adding a sentence that federal law may apply.

Can the regimen be completed if a supplier stops participating?

Is there another lawful source?

Will deposits be refunded if the pathway closes before treatment?

Who stores the records and samples if the center shuts down?

Can the home physician obtain the information needed for follow-up?

The patient does not need a constitutional-law seminar.

The patient needs to know which parts of the treatment can disappear.

Institutions need the legal analysis precisely so the patient does not have to perform it.

This allocation of expertise is another reason competent regulation can support rather than diminish autonomy. The patient decides whether a chance is worth taking. Lawyers, regulators, manufacturers, pharmacists, and clinicians are responsible for making the boundaries of that chance visible.

Mexico presents a different federalism lesson because it is a sovereign country rather than a subnational jurisdiction.

A Mexican authorization does not have to coexist with FDA authority over clinical practice in Mexico. COFEPRIS governs the relevant national layer. But the FDA can still affect what happens when a product is imported into the United States, when an American sponsor conducts development, when data is submitted for U.S. approval, or when patients return with complications.

International borders replace constitutional federalism with regulatory pluralism.

The core problem remains: one treatment can move through multiple legal systems.

This is increasingly normal in advanced therapy.

Clinical trials are multinational. Manufacturing is global. Cells can be collected in one country and processed in another. Sequencing data can cross borders instantly. Components can be sourced from multiple jurisdictions. A patient's medical record can be stored on a cloud service whose servers are elsewhere.

The old idea that a country can regulate “the treatment” entirely inside its territory is becoming less accurate.

Regulation has to follow functions.

Who controls manufacturing quality?

Who controls clinical practice?

Who controls data privacy?

Who approves the research protocol?

Who monitors advertising?

Who receives adverse-event reports?

Who can recall a product?

Which authority can inspect the facility?

The treatment is a bundle of regulated functions distributed across space.

This is why flexible jurisdictions can attract experimentation without necessarily becoming lawless. They can choose to regulate some functions differently while still enforcing others strictly.

The best version of Montana would do exactly that.

It would be permissive about patient eligibility and the evidentiary threshold for exceptional access while being strict about product identity, manufacturing provenance, review independence, claims, reporting, and follow-up.

That combination could make the state a valuable test of whether the federal balance is set too conservatively.

The worst version would use state medical authority as a shield for providers whose products would not survive serious federal scrutiny.

The difference will emerge from enforcement, not statutory language alone.

States have another source of power that receives less attention than direct legal conflict.

They can collect data.

A state experimental-treatment registry with high-quality public reporting could influence national policy even if federal law ultimately limits the treatments themselves. It could show which therapies patients seek, what they pay, what outcomes occur, which manufacturing models work, and where existing expanded-access pathways fail.

Evidence can change federal law more durably than defiance.

If Montana demonstrates that a category of individualized treatment can be administered safely under faster review and produces usable outcome data, federal regulators will face pressure to adopt the model. If the state produces preventable harms, opaque pricing, and weak evidence, federal authority will look more necessary.

The state is therefore conducting an institutional trial whether it calls it one or not.

Its control group is the existing system.

That comparison should be explicit.

How long does it take a patient to receive treatment under the state pathway versus expanded access?

How many requests fail because a manufacturer refuses?

What is the administrative burden on physicians?

What proportion of treatments come from active federal development programs?

How often do serious adverse events occur?

How complete is follow-up?

What do patients pay?

How many Montana residents receive subsidized access?

Do centers publish results?

Does federal enforcement occur?

These are the outcomes of federalism.

The state should want them measured.

There is a temptation in American politics to treat federalism as an intrinsic virtue. Fifty laboratories of democracy. Let states experiment.

Experiments can fail.

That is what makes them experiments.

The moral justification for state variation is not that every state choice deserves respect. It is that controlled variation can reveal better policy and allow local preferences to matter without forcing the entire country to move at once.

For that logic to work, results have to be visible.

A state that experiments without measurement is not a laboratory.

It is merely different.

Personalized medicine may be one of the fields most capable of benefiting from genuine policy variation because the existing federal system is under real strain. One-person drugs, platform variants, adaptive phage cocktails, and living cellular products do not fit neatly into rules built around stable mass-manufactured products and large trials.

The response should not be to abandon national regulation.

The national layer contains exactly the shared memory these therapies need.

The response should be to decide which decisions need national consistency and which can safely move closer to the patient.

That allocation of authority is the real federalism experiment.

Montana has forced the question sooner than most states.

The answer will not be found in the slogan “states' rights” or “FDA authority.”

It will be found in the treatment's path from design to manufacturing to shipment to bedside to follow-up.

Follow the product and the jurisdiction keeps changing.

Follow the patient and the need for continuity remains.

The law has to learn to do both.
