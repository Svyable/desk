# 3I/ATLAS natural-baseline audit

Bounded research pass for **THE ARTIFACT TEST**, focused on Chapter 1's claim that natural interstellar visitors build the control group. This note does not edit the active manuscript while #2282 is open.

## Primary evidence added

### JWST: the interesting chemistry is still cometary evidence

Cordiner et al. used JWST/NIRSpec at 3.32 au inbound and measured a CO2-dominated coma containing H2O, CO, OCS, water ice and dust. The reported CO2/H2O mixing ratio was **8.0 ± 1.0**, described as among the highest measured in a comet and **6.1 sigma above** the long-period/Jupiter-family comet trend used by the authors when C/2016 R2 is excluded.

Primary paper: Martin A. Cordiner et al., *JWST detection of a carbon dioxide dominated gas coma surrounding interstellar object 3I/ATLAS*, arXiv:2508.18209, https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18209

Editorial consequence: this is exactly the kind of result the book needs. `Unusual relative to a Solar-System comparison set` and `difficult for nature` are not synonyms. The authors give several natural interpretations, including an intrinsically CO2-rich nucleus, formation near a CO2 ice line, irradiation history, and inhibited heat penetration affecting H2O sublimation. Chapter 1 should eventually let the reader feel the temptation of the 6.1-sigma number and then show why a tail relative to one reference distribution is not an artifact test.

Publication hold: do not call the ratio a `6.1-sigma anomaly` without naming the fitted comparison trend and the exclusion of C/2016 R2. Sigma here does not mean a 6.1-sigma probability that the object is artificial.

### Swift: an unfamiliar visitor can be unusually active without ceasing to be a comet

Xing et al. detected OH emission with Swift/UVOT on 31 July–1 August 2025, inferring a water production rate of **(1.35 ± 0.27) × 10^27 molecules/s (~40 kg/s)** at 3.51 au under their stated reddening assumption. They estimated an active area of at least 19 km² and noted that the implied activity fraction was high relative to most Solar-System comets under then-current nucleus-size limits. The paper also offers a natural complication: large icy grains in the coma may provide an extended water-vapor source.

Primary paper: Zexi Xing et al., *Water Detection in the Interstellar Object 3I/ATLAS*, arXiv:2508.04675, https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04675

Editorial consequence: activity fractions are model-dependent inferences, not direct images of a nucleus surface. Preserve the reddening, nucleus-size and extended-source dependencies if this number enters prose.

### Independent near-IR spectroscopy: the coma itself contaminates simple nucleus stories

Yang et al. reported water ice in the coma and modeled the continuum with an approximate mixture including 10-micron water-ice grains. They explicitly describe the inferred ice fraction as an order-of-magnitude estimate. That is useful counterpressure against converting unresolved brightness or spectral behavior into a precise solid-body description.

Primary paper: Bin Yang et al., *Spectroscopic Characterization of Interstellar Object 3I/ATLAS: Water Ice in the Coma*, arXiv:2507.14916, https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14916

## Argument revision this evidence should force

The manuscript currently says that a hundred characterized interstellar comets would improve the null distribution. Keep that direction, but make the statistical claim narrower when prose is revised. Solar-System comets are a useful comparison population, not a completed null distribution for planetesimals assembled under other stellar metallicities, radiation histories and disk conditions. With only three recognized interstellar objects, a property can be extreme against a Solar-System sample and still be ordinary in the unobserved interstellar population.

A better future chapter move is to use 3I/ATLAS itself as the demonstration. Its chemistry can be both genuinely unusual against familiar comet samples and supported by direct evidence of ordinary cometary activity. That is more instructive than a generic warning that `nature is weird`.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The book's proposed `artifact protocol` may be solving a problem standard astronomy already handles. Astronomers routinely compare new objects against physical models, propagate uncertainties, seek independent modalities, and revise classifications as spectroscopy and dynamics accumulate. 3I/ATLAS is a case in point: unusual abundance ratios did not require a special artifact ladder to remain bounded by cometary evidence.

This objection should be allowed to win unless later chapters identify a decision that ordinary small-body practice leaves genuinely underspecified: for example, a precommitted threshold for spending scarce technosignature/intercept resources, an artificial signature with a prospectively stated natural false-positive model, or a provenance test that standard characterization would not otherwise perform.

## Quantitative publication burden

Before calling a future feature `anomalous`, record:

- comparison population and its selection function;
- whether the feature was specified before or after target selection;
- measurement and model uncertainty;
- number of features searched before highlighting the outlier;
- known natural outliers and whether exclusions were made;
- whether the statistic is a tail probability under a model or merely distance from a fitted trend;
- which observation would discriminate the artificial model from the strongest natural model.

This is the denominator the artifact ladder needs if it is to be more than careful vocabulary.
