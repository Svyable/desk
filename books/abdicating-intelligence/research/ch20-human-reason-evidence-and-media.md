# Chapter 20 — Human reason evidence and media

## Editorial purpose

Ground the chapter's distinction between a fluent explanation and an accountable reason. Use a narrow, real legal precedent to show that in at least one consequential domain, an institution cannot treat model complexity as an excuse for failing to identify the specific reasons for an adverse outcome. Keep the legal claim sector-specific: ECOA and Regulation B govern credit decisions, not automated decision-making generally.

## Source 1 — CFPB Circular 2022-03 on complex algorithms and adverse-action reasons

**Source:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, *Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-03: Adverse action notification requirements in connection with credit decisions based on complex algorithms*, May 26, 2022.

**Official HTML:** https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/circulars/circular-2022-03-adverse-action-notification-requirements-in-connection-with-credit-decisions-based-on-complex-algorithms/

**Official PDF:** https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_2022-03_circular_2022-05.pdf

**Authority:** U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau guidance interpreting obligations under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B.

**What it establishes:** For adverse credit actions covered by ECOA and Regulation B, creditors must provide specific reasons even when using complex or opaque algorithms. The CFPB says the reasons must be accurate and cannot be replaced by a claim that the underlying technology is too complicated to understand. The Circular also notes that post-hoc explanation methods are approximations and that creditors still have to validate their accuracy.

**What it does not establish:** It does not create a universal legal right to explanation for every automated decision. It does not require every model to be intrinsically interpretable, nor does it prove that explanations alone make decisions fair, lawful, or contestable. The chapter should use it as a bounded precedent: complexity does not automatically erase a reason-giving obligation where law already imposes one.

**Rights:** The CFPB is a U.S. federal agency. Original U.S.-government-authored text is generally not subject to U.S. copyright under 17 U.S.C. § 105. Agency seals, logos, trademarks, photographs, embedded third-party material, or separately credited material can have additional restrictions. This project should cite and paraphrase the official text rather than reproduce CFPB page design or graphics.

**Publication suitability:** High for factual paraphrase, citation, and short attributed quotation. No CFPB graphic is needed.

**Credit if quoted:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, *Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-03*, May 26, 2022.

## Source 2 — Regulation B notification rule and official interpretation

**Source:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, Regulation B, 12 CFR § 1002.9, *Notifications*, plus the official interpretation of paragraph 9(b)(2).

**Rule:** https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/9/

**Official interpretation:** https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/interp-9/

**Authority:** Federal regulation and official interpretation administered by the CFPB.

**What it establishes:** The statement of reasons for an adverse credit action must be specific and identify the principal reason or reasons. The official interpretation further says disclosed reasons must relate to and accurately describe the factors actually considered or scored. Generic statements about internal standards or merely failing to achieve a qualifying score are insufficient.

**Why this matters for the chapter:** This is unusually close to the chapter's distinction between a reason and a plausible story. In this regulated setting, an explanation has to connect back to factors that actually participated in the decision. A polished rationale that merely sounds compatible with the result is not the same thing.

**What it does not establish:** Regulation B is not a general-purpose AI accountability statute. Do not generalize its specific notice obligations to employment, criminal justice, health care, education, platform moderation, or public benefits without separate legal authority.

**Rights:** Federal regulatory text and official U.S.-government-authored interpretation are generally not subject to U.S. copyright under 17 U.S.C. § 105. Use clean text citation/paraphrase rather than reproducing agency branding.

**Publication suitability:** High.

## Source 3 — CFPB Circular 2023-03 on actual reasons and sample forms

**Source:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, *Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2023-03: Adverse action notification requirements and the proper use of the CFPB's sample forms provided in Regulation B*, September 19, 2023.

**Official release:** https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-issues-guidance-on-credit-denials-by-lenders-using-artificial-intelligence/

**Official PDF:** https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_adverse_action_notice_circular_2023-09.pdf

**Authority:** CFPB guidance on Regulation B adverse-action notices.

**What it establishes:** Creditors cannot satisfy the notice requirement merely by choosing the closest generic item on a sample checklist when it does not accurately reflect the actual reason. The guidance reinforces that reasons should specifically and accurately describe the factors actually relied upon, including when advanced algorithms use forms of data that do not fit older canned categories.

**What it does not establish:** It does not say that a complete causal explanation of a model is always required. It concerns the principal reasons for covered adverse credit actions and the accuracy of those stated reasons.

**Rights:** Same federal-source posture as above. Cite/paraphrase; do not reproduce page graphics, seals, or logos.

**Publication suitability:** High.

## Source 4 — NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0

**Source:** National Institute of Standards and Technology, Elham Tabassi, *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)*, NIST AI 100-1, January 26, 2023.

**Official landing page:** https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-ai-rmf-10

**Framework hub:** https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework

**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1

**Authority:** NIST voluntary framework for AI risk management, designed for organizations developing, deploying, or using AI systems.

**What it establishes:** NIST treats AI risk management as an organizational and lifecycle activity rather than a final approval event. The framework is explicitly voluntary, non-sector-specific, and use-case agnostic. Its Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage structure is useful background for the chapter's claim that meaningful authority has to reach upstream into objectives, risk tolerances, deployment choices, and monitoring.

**What it does not establish:** NIST does not create a legal right to a “human reason,” and the AI RMF is not binding law. Do not present it as a statutory explanation requirement.

**Rights:** NIST is a U.S. federal agency. Original NIST government-authored material is generally not subject to U.S. copyright under 17 U.S.C. § 105, but embedded third-party material and marks may have separate restrictions. Citation and paraphrase are sufficient here; no NIST artwork is needed.

**Publication suitability:** High for background framing and citation.

## Figure decision

Do **not** reproduce CFPB sample adverse-action forms, agency page screenshots, seals, logos, NIST diagrams, or screenshots. The documentary value is in the rule and the reason-giving obligation, not in the visual appearance of federal forms. Reproducing official forms would also risk making the book's conceptual framework look like legal-compliance advice.

### Original figure

**File:** `../media/ch20-reason-chain.svg`

**Title:** A reason has to lead somewhere

**Concept:** The upper path shows a consequential decision connected to a specific reason, the actual factors/evidence/rule, an accountable authority, and a challenge path that can reach both the outcome and the governing rule. The lower path shows “synthetic legitimacy”: an opaque output followed by a fluent explanation that dead-ends because it is not tied to what actually drove the decision, no accountable owner is visible, and correction cannot reach the system.

**Rights:** Original project artwork created for *Abdicating Intelligence*. It reproduces no CFPB, NIST, lender, vendor, product, form, interface, logo, seal, or third-party artwork.

**Credit line:** Original diagram by Sven Hardy Benson / *Abdicating Intelligence* project.

**Alt text:** A conceptual diagram contrasts an accountable reason chain with a dead-end explanation. In the accountable path, a consequential decision leads to a specific reason, the actual factors and evidence used, an accountable authority, a challenge or review path, and correction or rule change. In the dead-end path, an opaque output leads only to a plausible explanation with no traceable factors, accountable owner, or effective appeal.

**Placement:** Immediately after the chapter's line, “A reason is not merely text that makes an outcome sound sensible. It is a claim that can be tested against the process and the world.” This is where the prose has already distinguished causal provenance from persuasive explanation, so the figure clarifies rather than interrupts the argument.

**Formatting:** Full-width figure. Keep the caption attached to the image and avoid a page break between them. Preserve grayscale legibility, generous whitespace, and readable labels at print size. Do not add numerical scores or imply a maturity scale; the diagram is conceptual.

**Caption:** *A reason has to lead somewhere.* An accountable explanation connects an outcome to the factors that actually affected it, to an authority that owns the rule, and to a path for challenge or correction. Fluent prose without that chain can manufacture the appearance of accountability. Conceptual diagram; not a universal legal standard or compliance flowchart.

## Manuscript integration boundary

A defensible evidence paragraph is:

> Credit law offers a narrow but useful precedent. Under Regulation B, an adverse-action notice must give specific principal reasons, and the official interpretation says those reasons must relate to and accurately describe the factors actually considered or scored. In 2022 the CFPB made the implication for complex algorithms explicit: opacity does not excuse a creditor from providing accurate reasons. In 2023 it added that a lender cannot simply choose the nearest generic reason from a sample checklist if that is not what actually drove the outcome. This is not a universal right to explanation for automated systems. It establishes something smaller and more concrete: in at least one consequential domain, a plausible story after the fact is not enough.

Do not write that ECOA/Regulation B governs non-credit automated systems. Do not imply that the notices reveal a model's full causal structure. Do not conflate accurate adverse-action reasons with fairness, due process, or a meaningful appeal system; those are separate institutional properties.

## Reader-facing formatting notes

- Put the credit-law example after the “causal provenance” section, not in the opening. The chapter should establish the conceptual distinction before introducing legal precedent.
- Keep the legal example to one compact paragraph so the chapter does not become a consumer-credit chapter.
- Place the original figure directly after the line about a reason being testable against the process and the world.
- Keep the figure caption to four short sentences at most and retain the “not a universal legal standard” qualification.
- Link the first manuscript mention of Regulation B/CFPB guidance to the official CFPB pages; avoid raw URL dumps in reader-facing prose.
- Preserve the chapter's explicit sentence that “a right to a human reason” is a design principle used by the book, not a claim about a universally recognized current legal right.

## Rejected or deferred media

- **CFPB sample forms / screenshots:** Rejected. They are visually bureaucratic rather than explanatory and would blur the line between conceptual argument and legal-compliance guidance.
- **Agency seals and logos:** Rejected. No explanatory value; avoid endorsement/trademark confusion.
- **NIST AI RMF graphics:** Deferred. The chapter already contains a governance claim, but reproducing the framework's visual architecture would pull attention toward generic AI governance rather than the sharper question of whether a reason can be traced and challenged.
- **Stock imagery of rejected borrowers or applicants:** Rejected. Decorative, potentially stereotyped, and weaker than the documentary/legal source itself.

## Highest-value next research opportunity

Find direct empirical work comparing **faithful explanations tied to the actual decision process** with persuasive post-hoc rationales in consequential human-AI decisions. The most useful study would measure whether users can detect errors, challenge outcomes, correct source data, or change institutional decisions—not merely whether they report greater trust or satisfaction after receiving an explanation. A second useful line would examine appeal systems where the reviewer has genuine authority to overturn both an individual outcome and a flawed rule, rather than routing the appeal back through the same model.
