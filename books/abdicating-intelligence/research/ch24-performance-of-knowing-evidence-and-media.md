# Chapter 24 — Performance of Knowing evidence and media

## Editorial purpose

Support the chapter's central distinction between a polished artifact and the capability behind it without turning the chapter into a detection manual. The strongest evidence should show that authorship/source cues are becoming unreliable and that explanation quality does not automatically produce calibrated reliance. Keep the claims narrower than the rhetoric of "AI can fool everyone."

## Source 1 — Chein, Martinez & Barone on human/AI text discrimination

**Source:** J. M. Chein, S. A. Martinez, and A. R. Barone, “Human intelligence can safeguard against artificial intelligence: individual differences in the discernment of human from AI texts,” *Scientific Reports* 14, 25989 (2024), published October 29, 2024.

**DOI / publisher page:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-76218-y

**Authority:** Peer-reviewed empirical study in *Scientific Reports*, with 187 participants evaluating human- and AI-generated texts and social-media-style comments.

**What it establishes:** Participants were only modestly better than chance at identifying the origin of the longer texts: average accuracy was 57%. AI-generated texts were correctly recognized as AI 53% of the time; general-interest material was easier to classify than science-focused material. The study also found substantial individual variation in discrimination performance. This supports the chapter's bounded claim that surface form is an increasingly weak proxy for authorship or underlying human capability.

**What it does not establish:** It does not show that all AI text is indistinguishable from human text, that experts can never detect AI, that polished writing is inherently deceptive, or that authorship detection is the same thing as assessing expertise. The experiment concerns source discrimination, not whether a person can reconstruct or defend the reasoning in an artifact.

**Rights / license:** The article is open access under **CC BY-NC-ND 4.0**. That license is not compatible with commercial republication or adaptation of the article's figures for this book. Do **not** reproduce, crop, redraw, trace, or adapt its figures, tables, screenshots, or layout. Factual findings may be cited and paraphrased with normal scholarly attribution.

**Publication suitability:** High for bounded factual citation and paraphrase. Not suitable as a reusable visual asset in a commercial edition.

**Recommended manuscript use:** One compact paragraph immediately after the chapter establishes that the cues people use to infer competence are becoming cheaper to manufacture. The point is signal decay, not deception.

## Source 2 — Alter & Oppenheimer on processing fluency

**Source:** Adam L. Alter and Daniel M. Oppenheimer, “Uniting the Tribes of Fluency to Form a Metacognitive Nation,” *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 13(3), 219–235 (2009).

**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868309341564

**PubMed record:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19638628/

**Authority:** Peer-reviewed review article synthesizing a broad literature on processing fluency as a metacognitive cue in judgment and decision-making.

**What it establishes:** Ease of processing can influence judgments across multiple domains; the review discusses findings in which more fluent stimuli can be judged as more true or otherwise evaluated more favorably than less fluent counterparts, depending on context.

**What it does not establish:** Fluency is not a universal truth-bias switch and does not imply that every clear explanation is misleading. The review predates modern generative AI and should be used only for the general psychological mechanism that people use ease-of-processing as one cue among many.

**Rights / license:** The publisher page is not an open commercial-reuse license. Treat the article as a citation source only. Do not reproduce or adapt publisher figures, tables, or page design. Keep quotations minimal.

**Publication suitability:** High for scholarly citation and paraphrase; not a media source for direct reuse.

## Source 3 — Chen et al. on explanations and overreliance

**Source:** Valerie Chen, Q. Vera Liao, Jennifer Wortman Vaughan, and Gagan Bansal, “Understanding the Role of Human Intuition on Reliance in Human-AI Decision-Making with Explanations,” *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, Vol. 7, Article 370 (October 2023).

**Microsoft Research page:** https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/understanding-the-role-of-human-intuition-on-reliance-in-human-ai-decision-making-with-explanations/

**Authority:** Peer-reviewed HCI study using think-aloud and mixed methods across two prediction tasks and two explanation types.

**What it establishes:** In the authors' tested tasks, feature-based explanations did not improve decision outcomes and increased overreliance on AI, while example-based explanations improved performance relative to feature-based explanations. The useful point for Chapter 24 is that an explanation is not automatically a calibration device; how it is structured and how it interacts with user knowledge matters.

**What it does not establish:** It does not prove that explanations generally make users worse, that example-based explanations are always superior, or that the tested tasks generalize to medicine, law, hiring, or all professional work.

**Rights / license:** Use the paper as a factual/citation source only unless a specific reusable asset's license is separately verified. Do not reproduce ACM figures, screenshots, tables, or visual layouts in the commercial book.

**Publication suitability:** High for bounded paraphrase in the chapter's explanation section. No visual reuse needed.

## Manuscript integration boundary

A defensible source-discrimination paragraph is:

> The signal problem is already measurable. In a 2024 *Scientific Reports* study, participants were only modestly better than chance at distinguishing human- from AI-generated longer texts: average accuracy was 57 percent, and AI texts themselves were correctly recognized as AI only 53 percent of the time. That is not evidence that authorship is impossible to detect, much less that expertise is. It is evidence that the page is becoming a weaker witness about who—or what—produced it.

Do not generalize from source discrimination to professional competence or claim that readers cannot distinguish AI output in every domain.

A defensible explanation paragraph is:

> Explanation is not automatically calibration. In a 2023 human-AI decision study, the effect depended on the form of explanation: the tested feature-based explanations failed to improve decision outcomes and increased overreliance, while example-based explanations performed better in those tasks. The result is not a universal ranking of explanation methods. It is a warning that fluent rationale can become another interface cue rather than a faithful route to better judgment.

## Highest-value next research opportunity

Find longitudinal or professional evidence that directly separates **artifact quality** from the producer's ability to reconstruct, challenge, transfer, or repair the underlying reasoning. The ideal study would compare matched AI-assisted and unassisted outputs, then test the same participants on altered cases, source challenges, anomalies, and failure recovery. A second high-value line is hiring or credential research showing which assessment signals retain predictive validity once generative assistance is allowed, rather than merely documenting whether evaluators can detect AI use.
