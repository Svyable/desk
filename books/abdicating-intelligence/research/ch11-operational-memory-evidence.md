# Chapter 11 — operational memory, retrieval, and migration

Rechecked 2026-09-14 for the media/research enhancement pass on “The Memory Outside Us.”

## Why this chapter benefits from a documentary example

The chapter argues that external storage becomes institutional memory only when it can be retrieved, interpreted, and allowed to change action. The Library of Congress card catalog provides an unusually clean real-world example because a migration to a newer information system improved access without making the older system fully redundant.

## Library of Congress card catalog: external memory with a retrieval path

The Library of Congress says its Main Card Catalog contains author, title, subject, and related entries for most books cataloged through 1980 (1978 for subject cards). The catalog was the Library’s principal bibliographic access system before the online catalog.

The Library’s current research guide says the retrospective conversion of the card catalog to online form was not complete or fully accurate. Thousands of catalog cards were never converted. Some converted records do not preserve all of the information carried on the original cards, including subtitles, content notes, and series statements. Typographical errors and misspellings introduced during conversion can also prevent older records from appearing in keyword searches. For exhaustive research on older holdings, the physical catalog can therefore still be necessary.

A 2025 Library of Congress preservation account reports that the physical card-catalog system contains 22,314,000 cards in 22,732 drawers, 347 vertical cabinets, plus an estimated 2,000–2,500 portfolios. The Library notes that some cards contain unique information not available from other sources. During the current relocation of the catalogs to offsite storage, staff are creating inventory records so the boxes can still be requested and retrieved rather than simply warehoused.

Sources:
- Library of Congress Main Card Catalog: https://www.loc.gov/research-centers/main/main-card-catalog/
- Library of Congress research guide, introduction and continuing research value: https://guides.loc.gov/card-catalog
- Library of Congress card-catalog history: https://guides.loc.gov/card-catalog/card-catalog-history
- Library of Congress Preservation blog, “Preservation in Motion: Moving the Library’s Card Catalog” (2025-05-19): https://blogs.loc.gov/preservation/2025/05/moving-the-card-catalog/

### Evidentiary boundary

This case does **not** establish that digital catalogs are worse than card catalogs, that information should remain on paper, or that every migration loses important information. The online catalog is faster and more useful for most searches. The narrower lesson is that a successful migration can still alter fields, indexing, context, or findability, and the losses may only become visible when a user needs information the new representation omitted.

That makes the case useful for Chapter 11’s distinction between **storage** and **operational memory**. A record can physically survive while becoming functionally forgotten if nobody can locate or interpret it at the relevant moment.

## Federal records-management anchor

The National Archives and Records Administration describes a federal-record life cycle of creation or receipt, maintenance and use, and disposition. NARA’s guidance also names file plans, indexes, controlled vocabularies, taxonomies, data dictionaries, and access procedures among the mechanisms used to maintain and use records.

This is useful because it grounds the manuscript’s claim that memory infrastructure is more than storage media. Indexing, metadata, access, and retrieval are part of the system that makes a stored record operational.

Sources:
- NARA records-management FAQ: https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/faqs/general.html
- NARA Records Scheduling and Appraisal: https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/sch-appraisal

### Evidentiary boundary

NARA guidance is about federal records management, not cognitive science. It is used here as an institutional example of a mature records system explicitly treating maintenance, retrieval structures, and disposition as different functions.

## Editorial implication

The Library of Congress case gives Chapter 11 a concrete surprise: an older external-memory system can remain necessary *after* digitization because migration changes not just where information lives but what can be found. That is a stronger illustration than a generic argument about backups.

The chapter can therefore make a sharper distinction among three things that are often collapsed:

1. **Storage** — information exists somewhere.
2. **Retrieval** — the relevant information can be found when needed.
3. **Memory** — retrieved information is interpreted in context and changes present action.

The AI connection should remain an analogy, not an empirical equivalence. The useful question is what happens when retrieval and synthesis are delegated to a system whose omissions may be less visible than an empty card drawer.
