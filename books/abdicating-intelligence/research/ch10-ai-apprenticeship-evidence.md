# Chapter 10 research audit — AI, novice performance, and apprenticeship

Checked 2026-09-12 for `manuscript/ch10-a-profession-without-beginners.md`.

## Why this audit exists

Chapter 10 asks whether AI can make a profession look more capable in the present while weakening the process that produces future experts. That is a plausible mechanism, but the current prose moves too quickly from intuition to a broad succession-risk thesis. The best empirical evidence recovered so far is more interesting because it cuts both ways.

## Evidence that AI can accelerate novice performance

### Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond — *Generative AI at Work*

NBER Working Paper 31161 (2023; later published in *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2025).

https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

Field evidence from 5,179 customer-support agents. Access to a generative-AI conversational assistant increased productivity by about 14% on average, with substantially larger gains for novice/lower-skilled workers (34% in the paper's abstract) and minimal effects for experienced/high-skilled workers. The authors report suggestive evidence that the system disseminated practices associated with more capable workers and helped newer workers move down the experience curve.

**What this supports:** AI can compress part of an experience gradient. A novice need not reproduce every historical step of an expert's path to benefit from patterns learned from experts.

**What it does not establish:** customer-support performance is not the same as independent professional judgment; assisted productivity does not by itself establish durable unassisted learning; the result cannot be generalized mechanically to law, medicine, or software engineering.

## Evidence that assisted gains need not disappear when assistance is removed

### Cruces et al. — *Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps? Evidence from a Randomized Experiment*

NBER Working Paper 34851 (2026; revised May 2026).

https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851

Randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults aged 25–45 performing a workplace-style business problem-solving task. AI improved performance for all groups and sharply narrowed the education-based performance gap during assisted work. Participants then completed a non-AI follow-up module. Treated participants did not perform worse than controls once AI was removed, and lower-education participants retained part of the gain, although a substantial education gap reappeared. The authors report that follow-up performance was higher when intensive AI use was combined with sustained participant effort.

**What this supports:** the strongest version of the deskilling story is already too simple. Assistance can coexist with learning, and some gains can transfer after the tool is removed.

**What it does not establish:** a short experimental task is not a professional apprenticeship; partial retained gain does not show that AI-assisted workers acquire the same tacit knowledge, error repertoire, calibration, or long-horizon judgment as workers who perform the underlying task themselves.

## Evidence that AI can substitute for some expertise-sharing benefits

### Dell'Acqua et al. — *The Cybernetic Teammate*

NBER Working Paper 33641 (2025); published online in *Organization Science* in 2026.

https://www.nber.org/papers/w33641

Pre-registered field experiment with 776 professionals at Procter & Gamble working on real product-innovation challenges. Individuals with AI could match the performance of teams without AI on the studied work, and the paper reports effects on performance and expertise sharing.

**What this supports:** some benefits historically supplied by a more expert colleague or team can be delivered through an AI-mediated workflow. This is a real counterargument to preserving old production chains merely because they once carried apprenticeship.

**Boundary:** matching team output on bounded innovation tasks does not show that the solo AI-assisted worker acquires the teammate's durable expertise or can replace the institution's future expert stock.

## Strongest counterargument to Chapter 10

The chapter's opening fear can be stated too strongly. If AI makes novices productive sooner, exposes them to high-quality expert patterns, provides immediate feedback, and some of those gains persist without the tool, then removing low-learning junior work may improve rather than destroy apprenticeship. The historical production chain is not sacred. Fifty bad drafts are not automatically educational capital.

This forces the chapter to distinguish **assisted output**, **learning while assisted**, and **independent transfer**. The succession problem becomes serious only where a profession depends on capabilities that are not being acquired through the new workflow and cannot be reliably supplied by the system when needed.

## Safer manuscript claim

Do not claim that AI necessarily spends down expertise or that performing the old junior task is the only route to judgment. The evidence supports a narrower question:

> Which capabilities transfer from AI-assisted work into independent performance, and which capabilities disappear from the training distribution when the machine absorbs the routine cases that once produced them?

The relevant empirical test is longitudinal and domain-specific. Compare cohorts trained under different assistance regimes on later novel, adversarial, exception-heavy, and tool-unavailable tasks. Measure calibration and error detection as well as output quality.

## Editorial implication

Chapter 10 should replace several generic professions-and-policy inventories with this evidence conflict. The chapter becomes stronger if it concedes early that AI may be an unusually effective tutor and experience compressor, then asks what the surviving transfer tests do and do not establish. The unresolved risk is not 'beginners stop doing drudgery.' It is that institutions may optimize visible assisted output faster than they learn whether the invisible capabilities needed for exceptions, verification, teaching, and recovery are still reproducing.
