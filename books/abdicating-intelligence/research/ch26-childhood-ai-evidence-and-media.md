# Chapter 26 — Childhood AI evidence and media packet

## Purpose

Support **“The Childhood Question”** with evidence that distinguishes three claims that are easy to collapse: children can form social/parasocial models of conversational agents; those models and trust are teachable rather than fixed; and current evidence does **not** justify broad claims that conversational AI will inevitably damage children's relationships or cognition.

## Evidence anchor 1 — children can treat conversational agents as social beings

**Source:** Anna Hoffman, Diana Owen, and Sandra L. Calvert, “Parent reports of children's parasocial relationships with conversational agents: Trusted voices in children's lives,” *Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies* 3(4), 2021, 606–617.  
**DOI / source URL:** https://doi.org/10.1002/hbe2.271  
**Authority:** Peer-reviewed empirical article.  
**Design:** Parent-report naturalistic study; 70 qualifying families with conversational agents, with a smaller subset used for factor analysis. Children were ages 3–10 in the analyzed subset described by the article.  
**Finding usable here:** The study reported dimensions of attachment, personification, and social realism in children's relationships with conversational agents. This supports the narrow claim that some children can relate to conversational agents in socially meaningful, human-like ways.  
**Do not claim:** that conversational agents replace human relationships; that parasocial attachment is necessarily harmful; that modern LLM companions produce the same effects; or that the study establishes long-term developmental outcomes.  
**Rights posture:** Open-access article, but no source artwork is needed. Use citation and bounded paraphrase only unless the exact license for a particular figure is separately verified before reuse.

## Evidence anchor 2 — children's models of conversational agents can change through education

**Source:** Jessica Van Brummelen, Mingyan Claire Tian, Maura Kelleher, and Nghi Hoang Nguyen, “Learning Affects Trust: Design Recommendations and Concepts for Teaching Children—and Nearly Anyone—about Conversational Agents,” *Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence* 37(13), 2023/2024 record, 15860–15868.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v37i13.26883  
**Open record:** https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/26883  
**Preprint:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05063  
**Authority:** Peer-reviewed conference publication; authors affiliated with MIT/Wellesley in the publication record.  
**Finding usable here:** In workshops and associated studies, participants' reported perceptions and trust toward conversational agents changed; participants often connected trust changes to learning about how agents are programmed, where information comes from, and how agents handle information.  
**Why it matters:** This is useful evidence against treating children's trust or anthropomorphism as an immutable trait. AI literacy can include building a more accurate model of what the agent is.  
**Do not claim:** that the curriculum prevents overreliance, improves long-term safety, or establishes an optimal age for AI use. The work itself describes limitations and design recommendations rather than a universal developmental prescription.  
**Rights posture:** Cite/paraphrase. Do not reproduce publisher PDF figures or layouts without separate license verification.

## Evidence anchor 3 — current child-centered governance guidance

**UNICEF, Guidance on AI and children, Version 3.0 (2025/2026 current page).**  
Source: https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children  
The current guidance groups child-centered AI around safety, privacy, non-discrimination, transparency/accountability, development and well-being, inclusion, and preparing/skilling children for AI. This supports the manuscript's framing that protection and preparation are linked rather than opposites.

**UNICEF, Child Centric AI (2026).**  
Source: https://www.unicef.org/digitalimpact/stories/child-centric-ai  
Useful current formulation: developmental appropriateness should shape system behavior, and privacy/data collection should not be traded for engagement optimization. Treat this as policy/design guidance, not empirical proof of developmental effects.

**UNESCO, Guidance on Generative AI in Education and Research (released 7 September 2023).**  
Source page: https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-must-be-kept-check-school  
UNESCO called for a human-centered approach, data/privacy safeguards, teacher training, and a minimum classroom-use age of 13 in that guidance. This is a policy recommendation, not a biological threshold or scientific finding that independent thought is established at 13.

## Optional manuscript-strengthening paragraph

A useful empirical bridge belongs near the manuscript's discussion of social lessons and “defaults become ontology”:

> Long before today's large language models, researchers were already finding that some young children treated conversational agents as more than appliances. In a 2021 parent-report study of children ages three to ten, Anna Hoffman and colleagues found patterns of attachment, personification, and social realism around agents such as Alexa, Siri, and Google Assistant. That does not show that a chatbot damages human relationships, or even that the attachment is harmful. It shows something more basic: the social interpretation is real enough to measure. Later educational work by Jessica Van Brummelen and colleagues found that children's and parents' reported trust and models of conversational agents could change when they learned more about how the systems were programmed and where their information came from. The child's model of the machine is itself teachable.

Keep the caveat sentence. It prevents smart-speaker-era evidence from being silently upgraded into a claim about contemporary companion LLMs.

## Media decisions / rejected reuse

- **Do not reuse screenshots of Alexa, Siri, Google Assistant, ChatGPT, or commercial child-facing AI products.** They add trademark/interface-rights baggage and date quickly.
- **Do not reproduce figures from Hoffman et al. or Van Brummelen et al.** The chapter does not need their original artwork; bounded factual paraphrase is stronger and cleaner.
- **Do not reproduce UNICEF/UNESCO logos, report covers, infographics, or photography.** Institutional authority comes from citation, not visual branding; individual image rights can differ from the surrounding page/document.
- **Do not use stock imagery of children with robots.** It is decorative and tends to smuggle in a stronger “companion” claim than the evidence supports.

## Highest-value next research opportunities

The biggest gap is longitudinal evidence on **children using modern LLM conversational systems over months**, with measures that distinguish immediate satisfaction from later transfer, source judgment, social calibration, independent problem solving, and relationship quality. Particularly valuable would be preregistered studies comparing answer-first systems with systems that deliberately preserve prediction, explanation, perspective-taking, or source-checking.

A second high-value target is evidence on **co-use**: whether parent/teacher participation changes children's anthropomorphism, disclosure, trust calibration, or learning from conversational AI. Small recent studies are appearing, but sample sizes and novelty effects make strong developmental claims premature.
