# Research brief — Abdicating Intelligence

The book's research question is not whether AI makes people smarter or dumber in the abstract. It is which forms of cognitive delegation change assisted performance, later independent performance, expertise formation, verification, institutional memory, question formation, accountability, and recovery capacity — and under what conditions.

The expanded manuscript also asks how credible-looking output changes the social signals of expertise, when multiple machine judgments are genuinely independent, what can responsibly be said about children growing up with conversational AI, how AI changes hypothesis and problem formation, when human sign-off represents real judgment rather than ceremony, and what institutions or societies need in order to remain capable while depending on external cognitive infrastructure.

The manuscript deliberately carries counterevidence. Cognitive offloading can be adaptive. Transactive and distributed cognition are foundational to civilization. AI assistance has produced large gains for less experienced workers in at least some field settings, and superhuman AI has been associated with improved and more novel human play in Go. Machine ensembles can improve reliability, AI tutoring can expand access to individualized support, AI-generated hypotheses can widen scientific search, and globally supplied models can give small organizations capabilities they could never build alone. Those cases constrain any deskilling or sovereignty claim.

The strongest negative claims are similarly scoped. Automation research supports risks around out-of-the-loop performance, complacency, and automation bias, but these are properties of human-automation systems rather than proof that automation is intrinsically harmful. Education evidence distinguishes immediate assisted performance from later unassisted learning and shows that design can change the outcome. Evidence about the long-run developmental effects of conversational AI on children remains incomplete and should be presented as an open research problem rather than a settled harm. Model agreement is not treated as useless; the relevant question is error correlation and functional independence.

## Research rules

- Prefer primary sources, peer-reviewed research, court orders, government guidance, and first-party institutional material.
- Do not turn one experiment into a universal law.
- Distinguish cross-sectional evidence from causal evidence.
- Preserve the difference between individual cognition and distributed system capability.
- Treat professional deskilling as a pipeline hypothesis unless longitudinal evidence supports a stronger claim.
- Treat the legal phrase “right to a human reason” as the author's normative formulation, not a statement of universal current law.
- Treat children's long-term cognitive and social outcomes as uncertain where longitudinal evidence is not yet available.
- Treat multi-agent or multi-model agreement as stronger evidence only to the degree that the systems contribute genuinely different information or error modes.
- Treat cognitive sovereignty as agency under interdependence, not as a claim that countries or organizations should become technologically self-sufficient.
- Separate question selection and objective choice from answer quality; a factually correct answer can still optimize inside a poor frame.
- Update the source ledger when new factual examples enter the prose.
- If a later source materially contradicts the thesis, preserve the contradiction and revise the argument rather than explaining it away.

The chapter briefs are argument tests, not prose outlines. Their falsification notes identify claims that would make the chapter misleading if ignored.
