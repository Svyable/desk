# Chapter 9 research update — assistance, learning, and the junior pipeline

## Why this note exists

Chapter 9, “The Junior Problem,” uses Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond’s *Generative AI at Work* as its opening empirical case. The chapter correctly treats short-run productivity as distinct from long-run professional formation, but the source ledger still points to the 2023 NBER working paper and the prose describes the study as an “early” study. The paper is now peer-reviewed and published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* (May 2025), and the final version contains evidence that makes the chapter’s strongest counterargument stronger.

This is a research note only. It does not justify a broad rewrite of the chapter.

## Primary / authoritative source check

Final publication:

- Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey R. Raymond, “Generative AI at Work,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 140, no. 2 (May 2025): 889–942.
- Stanford Graduate School of Business publication record: https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/generative-ai-work
- Earlier NBER working-paper record, useful for version history: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

The final publication reports 5,172 customer-support agents and a 15% average increase in issues resolved per hour. Gains were concentrated among less experienced and lower-skilled workers; the most experienced and highest-skilled workers saw small speed gains and small quality declines. The final abstract also states that the authors find evidence that AI assistance facilitates worker learning and improves English fluency, particularly among international agents.

The 2023 working-paper abstract reported 5,179 agents and a 14% average productivity increase, including a 34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers. Those figures should not be silently mixed with the final journal version.

## What this changes in the argument

The chapter currently makes a careful move: the observed productivity gain “did not need to answer the career question,” while organizations redesigning permanent workflows still do. That distinction remains sound.

But the final paper means the countercase deserves more weight than “AI can reduce skill gaps.” In this setting, assistance was not merely a crutch that made novices look better while leaving their underlying capability untouched. The authors report evidence consistent with learning: newer workers moved down the experience curve faster, and the final paper reports improved English fluency among international agents.

The stronger version of the chapter’s argument is therefore not:

> AI makes novices productive now, but we do not know whether they learn.

It is closer to:

> Some forms of AI assistance can accelerate learning while they accelerate production. The unresolved succession risk appears when organizations use that success to remove the work, people, feedback, or exposure from which future expertise is produced.

That is a harder claim to dismiss because it does not require assuming that assistance and learning are opposites.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The customer-support evidence is directly inconvenient for a simple deskilling story. Less experienced workers benefited most, the system appears to have diffused practices associated with stronger workers, and the published paper reports evidence of learning rather than only contemporaneous output gains.

A second limit is scope. This was one customer-support setting with a particular assistive system. It does not establish that AI assistance accelerates durable skill formation in law, medicine, engineering, software, or other professions where expertise includes judgment over rare cases, responsibility, tacit organizational knowledge, and long training horizons.

Nor does it establish the opposite. Chapter 9 should resist turning plausible pipeline risk into an already-demonstrated profession-wide decline. The empirical question is not merely whether junior headcount falls, but whether the remaining training system produces enough people with the capabilities society still assigns to humans.

## Suggested surgical manuscript pass

When Chapter 9 is next edited, consider only a small update around the opening study:

1. Treat the QJE article as the controlling citation/version rather than the 2023 working paper.
2. Use the final sample and headline result consistently: 5,172 agents; 15% average productivity gain.
3. Acknowledge the paper’s learning evidence immediately rather than leaving “learning” mostly as a theoretical possibility later in the chapter.
4. Preserve the chapter’s strongest existing sentence-level distinction: short-run assistance evidence does not settle the long-run succession question.
5. Do not claim that entry-level employment decline, where observed elsewhere, proves a causal collapse of apprenticeship. Hiring can move for many reasons, and exposure measures do not identify the mechanism by themselves.

## Editorial payoff

This revision would make the chapter less symmetrical and more intellectually interesting. The same study can support both sides at once: AI can help a novice learn faster, and successful augmentation can still change the economics of hiring novices. The tension is not “learning versus AI.” It is whether a production system that needs fewer beginners will deliberately preserve enough developmental opportunity to reproduce the human expertise it still expects to exist later.
