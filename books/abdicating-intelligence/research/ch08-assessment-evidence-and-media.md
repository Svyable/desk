# Chapter 8 — assessment evidence and media provenance

Rechecked 2026-09-14 for the media/research enhancement pass on “The Student Who Gets It Right.”

## Core evidentiary distinction

Chapter 8 is strongest when it treats artifact quality and demonstrated capability as related but separable variables. Generative AI does not make student artifacts meaningless; it weakens the assumption that the finished artifact, by itself, identifies which cognitive work the student can independently reproduce, explain, transfer, or extend.

### Bastani et al. (2025): assisted performance can diverge from later unassisted performance

Hamsa Bastani, Osbert Bastani, Alp Sungu, Haosen Ge, Özge Kabakcı, and Rei Mariman conducted a randomized field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students using GPT-4-based tutors. Their two treatment designs are especially useful for this chapter because both improved performance during AI-assisted practice, yet the later unassisted assessment separated the conditions.

The published paper reports that GPT Base improved assisted practice grades by 48% relative to control and GPT Tutor by 127%. When AI was removed, students in GPT Base scored 17% below control on the unassisted exam, while the negative effect was essentially eliminated in GPT Tutor. The authors explicitly caution about context and generalizability: one school, mathematics, Fall 2023 deployment, particular models and interfaces, and short-term outcomes.

### Kestin et al. (2025): pedagogically designed AI tutoring can improve learning

Greg Kestin, Kelly Miller, Anna Klales, Timothy Milbourne, and Gregorio Ponti reported a randomized controlled trial in an introductory Harvard physics course. Their custom AI tutor was built around explicit pedagogical practices including active learning, scaffolding, targeted feedback, self-pacing, and cognitive-load management. The authors report stronger post-test learning gains in less time than the in-class active-learning comparison, along with higher student-reported engagement and motivation.

This study is important as counterevidence against a simplistic “AI assistance makes assessment invalid” or “AI necessarily harms learning” interpretation. It supports the narrower distinction used in Chapter 8: assistance can be designed to build capability, but a polished assisted artifact is not automatically evidence of independent capability.

### OECD (2026): assessment and skill development are now explicit policy concerns

The OECD policy spotlight **“Policies supporting responsible and systematic GenAI adoption in higher education”**, published July 17, 2026, identifies GenAI’s effects on teaching and assessment, academic integrity, reliability, equity, and the development of students’ knowledge and skills as system-level concerns. It also describes emerging policy responses including common guidance, procurement approaches, GenAI competency development, evidence collection, pilot evaluations, and specialised tools for higher education.

### UNESCO guidance: pedagogical validation, not mere technical capability

UNESCO’s 2023 *Guidance for generative AI in education and research* calls for human-centred, age-appropriate and pedagogically validated use of generative AI in education. Its value here is institutional rather than causal: it supports the chapter’s move from “Can the model produce a good answer?” to “What is the educational objective, and what evidence shows the system advances it?”

**Working title:** “The same polished artifact can hide different capabilities.”

**Purpose:** Make the chapter’s assessment problem visible without repeating Chapter 6’s tutor/substitute learning-loop figure. The lower half shows several assessment probes—oral defense, transfer task, unassisted reconstruction, and tool-assisted extension—because each answers a different question about competence.

The figure intentionally avoids a binary “AI used / AI not used” frame. The assessment question is instead: **what capability is this evidence supposed to establish?**

## Editorial implication

The chapter should resist the easy “AI broke homework” framing. The stronger claim is that generative systems weaken an old measurement shortcut: institutions could often treat the artifact as a rough proxy for the student because producing the artifact previously required more of the target capability. That proxy is becoming noisier.

The response is not simply more proctoring. It is better construct validity: decide whether the institution is trying to measure independent recall, explanation, transfer, judgment, collaborative tool use, or some combination, and choose evidence accordingly.
