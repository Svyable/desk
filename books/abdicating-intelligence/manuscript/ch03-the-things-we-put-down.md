# The Things We Put Down

A reminder is a small confession.

You write “call dentist” on a calendar because you do not fully trust the version of yourself who will exist Thursday afternoon. You put keys in the same bowl because searching is expensive. You leave a document open on the screen so the unfinished thought remains visible after lunch. You ask another person to remember something because their memory now becomes part of your plan.

None of this feels like technology in the grand sense.

It is cognition arranged in the environment.

Psychologists use the phrase *cognitive offloading* for actions that reduce the demands of a task by changing how information is stored or processed outside the mind. The term covers behavior much older than computers. Fingers can hold a count. Marks on paper can hold an intermediate result. A shopping list can hold intentions. Rotating a physical object can be easier than rotating a mental representation.

The basic idea is liberating because it punctures a flattering myth about intelligence. Smart people are not people who keep everything inside their heads. Often they are people who arrange the world so their limited attention can be spent well.

A laboratory, a cockpit, a kitchen, and a programmer's desk are full of external memory. Labels, gauges, notebooks, version control, alarms, mise en place, checklists, tickets, dashboards, and comments all let one moment of cognition leave a trace for another.

Civilization scales through traces.

The mistake begins when we use *offloading* as though it named one uniform exchange. Writing a reminder and outsourcing a diagnosis are both externalizations, but they move different things. One stores an intention. The other may move observation, interpretation, causal reasoning, uncertainty assessment, and action selection into another system at once.

We need a more granular account of what gets put down.

Memory is the obvious first category.

The experiments by Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu, and Daniel Wegner published in *Science* in 2011 are useful because their result is subtler than the slogan that followed them. Participants who expected information to remain accessible later showed different recall behavior from those who expected it not to be available. People could become better at remembering where information was stored than the information itself.

That is not memory failure.

It is memory allocation.

Wegner had spent years studying transactive memory in groups: the way people in relationships and teams divide responsibility for knowing. One person remembers family dates. Another remembers financial details. A laboratory knows which technician understands a machine. A company may not need every employee to know every policy if employees know who knows.

The Internet expanded the possible partner.

A person could treat the network as an addressable extension of memory: not “I know this,” exactly, but “I know how to recover this.” Search turned retrieval strategy into part of everyday cognition.

There is an economic elegance to this. Biological memory is scarce. Facts vary in durability and value. If a low-value fact can be retrieved quickly from a reliable external store, retaining it internally may be a poor use of effort. The system as a whole becomes more capable by dividing labor between internal representation and external access.

The arrangement works until the retrieval environment changes.

A bookmark disappears. A link rots. A database is reorganized. A search engine ranks different material. A subscription ends. A cloud service shuts down. A company changes vendors. A model update alters which sources it retrieves and how it summarizes them.

The memory did not merely sit outside the person.

It sat inside an institution.

That is the first reason AI offloading deserves a broader frame than personal convenience. External cognition has owners, interfaces, business models, failure modes, and update schedules. A notebook can be lost, but it does not silently rewrite last year's page because a vendor improved the notebook. A generated memory can.

The second category we offload is calculation.

This is familiar enough to feel safe. Calculators are wonderful because arithmetic is often a component of a larger problem rather than the intellectual point. Offloading multiplication can free attention for modeling.

But even calculation demonstrates a general principle: externalization is safest when the user retains a coarse internal model of the expected result.

An engineer does not need to multiply every figure by hand to notice that a bridge load calculation is off by three orders of magnitude. Dimensional analysis, order-of-magnitude estimates, and physical intuition create independent structure. The exact answer may come from software; the human brings a range in which the answer could plausibly live.

This roughness is not a defect.

It is a verification surface.

Generated AI answers often arrive in domains where users do not possess even a range. A patient asks what a symptom means. A manager asks why churn rose. A student asks for the interpretation of a poem not yet read. A citizen asks what a proposed law would do. A programmer asks why unfamiliar code crashes.

The system's answer becomes the first model.

Once the first model exists, later thought is not neutral. Attention is directed toward evidence that fits the frame. Alternative explanations become harder to generate. The cost of offloading therefore includes not only lost practice but path dependence.

We offload framing.

That is a much bigger transfer than arithmetic.

We also offload search.

Traditional search externalized memory but left a visible selection problem. The user received documents, snippets, links, and rankings. The ranking system already shaped attention, sometimes powerfully, but sources remained comparatively distinct. Generated search can perform the selection and synthesis before the user sees the material.

The interface gains convenience by hiding intermediate cognition.

This is a recurring design trade.

A good graphical interface hides machine state so a person can act without understanding registers and processes. A good automatic transmission hides gear selection. A good compiler hides machine instructions. Abstraction is one of the foundations of modern technical progress.

The danger is not hidden complexity by itself.

The danger is hidden contestability.

If the concealed layer can affect high-consequence conclusions, users need some way to inspect or challenge it without becoming engineers of the whole stack. A banking customer need not understand the code behind a credit decision, but a society may still require explanation, appeal, auditing, and evidence that protected classes are not being harmed. A doctor need not understand every parameter in a model but may need access to the findings, provenance, uncertainty, and known limits that matter for care.

The right amount of visibility depends on consequence.

Cognitive offloading research helps here because it makes clear that effort has value and cost. People use external reminders partly because maintaining intentions internally consumes cognitive resources. Experiments by Sam Gilbert and colleagues have shown that people can strategically choose reminders and that their choices depend on confidence, effort, and metacognitive judgment. Offloading can improve performance.

A person who refuses all reminders in order to preserve memory may simply miss appointments.

There is no wisdom in preventable failure.

But the optimal strategy changes when the external system is unreliable or when the act of internal processing produces a capability needed later.

Consider language learning. Looking up a word can solve the immediate comprehension problem. Repeated retrieval can help make the word available without lookup. The immediate goal and the developmental goal differ. A tool optimized only for immediate success can interfere with the process by which future success becomes independent of the tool.

Education is full of this conflict.

The student wants the problem solved. The teacher wants the student changed by solving it.

A tutor who gives the answer too early can be excellent at task completion and terrible at teaching. A tutor who withholds help forever can turn learning into pointless frustration. The art lies in controlling when cognition remains internal, when support enters, and how support fades as competence grows.

Generative AI turns that old pedagogical question into a general economic question.

What happens when every worker has a tutor that is also willing to do the job?

A junior analyst can ask for a market model and receive one. A junior lawyer can ask for a motion. A junior developer can ask for the function. A new manager can ask for a performance plan. If the tool supplies excellent artifacts, organizations will reasonably want the artifacts.

The developmental effect may be invisible because the immediate metrics improve.

That is why performance and learning must be separated.

A system can raise assisted performance while reducing unassisted capability. It can also raise both. The difference cannot be inferred from the quality of the output alone.

This distinction appears in research on students using generative systems. In one large field experiment in high-school mathematics, students with access to a general-purpose GPT-based assistant performed better during practice, but those gains did not fully translate when access was removed; a more carefully designed tutoring version reduced the negative learning effect. The result is not a verdict on AI in school. It is evidence that interface and instructional design can determine whether assistance becomes scaffolding or substitution.

The same logic applies outside school even when nobody calls it learning.

Work is a learning environment whether the organization acknowledges it or not. Repeated tasks teach the shape of a domain. Customer-service agents learn which complaints signal deeper problems. Accountants learn where books tend to be wrong. Nurses learn which patients deteriorate unexpectedly. Programmers learn which clean-looking changes create ugly edge cases.

If AI takes over the repetitions, the organization should ask what learning was hidden inside them.

Some repetitions deserve to disappear. Copying data between systems teaches mostly endurance. Formatting a standard report may add little after the first few examples. Boilerplate drafting can consume time without building much judgment.

Other repetitions are the substrate of pattern recognition.

The hard part is that they look similar in a task inventory.

Both can be labeled “routine.”

Routine is an economic description, not a cognitive one.

A task may be routine for an expert because experience has compressed it. The same task may be developmental for a novice because the patterns are still being formed. Automating it across the organization can therefore have unequal effects. The senior person loses drudgery. The junior loses exposure.

This is the junior problem that later chapters will return to.

For now, the broader point is that offloading changes three time horizons at once.

There is the present task: did the work get done?

There is the future person: did the user become more or less capable because of how the work was done?

There is the future institution: will enough people still understand the process when conditions change?

Most software is evaluated heavily on the first horizon because it is easiest to measure. AI productivity studies naturally begin there. How many tickets were resolved? How fast was the code written? How many documents were produced? These are sensible metrics.

They are not enough for tools that mediate learning.

A company can borrow capability from its future workforce without recording the liability.

That is cognitive debt.

The phrase should not be taken as a claim that every convenience creates a debt. Often a tool removes a task permanently and nothing is owed. The debt appears when the organization still expects a capability later but stops funding the practice that produces it now.

If nobody will ever need to navigate manually, manual navigation can vanish.

If people will still be expected to take over when automated navigation fails, the calculus changes.

This is why the most important question about offloading is not “Where does cognition happen?” The extended-mind tradition in philosophy and distributed-cognition research already gives us good reasons not to fetishize the skull. Cognition can be spread across people and artifacts.

The important question is “What happens when the distribution changes?”

Which node in the system loses practice? Which gains authority? Which becomes a single point of failure? Which errors become correlated? Which representations disappear because the interface no longer requires them? Which kinds of recovery remain possible?

A shopping list is a tiny distributed cognitive system.

An AI-mediated hospital is a large one.

The moral language of laziness and cheating is poorly suited to either. We need systems language instead.

What is being stored? What is being inferred? What is being selected? What is being practiced? What is being trusted? What must remain possible after the offload?

Those questions make room for the obvious benefits. A reminder can liberate memory. A model can make expertise available to people who never had access to it. A translation system can let two strangers collaborate. A diagnostic assistant can surface a rare condition. A coding agent can let a small team build software that once required a department.

Offloading is leverage.

Leverage increases both reach and dependence on the fulcrum.

The mature goal is not to keep every thought inside us.

It is to know which thoughts we can safely put down.
