# The Junior Problem

Every profession has work that senior people are delighted to stop doing.

The first draft. The routine search. The data cleaning. The simple ticket. The standard contract. The repetitive calculation. The customer question everyone has answered a hundred times.

These tasks are obvious targets for automation because they are expensive in aggregate and unglamorous in detail.

They are also where juniors live.

This creates a problem that appears first as a triumph.

In one of the most influential early studies of generative AI in real work, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond examined more than five thousand customer-support agents. Access to an AI assistant increased productivity on average, with much larger gains among less experienced and lower-skilled workers. The system appeared to help diffuse patterns associated with stronger performers.

This is the optimistic story of AI and apprenticeship.

A novice gains access to distilled experience at the moment of need. The machine suggests a phrase, a solution, a troubleshooting path. It can compress the distance between newcomer and veteran. Expertise that once spread through overheard conversations and years of exposure becomes available instantly.

The result matters because it shows that AI can reduce skill gaps rather than merely reward the already skilled.

But the same finding raises a second question.

What does the novice become after years of assistance?

The productivity study measured real and valuable outcomes in the period observed. It did not need to answer the career question for the result to matter. Yet organizations making permanent workflow changes do need to answer it.

A junior worker is not a small senior worker.

They occupy a developmental stage.

The routine case has different value to them because it creates a reference distribution. A customer-service agent who handles hundreds of ordinary billing problems learns what ordinary looks like. A junior lawyer reading dozens of contracts learns which clauses recur and where negotiations actually concentrate. A new programmer fixing straightforward defects learns the architecture before being entrusted with the strange ones.

If AI absorbs the normal distribution, humans can be pushed toward exceptions earlier.

This can accelerate development when the system provides enough context and feedback.

It can also produce novices who encounter only cases they are least prepared to understand.

The senior person has a library of ordinary experience from which the exception becomes legible. The junior receives the exception without the library.

This is the junior problem.

Organizations will be tempted to solve it by hiring fewer juniors.

The arithmetic is compelling. If one senior professional with AI can produce the work of several traditional teams, the firm can reduce entry-level headcount while maintaining output. Fewer people need to spend years on routine tasks because routine tasks are no longer scarce.

For an individual firm, that can be rational.

For a profession, it can become a succession problem.

Where do seniors come from?

The question sounds almost naive because labor markets make people appear as resources already formed. A company posts a job for ten years of experience and receives applicants. The training history behind those ten years belongs to somebody else.

When every firm follows the same optimization, the external supply shrinks.

This is familiar in skilled trades, nursing, teaching, and other fields where apprenticeship capacity can become a bottleneck. Generative AI may extend the pattern into occupations that assumed expertise would reproduce itself through ordinary office work.

The problem is not that junior jobs deserve preservation as a social entitlement.

Many junior tasks are terrible. Entire generations of young professionals have spent nights formatting documents, copying numbers, searching databases manually, and performing work that senior people called “training” because nobody had an incentive to redesign it.

Automation gives us a chance to remove hazing disguised as pedagogy.

But removal should be followed by curriculum.

If a routine task was teaching something important, teach that thing directly.

This seems obvious in schools and oddly radical in companies.

A company is not a university, managers will say. It exists to produce goods and services, not to educate employees.

True.

It also exists next year.

If future performance requires expertise that takes years to develop, training is production with a longer time horizon.

The best organizations have always understood this. They rotate people through roles, pair juniors with mentors, run postmortems, create internal courses, require certifications, and preserve opportunities to lead under supervision. AI increases the return on making those practices explicit because accidental learning from routine production is likely to decline.

Cognitive apprenticeship offers a useful framework. Allan Collins, John Seely Brown, and Susan Newman developed the idea in educational research, and Brown, Collins, and Paul Duguid argued more broadly that knowledge is situated in activity and culture. People learn not just declarative facts but how practitioners see, decide, and act in context.

Apprenticeship traditionally makes hidden thinking visible.

The expert demonstrates. The novice attempts. The expert coaches. Support fades. The novice takes on more difficult work.

Generative AI can strengthen every step.

It can demonstrate unlimited examples. It can explain an expert move at multiple levels. It can create practice cases. It can compare a novice's attempt with alternatives. It can provide immediate feedback when no mentor is available.

It can also collapse the sequence by doing the attempt itself.

Again, the same capability points in opposite developmental directions.

The most promising workplace designs will separate production mode from apprenticeship mode.

In production mode, use the strongest automation that meets risk requirements. Do not force a qualified worker to manually recreate what the system can safely do faster.

In apprenticeship mode, deliberately expose the learner to the reasoning that production mode hides.

Give the junior the problem before the generated solution. Ask for an estimate before the forecast. Require a draft of the issue tree before the model expands it. Let the learner review an AI answer with seeded errors. Compare their decision with an expert and with the system. Track which kinds of errors recur.

Then return to production.

This will feel artificial because the old workplace did not need such a distinction.

The old workplace was already a training simulation. It just happened to produce billable work at the same time.

Once AI decouples those functions, simulation has to become intentional.

The challenge is incentive alignment.

Juniors want to look competent. If using AI produces stronger work, they will use it, especially when performance reviews reward output. Seniors want fast deliverables. They will not always spend scarce time unpacking reasoning that the model handled correctly. Clients want lower prices. Shareholders want margin.

Nobody in the local transaction naturally pays for the novice to be slower today so the profession has an expert later.

Professional institutions may need to help.

Licensing requirements can preserve competencies. Firms can define promotion standards based on demonstrated judgment rather than years served. Clients in high-consequence domains can demand named responsible professionals with evidence of competency. Insurers may price organizations differently depending on training and oversight. Industry groups can share simulated cases and benchmarks for human-plus-AI proficiency.

The exact mechanisms will vary.

The important point is that succession must become visible as a system property.

There is another possibility: perhaps we will need far fewer human experts because machines themselves will become the senior layer.

This is the strongest counterargument to the junior problem.

If AI eventually handles routine work, exceptions, verification, and system redesign better than humans, preserving a large pipeline of human expertise may be economically unnecessary. Training people to perform obsolete cognitive labor could resemble training elevator operators after automatic controls became reliable.

We should take that possibility seriously.

The answer depends on what roles humans retain.

If society is comfortable assigning full authority to machine systems in a domain, then human succession can shrink dramatically. Some skills may become historical crafts.

If humans remain legally, ethically, or practically responsible for high-consequence decisions, then reducing the pipeline while preserving responsibility is incoherent.

You cannot insist on human accountability and stop producing accountable humans.

The transition may be long enough that this contradiction matters even if ultimate automation is extensive.

Institutions live in transition.

A hospital cannot staff itself based on a forecast that autonomous medicine will be solved in fifteen years. A government cannot abandon procurement expertise because future agents might manage vendors. A court cannot stop training lawyers while humans remain officers of the court.

The option value of expertise is high under uncertainty.

This suggests a conservative principle for slow-to-build capabilities: reduce the pipeline gradually enough that reality can prove the capability unnecessary before it disappears.

That is not technological pessimism.

It is avoiding an irreversible bet.

Rebuilding expertise can take longer than deploying software.

A model can be installed in months. A senior clinician takes years. An experienced judge takes a career. An engineer who understands a complex industrial system may embody decades of incident history not fully documented anywhere.

The asymmetry should affect how quickly we allow cognitive capital to depreciate.

There is also a social dimension. Entry-level work is how people enter institutions, learn norms, build networks, and acquire the tacit knowledge that no manual contains. If automation removes the bottom of the hierarchy, organizations may become more closed.

Who gets the remaining junior positions?

Likely people with stronger credentials, better networks, or access to private training. The old routine job, however inefficient, sometimes functioned as a paid bridge into expertise. Replacing it with an expectation that applicants arrive already capable can shift training costs onto families and schools.

AI could reverse this too.

Cheap tutors and simulation could let people build skills outside elite institutions. A motivated learner could practice law-like reasoning, coding, finance, languages, and science with extraordinary support before receiving a formal opportunity. The same technology that removes junior work can democratize preparation for the work that remains.

The direction depends on access and credentialing.

If employers recognize demonstrated capability, AI tutoring can widen entry.

If employers respond to uncertainty by demanding more prestigious credentials, entry can narrow.

Technology does not decide which equilibrium we choose.

The junior problem is therefore larger than corporate training. It is about how a society manufactures expertise when production no longer requires beginners in the old quantities.

For centuries, learning and labor were entangled.

AI begins to untangle them.

We should welcome the removal of pointless labor.

Then we should build a better learning system in the space it leaves.

The worst outcome would be to keep all the senior responsibility, eliminate all the junior practice, and discover a decade later that experience was not a renewable resource after all.
