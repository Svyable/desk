# The Junior Problem

Every profession has work senior people are delighted to stop doing.

The first draft. The routine search. The data cleaning. The simple ticket. The standard contract. The repetitive calculation. The customer question everyone has answered a hundred times.

These are obvious targets for automation: expensive in aggregate, unglamorous in detail.

They are also where juniors live.

That creates a problem that first appears as a triumph.

In one of the most influential early studies of generative AI in real work, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond examined more than five thousand customer-support agents. Access to an AI assistant increased productivity on average, with much larger gains among less experienced and lower-skilled workers. The system appeared to help diffuse patterns associated with stronger performers.

That is the optimistic story of AI and apprenticeship.

A novice gets distilled experience at the moment of need: a phrase, a solution, a troubleshooting path. Knowledge that once spread through overheard conversations and years of exposure can arrive on demand. AI can narrow skill gaps rather than merely reward the already skilled.

But the same result leaves a career-sized question unanswered.

What does the novice become after years of assistance?

The productivity study measured real and valuable outcomes during the period observed. It did not need to answer that question. Organizations redesigning permanent workflows do.

A junior worker is not a small senior worker.

The routine case has different value to someone still building a reference distribution. A customer-service agent who handles hundreds of ordinary billing problems learns what ordinary looks like. A junior lawyer reading dozens of contracts learns which clauses recur and where negotiations actually concentrate. A new programmer fixing straightforward defects learns the architecture before being handed the strange ones.

If AI absorbs the normal distribution, humans can reach the exceptions earlier.

Sometimes that accelerates development. Sometimes it hands a novice the cases least legible without the cases that came before them.

The senior has a library of ordinary experience from which the exception becomes visible.

The junior gets the exception without the library.

This is the junior problem.

Organizations will be tempted to solve it by hiring fewer juniors.

The arithmetic is compelling. If one senior professional with AI can produce the work of several traditional teams, the firm can cut entry-level headcount while maintaining output. For an individual firm, that may be entirely rational.

For a profession, it can become a succession problem.

Where do seniors come from?

Labor markets make experienced people look like natural resources. Post a job requiring ten years of experience and applicants appear. The ten years happened somewhere else.

When every firm makes the same optimization, “somewhere else” begins to disappear.

This problem is familiar in skilled trades, nursing, teaching, and other fields where apprenticeship capacity can become a bottleneck. Generative AI may extend it into occupations that assumed expertise would reproduce itself through ordinary office work.

The answer is not to preserve junior drudgery as a social entitlement.

Many junior tasks are terrible. Generations of young professionals have spent nights formatting documents, copying numbers, searching databases manually, and performing work senior people called “training” because nobody had an incentive to redesign it.

Automation gives us a chance to remove hazing disguised as pedagogy.

But removal should be followed by curriculum.

If a routine task was teaching something important, teach that thing directly.

This sounds obvious in a school and oddly radical in a company.

A company is not a university, managers will say. It exists to produce goods and services, not to educate employees.

True.

It also exists next year.

If future performance requires expertise that takes years to build, training is production on a longer clock.

Good organizations already behave this way. They rotate people through roles, pair juniors with mentors, run postmortems, create internal courses, require certifications, and preserve opportunities to lead under supervision. AI raises the value of making those practices explicit because accidental learning from routine production may decline.

Cognitive apprenticeship offers a useful framework. Allan Collins, John Seely Brown, and Susan Newman developed the idea in educational research, and Brown, Collins, and Paul Duguid argued more broadly that knowledge is situated in activity and culture. People learn not only facts but how practitioners see, decide, and act in context.

Traditional apprenticeship makes hidden thinking visible:

1. The expert demonstrates.
2. The novice attempts.
3. The expert coaches.
4. Support fades.
5. The novice takes on harder work.

Generative AI can strengthen every step. It can demonstrate unlimited examples, explain a move at multiple levels, create practice cases, compare attempts, and provide feedback when no mentor is available.

It can also delete step two.

That is the hinge.

The most useful workplace designs may need to distinguish **production mode** from **apprenticeship mode**.

In production mode, use the strongest automation that meets the risk requirements. Do not make a qualified worker manually recreate what the system can safely do faster.

In apprenticeship mode, expose the learner to reasoning production mode hides. Give the junior the problem before the generated solution. Ask for an estimate before the forecast. Require an issue tree before the model expands it. Seed errors into an AI answer and ask the learner to find them. Compare the learner's decision with an expert's and with the system's. Track the mistakes that recur.

Then go back to production.

This will feel artificial because the old workplace did not need a separate training environment.

The old workplace was already one. It just happened to produce billable work at the same time.

Once AI separates production from practice, practice has to become intentional.

The incentives run the other way.

Juniors want to look competent. Seniors want the deliverable by five. Clients want lower prices. Shareholders want margin. If AI makes the work better and faster, everyone in the immediate transaction has a reason to use it.

Nobody in that transaction naturally pays for the novice to be slower today so the profession has an expert ten years from now.

That is why succession has to become visible as a system property.

Licensing requirements can preserve competencies. Firms can promote on demonstrated judgment rather than time served. High-consequence clients can require named responsible professionals with evidence of competence. Insurers may eventually care about training and oversight. Industry groups can share simulated cases and benchmarks for human-plus-AI proficiency.

The mechanism will vary by field. The accounting problem does not: somebody has to pay to manufacture future expertise.

There is a stronger objection.

Perhaps we will need far fewer human experts because machines themselves will become the senior layer.

If AI eventually handles routine work, exceptions, verification, and system redesign better than humans, preserving a large human pipeline may be economically unnecessary. Training people to perform obsolete cognitive labor could become as peculiar as training elevator operators after automatic controls became reliable.

That possibility deserves to be taken seriously.

The answer depends on what roles humans retain.

If society is willing to assign full authority to machine systems in a domain, human succession can shrink dramatically. Some skills may become historical crafts.

If humans remain legally, ethically, or practically responsible for consequential decisions, shrinking the pipeline while preserving the responsibility is incoherent.

You cannot insist on human accountability and stop producing accountable humans.

And transitions matter. A hospital cannot staff itself on a forecast that autonomous medicine will be solved in fifteen years. A government cannot abandon procurement expertise because future agents might manage vendors. A court cannot stop training lawyers while humans remain officers of the court.

For slow-to-build capabilities, expertise has option value.

Reduce the pipeline slowly enough that reality can prove the capability unnecessary before the capability disappears.

That is not technological pessimism. It is refusing an irreversible bet on a forecast.

Software deploys faster than expertise grows back.

A model can be installed in months. A senior clinician takes years. An experienced judge takes a career. An engineer who understands a complex industrial system may carry decades of incident history that was never fully written down.

That asymmetry should matter.

So should access.

Entry-level work is how people enter institutions, learn norms, build networks, and acquire tacit knowledge no manual contains. Remove the bottom of the hierarchy and the institution can become more closed.

Who gets the remaining junior positions?

Often the people who arrive with stronger credentials, better networks, or access to private preparation. The inefficient old junior job sometimes functioned as a paid bridge into expertise. If employers expect applicants to arrive already capable, training costs migrate backward onto families and schools.

AI can push the other way too.

Cheap tutoring and simulation can let people practice law-like reasoning, coding, finance, languages, and science before receiving a formal opportunity. The same technology that removes junior work can widen preparation for the work that remains.

Whether entry widens or narrows will depend partly on what institutions recognize. If employers reward demonstrated capability, cheap tutoring can open doors. If uncertainty makes them demand still more prestigious credentials, it can close them.

The technology does not settle that question for us.

The junior problem is therefore larger than corporate training. It is about how a society manufactures expertise when production no longer requires beginners in the old quantities.

For centuries, learning and labor were tangled together.

AI is beginning to pull them apart.

Good. Much of the labor deserved to go.

But experience was never a renewable resource merely because companies could buy it on the market.

If we remove the work that used to make experts, we need to know what will make them instead.
