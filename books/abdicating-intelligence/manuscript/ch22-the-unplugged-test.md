# The Unplugged Test

The most revealing moment in a technology's life may be the moment it is removed.

A power outage shows which building systems were truly independent. A lost phone shows which numbers were never learned. A network failure shows which business processes were cloud processes wearing local names.

Removing AI will reveal what kind of capability its users actually possess.

This does not mean we should evaluate people only without tools.

A carpenter without tools is not a purer carpenter. A scientist without instruments is not a better scientist. A programmer without documentation is not demonstrating the skill the job normally requires.

Combined capability is real capability.

The unplugged test serves a narrower purpose.

It measures the reserve.

What can the person or institution still do when the dominant cognitive layer is unavailable, untrusted, or inappropriate?

The answer matters only for functions where reserve matters.

If an image generator is down, a marketing team can wait. If an automated medication system produces contradictory information during an emergency, clinicians need a different path.

The first virtue of the unplugged test is honesty.

Organizations are prone to policy fictions.

A document says humans retain expertise. The workflow says humans always use the model. A manager says staff can take over. Nobody has taken over in three years.

A test turns belief into evidence.

The exercise can be small.

Give a team a case and withhold generated synthesis. Ask them to locate primary sources. Remove one decision-support feature in simulation. Ask a senior employee to explain the process from records. Have a junior solve a neighboring problem independently after months of assisted work.

The point is not to catch people failing.

It is to discover what the actual system has become.

If performance remains strong, the organization has evidence that reserve is healthy.

If performance is weak, several conclusions are possible.

Perhaps training is insufficient.

Perhaps the interface has removed too much active engagement.

Perhaps the skill truly no longer needs to be maintained and policy should stop pretending otherwise.

All three are useful findings.

This is why the unplugged test should not become a moral exam.

Failure may be the correct result if automation has made the old capability unnecessary.

Suppose accountants no longer perform a particular manual reconciliation because software has made the operation reliably automatic and independent controls catch errors. Testing the old manual procedure may reveal poor skill and no meaningful risk.

Retire the test.

The purpose is not heritage preservation.

It is identifying gaps between responsibilities and capabilities.

The second virtue is measuring transfer.

In education and training, assisted performance can look excellent while independent performance remains uncertain. Periodic removal of assistance reveals whether the learner has built representations that travel beyond the specific interaction.

The test should not merely repeat the same question without AI. Transfer means changing the surface while preserving the underlying concept.

Can the learner solve a related problem?

Can the programmer debug code they did not generate?

Can the analyst identify a flawed assumption in a new market?

Can the clinician recognize a pattern when the decision-support interface is masked?

This is more informative than asking people to reproduce memorized procedures.

The third virtue is failure discovery.

When AI is removed, hidden dependencies appear.

Perhaps the team cannot find source documents because everyone relied on conversational retrieval. Perhaps data exports are incomplete. Perhaps no one knows the escalation contact because the agent handled routing. Perhaps the supposedly independent backup model uses the same identity service and fails at the same time.

The exercise maps cognitive infrastructure.

Cybersecurity teams call related exercises tabletop exercises: participants walk through a hypothetical incident to expose assumptions before a real one forces the issue. Knowledge organizations can conduct cognitive table-tops.

“The model provider is unavailable for forty-eight hours. What stops?”

“The system's recommendations are suspected of a systematic error. How do we identify affected decisions?”

“A regulation changes tomorrow. Who can modify the decision logic and validate the change?”

“The model's memory store is corrupted. Which records remain authoritative?”

“The expert who built the workflow has left. Can another team reconstruct why the controls exist?”

These questions convert abstract AI risk into operational knowledge.

They also protect organizations from overbuilding reserve.

A drill may show that a simple fallback is enough. Perhaps the critical function can operate at twenty percent capacity with deterministic rules. Perhaps users can switch providers in hours. Perhaps most activity can safely pause.

Resilience becomes cheaper when designed around minimum viable cognition.

This phrase sounds strange, but the concept is practical.

What is the smallest set of cognitive functions the system must preserve to remain safe and legitimate?

Not productive at full speed.

Safe and legitimate.

For a bank, that may include access to balances, transaction integrity, fraud containment, and customer redress. For a hospital, patient identity, medication safety, emergency protocols, and clinical authority. For a government agency, authoritative records, legal rules, appeal, and continuity for urgent cases.

AI can sit above that core and deliver extraordinary service.

The core should not vanish merely because the service is good.

The unplugged test has a personal version that is less formal.

Every so often, do a valued cognitive task before asking the model.

Write the paragraph first if writing is how you discover your view.

Estimate the answer before checking.

Navigate part of a familiar city without turn-by-turn directions if orientation matters to you.

Read the original source before the summary when the subject matters.

Try to remember before searching when you want the memory to remain available.

These are not universal commandments.

They are self-tests.

The result tells you whether the partnership with the tool is changing a capability you care about.

People may discover they are better with AI and still strong without it.

Excellent.

They may discover they are weaker without it and do not care.

Also fine.

They may discover that a capability central to their profession or identity has faded more than intended.

Now they can choose.

Choice is the point.

Abdication happens quietly when the choice disappears before anyone notices it was being made.

The unplugged test makes the choice visible.

There is a danger that institutions use such tests punitively, especially against junior workers. A company might automate routine work, provide little training, then blame employees for poor unassisted performance. That would invert responsibility.

If the organization designs the learning environment, it shares responsibility for the capability produced.

Tests should diagnose systems, not merely rank individuals.

When a whole cohort performs poorly on the same reserve skill, ask what work stopped teaching.

This is similar to safety culture after incidents. Blaming the last operator can prevent learning about the architecture that made error likely.

Cognitive reserve is an organizational outcome.

The same applies to schools. If students use AI under rules that encourage completion, an exam showing weak transfer is information about pedagogy, not proof of moral failure.

Redesign the assistance.

The unplugged test should also evolve as models improve.

Some capabilities will cease to deserve reserve. Others will become more important.

As machines get better at producing first drafts, human value may shift toward objective setting, source judgment, interpersonal trust, and governance. Test those.

As agents become better at action, humans may need less procedural memory and more understanding of authorization boundaries and failure containment. Test that.

The reserve should track the residual responsibility.

This leads to a simple rule.

Never require a human to be accountable for a capability you refuse to train or test.

If the person is only ceremonial, remove the ceremony and redesign accountability honestly.

If the person truly matters, invest in making them capable.

The unplugged test is one way to find out which world you are in.

It is a moment of artificial scarcity in an age of abundant intelligence.

For a short time, the answer is not instantly available.

What remains is revealing.
