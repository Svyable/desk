# Useful Friction

The smoothest possible interface is not always the best one.

This sounds heretical in software because decades of good design have taught us to remove unnecessary steps. Make the button obvious. remember the preference. fill the form automatically. eliminate the confirmation that nobody reads. reduce time to value.

Most friction deserves to die.

Some friction is where judgment enters.

A surgeon pauses before an operation to confirm patient, procedure, and site. A software deployment may require a second approval before reaching production. A bank may delay a large transfer. A cockpit checklist forces attention through steps that skilled pilots already know.

The delay is deliberate because the cost of an unexamined error exceeds the cost of interruption.

AI will make it possible to remove cognitive friction from almost every knowledge task.

The system can infer intent, retrieve context, draft the output, choose tools, and execute. It can make thought feel like autocomplete for life.

The question is where smoothness becomes slippage.

Useful friction has three jobs.

It can protect consequence.

It can produce learning.

It can preserve agency.

The three overlap but should not be confused.

Safety friction slows action before an irreversible or high-impact step. It asks for confirmation, independent evidence, or a second party. The goal is error control, not education.

Learning friction makes the user generate, retrieve, predict, or explain before assistance arrives. The goal is to change future capability.

Agency friction makes the user state or revisit the objective rather than letting the system infer it silently. The goal is to keep important preferences and commitments from disappearing into automation.

A good AI product should know which kind of friction it is adding.

Otherwise caution becomes annoyance and learning becomes patronizing.

Consider the simple act of drafting an email.

For an ordinary scheduling message, full generation is harmless convenience. Asking the user to compose a first version “for learning” would be ridiculous.

For a message ending a partnership, apologizing for harm, disciplining an employee, or making a consequential promise, the system might behave differently. It can still help with wording while ensuring that the user owns the decision being communicated.

“What outcome do you want from this conversation?” may be more valuable than “Here are three polished versions.”

The extra question is agency friction.

It prevents language quality from substituting for intention.

Now consider a student solving an equation.

If the educational objective is mastery, the system can ask for a first step before revealing its own. That is learning friction.

If the user is an engineer checking arithmetic inside a larger model, the same friction wastes time.

Context decides.

This is why universal “AI should always ask questions” design is as bad as universal instant answers.

The interface needs a model of stakes and purpose.

Users should also be able to choose modes.

A writing system might have “draft for me,” “think with me,” and “teach me” modes. The capability underneath can be similar while the interaction contract changes.

In draft mode, optimize completion.

In thinking mode, expose alternatives and ask for choices at consequential branches.

In teaching mode, fade assistance and test transfer.

The names do not matter.

The separation does.

Without it, the most convenient behavior tends to dominate every context because convenience is what users reward immediately.

Useful friction has another enemy: scale.

A confirmation that works once can become meaningless after a thousand repetitions. Security researchers call this habituation in other contexts. People click through warnings they see constantly. A reviewer confronted with endless AI approvals will approve by default.

Friction must be scarce enough to retain signal.

This means systems should automate obvious checks and reserve interruption for uncertainty, consequence, or meaningful choice.

Good friction is selective.

A car does not ask permission before applying antilock brakes. It may require attention before a driver delegates more control. A bank does not ask a human to verify every arithmetic operation. It may pause an unusual transfer.

The same principle should govern cognitive agents.

Let the model act freely inside reversible boundaries.

Interrupt when crossing boundaries that change obligations, money, rights, safety, or public commitments.

This is sometimes described as an autonomy budget. The language is useful because it treats permission separately from capability. A system can be able to do something and still require authorization because the consequence belongs to somebody else.

Agency friction should occur before the consequence, not after.

A user receiving a summary of actions already taken is informed, not in control.

This distinction will matter as agentic systems become more common.

If an AI can negotiate subscriptions, schedule medical visits, purchase travel, respond to customers, deploy code, and file documents, the interface cannot ask for approval at every step. That would destroy the point of agency.

But blanket preauthorization can turn one vague instruction into hundreds of commitments the user did not anticipate.

The system needs boundaries that are legible.

Spend up to this amount.

Do not contact these people.

Draft but do not submit legal filings.

Schedule routine care but escalate anything involving a new diagnosis.

Deploy to staging automatically; production requires approval.

These constraints create friction around categories of consequence rather than individual clicks.

They preserve leverage and agency together.

Learning friction requires a different pattern.

The system should not merely block assistance. It should make the user's effort informative.

Ask for a prediction before showing the answer.

Ask which evidence matters before retrieving it.

Ask the learner to rank competing explanations.

After revealing the model's result, compare rather than congratulate.

Return to the concept later without assistance.

These interactions use AI's strength to create feedback loops.

A static textbook cannot generate a new counterexample tailored to the learner's exact misconception. A model can.

Useful friction can therefore be more personalized than old friction.

Instead of forcing everyone through the same exercise, assistance can adapt to demonstrated competence.

This is important for experts, who should not be made to prove basic knowledge constantly. A system that knows a user has repeatedly demonstrated a skill can reduce friction until performance changes or the context becomes high stakes.

The goal is not struggle.

It is calibrated engagement.

There is a deeper cultural question. Consumer technology has trained people to interpret friction as product failure.

We may need a new category of interface that says, in effect, this step exists because *you* matter here.

A pause before sending a life-changing message is not an obstacle. A prompt asking a doctor to explain why they disagree with a high-confidence model is not necessarily inefficiency. A student being asked to attempt the proof first is not bad automation.

The challenge is earning the user's trust that the friction has purpose.

Explain it.

“This step is required because the action is irreversible.”

“I’m asking for your estimate first so we can compare it with the model and improve calibration.”

“You’ve asked me to help you learn this topic, so I’ll give a hint before the solution.”

Purpose turns friction from arbitrary resistance into part of the contract.

Users should be able to change that contract where safety permits.

Some people want a tutor. Others want a tool. The same person wants different things at different times.

The danger is paternalism disguised as cognitive preservation.

Designers should not force users to perform obsolete tasks for their own supposed good. Adults can choose convenience. Workers should not be subjected to artificial busywork because managers want evidence of “human involvement.”

Useful friction must have a reason tied to consequence, learning, or agency.

If the reason cannot be named, remove the step.

This rule is surprisingly demanding.

Many existing procedures fail it.

Organizations preserve approvals because an old incident occurred, even after controls changed. Schools require handwritten work because cheating technology changed, even when handwriting is irrelevant to the objective. Software asks users to confirm things they cannot meaningfully assess.

AI gives us a chance to rebuild friction rather than merely adding more.

Deterministic checks can remove low-value review. Better provenance can make high-value review faster. Risk models can route attention. Personalized tutoring can target only concepts that need practice.

We can have less friction overall and more friction where it matters.

That is the mature promise.

There is also a social form of useful friction: another person.

Human disagreement is inefficient. Meetings wander. colleagues misunderstand. reviewers ask questions that seem obvious. Yet other minds provide independence no interface can fully simulate if the underlying system is shared.

AI can make individual work so capable that collaboration appears less necessary.

A person can brainstorm alone with a model, draft alone, review alone with another model, and publish without encountering a colleague.

This may be efficient and cognitively narrowing.

Organizations should preserve human contact not as a ritual but where different experience adds genuine independent structure.

A cross-functional review, bedside conversation, code review, editorial discussion, or public hearing can reveal assumptions that a personalized AI interaction smooths away.

Friction between people is one source of institutional intelligence.

Too much becomes bureaucracy.

Too little becomes group isolation mediated by machines.

The right amount will be contested forever.

That is healthy.

The point of useful friction is not to solve cognition with one design rule. It is to reject the idea that zero resistance is a universal objective.

A perfectly smooth road is useful if the destination is already chosen.

Thinking often includes deciding where to go.
