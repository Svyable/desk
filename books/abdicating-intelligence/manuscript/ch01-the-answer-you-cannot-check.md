# The Answer You Cannot Check

The strange thing about the citations was not that they were obscure.

Law is full of obscurity. A lawyer can spend an afternoon following a reference into a reporter nobody outside the profession has heard of, through a procedural history that reads like a family argument conducted over decades. An old case can matter because a later court preserved one sentence. A newer case can look important and turn out to say almost nothing. Citation is partly a system for letting another person retrace the route.

The citations in a 2023 filing in *Mata v. Avianca* had a different problem.

They did not lead anywhere.

The underlying case began conventionally enough. A passenger had sued the airline Avianca. In responding to a motion to dismiss, attorneys for the plaintiff submitted judicial opinions to support their argument. Opposing counsel and the court tried to find the cited cases. Several could not be located. The lawyers eventually disclosed what had happened: one of them had used ChatGPT for legal research.

The system had produced case names, quotations, docket numbers, courts, and surrounding language in the form of legal authority. The form was persuasive enough to travel into a federal filing. The authorities were not real.

When the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York imposed sanctions in June 2023, it did not declare that lawyers could never use artificial intelligence. The court made almost the opposite point. Technological assistance was not the offense. Lawyers have always used databases, search systems, junior associates, treatises, form books, and each other. The problem was the professional gate that failed to close.

A lawyer signed his name beneath work he had not verified.

The episode became famous because it looked absurd. A chatbot inventing cases is the sort of mistake that fits neatly into a headline. It invites an easy lesson: do not trust the machine.

That lesson is too small.

The more important question is what happens when the machine improves.

A visibly unreliable tool makes verification feel necessary. A tool that is wrong one time in five trains suspicion quickly. But suppose the system is right ninety-nine times out of a hundred. Suppose it gets the citation, proposition, procedural posture, and pin cite right almost every time. Suppose its language is better than the first-year associate's and its search is broader than the tired partner's. Verification becomes harder to justify in the moment because checking begins to feel like duplicating work that has already been done.

The danger may therefore increase as the system becomes more useful.

This is an old problem in automation. Human-factors researchers have spent decades studying what happens when a person supervises a system that usually performs well. Reliability does not simply remove work. It changes the work. The human may move from active operator to monitor, from producing decisions to inspecting decisions, from maintaining a continuous model of the situation to being called back when something unusual occurs.

The new role sounds easier.

Often it is not.

To verify an answer, you need enough independent structure in your own mind to recognize the possibility that the answer is wrong. The checker must know what kinds of mistakes exist, what evidence ought to be present, which details are load-bearing, and what would count as a reason to stop. If the system performs the very tasks through which that structure was once learned, verification can become ceremonial.

The person still clicks approve.

The approval no longer means what it used to mean.

This is the verification trap at the center of *Abdicating Intelligence*. We imagine that automation lets a machine do the routine work while humans keep judgment. Sometimes it does. But judgment is not an object placed in a vault while the routine work is outsourced. Judgment is partly made from the routine work.

A senior litigator's instinct that a citation feels wrong did not appear at graduation. It came from years of reading cases, getting holdings slightly wrong, watching judges distinguish precedent, seeing how procedural posture changes meaning, and discovering that two apparently similar sentences can do opposite legal work. A physician's discomfort with a diagnosis, a mechanic's suspicion of a sound, a programmer's sense that a clean abstraction is hiding an ugly state transition—these are compressed histories of attention.

The compression is useful precisely because the history is no longer conscious.

We call the result experience.

The easiest economic model of AI treats experience as a stock held by workers while tasks flow around it. Automate some tasks and the worker spends the saved time on more valuable ones. That can happen. There is good evidence from some workplaces that generative systems raise productivity, especially for less experienced workers. A machine can expose a novice to the patterns of stronger performers and shorten the distance between not knowing and competent action.

But experience is also a process. Today's expert is yesterday's novice after thousands of encounters with the work. Change the encounters and you change the expert that eventually emerges.

A profession can therefore become more productive this quarter while quietly consuming the training pipeline that would have produced its senior judgment ten years from now.

The problem is not unique to AI. Industrialization repeatedly changed how skills were learned and which skills survived. Navigation moved from celestial observations and paper charts toward radio aids and satellite positioning. Accounting moved from hand-kept ledgers to spreadsheets and enterprise systems. Spell-checkers removed a certain kind of proofreading burden. Search engines changed what people remember and what they remember how to find.

Most of these changes were enormous improvements.

The fact that a skill atrophies is not by itself an argument to preserve it.

Nobody needs a national campaign to restore the ability to calculate logarithms with tables. A lost skill matters when it is a prerequisite for something we still need: recovery, verification, adaptation, invention, explanation, accountability, or the education of the next person who must understand the system when it behaves differently from its design.

That distinction is more useful than nostalgia.

The lawyers in *Mata* did not need to reproduce the whole history of legal research before submitting a brief. They needed a reliable chain from claim to authority. A search tool could accelerate that chain. An AI system could summarize cases, suggest arguments, locate contrary authority, and perhaps one day outperform a lawyer at all of it. But the institutional act remained attached to a person whose signature told the court: I stand behind this.

A signature is a compressed social technology.

It assigns responsibility without requiring the reader to witness every step that preceded it. Courts work because filings arrive from identifiable parties under rules that attach consequences to misrepresentation. Engineering approvals, medical orders, audited financial statements, building inspections, and scientific papers rely on related mechanisms. The person approving the work does not need to be the only intelligence involved. They do need to possess enough access, competence, and authority for approval to mean something.

Artificial intelligence puts pressure on all three.

Access becomes difficult when the system's internal process is opaque or when the relevant evidence has been compressed into a generated answer. Competence becomes difficult when users practice less of the underlying work. Authority becomes ambiguous when an organization treats the machine's recommendation as practically binding while retaining a human name at the end of the chain.

The institution can preserve the appearance of human responsibility while relocating the effective decision somewhere else.

That is abdication by paperwork.

It is tempting to solve this with a rule: humans must always verify AI outputs.

The rule sounds prudent and is almost useless by itself.

Verify how? Against what? With how much time? Using which source? With what level of expertise? What happens when the verifier disagrees? Is disagreement logged? Does the person have the authority to stop the process? How often are supposedly verified decisions later sampled against reality?

A human in the loop can be a safeguard. It can also be a decorative loop drawn around an automated decision.

Imagine a radiologist reviewing an AI-highlighted image. If the system reliably identifies abnormalities, the radiologist may become faster. But the pattern of attention changes. Findings marked by the tool become salient. Unmarked regions compete for less attention. If the radiologist knows the system is highly accurate, disagreement now carries a psychological burden: the human is not simply reading an image but overruling something statistically stronger than ordinary intuition.

Medical research on automation bias long predates today's generative systems. Decision-support systems can help clinicians, and they can also create omission and commission errors when users fail to act because the automation missed something or act because it recommended something incorrect. Training and accountability help, but the literature does not support the fantasy that expertise makes people immune.

The machine changes the evidence environment in which expertise operates.

That matters because intelligence is partly selective attention. A person cannot inspect every possibility equally. Expertise makes some things stand out. Automation also makes some things stand out. The combined system has a different attentional shape from either member alone.

We should therefore judge human-machine systems as systems, not by placing a benchmark score for the model beside a benchmark score for the person and declaring the larger number the winner.

The real unit is the loop.

What does the human see? What does the machine see? Who acts first? What is hidden? Where can disagreement enter? How is error discovered? What gets practiced? What gets forgotten? What happens when the ordinary case becomes extraordinary?

Those questions are less glamorous than asking whether a model passes an exam. They are also closer to how institutions fail.

A system can be superhuman on the central task and still make the combined organization brittle. A system can be weaker than the best human and still make the organization far better because it gives ordinary workers access to patterns they would otherwise learn slowly. The direction depends on architecture.

The lawyers in *Mata* had architecture, at least on paper. Courts require citations that can be inspected. Opposing counsel has incentives to inspect them. Judges can demand copies. Attorneys are professionally responsible for what they file. The failure became visible because other parts of the institution still knew what a legal citation was supposed to do.

That last condition deserves attention.

The court could detect fabricated precedent because law remained a community with shared source conventions and people able to use them. The invented cases encountered readers who expected authority to be traceable outside the filing.

Now imagine a future institution in which nearly every first draft, research pass, factual synthesis, and citation check is performed by systems descended from the same underlying models, trained on overlapping corpora, and accessed through a small number of vendors. The organization may still have many humans. It may still have many review stages. Yet the cognitive independence between those stages can shrink.

A machine writes. Another machine checks. A third machine summarizes the check. A human receives a green indicator.

There may be more review events and less independent review.

Redundancy is not the same as independence.

Two calculators implementing the same mistaken formula do not corroborate each other. Two models inheriting the same source error may agree beautifully. Two departments using the same generated summary may believe they reached a consensus when they merely received the same upstream compression.

The more cognition becomes infrastructural, the more provenance matters.

Provenance is not a mystical demand that every user understand every layer. Modern civilization is impossible without trust. I do not verify the metallurgical history of a bridge before crossing it. I do not sequence the ingredients of a prescription. I rely on systems of standards, testing, professional responsibility, redundancy, inspection, and accumulated performance.

Trust is rational when the chain behind it is designed to deserve trust.

The challenge with generated intelligence is that its interface can make trust feel personal and immediate. It speaks in the same window where it explains. It can adapt to the questioner, remember preferences, and present a clean conclusion without the visible mess of institutional production. The very qualities that make it pleasant can hide the difference between an answer and an accountable process.

A confident paragraph has no bolts to inspect.

So we inspect the surrounding system.

For low-stakes work, the surrounding system can be light. Let the machine draft the dinner plan, rewrite the invitation, or suggest a spreadsheet formula. Mistakes are cheap and reversible. The cost of full verification would exceed the harm.

For consequential work, the structure must thicken. Primary sources should remain reachable. High-impact claims should carry traceable evidence. Decisions should record who or what made them and which model or data informed them. People asked to approve should have enough time and competence to do more than acknowledge receipt. Disagreement should have somewhere to go.

Most importantly, the organization must decide which human capabilities it intends to keep.

That question feels odd because capability preservation has usually been treated as an accidental by-product of employment. People learned the work because the work needed to be done. If machines take over the repetitions but humans remain responsible for exceptions, practice can no longer be accidental. It has to be designed.

Pilots train for events they hope never to encounter. Emergency teams rehearse failures that ordinary operations are built to prevent. Cybersecurity groups run exercises against attacks that may not occur. Nuclear plants use simulators because waiting for a real emergency would be an insane training plan.

Knowledge work may need its own version of this logic.

A lawyer whose system performs routine research may still need structured exercises in tracing authority. A doctor supported by diagnosis systems may need cases in which the assistance is withheld or deliberately ambiguous. An engineer using code-generating agents may need to reason through failures without asking the same agent that wrote the system to explain itself.

This will look inefficient on a spreadsheet.

So does a fire drill.

The efficiency calculation changes when the capability being maintained is part of the safety system.

There is a deeper reason to care. Verification is not only error control. It is one of the ways institutions stay connected to reality.

A court citation points outside the brief. A laboratory result points outside the hypothesis. A financial audit points outside management's description of the company. A source link points outside the summary. Each mechanism creates friction between assertion and world.

Generated intelligence can reduce that friction in both senses. It can make checking easier, and it can make unsupported assertions easier to produce.

Which effect dominates is a design choice.

The court in *Mata* still had enough cognitive sovereignty to ask a simple question: where are these cases?

A society that keeps asking equivalent questions can use extraordinary machines without surrendering judgment to them.

A society that loses the ability to ask has a different problem.

The answer may be excellent.

Nobody will know.
