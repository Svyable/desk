# The Answer You Cannot Check

The strange thing about the citations was not that they were obscure. Law is full of obscure citations. A lawyer can spend an afternoon following a reference into a reporter nobody outside the profession has heard of, through a procedural history that reads like a family argument conducted over decades. Citation is partly a system for letting another person retrace the route.

The citations in a 2023 filing in *Mata v. Avianca* had a different problem.

They did not lead anywhere.

The underlying case was ordinary enough. A passenger sued the airline Avianca. In opposing a motion to dismiss, attorneys for the plaintiff submitted judicial opinions meant to support their argument. Opposing counsel and the court tried to locate several of the cited cases and could not. The lawyers eventually disclosed that one of them had used ChatGPT for legal research.

The system had supplied names of cases, quotations, docket numbers, courts, and prose shaped like legal authority. The form was convincing enough to travel into a federal filing. The authorities were not real.

When the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York imposed sanctions in June 2023, it did not treat technological assistance as the offense. Lawyers have always used search tools, databases, treatises, junior associates, form books, and each other. The failure was simpler: attorneys submitted supposed judicial opinions without doing the work required to establish that the opinions existed and said what the brief claimed they said.

A signature remained at the bottom of the filing after meaningful verification had disappeared upstream.

The episode became famous because the error was comic in a way legal malpractice rarely is. A chatbot invented cases. The lesson writes its own headline: do not trust the machine.

That lesson is too easy, and it becomes less useful as the machines improve.

An obviously unreliable tool trains suspicion. If one answer in five is wrong, checking feels natural. Suppose instead that the system is right ninety-nine times out of a hundred. It finds the right authority, summarizes the holding correctly, distinguishes procedural posture, and produces cleaner prose than a tired lawyer working late. The better it gets, the stranger verification begins to feel. Checking starts to look like duplicating work that has already been done well.

Reliability does not eliminate the human problem. It changes it.

Human-factors researchers have studied versions of this for decades. When automation performs well, the human role often shifts from operating to monitoring, from making the decision to reviewing one, from maintaining a continuous model of the situation to being summoned when something unusual occurs. The new role sounds easier because less work appears to remain. In practice, the remaining work can demand more judgment at exactly the moment practice is declining.

Verification is not a button. To check an answer, you need enough independent structure in your own mind to know what kinds of errors are possible, which details carry the conclusion, what evidence ought to exist, and what would justify stopping the process. If the system performs the very tasks through which that structure used to be learned, review can survive as ceremony after the underlying capacity has weakened.

The person still clicks approve. The approval no longer means what it used to mean.

This is the verification trap at the center of *Abdicating Intelligence*. We often imagine automation as a clean division of labor: machines take routine work, humans retain judgment. Sometimes that division works beautifully. But judgment is not a separate asset sitting safely on the balance sheet while the routine work disappears. Much of judgment is made from repeated contact with the routine work.

A senior litigator's sense that a citation feels wrong did not arrive fully formed at graduation. It was built by reading cases, misreading them, watching courts distinguish precedent, seeing procedural posture change the force of a sentence, and learning how legal arguments fail under opposition. A mechanic's suspicion of a sound, a physician's discomfort with a diagnosis, a programmer's sense that a clean abstraction is hiding an ugly state transition: these are compressed histories of attention.

We call the compression experience.

That does not make automation an enemy of expertise. The opposite can be true. In some workplaces, generative systems have produced large productivity gains, especially for less experienced workers. A good tool can expose a novice to patterns that once took years to encounter, make tacit conventions visible, and shorten the distance between confusion and competent action.

The important distinction is between experience as a possession and experience as a process. If expertise is something workers simply have, automating low-value tasks leaves the valuable stock intact. If expertise is also produced by thousands of encounters with those tasks, changing the encounters changes the expert that eventually emerges.

A profession can become more productive this quarter while altering the pipeline that creates senior judgment ten years later.

That possibility is not unique to AI, and it is not automatically bad. Civilization is full of skills we were right to stop practicing. Few people need to restore facility with logarithm tables simply because calculators made it unnecessary. Navigation, accounting, spelling, search, manufacturing, and diagnosis have all shifted work away from older forms of human repetition.

Skill loss matters when the lost skill was carrying something we still need: the ability to recover when the system fails, verify a consequential claim, adapt to an unfamiliar case, explain a decision, train the next person, or refuse an automated conclusion that does not survive contact with reality.

That is a narrower standard than nostalgia, and a harder one.

The lawyers in *Mata* did not need to reenact pre-digital legal research before filing a brief. They needed a trustworthy chain from claim to authority. An AI system could help locate cases, summarize them, surface contrary precedent, draft an argument, and perhaps eventually outperform many lawyers at all of those tasks. None of that removes the institutional meaning of the lawyer's signature.

A signature is a small piece of social infrastructure. It assigns responsibility without forcing the reader to observe every step that preceded it. Courts accept filings from identifiable parties under rules that attach consequences to misrepresentation. Medical orders, engineering approvals, audited financial statements, building inspections, and scientific papers rely on related mechanisms. The person approving the work need not be the only intelligence involved. But approval must still correspond to access, competence, and authority.

AI puts pressure on all three.

Access weakens when the evidence behind a conclusion is compressed into an answer that is easier to read than to inspect. Competence weakens when reviewers stop practicing enough of the underlying work to recognize abnormal cases. Authority becomes ambiguous when the organization treats the machine's recommendation as practically binding while preserving a human name at the end of the chain.

An institution can keep the appearance of human responsibility after relocating the effective decision elsewhere.

That is abdication by paperwork.

The obvious response is to require a human in the loop. That phrase sounds reassuring because it names a person without describing a job.

What does the human actually do? Which sources can they see? How much time do they have? What level of expertise is expected? What happens when they disagree? Does the system record the disagreement? Can the reviewer halt the process, or only acknowledge it? Are supposedly verified decisions ever sampled against later reality?

Without answers to those questions, "human review" can mean anything from independent judgment to a checkbox placed after automation.

Medicine makes the problem intuitive. Imagine a radiologist reviewing an image after an AI system has highlighted areas it considers suspicious. The tool may improve detection and speed. It may also change attention. Marked regions become salient; unmarked regions compete for less of it. When the system is highly accurate, disagreement acquires an additional burden: the radiologist is not merely interpreting an image but overriding a system known to outperform ordinary intuition on some class of cases.

Research on automation bias predates generative AI by decades. Decision support can reduce error, and it can produce new omission and commission errors when people defer to incorrect recommendations or fail to notice what the automation did not flag. Expertise helps. It does not make people immune to the structure of the interface in front of them.

The machine changes the evidence environment in which expertise operates.

This is why benchmark comparisons between "the AI" and "the human" are often less informative than they look. Institutions rarely deploy an isolated model against an isolated person. They build a loop: the machine sees something, the human sees something, one acts first, some evidence is hidden, some disagreement is expensive, some errors are detectable and others are not.

The loop is the real unit.

A system can be superhuman on its central task and still make the organization brittle. A weaker system can make an organization much better by giving ordinary workers access to patterns they would otherwise acquire slowly. The result depends on architecture: what remains visible, what remains practiced, how errors are discovered, and whether a human decision can still be meaningfully independent.

*Mata* is useful because the surrounding institution still had independent structure. Citations were supposed to point outside the brief. Opposing counsel had incentives to inspect them. Judges could demand copies. Attorneys were responsible for what they filed. The fabrication became visible because other participants still knew what a legal citation was supposed to do.

That last condition is more important than the chatbot's mistake.

Imagine an organization in which the first draft, factual synthesis, citation check, and supervisory summary all come from systems built on overlapping models and overlapping source corpora. The organization can add review stages without adding much independent evidence.

A machine writes. Another machine checks. A third summarizes the check. A human receives a green indicator.

There may be more review events and less independent review.

Redundancy is not independence. Two calculators implementing the same mistaken formula do not corroborate each other. Two models inheriting the same source error can agree perfectly. Five departments reading the same generated synthesis can mistake shared upstream compression for institutional consensus.

As cognition becomes infrastructure, provenance becomes part of intelligence.

Provenance does not require every user to understand every layer. Modern life depends on justified trust. We cross bridges without personally auditing their metallurgy and take medicines without rerunning the trials. Trust works because institutions surround difficult claims with standards, testing, records, professional duties, adversarial review, and accumulated evidence.

Generated intelligence can feel different because the interface collapses that institutional mess into conversation. The system explains and recommends in the same voice. It can adapt to the user and present a clean conclusion without showing the production process that would once have signaled uncertainty, disagreement, or provenance.

A confident paragraph has no bolts to inspect.

So the surrounding system matters more, not less.

For low-stakes work, that system can remain light. Let the machine draft the dinner plan, rewrite an invitation, or suggest a spreadsheet formula. Mistakes are cheap and reversible, and exhaustive verification would cost more than the error.

Consequential work needs thicker structure. Primary sources must remain reachable. Important claims need traceable evidence. Decisions should preserve enough information about models, data, and human judgment to reconstruct how the conclusion was reached. Reviewers need time and competence to do something more than acknowledge receipt. Disagreement needs somewhere to go.

And institutions have to decide which capabilities they intend to keep.

Capability preservation used to happen accidentally because people learned by doing the work that still needed doing. If machines absorb the repetition while humans remain responsible for exceptions, practice has to become deliberate. Pilots train for emergencies they hope never to encounter. Nuclear operators use simulators because waiting for the real failure would be an absurd training plan. Cybersecurity teams rehearse attacks that may never occur.

Knowledge work may need an equivalent.

A lawyer whose system performs routine research may still need regular practice tracing propositions back to authority. A physician supported by diagnosis software may need cases in which assistance is withheld or deliberately uncertain. An engineer using coding agents may need to debug failures without asking the same system that produced the code to certify its own explanation.

This will look inefficient in a utilization spreadsheet.

So does a fire drill.

The calculation changes when the capability being maintained is itself part of the safety system.

Verification, then, is not merely error control. It is one of the ways institutions remain connected to a world outside their own assertions. A court citation points outside the brief. A laboratory result points outside the hypothesis. An audit points outside management's account of the company. A source link points outside the summary.

AI can reduce the cost of checking those links. It can also reduce the cost of producing assertions that look as though the checking already happened.

Which effect dominates is not a property of the model alone.

It is a property of the system built around it.

The court in *Mata* could still ask the question that mattered: where are these cases?

The future of human judgment may depend less on whether machines can answer questions than on whether people and institutions retain the capacity to demand that an answer touch reality somewhere outside itself.
