# The Vanishing Repetition

A child learning multiplication can experience the same problem as punishment and as construction.

The surface is identical. Seven times eight. Six times nine. Twelve times seven. Again. Again.

From the outside, repetition is boring. From the inside, something is being changed.

The change is easy to miss because expertise eventually hides its own history. An adult who knows that seven times eight is fifty-six does not feel the hundreds of earlier encounters that made the answer available. A musician no longer consciously identifies every interval. A doctor reading a routine result does not replay the years of examples that shaped what looks normal. A programmer does not narrate every possible state transition before feeling that a function is dangerous.

Automaticity is compressed practice.

This matters for artificial intelligence because the repetitions most attractive to automate are often the repetitions through which automaticity was built.

The business case writes itself. If a junior employee spends four hours producing a draft that a model can produce in four minutes, the ninety-nine percent reduction in first-pass labor appears to be pure gain. The senior reviewer still checks the result. The client receives faster service. The junior can be reassigned to more interesting work.

Sometimes that is exactly what happens.

But the junior's four hours were not necessarily only production. They were also exposure.

They had to decide where to begin, discover what they did not understand, search for an example, make a choice, notice that the choice broke something later, and repair it. The artifact was the visible product. A changed worker was the hidden product.

Workplaces have rarely accounted for that second product because they did not have to. Training was embedded in production. A new analyst learned modeling while building models for work. A new lawyer learned argument while drafting motions that more experienced lawyers corrected. A new engineer learned a codebase by changing it and being told why the change was wrong.

The firm paid for labor and received learning as a side effect.

AI can separate the two.

It can produce the artifact without producing the same learning in the person sitting beside it.

Cognitive psychology gives several reasons to expect the mode of engagement to matter. One is the generation effect. In classic experiments by Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf, people remembered material better when they generated it than when they merely read it. The effect has boundaries and depends on task and material, but the general idea has proved durable: producing information can create different memory than receiving the same information.

Another is retrieval practice. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke showed that testing can improve long-term retention even when repeated study feels more fluent. The act of trying to retrieve information changes later availability.

Again, the point is not that every fact should be recalled without tools. It is that memory and understanding are shaped by what the learner is required to do.

A system that supplies the answer before retrieval removes the retrieval event.

A system that completes the draft before the learner forms a structure removes the generation event.

A system that diagnoses the error before the learner predicts what should have happened removes the comparison between expectation and outcome.

Each removal may save time.

Together they can change what is learned.

This is where the debate about “friction” becomes confused. Some people hear an argument for cognitive effort and imagine a defense of suffering for its own sake. Education has enough useless difficulty already. Bad interfaces, arbitrary memorization, bureaucratic procedures, and poorly designed assignments consume attention without building transferable capability.

The opposite mistake is to treat all effort as waste once a tool can remove it.

Some effort is the mechanism.

Robert Bjork and colleagues use the language of “desirable difficulties” for conditions that can make learning feel harder in the short term while improving later retention or transfer. The phrase is useful if kept narrow. Difficulty is not desirable merely because it is difficult. The task must create processing that helps future performance.

Manu Kapur's work on productive failure makes a related point in problem solving. Under some conditions, learners who struggle with a problem before receiving instruction can develop representations that make later teaching more meaningful. But this is not a universal prescription to abandon guidance. Research on worked examples and cognitive load has long shown that novices can learn efficiently from well-designed demonstrations rather than being forced to rediscover everything from scratch.

Good teaching modulates help.

It does not worship either struggle or answers.

Artificial intelligence can become exceptionally good at modulation if we design for it.

A model can notice that a learner is stuck, give a hint rather than a solution, ask for a prediction, present a worked example on a neighboring problem, then return to the original task. It can adapt the amount of assistance to the learner's state. Human tutors do this naturally when they have time and skill. AI could make the pattern cheap and widespread.

The same model can also erase the learning sequence by producing a finished answer on demand.

The capability is neutral between tutor and substitute.

The product incentives may not be.

Users often judge tools by immediate completion. A coding assistant that makes the developer wrestle with an unfamiliar concept before showing code may be educationally excellent and commercially irritating. A writing tool that asks what the author thinks before offering a paragraph creates useful friction and loses a speed benchmark to the tool that instantly generates ten polished options.

Convenience wins local comparisons.

Learning is delayed and harder to attribute.

This creates a market failure inside cognition. The person choosing the tool now may not bear the full future cost of reduced practice. The manager choosing the workflow may leave before the expertise pipeline thins. The company can hire experienced people from elsewhere for a while, masking the problem. Industry as a whole can underinvest in apprenticeship because every firm hopes another firm will train the next generation.

Economists recognize the structure even if the asset is unusual.

Skills are capital that depreciates and must be reproduced.

Generative systems can accelerate that reproduction or interrupt it.

The key is to stop treating “use AI” as one intervention.

Imagine two junior programmers with access to equally capable models.

The first describes a feature and receives code. The code passes tests. When it fails, the programmer pastes the error and receives a patch. The cycle continues. Output rises quickly.

The second asks the system to review a plan before coding. The model challenges an assumption. The programmer predicts which function should own a state transition. The model generates tests but withholds implementation. The programmer writes a version, compares it with a model-generated alternative, and asks why the approaches differ. When an error appears, the model first requests a hypothesis.

Both are “AI-assisted programmers.”

Their developmental environments are not the same.

The first may still learn a great deal. Reading generated code teaches patterns. Debugging real failures is exposure. The second may also waste time if the tool turns every trivial task into a seminar. The point is not to declare one superior by definition.

It is to measure what changes.

Can the programmer solve a neighboring problem without assistance? Can they detect a plausible but incorrect suggestion? Do they improve at predicting where defects will occur? Does their code review become sharper? Can they explain the system to a colleague?

These are learning outcomes inside work.

Most companies do not track them.

They track tickets.

The mismatch will become more important as models improve because obvious errors currently force engagement. A mediocre coding assistant accidentally teaches by making the user fix it. A superb assistant may remove that requirement. The better the output, the less evidence the user receives about their own underlying capability.

This produces an illusion of competence.

Psychology has studied related effects in many forms. Fluency can be mistaken for learning. Material that feels easy to process can produce confidence without durable retrieval. A learner reading a clear explanation may feel they understand it until asked to reconstruct the argument without the explanation present.

Generative systems are fluency machines.

They can make difficult domains feel navigable before the user has acquired a map.

That feeling is valuable. Lowering intimidation can invite exploration. A person can ask elementary questions without embarrassment, request another explanation, and move quickly across prerequisite gaps. AI can democratize access to intellectual territory that formal education made expensive or socially uncomfortable.

But confidence should be tested against independence occasionally.

Otherwise the user may confuse access with possession.

We already make this distinction with physical tools. Owning a defibrillator is not the same as knowing emergency medicine. Having a translation app is not the same as speaking the language. Access to a piano is not musicianship.

Cognitive tools blur the distinction because they produce the evidence by which competence is usually judged.

The report is good. The code runs. The answer is correct.

What exactly belongs to the person?

That question can sound territorial, as though credit mattered more than results. In many settings it does not matter. If a team reliably produces excellent work with machines, the combined capability is the economically relevant capability.

It matters when the machine is unavailable, wrong, manipulated, conflicted, or asked to operate outside prior experience.

It matters when the person must teach somebody else.

It matters when society assigns accountability to the human.

And it matters when innovation requires generating a new frame rather than selecting among the frames the system makes salient.

Novelty grows from prior structure. A person does not need to know everything, but they need enough internal organization to notice contradictions and combine ideas in ways that are not already prepackaged.

The relationship between practice and expertise is therefore not a romantic defense of craft. It is an engineering constraint on succession.

K. Anders Ericsson's influential work on deliberate practice helped popularize the idea that expert performance is built through sustained, structured practice rather than bestowed whole by talent. The research has sometimes been flattened into a magical number of hours, which is not what serious readers should take from it. Domains differ. Opportunity differs. Coaching matters. Genetics and environment matter. Practice explains more in some activities than others.

The useful residue is simpler.

Expertise has a production function.

People become better through particular kinds of experience.

If an industry removes those experiences, it should identify the replacement.

Aviation uses simulators. Medicine uses supervised training, simulation, rounds, case review, and progressively greater responsibility. The trades use apprenticeships. Chess players study games and solve positions. Musicians practice passages no audience will ever hear in isolation.

Knowledge work often assumed that production itself would provide the repetitions.

That assumption is expiring.

We may need deliberate practice inside occupations that never thought of themselves as practice-based.

A law firm could maintain a bank of cases where juniors research without generative synthesis, then compare their path with an AI-assisted path. A consulting firm could ask analysts to make a forecast before seeing model output and score calibration over time. A software team could rotate “manual debugging” sessions on generated systems. A hospital could periodically mask decision support in simulation.

None of these activities should be universal or constant.

Their purpose is not to slow work.

It is to keep a learning loop alive where ordinary production no longer does.

The cost will be politically difficult inside organizations because the benefit is distributed over time. The senior partner who wants a brief tomorrow does not naturally value the junior lawyer's skill in 2036. The engineering manager measured on current velocity will not be rewarded for preserving debugging intuition that becomes important during a future outage.

Institutions need explicit reasons to invest beyond the quarter.

Safety can be one. Succession can be another. Regulatory requirements may eventually play a role in high-consequence fields. Professional bodies can define competencies that must remain demonstrable even when tools normally provide assistance.

But the strongest reason is strategic.

An organization that only knows how to operate its tools becomes interchangeable with anybody who can buy the same tools.

Human capability is valuable not because it competes with the machine at the machine's strongest routine tasks. It is valuable because it can reinterpret the problem, detect when the objective is wrong, connect local anomalies to institutional context, negotiate responsibility, and act under novel constraints.

Those abilities also need practice.

We should therefore be careful about what we allow to vanish.

The repetition that looks obsolete may be carrying a lesson nobody bothered to name because the old workflow taught it automatically.

Before removing the task, ask what the task was teaching.

If the answer is nothing, automate it without guilt.

If the answer matters, automate the output and rebuild the lesson somewhere else.

That is the bargain.

The machine gets the repetition.

The human should not lose the learning by accident.
