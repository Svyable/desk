# The Student Who Gets It Right

A student can become better at homework and worse at mathematics at the same time.

The sentence sounds contradictory only if homework performance and learning are treated as the same variable.

They are not.

Education has always lived with this distinction. A parent can help too much. A teacher can guide a student through a problem so smoothly that the student cannot solve the next one. A worked example can clarify a method, while copying worked examples can create the appearance of mastery. Open-book success can hide weak recall. Cramming can raise tomorrow's score while doing little for next month.

Generative AI makes the distinction impossible to ignore because assistance can be extraordinarily competent.

In a field experiment involving nearly a thousand high-school mathematics students, researchers led by Hamsa Bastani studied what happened when students had access to GPT-based support during practice. One version provided relatively open access to a model. Another was designed as a more constrained tutor intended to guide students rather than simply supply answers.

The immediate results were encouraging for assisted performance. Students could solve more problems with AI help.

The unassisted results were the important part.

Students who had used the more open GPT interface performed worse than the control group when later tested without AI. The more carefully guardrailed tutor largely avoided that negative effect.

The experiment should not be inflated into a universal law. It took place in a particular subject, age group, curriculum, time period, and generation of models. Future systems will improve. Students will adapt. Teachers will invent new practices. The result does not show that AI harms learning by nature.

It shows something more useful.

Design matters enough to reverse the learning effect.

The same underlying technological capability can function as scaffold or substitute depending on how it enters the task.

That should change the entire argument about AI in education.

The question is no longer whether students should be allowed to “use ChatGPT.” That phrase is too coarse to be educational policy. A model can explain, quiz, hint, translate, critique, simulate, solve, outline, brainstorm, grade, or complete. Those actions place different cognitive demands on the learner.

Schools need to decide what they are trying to build.

If the goal is producing a correct artifact, broad assistance makes sense. If the goal is changing the student so that future performance improves, the sequence of assistance matters.

This seems obvious, yet education routinely confuses its product.

A school produces assignments and grades because assignments and grades are visible. The real product is less visible: memory, conceptual structure, skill, judgment, curiosity, habits of attention, and the ability to learn new things without the original teacher present.

AI can make the visible product almost free.

That creates a measurement crisis.

An essay used to serve two purposes at once. It was a piece of writing and evidence that a student had practiced organizing thought. A homework problem was an answer and evidence that a procedure had been attempted. A take-home project was an artifact and a record of sustained work.

Once a machine can produce the artifact, the artifact becomes weaker evidence about the student.

Schools can respond punitively by trying to detect machine use. That may work in narrow settings and fail as models improve. More importantly, it treats the old artifact as sacred rather than asking what evidence of learning should replace it.

Assessment has to move closer to capability.

Can the student explain the argument orally? Can they solve a related problem under different conditions? Can they identify an error in an AI-generated answer? Can they compare two sources and defend which one is stronger? Can they reconstruct the reasoning after the tool is removed? Can they use the tool to extend an idea rather than merely produce it?

These assessments are harder to scale.

That may be the point.

Mass education was built partly around artifacts that could be collected and graded efficiently. Generative AI attacks the correlation between artifact quality and individual competence. Preserving education will require either new forms of assessment or an acceptance that some credentials mean less.

The temptation will be to move everything into locked rooms without AI.

There is a place for that. Unassisted assessment remains one of the cleanest ways to measure what a person can do without the system. If independence matters, test independence.

But an education designed only around AI-free performance would be as artificial as one designed only around AI-assisted performance. Students will live and work with intelligent tools. They need to learn the combined system too.

The curriculum therefore needs two kinds of competence.

What can you do alone?

What can you do with leverage?

The second is not trivial. Effective tool use requires framing problems, evaluating output, choosing sources, managing context, iterating, and knowing when to stop. A student who understands a domain can often extract more value from AI than a student who does not, because the stronger student can ask better questions and detect subtle errors.

Domain knowledge and tool skill reinforce each other.

The risk is allowing tool skill to masquerade as domain knowledge during the period when domain knowledge is still being built.

This is why timing matters.

A calculator introduced after a student understands number relationships serves a different role from a calculator introduced before those relationships exist. Translation assistance used after a learner attempts a sentence creates a different cognitive event from translation assistance used instead of attempting it. Code generation used to compare implementations differs from code generation used before the learner understands variables and control flow.

No universal age or sequence will fit every subject.

But the principle is general: preserve enough unassisted struggle for the learner to form the representations that make assisted work meaningful.

Then use the machine aggressively.

An AI tutor can do things human schooling has struggled to provide at scale. It can answer the fifth follow-up question without impatience. It can generate ten examples targeted to one misconception. It can shift reading level. It can practice a language at midnight. It can simulate a historical figure while clearly labeling the simulation. It can create quizzes from a student's own errors. It can compare a student's explanation with multiple expert models.

The opportunity is enormous.

The danger is that tutoring and completion share an interface.

The student asks a question. The system can decide whether to answer it.

A human teacher uses social context to make that choice. Is the student stuck after real effort? Are they avoiding work? Is the assignment diagnostic? Does frustration now exceed productive struggle? Is there a misconception worth exposing before the answer appears?

An AI tutor can in principle infer some of this from interaction history. It can also be optimized for satisfaction, engagement, speed, or answer ratings instead of learning.

Those objectives are not the same.

A student may prefer the tutor that gives more help.

Parents may prefer the tutor that raises homework scores.

Schools may prefer the system that reduces support tickets.

The learning objective needs institutional protection because it may lose local preference contests.

This is familiar from nutrition. Immediate taste and long-term health can diverge. We do not solve the problem by banning flavor. We design norms, labels, products, and environments around a broader objective.

Education will need an equivalent literacy around cognitive nutrition.

Some AI interactions are candy. Some are protein. Most are mixed.

The metaphor is imperfect because cognition is more contextual than metabolism. Still, it captures a useful asymmetry: the immediate experience does not reveal the long-term effect.

A polished explanation can feel like understanding.

A difficult retrieval can feel like failure.

Learning often reverses those feelings later.

Teachers have always managed this gap. Generative systems make it scalable and measurable if educational designers choose to care.

Imagine a tutor that records not merely whether the student reached the answer but how assistance changed over time. It can track the number of hints required, confidence before and after, ability to solve neighboring problems, retention after delay, and whether errors recur.

The system can gradually withdraw support as competence rises.

In rehabilitation medicine, this would be unremarkable. Support is useful when it enables movement, harmful when it becomes unnecessary dependence, and adjusted according to function.

Education can treat cognitive assistance the same way.

The concept of fading matters because permanent full support conceals growth.

A student should periodically encounter the task without the tool—not as punishment, but as measurement.

The result can then improve the assistance. If the student cannot transfer, the tutor has learned something about its own teaching.

This is a different philosophy from the answer machine.

The answer machine is judged by whether its answer is good.

The tutor is judged by what the student can do later.

That distinction should become a procurement question for schools.

Does the system optimize completion or learning? What evidence supports the claim? Can teachers control levels of assistance? Can the system expose its intervention history? Does it preserve student privacy? Does it encourage source inspection? Can it be configured to ask before telling?

UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education emphasizes human-centered, age-appropriate, equitable, and pedagogically validated use. Those principles can sound abstract until translated into product behavior.

Pedagogical validation means the system must be evaluated on learning, not merely user satisfaction or answer accuracy.

Equity also changes under this frame.

AI can make high-quality individualized support available to students who never had private tutors. That is one of its strongest democratic possibilities. But if affluent schools use AI as a supplement to expert teaching while under-resourced schools use it as a replacement for teachers, the same technology can widen differences in human support.

The distinction is not whether AI is present.

It is what disappears when AI arrives.

A student with a great teacher and a great AI tutor may receive more attention than any previous generation. A student given a tablet because staffing is expensive may receive fluent answers and less human relationship.

Education is cognitive and social infrastructure.

People learn what knowledge is for by watching other people care about it. They learn what confusion looks like, how disagreement feels, how an adult changes their mind, what a serious question does to a room. A machine can simulate some of these interactions. It cannot automatically replace the institution in which learning acquires social meaning.

That is not an argument from sentimentality.

Motivation, identity, norms, and belonging affect whether people persist through difficulty. The best tutoring system in the world still enters a human environment.

The future classroom therefore should not be organized around defending a pre-AI workflow.

It should be organized around separating production, assistance, and evidence of learning.

Let machines produce when production is the goal.

Let them tutor when learning is the goal.

Test independence when independence matters.

Teach combined capability because combined capability will matter too.

And stop grading an artifact as though nobody else could have helped make it.

The student who gets the right answer has accomplished something.

The educational question is what, exactly, they can do next.
