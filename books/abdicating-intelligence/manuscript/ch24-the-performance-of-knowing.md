# The Performance of Knowing

For most of modern office history, a polished document carried a faint biography inside it.

The biography was never reliable, but it was there.

A careful memo suggested that somebody had read the material closely enough to organize it. A concise technical explanation suggested that somebody had spent time understanding what could be left out. A legal brief with precise citations implied that at least one person had located those authorities. A competent translation implied command of two languages or access to someone who had it. Even a mediocre report contained traces of the labor that made it.

Those traces are disappearing.

A person can now produce the surface features of expertise before acquiring the expertise itself. The sentences can be balanced. The terminology can be correct. The structure can resemble the work of a professional who has written a hundred similar documents. The citations can look right. The tone can be calm in exactly the way people associate with competence.

Sometimes the competence is real. Sometimes it belongs mostly to the system that produced the page.

The reader receives the artifact either way.

This creates an unusual epistemic problem because humans have always used appearance as one of the clues to what deserves trust. We do not independently reproduce every argument we encounter. We infer. We notice whether the speaker seems to understand the subject, whether the explanation is coherent, whether the vocabulary fits, whether the person can answer a follow-up question, whether the work resembles other competent work we have seen.

These cues are not foolish. They are part of how social knowledge works.

They are also becoming cheaper to manufacture.

Psychologists have spent decades studying what they call processing fluency: the subjective ease with which information is perceived or understood. Adam Alter and Daniel Oppenheimer reviewed a large literature showing that fluency can influence judgment across surprisingly different settings. Statements that are easier to process can feel more familiar, more plausible, or more true than harder-to-process versions, depending on context. Related work on the truth effect has shown that repetition and familiarity can alter truth judgments even when the underlying proposition has not become better supported.

No one needed generative AI for this to happen.

AI changes the scale.

It can manufacture fluency on demand.

A hesitant thought can become a confident paragraph. A rough set of notes can become a board-ready strategy. A novice can ask for the vocabulary of a specialist. An executive can receive a one-page explanation of a field they have never studied. A student can make a weak argument sound finished before the argument has survived contact with a serious objection.

That is useful.

It is also a change in what polish means.

We have spent much of the book worrying about whether people will lose internal skills because machines perform tasks for them. There is a second problem hiding beside it: other people may no longer be able to tell who has the skill.

The performance of knowing has become separable from knowing.

This matters in hiring first.

A company once used a writing sample partly to learn whether an applicant could write. The sample was already imperfect evidence. A friend could edit it. A coach could help. A person could spend far more time on the sample than a normal workday would allow. But the artifact still carried some information about the applicant's ability to turn thought into prose.

Now the same artifact may reveal something different: the applicant's ability to direct a language model, choose among drafts, and recognize a good final answer.

That may be exactly the skill the company needs.

The mistake is pretending the test stayed the same.

If the job is AI-mediated writing, banning AI from the sample may test a capability the workplace has already stopped valuing. If the job also requires independent judgment under conditions where the model can fail, allowing unrestricted AI may conceal the capability the organization actually needs to see.

The right assessment depends on the work.

This sounds obvious until institutions inherit old signals long after their meaning has changed.

Degrees do this. Job titles do this. Publication counts do this. Years of experience do this. A credential begins as a rough proxy for some underlying capability. Over time people optimize the proxy, the work changes, and the credential keeps circulating because everybody already understands what it is supposed to mean.

AI accelerates the decay of some proxies.

A beautiful answer may no longer be strong evidence that the person could have found the answer. A correct program may no longer show that the programmer understands the system around it. A persuasive slide deck may say almost nothing about whether the presenter knows which assumptions are fragile.

The artifact can improve while the signal deteriorates.

Education faces the same problem in a sharper form.

For generations, teachers have used student output as evidence of student learning. The essay mattered partly as an essay and partly as a window into the mind that produced it. A solved problem showed more than the final number. It showed, imperfectly, whether the student could move through the reasoning.

Generative systems can close the window while improving the view.

A teacher may receive cleaner prose from a student who understands less. Or cleaner prose from a student who understands more because the system helped them revise. The page alone cannot distinguish the two.

This is why the argument about AI in education cannot be reduced to plagiarism detection.

Detection asks who generated the text.

Learning asks what changed in the learner.

The distinction is larger than school.

Organizations need to know what changed in the worker.

A junior analyst who produces ten excellent AI-assisted market briefs may be learning how to frame evidence, challenge assumptions, and compare alternatives. Or the analyst may be learning how to produce the appearance of completed analysis while the model carries the structure of the task.

Both people look productive in the dashboard.

Only one may be becoming more capable.

The same ambiguity appears at senior levels.

Generative systems are unusually good at helping people speak across fields. A finance executive can ask for a technical explanation. An engineer can translate a design argument into language for a regulator. A physician can draft a plain-language note for a patient. This reduces the tax imposed by specialized language.

That is a genuine gain.

Expertise has often protected itself with needless obscurity. Some professional prose is difficult because the subject is difficult. Some is difficult because insiders learned to treat difficulty as status. AI can strip away part of that distinction.

We should not mourn every signal that dies.

A world in which a smart outsider can participate in a technical conversation sooner may be better than one in which access requires years of learning the local dialect before anyone will listen.

But the outsider still needs some way to know where the explanation stops being reliable.

Fluent systems complicate that boundary because they do not become visibly embarrassed when they cross it.

Humans have a similar problem, of course. The confident person at the table has always been capable of speaking beyond their competence. Entire industries of persuasion existed before machine learning. A smooth explanation has never been proof.

The difference is production cost and consistency.

A person who does not know a field usually leaks the fact under sustained questioning. They become vague. They repeat phrases. They cannot adapt when the example changes. They fail to notice that two parts of their story conflict.

A strong language model can remain articulate through much longer stretches of uncertainty. It can generate another explanation in a different style. It can produce references. It can simulate caution. It can make a weak foundation look stable because each individual sentence is locally plausible.

The human reader therefore needs different signals.

One is provenance.

Where did the important claim come from?

Another is prediction.

What would we expect to observe if this explanation is correct?

Another is sensitivity.

Which assumption, if changed, would alter the conclusion?

Another is transfer.

Can the person apply the idea to a case that was not in the original prompt?

Another is error recognition.

Can the person tell you what would make them stop trusting the result?

These are harder to fake with surface fluency because they require the knowledge to survive movement.

Not impossible. Harder.

A future expert may therefore be recognized less by the ability to produce an answer and more by the ability to interrogate one.

That does not mean every conversation should become an oral examination.

A manager does not need to surprise employees with blackboard proofs before approving a routine spreadsheet. A patient should not have to defend medical knowledge before benefiting from decision support. A citizen should not be required to understand a model's internals before using a public service.

The point is not universal suspicion.

It is that high-stakes institutions need signals matched to the capability they actually depend on.

If a software engineer is responsible for an authentication system, the organization may need to know whether the engineer can reason about failure paths, not whether they can type code without assistance. If a lawyer signs a filing, the relevant capability includes knowing whether the authorities exist and support the proposition, not whether the lawyer wrote every sentence manually. If a researcher presents a result, the community cares whether the methods and inference survive scrutiny, not whether the researcher personally formatted the prose.

AI can make these distinctions cleaner if we let it.

For a long time, institutions bundled capabilities because the bundle was convenient to observe. The person who could produce the artifact was assumed to possess the knowledge behind it. The person who passed the exam was assumed to understand the material. The person who had done the junior work was assumed to be ready for the senior work.

Those assumptions were always approximate.

Now they are becoming visibly unstable.

That gives us a chance to ask what we were actually trying to measure.

The answer may be different in each domain.

A surgeon needs physical skill, pattern recognition, procedural knowledge, and judgment under uncertainty. A scientist needs command of evidence, methods, and the ability to generate or recognize important questions. A programmer needs to understand systems well enough to create, modify, and debug them. A public official needs to understand the reasons behind a decision well enough to defend, revise, or reverse it.

None of these descriptions contains the phrase “must personally produce all intermediate text.”

The machine can take more of the intermediate work.

The institution still has to see the capability that remains.

This is where explanations can become deceptive in a new way.

It is tempting to think that if an AI system explains its recommendation, the user will become appropriately calibrated. Sometimes explanations help. Sometimes they increase confidence without improving performance. Studies of clinicians and other professionals using decision support increasingly find that the effect of explanation depends on the task, the user, and the quality of the explanation. There is no general law that more rationale produces better judgment.

A fluent explanation can itself become another performance of knowing.

The system says why.

The human feels informed.

Neither fact guarantees that the reason is causally faithful, complete, or decision-relevant.

The solution cannot be to demand that machines become less articulate.

Clarity is valuable.

We need to stop treating clarity as the same thing as evidence.

This change will be uncomfortable because modern institutions love artifacts. Artifacts fit databases. They can be scored, ranked, stored, and audited. Understanding is harder to count.

A report can be uploaded.

A person's ability to recognize the one case in a hundred that breaks the report's assumptions is much harder to represent in a dashboard.

Yet that ability may become more valuable as reports become nearly free.

The economic direction is familiar. When one input becomes abundant, scarce complements become more important.

If competent-looking output becomes abundant, credible evidence of judgment becomes a scarce complement.

We can already see institutions improvising around the change. Teachers use live discussion, iterative drafts, source annotations, and in-class work. Employers use collaborative exercises and interviews that ask candidates to explain tradeoffs rather than merely submit finished products. Technical teams use code review, incident response, and design walkthroughs that expose how a person thinks when a system behaves unexpectedly.

None of these methods is perfect. They can privilege confident speakers, consume time, and recreate status biases under a new name.

The design problem remains.

But the target is becoming clearer.

We are moving from an era in which the product was evidence of the producer to one in which the product may be evidence only of access to a capable system.

That is not a tragedy.

It may free people from wasting years proving abilities that machines can now provide cheaply.

The danger is forgetting that some institutions still need to know what the person can do when the answer is disputed, the source is missing, the objective is wrong, or the system fails in a way the template never anticipated.

Expertise after AI will not disappear.

Its visible form will change.

The most valuable expert may be less impressive at first glance because everyone can now produce the impressive glance.

The difference emerges later.

Ask what would change their mind.

Ask where the claim came from.

Change one condition.

Introduce an anomaly.

Remove the familiar tool.

Let two sources disagree.

The expert does not need to know everything.

The expert knows how ignorance behaves inside the domain.

That is a harder performance to counterfeit.

And it points toward the next problem.

If one machine can perform knowing, perhaps we can protect ourselves by asking several.

That helps.

Sometimes enormously.

But only if several machines are actually several sources of judgment.
