# Abdicating Intelligence

*What Happens When Humanity Hands Over Its Ability to Think to Machines*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

There is an easy version of the argument about artificial intelligence.

Machines become capable. People use them. Some jobs change. Productivity rises in some places and disappoints in others. Regulators arrive late. Schools argue about cheating. Professionals discover that a tool which can draft the memo can also invent the case cited inside it. Everybody learns a new vocabulary and pretends the vocabulary settled the question.

The harder version begins after the tool works.

Suppose the answer is good enough that you stop solving the problem yourself. Suppose the directions are good enough that you stop learning the streets. Suppose the draft is good enough that junior employees no longer spend years writing bad first drafts under the eyes of people who know why they are bad. Suppose a diagnostic assistant becomes so reliable that a clinician spends less time building the mental model the assistant is checking. Suppose software can maintain software that no person alive fully understands.

Nothing dramatic has to happen. There need not be a rebellion, a black box in a bunker, or a machine announcing that it has replaced us. The change can arrive as a thousand reasonable decisions to save time.

That is what makes it interesting.

Humans have always extended themselves through tools and other humans. Memory lives in books, institutions, maps, colleagues, rituals, databases, and habits. No serious account of intelligence can pretend that a healthy mind is a sealed skull doing everything alone. The ability to offload thought is part of intelligence.

But there is a difference between extending a capacity and ceasing to possess the capacity required to supervise the extension.

A calculator can free a physicist from arithmetic without making mathematical understanding irrelevant. A checklist can protect a pilot from forgetting a step without teaching the aircraft what to do when the situation no longer matches the checklist. A map can increase range without making orientation useless. The problem is not that cognition leaves the head. The problem is what remains when it does.

This book is about that remainder.

It is also about practice. Human competence is inconveniently physical in time. We become better by doing things, retrieving things, getting things wrong, noticing the shape of the error, and doing them again. Many of the tasks most tempting to automate are the same tasks through which beginners become people capable of handling exceptions. If the first rung of a ladder is removed because machines can stand on it cheaply, the top of the ladder does not remain occupied forever by magic.

There is a second complication. The machines can teach us too.

Evidence from AI-assisted work does not support a simple story of universal deskilling. In some settings less experienced workers improve fastest. In Go, the arrival of superhuman systems was followed by human play that became stronger and, by one measure, more novel. A tool can compress years of exposure, surface patterns a teacher would miss, and let a learner practice against a patient opponent that never needs sleep.

So the question cannot be whether to use AI. That question is already stale.

The question is which arrangements leave people and institutions more capable after the assistance than before it.

We can build systems that merely complete. We can also build systems that expose uncertainty, ask for a prediction before revealing an answer, show competing hypotheses, require a human to identify what evidence would change the conclusion, and preserve the provenance needed to trace a result back to the world. Both systems may produce the same final paragraph. Only one may leave a stronger mind behind.

The distinction matters beyond personal self-improvement. A society delegates cognition collectively. Public agencies depend on vendors. Courts depend on filings. Doctors depend on instruments. Citizens depend on search systems and increasingly on generated summaries that decide which sources deserve to appear at all. Institutions can become cognitively dependent even while every employee feels more productive.

When dependence works, it looks like infrastructure.

When it fails, the missing knowledge becomes visible all at once.

The phrase *abdicating intelligence* is deliberately stronger than *using intelligence*. Abdication is not delegation. A sovereign can delegate a task and retain authority. Abdication gives up the role itself.

That is the line this book tries to locate.

Not in a nostalgic past before machines. Not at the boundary of the human skull. Not in a rule that says people must perform every difficult task manually to prove they still can.

The line appears where nobody remains able to understand enough to challenge the answer, where learning pipelines no longer produce experts, where institutions cannot recover without the vendor they depend on, where public reasoning becomes a set of personalized conclusions without inspectable common sources, or where the cost of convenience is paid later in the form of brittle judgment.

There is no virtue in keeping a skill merely because ancestors suffered to acquire it. Civilization advances by making difficult things easy. Few people should be required to navigate by stars on the way to the grocery store. Few accountants are improved by long division. Nobody needs to defend drudgery as a moral category.

Some friction is useless.

Some friction is the exercise.

The work ahead is telling the difference.
