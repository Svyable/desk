# The Pigeon Camera

The CIA Museum preserves a camera scarcely larger than a matchbox. Its dimensions are public: 4.7 by 2.4 by 2.2 centimeters. The Office of Research and Development made it light enough to strap to a pigeon's breast. A battery-powered motor advanced film and cocked the shutter. The bird supplied propulsion, navigation, altitude control, obstacle avoidance, and concealment.

The concept was excellent on paper. Pigeons were common enough to disappear into the background and flew far below reconnaissance aircraft or satellites, potentially yielding unusually detailed imagery. The camera could begin immediately or after a delay.

Then the bird had to fly where the collectors needed it to fly. CIA's own museum account says the pigeon cameras were ultimately ineffective because birds had trouble crossing the exact target locations required. That sentence is more valuable than a triumphant gadget story. Miniaturization solved the camera. Biology refused to become a flight-control computer.

The mission was not defeated by an impossible sensor. It was defeated by the gap between homing and routing.
