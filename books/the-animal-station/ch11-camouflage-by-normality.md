# Camouflage by Normality

The pigeon camera exploited a kind of stealth that engineers rarely put into equations: statistical normality. A pigeon near a building does not demand explanation. Its concealment comes from belonging there.

This is different from making an object physically difficult to detect. The observer detects the bird perfectly well and discards it as irrelevant. Intelligence tradecraft has always valued such camouflage—objects, behaviors, and people that fit the scene closely enough to escape scrutiny.

Animals are unusually good carriers of this property because cities and landscapes are already populated by them. Yet the advantage is fragile. Add an obvious harness, strange behavior, repeated routing, or an implausible species and normality collapses.

The deeper principle is useful far outside espionage. Detection systems do not merely ask whether something is visible. They rank what deserves attention. A covert system can therefore hide by resembling background rather than by vanishing. The pigeon was a sensor carrier and also a lesson in the economics of human attention.
