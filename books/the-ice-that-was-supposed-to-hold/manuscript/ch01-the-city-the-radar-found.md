# The City the Radar Found

In April 2024, a NASA Gulfstream III crossed the Greenland Ice Sheet carrying a radar meant to look downward through ice and into bedrock. The mission was not searching for a military installation. The scientists were interested in the geometry of the ice sheet: thickness, layering, the interface between frozen mass and the ground beneath it. Then a different kind of geometry appeared in the data.

Straight lines are suspicious under an ice sheet.

The radar had flown over Camp Century, a United States Army base abandoned in 1967 and gradually swallowed by new snowfall. Earlier ice-penetrating radar had already located the debris field as a disturbed band inside the firn. The 2024 flight used a newer instrument and a different viewing geometry. In the processed image, individual structural elements of the old camp became visible with unusual clarity. What had once been Main Street, lateral galleries, rooms, steel arches, timber, pipes, cables and the remains of an underground settlement registered again as engineered order inside a medium that had spent more than half a century deforming it.

NASA published the image in November. The phrase that traveled furthest was “city under the ice.” It was irresistible and not wrong. Camp Century had possessed a hospital, chapel, theater, mess hall, laboratories, dormitories, workshops and a barbershop. It had been supplied by over-snow convoys from Thule Air Base and powered, for a time, by a portable nuclear reactor assembled from components hauled across the Greenland ice. Television crews had visited. The Army had made a film. Walter Cronkite went there. The secret was never simply that a base existed.

The secret was what the Army hoped the ice could become.

Project Iceworm, developed inside the Army’s strategic planning machinery around 1960, imagined a network vastly larger than Camp Century: thousands of kilometers of covered trenches beneath the Greenland Ice Sheet, with intermediate-range ballistic missiles distributed among launch sites whose exact positions could be changed. The scale varied across planning documents, but the mature concept was extraordinary—hundreds of missiles, dozens of launch-control centers, a permanent military population in the thousands, an underground transportation system and a footprint on the order of 130,000 square kilometers. Greenland’s ice was being considered not as empty scenery but as a weapons platform.

That statement needs an immediate boundary. Camp Century itself never became a nuclear missile base. No Iceworm missile force was deployed there. The Army’s proposal never matured into an operational program. Danish permission for nuclear missile deployment under Greenland was never obtained. The documents that make Iceworm legible today are planning records, historical reconstructions and declassified diplomatic material. They establish that serious people studied the idea. They do not turn an unrealized design into a hidden deployed arsenal.

That distinction is the difference between history and folklore.

Camp Century attracts folklore because it contains almost every ingredient required to produce it: nuclear hardware, a hidden military purpose, Arctic isolation, secret planning, international ambiguity, abandoned waste, a facility physically lost inside moving ice and a later scientific rediscovery. It is exactly the kind of subject that becomes less interesting when every dark corner is filled with speculation. The documented story is stranger because the failures are measurable.

The base failed first as architecture.

The Army dug Camp Century in firn, the porous transitional material between snow and glacial ice. Trenches were excavated, arched steel roofs were set across them, and snow was pushed back over the structures. Inside the protected cavities stood conventional prefabricated buildings. The method looked modular, quick and militarily attractive. In photographs, the long corridors possess the unnerving neatness of a subway station built by men who have temporarily convinced themselves the surrounding continent is inert.

It was not inert.

Ice moves even when a human standing on it experiences only stillness. It flows downslope under its own weight. Firn compacts. Stress closes voids. Walls creep inward. Roofs sag. The rate depends on temperature, density, geometry and load, but the direction of the argument was simple: a cavity inside an ice sheet is an invitation for the ice to remove the cavity.

Camp Century’s engineering record gradually became a measurement of that invitation being accepted. Galleries required maintenance. Roofs bowed. Floors and walls shifted. The large reactor trench was especially troublesome. Army engineers did not merely discover that the ice was moving; they quantified the cost of keeping space open against that movement. A missile network whose strategic value depended on immense underground mobility would have multiplied the maintenance problem across distances so large that Camp Century stopped looking like a prototype and started looking like a warning.

The base failed next as a strategic premise.

Iceworm was one entrant in an argument inside the American military about nuclear deterrence. The Army wanted a larger role in long-range strategic missiles at a time when the Air Force’s Minuteman and the Navy’s Polaris were creating competing ways to make nuclear forces hard to destroy. The Greenland scheme offered concealment by scale. If missiles could be moved through a vast network under the ice, a Soviet planner would face a targeting problem. A launcher that had been at one coordinate might later be somewhere else. The ice cap itself became part of the survivability calculation.

But a weapons system is not an architectural drawing. It has to compete against alternatives. Minuteman silos were simpler. Polaris submarines were mobile in a medium that did not squeeze their corridors shut. Iceworm required the United States to build and operate a giant industrial organism inside the territory of an ally whose government had declared a policy against nuclear weapons in Greenland. The plan’s strategic virtues therefore arrived bundled with engineering, organizational and diplomatic liabilities. Project Iceworm was rejected before the Army solved those problems.

Camp Century failed, eventually, as a disposal assumption too.

When the installation was abandoned, much of its infrastructure was left in place. The reactor itself was dismantled and removed, including the fuel and principal reactor components, but liquid and solid wastes remained in the ice. Later inventories reconstructed roughly 9,200 metric tons of physical material, about 200,000 liters of diesel fuel, wastewater and sewage in sumps, chemical contaminants including probable polychlorinated biphenyls, and radiological residues from reactor operations. In the 1960s, leaving material inside the accumulation zone of the ice sheet could be described as burial. Snow would continue to fall. The base would move downward relative to the surface. The ice appeared to provide a vault without a maintenance bill.

In 2016, William Colgan and colleagues turned that assumption around. Using climate-model projections, they argued that a future transition from net snowfall accumulation to net ablation at Camp Century was plausible under a high-emissions scenario. If melting eventually exceeded accumulation, material once treated as permanently entombed could begin moving back toward hydrological contact and, over longer timescales, exposure. The paper became a second rediscovery of Camp Century: not as Cold War trivia but as an environmental liability transmitted into a climate regime the builders had not designed for.

Then the prediction changed.

This part of the story is easy to lose because it refuses the dramatic ending. Denmark and Greenland established the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme in 2017. Researchers installed weather equipment, drilled firn cores, measured temperature and compaction and mapped the buried debris with radar. In 2021, Baptiste Vandecrux, Colgan and colleagues published a site-calibrated firn model using observations from the camp area. Their result was substantially less alarming for this century. They projected that persistent net accumulation would continue through 2100 under the climate scenarios they tested. The upper part of the debris field, about 32 meters below the surface in 2017, was projected to be beneath roughly 58 to 64 meters of firn by 2100. Modeled surface meltwater did not percolate more than about 1.1 meters. Their conclusion was that interaction between surface meltwater and the buried military waste before 2100 is extremely unlikely.

A good book about Camp Century has to preserve both papers.

The 2016 study was not fraudulent because later site data supported a colder, more strongly accumulating local future through 2100. The 2021 study does not prove that burial is permanent. The two analyses used different forcing and levels of local calibration. The newer monitoring program exists partly because the earlier work identified a question worth measuring. Scientific knowledge improved by making the threat less cinematic and more specific.

That correction is central to this book.

Camp Century is commonly described as a story about hubris: the Army thought it could tame the ice, the ice moved, the plan failed. There is truth in that shape, but it is too neat. The Army’s engineers knew ice moved. Camp Century was built partly to find out how much, where and at what cost. They measured closure. They adjusted supports. They learned. The failure was not ignorance of motion. It was the widening difference between an engineering effect that could be managed locally and a weapons architecture that required the effect to remain manageable across an absurdly larger system.

The climate story has the same trap. Saying that the builders assumed the waste would be frozen forever makes a good line. It also compresses several distinct claims: that snowfall would continue to bury the site, that the ice sheet would persist, that contaminants would remain immobile, that the political arrangements governing abandoned American material in Greenland would remain stable and that nobody in the future would care enough to reopen the question. Different assumptions have different lifetimes.

The science produced at Camp Century complicates the morality further.

Between 1963 and 1966, Army-supported drilling penetrated the Greenland Ice Sheet to bedrock, producing a 1,390-meter ice core. Danish and American scientists used oxygen isotopes in the core to reconstruct roughly 100,000 years of climate history. The Camp Century record became one of the early deep records showing that ice could function as a time machine: snow accumulated layer upon layer, preserving isotopic and chemical traces of past atmospheres and temperatures.

At the bottom of the core was something even stranger: subglacial sediment. For decades it was not the star of the archive. Samples moved between institutions and freezers. Later scientists rediscovered and reanalyzed the material using techniques unavailable to the original team. They found preserved plant remains and biomolecules. In 2021, Andrew Christ and colleagues showed that the sediment recorded at least one period in the past million years when this part of northwestern Greenland was ice-free and vegetated. In 2023, a larger team dated an ice-free interval to roughly 416,000 years ago, during Marine Isotope Stage 11. Their modeling implied that ice loss sufficient to expose Camp Century required at least about 1.4 meters of global sea-level-equivalent contribution from Greenland.

The military dug a hole because it wanted to know whether men, reactors, railways and missiles could live inside the ice. The hole eventually answered a different question: whether the ice itself had always been there.

It had not.

That is the deepest collision in the Camp Century story. A project designed around the strategic value of a seemingly permanent physical environment created evidence that the environment was less permanent than later scientists had assumed. The Army wanted stability at the scale of years and decades; paleoclimate scientists recovered instability at the scale of hundreds of thousands of years. The installation vanished downward while its drill core traveled outward through laboratories. The architecture became less accessible as the evidence became more valuable.

There are four clocks here.

The first is the operational clock: weeks of construction, winters of occupancy, reactor shifts, convoys, maintenance schedules.

The second is the engineering clock: years over which the ice deformed tunnels enough to change the economics of keeping them open.

The third is the political clock: decades over which classified plans were declassified, Danish and Greenlandic sovereignty changed in practice, environmental responsibility was renegotiated and a Cold War facility became an international liability question.

The fourth is the geologic clock: tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years recorded in the ice and sediment beneath the camp.

Camp Century keeps becoming a different object because those clocks do not agree.

To the Army in 1959, the ice sheet was terrain. To an Iceworm planner, it was concealment. To a reactor engineer, it was a remote logistics problem. To a glaciologist, it was flowing material. To a paleoclimatologist, it was an archive. To an environmental regulator, it is a debris field. To Greenland, it is also a sovereignty problem left by decisions made when Greenlanders had far less control over what foreign powers built on their land. To a radar instrument in 2024, it is a set of anomalous reflectors embedded in firn.

These descriptions can all be true without being interchangeable.

That is also the epistemic problem this book will keep returning to. Camp Century tempts us to take a fact from one scale and use it as proof at another. The existence of a secret missile study does not prove every rumor about hidden weapons facilities. The public scientific work at the camp does not mean the military purpose was transparent. The failure of Iceworm does not mean sub-ice engineering was foolish in general. The success of the ice core does not sanitize the politics that enabled its recovery. The presence of radioactive residues does not mean an operating reactor remains buried under Greenland. The fact that the base is still physically detectable does not mean it is melting into the ocean now.

A clean narrative would choose one of those statements and build toward it.

The ice refuses.

The 2024 radar image is a good opening because it does not resolve anything. It shows persistence without preservation. The old layout is still there enough for engineered lines to appear, but the camp is not waiting intact for someone to reopen a door. Its tunnels have deformed and collapsed. Snow accumulation has carried the debris deeper relative to the surface. The installation exists as structure, waste, documentary archive, diplomatic residue and scientific inheritance.

The scientists aboard the Gulfstream were looking for the bed of the ice sheet. Camp Century interrupted them on the way down.

It has been doing that to people for sixty-five years.
