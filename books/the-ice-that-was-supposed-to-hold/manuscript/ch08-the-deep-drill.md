# The Deep Drill

On July 2, 1966, the drill did something Camp Century itself was never designed to do.

It reached the bottom.

The hole passed through 4,550 feet of Greenland ice and then continued into roughly twelve feet of material beneath it. For the first time, a continuous deep core had penetrated an ice sheet from surface to bed at this scale. The base above was already in decline. The strategic missile network that had helped make deep-ice engineering interesting had been rejected. The portable reactor was gone. Tunnels were deforming. Yet in one of those tunnels, a scientific instrument reached a boundary more consequential than any wall the Army had built.

The drillers had been working toward it for years.

Deep ice coring in the early 1960s was not a mature industrial service one could order from a catalog. The U.S. Army’s Snow, Ice and Permafrost Research Establishment, later folded into the Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory, had accumulated polar drilling experience during the International Geophysical Year and afterward. Shallow cores could be taken with established tools. A continuous core through more than a kilometer of ice was another proposition.

The hole had to remain open. The drill had to cut without destroying the stratigraphy researchers wanted to measure. The core had to be extracted, logged, handled and preserved. As depth increased, pressure and temperature changed. Ice deformation threatened the borehole. Drilling fluid could contaminate or fracture core. A tool lost more than a kilometer down could terminate years of work.

The first deep-drilling system at Camp Century used thermal technology. Heat melted a ring around a cylindrical core. Thermal drills have an intuitive elegance in ice: use the phase transition instead of fighting the material mechanically. In practice, the method produced its own problems. Meltwater had to be managed. Refreezing could trap equipment. Core quality suffered, particularly after drilling fluid was introduced. Progress was slow.

The team kept changing the machine.

By 1965, CRREL engineers Herbert Ueda and Donald Garfield had adapted a cable-suspended electromechanical drill. Instead of lowering a long drill string from the surface, the cutting assembly descended on a cable carrying both mechanical load and electrical power. Rotating cutters shaved an annulus around the core. Chips were handled with an ethylene-glycol solution inside the drill. Borehole fluid countered pressure and helped preserve hole geometry.

The modified system could recover several meters of good core in a run.

This was not a detail. The entire scientific promise depended on continuity. A pile of ice fragments can answer some chemical questions. A continuous cylinder retains order. Depth becomes time only if the layers remain tied to sequence.

Camp Century gave the drill team something almost as important as ice: infrastructure.

A deep drilling project needs power, heated workspace, machine tools, storage, people who can work through Arctic conditions and a logistics chain able to deliver replacement parts. The military station supplied those things. A freestanding scientific camp of the early 1960s would have had to build much of that system from scratch.

The relationship between military purpose and scientific outcome is clearest here. The Army did not accidentally leave a drill lying around and scientists opportunistically use it. CRREL intentionally wanted deep cores. Glaciology mattered to military operations because the Army wanted to understand ice flow, temperature, accumulation and basal conditions. Scientists also wanted cores because they suspected the ice contained environmental history. The two motives shared hardware.

Neither motive controlled what the core would eventually mean.

The immediate engineering questions were practical. How does a large ice mass deform? Is the base frozen to bedrock or lubricated by water? What are the temperature and density profiles? Could measurements constrain the movement of the ice sheet well enough for construction planning?

The scientific questions widened as the cylinders came up.

Each segment contained snowfall compressed from a past surface. Dust, dissolved ions, trapped gases and isotopic ratios entered the snow under atmospheric conditions that no longer existed. At first glance, most of the core looked almost insultingly plain: translucent ice, bubbles, occasional visible layering. Its importance depended on instruments able to turn small chemical differences into time.

The core’s recovery involved a chain of ordinary handling decisions that later became historically important. Sections were cut, logged and allocated among laboratories. Some remained in American storage. Crucial deep basal material went to Copenhagen, where Danish ice-core researchers built one of the world’s strongest programs around Greenland cores. Over decades, samples were consumed, sublimated, moved, forgotten, rediscovered and reclassified.

The physical archive deteriorated while its scientific value increased.

That is one of the paradoxes of sample science. The ideal archive would remain untouched forever. The useful archive must be measured, and measurement often consumes material. A core drilled once can become more valuable as analytical techniques improve, but only if enough of it survives.

The Camp Century material survived unevenly.

The modern NSF Ice Core Facility holds discontinuous portions of the core, mostly from shallower than the deepest sections. Its inventory notes that many pieces are in poor condition, heavily frosted and changed by decades of sublimation. The deepest debris-rich ice and frozen basal sediment are in Copenhagen at the Niels Bohr Institute. Their survival owes less to a perfectly designed archival system than to a sequence of people deciding, over decades, not to throw odd old material away.

This is an uncomfortable fact about scientific heritage: foresight is often retrospective.

In 1966, no one could know which future instrument would make a few grams of basal sediment precious. Luminescence dating methods would improve. Cosmogenic nuclide techniques would develop. ancient DNA and biomolecular analysis would become possible. Microscopy and geochemistry would gain sensitivity. A sample considered secondary to the ice above it could become the object that changed the interpretation of Greenland’s long-term stability.

The drillers therefore created an option for scientists not yet born into the field.

The same logic applies to many physical archives. Lunar rocks collected by Apollo were deliberately left unopened for future techniques. Ocean cores are stored for decades. Biological specimens collected before genetics existed can later reveal population history. A specimen’s scientific value includes the unknown questions it may answer later.

Camp Century’s basal material nearly failed that test because it was not always treated as the star specimen.

The drilling team’s primary triumph was reaching bed and recovering continuous ice. The frozen sediment underneath was a strange coda. It was logged, divided and stored, but the landmark climate paper of 1969 focused on oxygen isotopes in the ice. The deeper dirt had to wait.

The drill itself had an afterlife too.

After Camp Century, the electromechanical system was moved to Byrd Station in Antarctica. There, CRREL pushed through more than two kilometers of ice. The technique became part of the lineage of modern deep ice-core drilling. Camp Century was therefore not just a source of one famous core; it was a technology proving ground for the act of turning an ice sheet into a vertical archive.

The military metaphor is unavoidable and should be handled carefully. The drill did not conquer the ice. It sampled it.

That difference is why the drill outlasted the tunnels conceptually. A tunnel required the ice to conform to human geometry for years. A borehole required a narrow path to remain open long enough to retrieve evidence. One demanded stability. The other exploited transience.

The scale difference was enormous. A 10-centimeter core could tolerate a level of environmental hostility that a 10-meter trench could not. A research instrument needed a temporary line through the ice. Iceworm needed a permanent network inside it.

Camp Century therefore produced, at the same moment, evidence for two opposite propositions.

The ice was too dynamic to be a cheap strategic building medium.

The ice was stable enough in stratigraphic sequence to preserve tens of thousands of years of climate information.

There is no contradiction once the timescales and geometries are kept separate.

This is one of the book’s recurring disciplines. A material can be unstable for architecture and stable for archives. A glacier can flow meters per year while preserving chemical order along a vertical core. A tunnel can close while an isotope ratio remains interpretable. “Stable” is not a property until the observer names what must remain unchanged.

The bed itself raised an even harder question.

For years, some models of the Greenland Ice Sheet allowed the possibility that portions of the base might be wet or that the ice might ride over a lubricated substrate. Camp Century’s drilling indicated frozen basal conditions at that site. The recovered sediment and debris-rich ice were physical evidence from a boundary that remote geophysics could only infer.

Direct access has special authority because it collapses some ambiguities. A radar reflection may have several causes. A piece of sediment in the core barrel is sediment.

But direct access creates new ambiguities immediately.

Was the sediment deposited by flowing water before the current ice sheet formed? Had it been disturbed by the glacier? Did the drill mix layers? How old was its last exposure to sunlight? Could plant fragments have been carried from elsewhere? Did cosmogenic nuclides record one exposure or several? Which grains belonged to which depositional event?

The object was real. Its history still had to be inferred.

This is why direct evidence should not be romanticized as the end of interpretation. It changes the class of problem.

The drill gave scientists material they could touch. It did not give them a label saying when Greenland was green.

That label took another fifty-seven years to earn.

The moment of breakthrough on July 2, 1966, was probably less dramatic than the date now deserves. Drilling crews had been advancing meter by meter. Equipment needed tending. Core runs had to be logged. The transition at the base became visible through changes in recovered material and drilling response. The team kept going into subglacial material rather than stopping at the ice-bed interface.

Twelve feet is not much depth in a book about a 4,550-foot hole.

It turned out to be the deepest part of the story.

The ice above contained a climate chronology approaching one hundred thousand years. The sediment below contained evidence that reached farther back, through intervals when the entire local premise of an ice sheet disappeared.

The Army had drilled to understand how the ice behaved.

At the bottom, it recovered evidence that the ice had once not been there at all.
