# Leaving It There

Abandonment is an engineering decision made by people who will not be present when its assumptions expire.

Camp Century shut down gradually rather than in one cinematic evacuation. Year-round operations ended. The reactor had already been dismantled and removed. Seasonal scientific work continued. By 1967, the Army was finished with the station as an active base.

Then it left a great deal behind.

This fact is often summarized as negligence and sometimes defended as normal practice for the time. Both descriptions are incomplete unless they specify what was left, what planners believed would happen to it and what alternatives were realistically available.

The first fact is inventory.

William Colgan and colleagues reconstructed Camp Century’s abandoned material in 2016 by combining Army engineering documents, facility maps, historical records and radar observations. Their estimate for solid physical waste was about 9,200 metric tons. Buildings, rail, pipes, cables and other infrastructure remained inside the ice.

They estimated roughly 200,000 liters of diesel fuel remained at the site.

The biological waste was much larger by volume: at least 24 million liters of grey water, including sewage, had been disposed into unlined sumps in the firn.

The authors also identified probable polychlorinated biphenyl contamination based on building materials and equipment of the era. PCBs mattered because they are persistent, bioaccumulative chemicals whose environmental significance can outlast the mechanical infrastructure that carried them.

Radiological waste was present too, but here precision becomes essential.

The PM-2A reactor was not entombed intact. Fuel and major reactor components were removed. Liquid waste associated with reactor operations had been disposed into the ice under a U.S.-Danish agreement that placed limits on annual activity. Historical documentation later examined by the U.S. Government Accountability Office indicates that the cumulative release remained within the agreed limit. Colgan’s reconstruction estimated bulk radioactivity at the time of disposal around 1.2 billion becquerels.

That sounds enormous until compared with activity, isotope, decay and exposure pathway.

A becquerel is one nuclear decay per second. Billions of becquerels can describe very different hazards depending on radionuclide, concentration, physical form and access to people or ecosystems. The Camp Century radiological inventory was not equivalent to spent reactor fuel or a weapons accident. In fact, Colgan’s paper noted that its historical activity was small compared with the radiological material dispersed in the 1968 Thule B-52 accident.

“Radioactive” is an evidence class, not a dose assessment.

The same is true of “toxic.” A PCB sealed inside a capacitor, diesel in a tank and sewage frozen through porous firn have different transport pathways. Lumping them into one category called hazardous waste makes a useful political point and a poor environmental model.

The second fact is geometry.

Camp Century did not remain where it was built relative to the surface. Snow accumulation buried the 1960s horizon. Ice flow moved the site horizontally. Firn compaction and tunnel collapse altered the internal arrangement. Liquid wastes dispersed into sumps did not preserve the geometry of tanks and buildings.

Any future remediation would therefore be closer to excavating a disturbed archaeological site than reopening a base.

The third fact is assumption.

Leaving material in the accumulation zone of an ice sheet made a certain kind of mid-century sense. Camp Century lay in a cold interior region where annual snowfall exceeded surface melt. New snow would accumulate, compact and carry the old base deeper below the surface. The tunnels were already closing. Removing thousands of tons of ordinary structural material would require reversing the entire logistics chain that had brought it inland.

The ice looked like a one-way disposal system.

This was not a belief that ice is chemically magic. It was a belief about surface mass balance and time. If accumulation continued, the debris would become progressively more isolated from surface ecosystems.

The belief contained an unstated horizon.

Forever is rarely written into an engineering calculation. A design life is.

When later critics say the United States and Denmark assumed Camp Century would remain frozen forever, they are translating a practical abandonment premise into its logical limit. The practical premise was that the accumulation regime was stable enough that burial was a safe long-term outcome. Climate change made the unstated horizon visible.

The ethical problem is that abandoned systems do not vanish when their original design life ends.

A waste decision can transfer cost to people who never received the benefit.

Camp Century’s electricity powered American military and scientific operations. Its waste remained in Greenland. The people responsible for the original decisions retired. Agencies reorganized. Greenland gained Home Rule and then Self-Government. Environmental standards changed. PCBs moved from ordinary industrial materials to globally controlled persistent pollutants. Climate projections changed the assumed behavior of the burial medium.

Responsibility aged more slowly than the institution.

This pattern is not unique to the Arctic. Abandoned mines leak acid long after companies dissolve. Cold War uranium sites remain federal cleanup responsibilities. Chemical dumps acquire neighborhoods. Ordnance persists on former ranges. Offshore wells require plugging decades after production.

The infrastructure is short-lived compared with liability.

What makes Camp Century unusual is that the disposal medium itself is dynamic on several timescales.

The ice buries waste vertically while moving it horizontally. Firn warms and densifies. Meltwater can percolate a limited distance downward in warm summers. Over much longer periods, climate can shift the boundary between accumulation and ablation.

A landfill designer usually tries to isolate waste from water.

At Camp Century, isolation depended on the future state of frozen water.

The 2016 paper translated this into a provocative forecast. Under a high-emissions climate simulation, the site could eventually transition from net accumulation to net ablation. Once annual surface loss exceeded gain persistently, the logic of perpetual burial would reverse. Material could begin moving closer to an erosional surface. Meltwater pathways could increase. Contaminants that had been isolated might become transportable.

This result deserved attention because it exposed an inherited assumption.

It also created a new risk of overstatement.

Headlines quickly imagined the base “melting out” near the end of the century. The actual process would not be a trapdoor opening in 2090. Even if surface mass balance turned negative, debris tens of meters deep would take time to approach the surface. Transport would depend on meltwater, firn structure, contaminant mobility and local topography.

Then better site observations arrived and the near-term projection changed substantially.

The revision does not make the abandonment decision wise in retrospect. It makes the environmental question more precise.

This is an important difference because inherited pollution often becomes politically useful before it becomes scientifically quantified. Governments have incentives to emphasize or minimize hazards depending on liability, sovereignty, budgets and diplomatic negotiation. Researchers can be pulled into those incentives even when their models are careful.

Camp Century contains all the ingredients for amplification.

The polluter was a superpower.

The activity was partly secret.

The site used a nuclear reactor.

The host territory had limited political agency at the time.

Climate change is transforming the Arctic.

The waste is literally hidden.

One can build an alarming story from those facts without inventing anything.

That makes calibration more important, not less.

The most significant contaminant may not be the one with the most frightening adjective. The most important pathway may not be surface melt. The relevant timescale may be centuries rather than decades. The environmental cost of excavating thousands of tons from deep firn might exceed the risk of monitored isolation for some future period.

Cleanup is not automatically the low-impact option.

That point is uncomfortable because it sounds like an excuse for inaction. It is instead an engineering requirement. Remediation has its own footprint. Recovering Camp Century material would require heavy transport, excavation through tens of meters of snow and firn, handling of mixed waste, worker exposure, staging and removal to a disposal system elsewhere. Disturbing previously immobile contaminants could create pathways that do not exist now.

A rational cleanup decision compares interventions, not slogans.

This is why monitoring became the near-term answer.

Denmark and Greenland established the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme in 2017. The program measured weather, firn temperature, accumulation, compaction and the depth of the debris. Radar mapped the site. Firn cores constrained physical properties. Radiological studies examined whether reactor-era contamination was producing a radiation-protection concern.

The result is a different relationship to the buried station.

The Army once measured the ice to decide how much infrastructure to put inside it.

The monitoring program measures the ice to decide whether the infrastructure needs to come out.

This inversion turns abandonment into an experiment.

That sounds cleaner than it is. No one should confuse “we can monitor the waste” with “the waste should have been left.” Monitoring is what happens after the historical choice becomes irreversible or expensive to reverse immediately.

It is stewardship after the fact.

The core lesson is not that previous generations were uniquely careless. It is that disposal assumptions are often environment assumptions in disguise.

A barrel is not contained because it is underground.

A chemical is not isolated because it is frozen.

A reactor component is not safe because the reactor is shut down.

A waste site is a coupled system: material, barrier, water, climate, biology, institution and time.

Camp Century simplified that system to one extraordinarily persuasive barrier.

Ice.

For decades, the barrier worked exactly as expected.

That success was the reason anyone could mistake it for permanence.
