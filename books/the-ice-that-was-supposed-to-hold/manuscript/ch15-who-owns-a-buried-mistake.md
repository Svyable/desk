# Who Owns a Buried Mistake?

The waste under Camp Century belongs to someone in at least four different senses, and none of them line up perfectly.

There is ownership as property.

There is responsibility as polluter.

There is sovereignty over the territory containing the material.

There is practical control over whatever might one day have to be done.

Cold War agreements were not written to make those categories agree seventy years later.

The 1951 Defense of Greenland Agreement between Denmark and the United States is the obvious place to begin. The agreement recognized Danish sovereignty over Greenland and created defense areas in which the United States received broad rights to build, operate and maintain military facilities. It also dealt with property. American equipment and material brought into Greenland remained U.S. property, and the agreement contemplated removal or disposal after consultation with Danish authorities.

That language sounds useful until one asks whether a collapsed tunnel is “property.”

A prefabricated building still standing on a foundation is easy. A steel arch crushed into firn is harder. Diesel that has leaked from its tank is not property in the same practical sense. Sewage discharged into an unlined sump is not a movable American asset waiting to be reclaimed. PCBs diffused into surrounding material do not carry a flag.

Law loses grip as matter disperses.

The original agreement also operated inside a political structure that changed profoundly afterward. Denmark held international sovereignty over Greenland when Camp Century was constructed. Greenlanders did not possess the degree of self-government they have today. Home Rule arrived in 1979. The 2009 Self-Government Act recognized the Greenlandic people’s right to self-determination and transferred broad fields of domestic authority while Denmark retained important constitutional responsibilities including much foreign and defense policy.

The buried camp therefore sits beneath a political order that did not exist when the United States left it.

That fact turns a cleanup question into a question about historical agency.

If an American installation created pollution under an agreement negotiated primarily by Washington and Copenhagen, can the cost of future response be shifted onto Greenland simply because the material is physically there?

The intuitive answer is no.

The legal answer depends on the contaminant, the applicable agreement, the meaning of disposal, subsequent diplomatic arrangements, environmental law and the actions eventually required.

The political answer depends on power.

This is where scientific uncertainty becomes diplomatically consequential. As long as the waste remains isolated tens of meters beneath accumulating firn, the immediate cost is monitoring. If evidence someday indicates a credible transport pathway, governments will face decisions about excavation, containment, removal and funding. The size of that future obligation depends on the physical model.

A centimeter of meltwater becomes a budget line only after passing through law.

The 2016 Colgan paper explicitly noted the possibility that climate change could create a “political dispute” over what the authors called knock-on political tensions around responsibility. That phrase was not an ornamental social-science addition to a glaciology paper. The researchers understood that environmental exposure would activate an unresolved institutional question.

The reaction in Greenland proved them right even before exposure occurred.

Greenlandic officials demanded clarity. The concern was not irrational because the historical distribution of benefit and authority was unequal. The United States received strategic access. Denmark managed sovereignty and alliance relations. Greenland inherited the landscape.

Modern monitoring became part of the response.

In 2017, the Danish government and the Government of Greenland agreed to establish the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme, with GEUS conducting field measurements and modeling. The arrangement did not settle ultimate liability. It settled the first practical question: obtain better evidence before making a cleanup decision.

That choice is easy to underestimate.

Environmental controversies often jump directly from discovery to blame. Monitoring can look like delay, especially to communities with a history of outside powers promising later action. Sometimes it is delay. At Camp Century it also produced information that materially changed the near-term hazard estimate.

The 2021 result—that debris will likely remain deeply buried and isolated from surface meltwater through 2100—reduced the urgency without resolving responsibility.

This is a politically awkward but scientifically healthy outcome.

A government can have a duty to own a historical problem even when the best evidence says no immediate cleanup is needed.

Responsibility is not identical to emergency.

This distinction appears across contaminated landscapes. A mine owner may be responsible for a tailings facility that is currently stable. A state may maintain surveillance around a nuclear waste site whose radiation dose is presently negligible. The obligation lies partly in ensuring the condition stays safe and in retaining the capacity to act if it does not.

Camp Century may therefore be best understood as a stewardship problem rather than a cleanup problem—at least for now.

Stewardship requires institutional memory.

The irony is sharp. The physical camp was designed to disappear under accumulation. Good stewardship requires the opposite for its records. Future Greenlandic, Danish and American officials need to know where the debris is, what it contains, which assumptions supported past decisions and how those assumptions have changed.

The safest part of the site may be the information kept above ground.

This is one reason archival work belongs inside environmental management. The 1961 reactor construction report, the camp engineering history, disposal records and diplomatic correspondence are not antiquarian material. They constrain contaminant inventories and possible locations. Every missing document expands the uncertainty range a future remediation plan has to cover.

A classified program can therefore create an environmental information hazard long after the classification rationale disappears.

If the records required to characterize waste remain restricted, destroyed or distributed across inaccessible archives, cleanup becomes more expensive and dangerous. Declassification is sometimes a form of remediation.

Camp Century’s record is unusually available compared with many Cold War sites. Technical reports are public. Historians have reconstructed strategic and diplomatic layers. Climate scientists have mapped the debris. That does not mean every disposal detail is known. It means the uncertainty is bounded enough to design monitoring.

The sovereignty question is similarly better documented than resolved.

The United States has a continued strategic presence in Greenland at Pituffik Space Base. Denmark and Greenland maintain agreements with Washington on defense cooperation. The relationship is active, not an archaeological dispute between vanished states.

That continuity creates leverage and constraint.

A future Camp Century remediation negotiation would occur among governments that still need one another. Greenland’s geographic importance has increased again as Arctic security, missile warning and great-power competition intensify. Denmark remains responsible for parts of defense and foreign policy under the Kingdom structure while Greenlandic leaders insist, increasingly, that decisions about Greenland require Greenlandic participation.

The old base therefore lies beneath a new negotiation about who speaks for the island.

There is a tendency in environmental justice writing to make contamination itself the whole proof of injustice. Camp Century’s stronger case is procedural.

The people living under the contemporary political consequences had less authority over the original strategic decision than the governments that made it.

That asymmetry can remain true even if the environmental hazard is presently low.

It also prevents us from using scientific reassurance as political erasure.

Suppose monitoring continues to show that the waste will remain safely buried through the twenty-first century. That would be good news. It would not make the history of consent more equal.

Suppose future evidence shows a need for cleanup. That would strengthen the practical stakes. It would not retroactively prove that every 1960s environmental decision was reckless by the standards of the time.

Ethical judgment and hazard assessment should inform each other without being forced to the same conclusion.

The four evidence classes help again.

Class 1 includes the treaty text, documented ownership provisions, the existence of Camp Century waste, Greenland’s constitutional changes and the current monitoring program.

Class 2 includes legal interpretations about how those provisions would apply to dispersed contamination under future conditions, because no fully litigated Camp Century cleanup case has settled the question.

Class 3 includes claims that one government has already accepted complete financial liability for every future contaminant pathway unless a specific agreement establishes it.

Class 4 includes broader proposals for Arctic environmental reparations or new trilateral governance structures. Those may be normatively compelling. They are not existing law because they would be useful.

This separation matters because political advocacy often rewards collapsing “ought” into “is.”

A historical wrong does not automatically supply a legal remedy.

A weak legal remedy does not erase the historical wrong.

Camp Century’s physical state makes the gap visible. The waste is neither actively poisoning a community nor gone. The site is a latent obligation.

Latent obligations are difficult for governments because budget systems discount them. No emergency appropriation is needed today. No constituency sees the debris. The political incentive is to let the next administration inherit the monitoring chart.

The ice makes procrastination unusually easy because burial looks like absence.

This is exactly why institutional design matters. Monitoring programs need durable funding, open data and handoff procedures independent of one minister’s interest. Source records need preservation. Thresholds for future action should be specified before an alarming measurement appears.

Otherwise the response will repeat the original problem: make a decision under current assumptions and leave future people to discover the horizon.

A useful Camp Century agreement for the twenty-first century would therefore not need to decide today that excavation must occur. It would define responsibilities conditionally.

If debris approaches a specified depth, who pays for more intensive surveys?

If contaminant concentrations appear in meltwater, who conducts sampling?

If radiological or PCB pathways exceed agreed thresholds, who leads remediation?

Who has veto authority over intrusive work on Greenlandic territory?

Where would recovered material go?

Which records must remain public?

Those questions are less dramatic than “Who owns the waste?” and more likely to matter.

They convert history into a decision tree.

The Army once tried to make Greenland strategically useful by treating the ice sheet as infrastructure.

The governments above Camp Century now have to make the old infrastructure politically manageable by treating uncertainty as something that can be governed.

The most dangerous inheritance may not be diesel or PCBs.

It may be an agreement that begins only after the ice has already changed.
