# The Ice That Was Supposed to Hold

## Camp Century, Project Iceworm, and the Long Afterlife of a Secret City

**Sven Hardy Benson**

Camp Century is usually introduced as a Cold War curiosity: an American city dug into the Greenland Ice Sheet, powered for a time by a portable nuclear reactor, connected to a classified plan called Project Iceworm that imagined hundreds of nuclear missiles moving through tunnels under the ice.

All of that is true except the part people most easily add afterward. The missiles were never deployed there.

That boundary is where this book begins.

Camp Century is unusually valuable because the secret was real enough to study and limited enough to measure. The Army built the base. It tested subsurface construction, rail movement, remote nuclear power and deep drilling. It seriously studied a vast hidden missile network. Ice deformation made the engineering harder as competing strategic systems became better. Iceworm died. Camp Century closed. Much of the physical infrastructure and waste remained.

Then the project began a second life that no planner had designed.

The deep ice core recovered at Camp Century became one of the founding archives of modern paleoclimate science. Decades later, sediment from the bottom of the same borehole yielded plant remains and direct evidence that northwestern Greenland had been ice-free during past warm periods. A 2023 study dated an ice-free depositional environment at the site to 416 ± 38 thousand years ago.

Meanwhile, the abandoned base became a climate problem of its own. A 2016 study warned that future warming could eventually reverse the accumulation regime that had buried the waste. Denmark and Greenland responded with a monitoring program. Site-specific measurements then changed the near-term answer: a 2021 model constrained by local observations found the debris likely to become more deeply buried through 2100 and surface meltwater extremely unlikely to reach it this century.

The correction belongs in the story as prominently as the warning.

The thesis is that Camp Century exposes four incompatible clocks operating on the same physical site: the operational clock of people and machines, the engineering clock of ice deformation, the political clock of secrecy and sovereignty, and the geological clock recorded by ice and sediment. The project repeatedly failed when an institution borrowed confidence from the wrong clock.

The book treats epistemology as part of the subject. A declassified plan proves that officials studied a plan, not that they deployed it. A real reactor does not prove a hidden missile force. A radar anomaly is not an object until its instrument model and geometry support that interpretation. A local basal sediment observation does not directly measure global sea level. A climate-model result remains conditional on forcing, calibration and process representation. Later evidence is allowed to defeat earlier claims.

For controversial material, four evidence classes are used throughout the research record:

1. **Verified / declassified / documented facts.** Authenticated official records, physical measurements, peer-reviewed observations, treaty text, directly documented infrastructure and published primary technical reports.
2. **Credible but incomplete inference or reporting.** Well-sourced historical reconstruction, model-dependent scientific conclusions, indirect instrument interpretations and testimony that lacks complete independent closure.
3. **Disputed or unsupported claims.** Assertions that exceed available provenance or independent confirmation, including the recurring claim that operational nuclear missiles were secretly deployed at Camp Century.
4. **Speculative synthesis / open conjecture.** Testable extensions, analogies to other secret physical programs, long-horizon environmental scenarios and broader institutional lessons.

The classes attach to claims, not to people. One source can contain more than one class.

This manuscript uses only public, lawfully available material. It makes no claim to classified access. Where the public record stops, the prose stops with it.
