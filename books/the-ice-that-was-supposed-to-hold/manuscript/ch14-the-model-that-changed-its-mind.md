# The Model That Changed Its Mind

The first Camp Century climate warning had an irresistible shape.

The United States built a secret-adjacent military base under what it assumed would be permanent Greenland ice. Climate change would reverse the burial. The waste would come back.

A 2016 paper gave that story quantitative form.

William Colgan and colleagues reconstructed the abandoned camp, mapped its likely present position and used regional climate simulations to estimate when the local surface mass balance might switch from net accumulation to net ablation. Under the high-emissions RCP8.5 scenario and the models they used, that transition could occur late in the twenty-first century. Once the annual balance turned persistently negative, the logic of disposal by burial would eventually reverse.

The result was important enough to trigger politics in Denmark and Greenland.

It also turned out to be too warm at the site.

That sentence is the sort climate arguments usually mishandle. One side hears “the model was wrong” and promotes it into a rejection of climate modeling. The other side worries that acknowledging a failed local projection will be used against the larger science and quietly changes the subject.

Camp Century deserves better because the correction is unusually instructive.

In 2017, Denmark and Greenland established the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme, led by the Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland. Researchers went back to the site with a specific list of variables the 2016 analysis had shown mattered: local weather, accumulation, firn temperature, firn density, compaction, meltwater infiltration and the geometry of the debris field.

They installed instruments.

They drilled firn.

They skied radar profiles across the buried camp.

They measured the thing the model was trying to predict.

The 2017 radar work found a debris field roughly two kilometers across with returns spanning a broad depth range. The upper horizon relevant to later modeling lay about thirty-two meters below the contemporary surface. The station had continued moving downward relative to the surface under decades of net snowfall.

That observation mattered because a future environmental pathway has to cross physical distance.

A model predicting more surface melt is not yet a model predicting waste exposure. Meltwater has to penetrate downward through porous firn. Accumulation can carry debris deeper at the same time. Firn warming changes pore structure and refreezing. Compaction changes depth without moving material through an equivalent mass of ice.

The environmental question is therefore not “Will Greenland warm?”

It is “Will enough water or ablation connect the surface to this debris horizon on a specified timescale?”

Baptiste Vandecrux, Colgan and colleagues rebuilt the projection around that question. Their 2021 analysis used outputs from regional and global climate models but adjusted them with local meteorological observations and evaluated a firn model against measurements from Camp Century. They ran three emissions pathways: RCP2.6, RCP4.5 and RCP8.5.

The result contradicted the near-term version of the 2016 concern.

All three scenarios retained persistent net accumulation at Camp Century through 2100. The upper debris horizon, around thirty-two meters deep in 2017, was projected to move deeper—to roughly fifty-eight to sixty-four meters below the surface by 2100 depending on scenario.

Modeled meltwater penetration did increase as the firn warmed.

It did not come close to the waste.

The maximum annual percolation depth in the simulations was about 1.1 meters, and most summers were substantially shallower. The authors concluded that interaction between surface meltwater and the buried debris field before 2100 was extremely unlikely under all three scenarios.

The camp would warm and become more deeply buried at the same time.

This is the sort of result that should make scientists happy and communicators nervous.

The local hazard went down.

The climate did not stop warming.

The model correction came partly from discovering that two climate products used for the site had a warm bias in simulated Camp Century air temperature. Once the researchers calibrated against observations and represented firn processes more accurately, the projected surface regime remained on the accumulation side of the ledger through the century.

This is not a story in which Model A was false and Model B discovered truth.

The 2016 study asked a useful first-order question with the available regional climate information. It identified a potential failure mode and explicitly called for monitoring. The monitoring then supplied local data. The 2021 study used those data to constrain a more detailed model and reached a different near-term answer.

The sequence is science behaving well.

A forecast should become vulnerable as measurements improve.

That sounds obvious until the forecast has political meaning.

The 2016 result implied a future cleanup dispute. Greenlandic officials reasonably wanted clarity about responsibility. News coverage emphasized radioactive waste and a secret Cold War base. The story acquired narrative momentum.

A later paper saying “the debris will probably be deeper in 2100 than it is today” does not travel as far.

Risk information has a ratchet. Dramatic first estimates enter public memory more easily than technical downward revisions.

This is one reason historical controversial science needs a source chronology rather than a pile of citations. A reader who sees only the 2016 paper can accurately report a result that is no longer the strongest site-specific estimate. A reader who sees only the 2021 paper can miss why the monitoring program was created and how large the model change was.

Both belong in the record.

The model revision also sharpens the four evidence classes.

Class 1: In 2017, radar observed the upper debris horizon at about thirty-two meters depth. Instruments measured local temperature, accumulation and firn properties.

Class 2: A calibrated model projects that horizon to roughly fifty-eight to sixty-four meters by 2100 and places maximum meltwater infiltration around 1.1 meters under the scenarios tested. The model is evidence, but conditional evidence.

Class 3: Claims that Camp Century waste will be released into the environment around 2090 are no longer supported by the strongest site-specific modeling and should not be repeated as settled fact.

Class 4: Beyond 2100, or under forcing and ice-sheet states not represented by the modeled scenarios, eventual remobilization remains an open long-term possibility. The ancient basal record shows the site is not geologically guaranteed to remain glaciated forever, but it does not provide a twenty-second-century hazard schedule.

Separating those classes prevents an epistemic accident common in climate discourse: using uncertainty as a lever only in one direction.

Uncertainty does not automatically make risk higher.

Sometimes better data lower the estimate.

That is not a concession to skepticism. It is the purpose of measurement.

The 2021 work produced another useful result almost incidental to the waste question. About a quarter of the modeled compaction in the upper sixty-two meters of firn occurred below twenty meters depth, based on measurements used to constrain the model. That matters for satellite altimetry because a lowering or raising surface can reflect not only ice-mass change but density changes within the firn column. A monitoring program created by an abandoned military base therefore fed back into a broader remote-sensing problem.

Camp Century keeps doing this. Every narrow question opens a second scientific use.

The model correction also offers a cleaner way to talk about falsification.

People sometimes demand that a scientific theory be “falsifiable” as if one failed forecast should kill an entire field. Climate models are not a single proposition. They are computational systems with parameterizations and boundary conditions. A local temperature bias can be diagnosed and corrected without falsifying greenhouse physics. Conversely, getting the global trend right does not excuse a local model that fails at the site where a decision is being made.

The appropriate unit of defeat is the claim.

The 2016 near-term Camp Century exposure pathway lost against later local evidence.

The broader claim that Arctic warming can transform long-lived infrastructure assumptions remains true in many places and is still relevant to Camp Century over longer horizons.

The distinction is exactly the one the Army faced with Iceworm.

Camp Century did not show that construction in ice is impossible.

It showed that the cost and deformation rates made a vast missile network unattractive compared with alternatives.

A good empirical result narrows the claim until it survives contact with scale.

There is something refreshing about a scientific paper that makes its own field less alarming at one site. It demonstrates that the apparatus can move in both directions.

If every new measurement increased the estimated threat, one should suspect a selection mechanism.

At Camp Century, new measurements lowered near-term risk.

They also increased confidence because the answer became local.

The site is still warming. Vandecrux and colleagues projected ten-meter firn temperature rising from about minus twenty-four degrees Celsius in 1966 to approximately minus twenty-one, minus twenty, or minus eighteen-point-six by 2100 depending on scenario. The firn regime is expected to shift toward more percolation. The absence of waste contact through 2100 is not the absence of change.

This is where communication usually wants a verdict and the ice offers a profile.

Safer through 2100.

Warmer through 2100.

Deeper burial through 2100.

More meltwater near the surface through 2100.

No contradiction.

The same environment can move in several directions depending on the variable.

The monitoring program should therefore continue for a reason more interesting than fear: it is a test bed for whether models keep earning the right to be trusted.

A future measurement could find accumulation lower than projected. Meltwater could penetrate more deeply than expected. Radar could revise the geometry of waste. Or decades of observations could keep confirming isolation.

Every outcome teaches something if the claim was specified before the measurement.

That last condition is the one Camp Century’s history keeps imposing.

A secret project is tempting because unknowns can absorb any explanation.

A climate model is tempting because complexity can absorb any discrepancy.

Both become scientifically useful only when they state what would count as failure.

At Camp Century, failure has been unusually generous with data.
