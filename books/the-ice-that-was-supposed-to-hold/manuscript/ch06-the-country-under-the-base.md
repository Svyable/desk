# The Country Under the Base

The maps were almost empty because the politics were not.

American military planning treated Greenland first as geometry. It sat between continents, beneath likely bomber routes, beside the shortest paths to Soviet targets and early-warning lines. Its interior was vast and sparsely populated. For an engineer drawing a sub-ice missile network, the white center of the island could look like unused space.

Nobody lives on an ice sheet because it is a good place for a town. That does not make the ice sheet politically empty.

Greenland in the Camp Century era was emerging from colonial status inside the Kingdom of Denmark. Denmark ended formal colonial classification in 1953 and integrated Greenland constitutionally as a Danish county, but the distribution of authority remained sharply unequal. Home Rule would not arrive until 1979. Self-Government, with stronger recognition of Greenlanders as a people under international law and broader control over domestic affairs, would not arrive until 2009.

The people most directly affected by American military infrastructure therefore inherited decisions made under a constitutional order in which they possessed far less diplomatic power than they do now.

That fact changes the meaning of “permission.”

The United States and Denmark signed the 1951 Defense of Greenland Agreement as NATO allies. The agreement recognized Danish sovereignty and gave the United States extensive rights to construct and operate defense facilities in designated areas. It was designed for a strategic emergency that both governments considered real. Soviet power had expanded in Europe. Long-range aircraft and nuclear weapons had turned the Arctic into a military corridor. Denmark could not defend Greenland alone. The alliance was not fictitious.

But a treaty can be valid between governments and still leave another question unanswered: who counted as the government of the place being used?

Greenlanders were not an equal negotiating party to the 1951 agreement.

That historical asymmetry becomes difficult to ignore at Thule. The establishment of the American air base led to the 1953 relocation of the Inughuit community from Uummannaq to Qaanaaq and surrounding areas. Danish authorities characterized the move in ways later challenged by residents and courts. The relocation’s legal and moral history is complex, but the basic point is not: national-security infrastructure changed Indigenous lives directly.

Camp Century was farther inland and did not displace a settlement. It still belonged to the same strategic system.

The station was supplied from Thule. American access to northwestern Greenland made the inland experiment possible. The agreement architecture that allowed foreign military presence on Greenlandic territory formed the political substrate beneath the physical ice.

This is why the question “Did Denmark know?” is too narrow.

Denmark knew of Camp Century. Danish officials were informed about significant aspects of the project. Danish liaison personnel participated. The PM-2A reactor was not installed without diplomatic discussion. Radioactive liquid discharge limits were negotiated. Scientific collaboration included Danish researchers who would become central to the development of ice-core climate science.

The specific Iceworm missile deployment plan was another matter.

Nikolaj Petersen’s archival reconstruction found that U.S. planners considered the political problem of seeking Danish consent for the missile system, and some American officials believed a path to approval might exist. The proposal never reached a formal request. Project Iceworm was rejected before the United States had to ask Denmark to accept the full system.

That distinction has produced decades of confused language because it sits beside a larger Cold War ambiguity over nuclear weapons in Greenland.

Denmark publicly followed a policy that nuclear weapons would not be stationed on Danish territory in peacetime. Yet American strategic operations at Thule created situations in which that policy was not as clean in practice as its public wording implied. The 1968 crash of a U.S. B-52 carrying nuclear weapons near Thule made the ambiguity impossible to keep abstract. Danish investigations after the Cold War examined what officials had known and tolerated.

It is tempting to take that real history and use it to fill the missing Iceworm step: if Denmark tolerated American nuclear activity at Thule, perhaps Denmark secretly accepted hundreds of buried missiles too.

That is precisely the kind of reasoning this book rejects.

One documented concealment cannot be used as blank permission for another.

The nuclear relationship around Thule increases the plausibility that American and Danish officials could manage sensitive matters through ambiguity. It does not provide evidence that the Iceworm deployment itself was approved. The archival record described by Petersen points the other way: the consent problem remained unresolved because the proposal died first.

The distinction becomes even more important when Greenland is added back into the story.

In the 1950s and early 1960s, the United States often dealt with Greenland through Denmark because that was the constitutional structure recognized internationally. Modern Greenlandic politics has spent decades renegotiating that inheritance. Questions about American basing, environmental liability, resource rights and defense agreements are no longer simply Washington-Copenhagen matters.

Camp Century’s waste afterlife helped expose the change.

When the 2016 paper on possible future remobilization of Camp Century’s abandoned material appeared, the question was not only scientific. If climate change eventually brought contaminants into contact with meltwater, who would be responsible? The United States built and used the installation. Denmark had sovereign authority and participated in the defense relationship. Greenland inherited the land, the environmental risk and far greater political agency than it had when the base was constructed.

A technical projection therefore reopened constitutional history.

This is what old infrastructure does when sovereignty changes around it.

A pipeline can become an international boundary problem. A dam can become the property of a successor state. A military base can become a contamination site governed by treaties written for an entirely different world. Camp Century did not move politically, but the meaning of the institutions above it did.

The 1951 agreement itself contains language that becomes unexpectedly modern when read beside the waste problem. Article XI states that U.S.-provided property in Greenland remains U.S. property and may be removed or disposed of after consultation with Danish authorities. The clause was written for operational property, not for climate-mediated environmental disputes seventy years later. Yet legal attention naturally returns to it because abandoned objects have a way of turning property rules into liability rules.

Who owns a collapsed tunnel?

Who owns contaminated firn?

Who owns diesel that leaked from an American tank into a Danish-administered ice sheet that is now under Greenlandic self-government?

Law prefers objects with boundaries. Ice is not helpful.

The United States did remove the PM-2A reactor. It did not remove all physical infrastructure and waste. Much of the material was left because the accepted engineering assumption was that accumulation would bury it. If burial remains stable, the practical urgency is low. If burial later becomes transport, the legal significance changes.

The 2021 Camp Century firn study reduced the near-term environmental alarm by finding that waste is very unlikely to interact with surface meltwater before 2100 under the modeled scenarios. That result did not erase the political problem. In some ways it improved it by giving governments time to decide what monitoring and responsibility should look like without pretending a crisis was already occurring.

Denmark and Greenland had already created a monitoring program in 2017. The program itself is evidence of institutional change. Instead of a Cold War engineering organization deciding how to use the ice, Danish and Greenlandic authorities fund scientists to watch what remains.

The direction of observation has reversed.

Camp Century was once a sensor pointed into Greenland: the Army measured the ice to learn whether it could support American strategy.

Now Greenland and Denmark point sensors at Camp Century: weather stations, radar and firn measurements are used to learn whether the old American strategy has left a future risk.

That reversal is more than a neat line. It changes who gets to define the relevant uncertainty.

For the Army in 1960, the uncertainty was whether tunnels would close too fast.

For Danish diplomats, it was how much American military freedom was compatible with sovereignty and alliance politics.

For Greenland today, the uncertainty includes what obligations follow from foreign military decisions made before Greenlanders possessed comparable political power.

For climate scientists, it is how accumulation, melt and firn structure will evolve at the site.

For environmental regulators, it is whether contaminants will ever become mobile enough to matter.

Those are different questions attached to the same buried hardware.

The sovereignty story also changes how we should read the word “remote.”

Remote from whom?

Camp Century was remote from Washington, Copenhagen and most of Greenland’s settlements. Thule’s strategic development, however, was not remote from the Inughuit. The phrase “empty Arctic” has repeatedly made northern projects easier to imagine because distance from metropolitan centers is mistaken for absence of human claims.

This mistake is not unique to Greenland. Nuclear testing sites, missile ranges, extraction projects and waste facilities have often been placed in deserts, islands, tundra or ocean areas described as empty because national decision-makers did not live there. The language of emptiness is frequently an administrative technology.

Camp Century gives the pattern a strange physical form. The base was built under a landscape with no permanent settlement at the site. The political claim to the landscape still existed above the tunnels.

A military plan could hide missiles from Soviet reconnaissance more easily than it could hide their sovereignty implications from history.

There is a second kind of political inheritance: scientific ownership.

The Camp Century deep core was a U.S.-Danish enterprise, and the scientific material later traveled across institutions and countries. Ice samples and subglacial sediment passed through American laboratories and the University of Copenhagen’s ice-core community. Later research required decisions about sample custody, subsampling and preservation. A 2024 paper documenting the history and physical characterization of the Camp Century basal material takes unusual care with this chain because the archive is irreplaceable.

That chain of custody is a gentler version of the same question raised by the base itself. Who controls material extracted from Greenland by a foreign military-scientific project under an older political order?

Modern researchers answer through institutional agreements, sample committees and collaboration. The arrangements are technical, but they carry historical weight.

The sediment is not merely dirt. It is evidence removed from Greenland under the logistical shelter of a Cold War base and now used to estimate the vulnerability of the ice sheet under global warming.

This does not make the science illegitimate. It makes provenance part of the science.

The same should be true of the military history.

There is a tendency to tell Camp Century from the American side because the most visually dramatic documents are American: Army films, diagrams, reactor reports, tunnel photographs. An American story asks whether the Army’s idea worked.

A Danish story asks what officials knew and what their nuclear policy meant in practice.

A Greenlandic story asks why foreign powers had such freedom to decide the use of Greenlandic territory and who carries responsibility for what remains.

No one of those stories cancels the others.

The project’s epistemology depends on keeping them all in frame.

This is especially important now because Greenland has again become the subject of intense geopolitical attention. Arctic shipping, critical minerals, missile warning, Russian military activity and Chinese interest have returned strategic language to the island. American officials continue to regard Pituffik as essential. Greenlandic leaders increasingly insist that decisions about Greenland must include Greenland.

That modern politics should not be back-projected carelessly onto the 1960s. It does, however, expose what the old arrangements lacked.

Camp Century was built during a period when geopolitical necessity could move faster than local consent.

Its buried remains have had to wait for the politics above them to catch up.
