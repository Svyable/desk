## Quantitative findings to preserve

Clark's performance section gives the manuscript a much sharper engineering record than the generic phrase `the ice moved`.

### Gross closure

The report says measured trench-deformation rates generally tracked predictions, but heat concentrations raised adjacent snow/firn temperature by several degrees and produced deformation **several times faster than predicted**. Across the camp, Clark gives an average of about **30 inches of gross vertical closure per year**.

That is about **0.76 m/yr** of vertical clearance loss as an average engineering burden, not an isolated spectacular failure.

Modern peer-reviewed reconstruction is consistent with this order of magnitude: Bierman et al. (2024) summarize Camp Century tunnel deformation at roughly **0.1–1.0 m/yr**, citing the Camp Century engineering literature.

Source: https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-18-4029-2024

### Table II: 1962–1963 arch-height changes

Clark reproduces a deformation table based on W. Tobiasson's 1963 measurements. Examples of yearly centerline-height loss include:

- Main Trench, station 0+21: **1.10 ft/yr**
- Trench 5: **1.65 ft/yr**
- Trench 21: **2.05 ft/yr**
- Trench 18: **3.25 ft/yr**
- Trench 6, station 0+50: **2.5 ft/yr**
- Trench 7, several stations: approximately **2.3–3.9 ft/yr**
- Trench 9, several stations: approximately **2.8–3.3 ft/yr**
- Trench 10, several stations: approximately **2.1–3.2 ft/yr**
- Trench 11, several stations: approximately **2.4–3.4 ft/yr**
- Trench 12, sampled stations: approximately **3.2–3.6 ft/yr**

These are not identical structures or thermal environments, so they should not be averaged casually into one universal trench constant. Their value is the spread: location, heat, arch state, geometry and local loading materially changed closure rate.

### Heat dependence

Clark states a striking empirical temperature sensitivity: creep was observed to be about **twice as fast at 14°F as at 2°F**, and about **100 times as fast at 28°F as at −40°F**.

This is an engineering constraint, not a climate slogan. The relevant heat sources were internal: buildings, the PM-2A plant, vehicles, lighting and people. A city under ice warms the material whose long-term strength the city requires.

### PM-2A / wide-trench distress

Trenches 3, 4 and 5, up to roughly **40 ft wide**, contained major PM-2A modules under Wonder Arches. Because construction haste left some arches incompletely or asymmetrically covered, they acquired nonsymmetrical loads and showed severe distress by the end of the first occupancy year.

Clark records that **twice during PM-2A's three-year life**, sections of these arches had to be raised approximately **6 ft** to prevent damage to enclosed buildings and structures.

This is a much stronger sentence than `the reactor trench distorted`. It converts the deformation into maintenance capital.

### Metal arches versus thermal environment

The report notes that trenches retaining metal roof forms often had lower closure rates, but Clark warns against crediting the metal alone: many such trenches also lacked heated buildings. Heat is therefore a major confounder when comparing arch performance.

The Main Trench and heated Trench 20 are useful contrast cases in the report's figures.

## Manuscript claim boundary

The numbers do **not** prove that subsurface ice facilities were impossible. Clark's own conclusion was that such camps were feasible and practicable for some purposes.

They do support a narrower claim: Camp Century's measured maintenance burden was already on the scale of feet per year in some galleries, and internal heat could multiply deformation. Any Iceworm-scale proposal must therefore carry a network-wide excavation, trimming, re-leveling, structural replacement and geodetic-maintenance budget rather than treating tunnel excavation as a one-time capital expense.

The relevant failure mode is not `ice unexpectedly moved`.

It is `the operating system required perpetual reconstruction of the medium it occupied`.
