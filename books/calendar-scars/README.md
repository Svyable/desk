## Thesis

A handful of extreme cosmic-radiation events left abrupt radiocarbon scars in annual tree rings. Those scars do two jobs at once: they extend the history of extreme solar particle storms far beyond the instrument era, and they act as calendar pins capable of turning otherwise floating archaeological chronologies into exact years. *Calendar Scars* follows the strange consequence: some of the sharpest dates in human prehistory now come from moments when the Sun appears to have behaved more violently than anything directly observed by modern instruments.

The argument is deliberately narrower than “the Sun rewrites history.” Verified Miyake events are rare, their exact physical production mechanism remains an active research problem, and a radiocarbon spike is useful as a calendar anchor only when suitable annual-growth material and sequence information survive. The book separates established isotope measurements from candidate events, historical interpretations, and modern-risk extrapolation.

## Reader promise

This is a book about clocks hidden inside damage.

It begins with a Japanese cedar ring whose carbon chemistry changed too fast for the calibration curve that was supposed to explain it. From there the story crosses accelerator mass spectrometers, polar ice, solar-particle physics, missing tree rings, Uyghur construction beams, Viking timber, a Neolithic lakeside village in Greece, and a modern technological system that has never experienced an instrumentally observed particle event on the scale inferred from some of those ancient scars.

## Strongest competing explanation

The apparent revolution may be narrower than the rhetoric around it. Most archaeological contexts do not preserve bark-edged timber sequences that span a known cosmogenic event. Dendrochronology, Bayesian radiocarbon models, stratigraphy, text criticism, and ordinary calibration may remain more important in aggregate. Meanwhile, the radiation events themselves could prove heterogeneous enough that “Miyake event” is a convenient observational category rather than one physical class with one recurrence law.

The book succeeds only if it preserves that possibility while showing why the verified cases are still extraordinary.
