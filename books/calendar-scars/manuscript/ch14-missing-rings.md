# Missing Rings

A perfect tree exists mostly in diagrams.

It grows one clean ring every year. Each boundary is obvious. Every ring goes all the way around the trunk. The carbon fixed inside belongs neatly to that growing season. Nobody cuts through the bark before the dendrochronologist arrives. No fungus stains the cells. No fire wounds the cambium. No drought suppresses growth to invisibility.

Real trees are less cooperative.

That is why the one-year correction to the second Miyake event belongs near the center of the story rather than in a footnote. The correction is an anatomy lesson about exactness.

Trees can miss rings.

Under severe stress, cambial growth may stop locally or nearly entirely. A ring present on one side of a trunk can disappear on another. At harsh treeline sites, one miserable growing season can produce only a few cells. If a narrow core or strip happens to pass through the wrong sector, the year can appear absent.

Trees can also make false rings.

Growth may begin, pause during drought or frost, and restart, producing an anatomical boundary inside one true annual ring. In some species and climates, these intra-annual density fluctuations are common enough that counting without cross-dating is an invitation to error.

Ring width alone does not save you.

Dendrochronology became an exact science by refusing to trust isolated counts. A sequence is cross-dated against others. Narrow and wide years form patterns. Skeleton plotting, statistical correlation, wood anatomy, and regional master chronologies help identify where a ring has been missed or invented by the eye.

The unit of certainty is the replicated chronology, not the heroic individual tree.

This principle is easy to forget when Miyake-event dating is described as “count twenty-eight rings from 993.” Counting is the final act. Establishing that there are exactly twenty-eight annual rings is the craft.

The 2013 correction makes this tangible. The radiocarbon excursion in the Japanese cedar was real. But a missing layer around 956 shifted the calendar labels after that point. Once the ring was recognized, the event’s assignment moved by a year.

Nothing in nuclear physics could fix a ring anatomy error.

Different disciplines catch different mistakes.

This is why the event itself can become a dendrochronological checksum. If a supposedly absolute chronology places the 993 radiocarbon rise in the wrong ring, the discrepancy demands attention. Perhaps the sequence contains a missing ring. Perhaps the reference date convention differs. Perhaps the measurement is contaminated. Perhaps the sample has been mislabeled. The isotope marker does not replace cross-dating, but it can expose errors invisible to ring-width matching.

The checksum metaphor should not be pushed too far. Biological systems are not digital files. A tree can smooth atmospheric carbon through stored reserves. Earlywood in some species may use carbon fixed in the previous year. Leaves and needles can contribute stored carbohydrates. Heartwood formation and secondary metabolism complicate chemical composition. Researchers often isolate alpha-cellulose precisely because it is a relatively stable structural component suitable for radiocarbon work.

The carbon in a ring has a biography too.

That biography matters most at subannual resolution. If earlywood contains some carbon remobilized from previous years, an abrupt atmospheric event can bleed backward or forward within the tree’s tissues. Different species allocate reserves differently. Deciduous trees that leaf out before new photosynthesis may rely more on stored carbon early in the season than evergreen conifers.

A universal “ring equals atmosphere that year” assumption is too simple.

The strongest studies account for species physiology and compare records.

Sampling introduces its own errors. A ring may be less than a millimeter wide. Separating it mechanically without shaving cells from neighboring years requires microscopy and patience. A few percent contamination from the event year into the prior year can make the rise appear gradual. The same contamination in reverse can suppress the peak.

The laboratory can manufacture a false time profile while measuring the right event.

Chemical preparation matters. Wood contains cellulose, lignin, resins, mobile compounds, conservation chemicals, and contamination acquired during burial or storage. Archaeological timber may have been treated decades ago with substances nobody imagined would matter to future isotope analysis.

Pretreatment is therefore not cleaning in the household sense. It is an attempt to isolate the carbon fraction whose relationship to growth is interpretable.

Then comes accelerator measurement, standards, blanks, normalization, and interlaboratory offsets.

One-year chronology sounds simple only because the complexity has been hidden inside protocols.

This is true of every mature measurement. A blood-pressure number hides cuff calibration, arm position, device algorithm, physiology, and operator procedure. GPS coordinates hide relativity, atomic clocks, orbital ephemerides, atmosphere, and signal processing. Exactness is compressed infrastructure.

Miyake dating is young enough that we can still see the infrastructure being assembled.

Global replication studies are part of it. Annual calibration datasets are part of it. Event catalogs with confidence classes are part of it. Wood archives are part of it. Laboratory proficiency and shared standards are part of it.

So is the willingness to publish errors.

Science culture often treats correction as reputational damage. Precision sciences cannot afford that posture. If a missing ring shifts a canonical event by one year, the correct response is not to preserve the original label out of pride. The whole value of annual dating depends on moving the label.

The correction is the product working.

There are other ways chronology can fail more subtly.

A dead tree can stand for years before being used. A beam can be seasoned. A timber can be reused from an older structure. Bark can be stripped, removing outer rings. Sapwood can decay preferentially, leaving heartwood whose last preserved ring predates felling by decades. An archaeological sample can therefore have flawless internal chronology while dating the wrong human action.

This is not a dendrochronology error.

It is an association error.

The distinction matters because exact methods attract blame for interpretations they never made. If a reused Roman beam appears in a medieval wall, the tree date is not “wrong.” The historical inference that the wall was Roman is wrong.

The same logic applies to radiocarbon old-wood problems. Carbon dating can correctly measure the death of long-lived wood while archaeologists mistakenly equate it with the date of burning or construction.

Exactness makes sample biography more important.

Por-Bajin worked well because the beam was structural, bark survived, local timber was available, and archaeological context supported rapid construction. L’Anse aux Meadows worked because metal-tool modification tied the samples to Norse activity and multiple trees converged. Dispilio worked because the aim was to anchor a master chronology, not to pretend every post was felled in the final ring year.

Good applications are designed around what the sample can actually date.

Another failure mode appears in event identification itself.

A radiocarbon sequence has ordinary variation. The eleven-year solar cycle modulates cosmic-ray production. Carbon-cycle processes create additional structure. Measurement uncertainty adds scatter. If researchers search enough years for a spike, some apparent jumps will arise by chance.

This is the multiple-testing problem wearing tree bark.

The 2026 modelling work makes the danger explicit. Weak candidate events can be distorted or mimicked by background solar-cycle variability. A single annual jump is therefore not sufficient evidence for a new Miyake event, especially near the lower amplitude threshold.

The solution is again replication and physics.

Does the feature appear in another tree?

Another region?

Another hemisphere?

Do beryllium-10 or chlorine-36 show compatible production?

Does the shape make sense under carbon-cycle transport?

Is the proposed event large enough to distinguish from natural modulation?

The strongest markers survive this gauntlet.

A field built around rare events also has a publication-bias problem. A dramatic spike is more interesting than a flat sequence. Laboratories may publish candidate excursions while null searches languish. Event catalogs can therefore become enriched for surprising noise unless systematic annual surveys publish the quiet years too.

Quiet years define the background.

Without them, “extreme” has no denominator.

This is why broad annual radiocarbon projects may ultimately matter more than spectacular individual papers. A continuous high-quality series through thousands of years would show both events and non-events, solar cycles and laboratory scatter, regional offsets and transport effects. It would turn event hunting into population science.

The goal is not more Miyake events.

The goal is an honest atmosphere.

Dendrochronology has another form of uncertainty that exact-year radiocarbon can expose: chronology replication across regions. A master tree-ring chronology may be absolutely dated because it overlaps living trees and historical timbers. A floating archaeological sequence may correlate weakly with it but not enough to accept. Researchers can be tempted to force the match because the archaeological expectation is strong.

A cosmogenic marker provides an external test.

If the radiocarbon scar falls where the proposed cross-date predicts, confidence increases. If it falls twenty years away, somebody has a problem.

This is epistemologically powerful because the marker derives from different properties of the wood. Ring-width matching depends on growth response to local or regional environment. The isotope event depends on atmospheric radiocarbon. Shared material, independent signal.

Independence is rarely perfect but it is valuable.

The deepest problem, though, is psychological.

Humans love exact numbers.

A date range of 780–830 advertises uncertainty. A year like 777 conceals its inferential chain. Once the number is printed without its conditions, readers treat it as a stone tablet.

Scientists are not immune. Exact dates can become citation fossils. A later paper repeats “constructed in 777” without noting that the claim refers to a foundation timber felled in summer 777 and interpreted as construction timber. Another repeats the later paper. The uncertainty migrates out of the sentence.

Precision can lose provenance.

The cure is not to burden every sentence with a methods section. It is to preserve the distinction between measured object and historical inference in the key places.

Ring 43 carries the 775 marker.

The terminal ring indicates felling in 777.

Context supports construction in that year.

Those are three claims, not one.

Likewise at L’Anse aux Meadows:

The 993 marker is found in the wood.

Twenty-eight rings lead to the bark edge.

The tree was cut in 1021.

Tool marks and archaeological context associate that cutting with Norse activity.

The site’s entire occupation span remains broader.

When written this way, the date becomes stronger because nobody is asking it to do impossible work.

Missing rings teach the same discipline. The chronology error in the second Miyake paper did not destroy confidence because the error was local and correctable. If researchers had concealed it, the method would be weaker. If critics had used it to dismiss all annual tree-ring dating, they would have confused fallibility with unreliability.

Reliable systems are systems that catch mistakes.

Aircraft are reliable not because parts never fail but because engineering anticipates failure modes. Accounting is reliable when reconciliations expose discrepancies. Science earns precision through redundancy.

Trees provide redundancy naturally. A forest grows many versions of the same years.

The atmosphere provides another layer. A global event enters many forests.

Ice adds another archive.

Laboratories add independent measurements.

The calendar scar becomes trustworthy because no single ring has to be perfect.

This is almost the opposite of how the discovery looks in retrospect. We remember one Japanese cedar, one spectacular spike, one scientist’s name. The mature method depends on multiplicity.

Many trees.

Many labs.

Many isotopes.

Many ways to be wrong.

That last phrase is the important one.

A method becomes powerful when it knows its ways to be wrong.

Missing rings are not an embarrassment at the edge of Calendar Scars.

They are the reason an exact year can deserve belief.
