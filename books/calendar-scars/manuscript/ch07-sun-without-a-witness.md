# Sun Without a Witness

The Sun left fingerprints but no face.

For the 774 event, that is the central frustration of solar physics. We have evidence that a tremendous flux of energetic particles reached Earth. We have multiple cosmogenic isotopes whose relative production can constrain the particle spectrum. We can use the carbon cycle to estimate when the atmospheric production happened. We can compare the inferred event with modern ground-level enhancements, solar energetic-particle storms, flares, and coronal mass ejections.

What we do not have is the thing solar physicists usually want most: the Sun itself under observation.

There is no magnetogram of the active region. No coronagraph image of an expanding CME. No radio dynamic spectrum. No spacecraft particle telescope. No neutron-monitor trace. No ultraviolet image of flare ribbons. No measurement of the interplanetary magnetic field arriving at Earth.

The source has to be reconstructed from the bruise.

This is an inverse problem, and inverse problems are where confidence goes to acquire manners.

In a forward problem, you specify a solar eruption and calculate what particles and fields should reach Earth. In the inverse problem, you begin with terrestrial isotope production and ask what solar event could have produced it. Many combinations can map to similar residues. A somewhat larger event with a softer spectrum can sometimes produce an isotope response similar to a smaller but harder event. Changes in geomagnetic shielding alter atmospheric yield. Carbon-cycle models convert production into concentration. Every inference carries assumptions downstream.

The most defensible conclusion is broad: the canonical Miyake events were extreme solar energetic-particle events with unusually large high-energy fluence.

The more detailed the reconstruction becomes, the more conditional the language should become.

This has not stopped people from trying to give the ancient events a modern name. “Superflare” is the favorite.

It is an understandable word and a dangerous one.

Astronomers observe flares on other stars far more energetic, in total radiated energy, than typical solar flares. Kepler and later missions revealed that stars broadly similar to the Sun can produce dramatic white-light flares. This forced a legitimate question: can the Sun produce a superflare, and if so, how often?

Miyake events entered that debate because they demonstrate particle extremes beyond the space-age sample. But particle fluence and flare radiated energy are not the same variable. A flare can be bright in electromagnetic radiation without producing an Earth-directed hard proton event of corresponding magnitude. Particle acceleration often depends strongly on CME-driven shocks, magnetic geometry, and connection to Earth.

The terrestrial scar is not a bolometer aimed at the solar surface.

This distinction matters because “superflare” imports imagery the evidence does not contain. It makes us picture the Sun brightening catastrophically, when the hard fact is a particle production signal in Earth’s atmosphere.

The 774 event could have involved an extraordinary flare and CME. It likely involved an eruption capable of accelerating enormous numbers of high-energy protons. But exact flare energy is not written in the tree ring.

Neither is geomagnetic storm strength.

The Carrington event has become the standard unit of cultural space-weather fear. In 1859, Richard Carrington and Richard Hodgson independently observed a white-light solar flare. Roughly seventeen hours later, a severe geomagnetic storm drove aurorae to low latitudes and disrupted telegraph systems. Telegraph operators reported shocks. In some cases systems reportedly operated with batteries disconnected, driven by geomagnetically induced currents.

Carrington is vivid because humans and machines witnessed it.

Miyake is chemically larger in one dimension and narratively poorer.

Comparisons therefore go wrong when writers say the 774 event was “ten times Carrington” or “a hundred times Carrington” without specifying what they mean. Carrington’s solar proton fluence is itself reconstructed indirectly because particle detectors did not exist. Its geomagnetic intensity is estimated from historical magnetograms and auroral observations. The nitrate record once proposed as a proton proxy turned out to be problematic. Different reconstructions of its energetic particles have changed over time.

We are comparing two incomplete historical events using different archives.

The safer comparison is with directly measured solar energetic-particle events and ground-level enhancements from the modern era. There, neutron monitors and spacecraft provide particle spectra. The February 1956 ground-level enhancement, often called GLE 5, is particularly important because it had a very hard spectrum. August 1972 produced intense particle fluence with a different spectral shape. October 1989 produced major events again.

Even against this instrument record, 774 sits far out in the tail.

Recent work has estimated that its high-energy particle fluence may have been tens of times larger than benchmark modern events, with exact factors depending on energy threshold and reconstruction. That gap is the unsettling part. The Sun we have measured directly has not shown us its full high-energy range.

This could mean several things.

One possibility is statistical boredom: the instrument era is simply too short. A one-in-a-thousand-year event is unlikely to occur during seventy years of good particle measurement. There is no need for exotic physics. Wait long enough and the tail appears.

A second possibility is that extreme events belong to a special regime. Perhaps large active regions, interacting eruptions, unusually efficient particle acceleration, or rare heliospheric conditions create “dragon king” events whose statistics do not follow the same smooth distribution as ordinary solar particle storms.

A third possibility is that our event catalog is biased. Weak ancient events are hard to detect, while large ones are obvious. The apparent gap between modern and Miyake-scale events may partly be a detection threshold.

Current research is trying to distinguish these.

The 2021 “Mind the Gap” paper argued that newly identified weaker events begin filling the observational gap and therefore favor a continuous high-energy tail rather than an entirely separate phenomenon. The conclusion was cautious because the sample remains tiny. A few new events can dramatically change a distribution when the tail contains only a handful of points.

Then the 2026 work on solar-cycle background made the lower tail less simple again. If ordinary cosmic-ray modulation can mimic or distort weak candidate events, the apparent bridge between modern storms and canonical Miyake events may contain false or mismeasured planks.

This is healthy science, though it makes terrible apocalypse content.

The honest picture is not a single recurrence curve descending cleanly from common events to civilization-threatening ones. It is a censored, noisy distribution inferred from different instruments across different eras: direct particle measurements for decades; neutron monitors for somewhat longer; cosmogenic isotopes for millennia; deep archives with changing geomagnetic shielding and chronology uncertainty.

Each regime sees a different slice.

The physics of acceleration adds another layer. Solar energetic particles can be accelerated during flares and in CME-driven shocks. Shock acceleration efficiency depends on geometry, turbulence, seed populations, and the shock’s speed relative to the surrounding plasma. Quasi-perpendicular shocks can produce hard spectra under some conditions. Multiple eruptions can modify the heliosphere, changing the environment through which a later shock travels.

The most extreme terrestrial particle event may therefore require not just a large eruption but a particular sequence.

Modern space-weather history provides suggestive examples. In July 2012, a powerful CME crossed Earth’s orbit but missed Earth because of longitude. The interplanetary environment had been “preconditioned” by an earlier eruption, reducing drag and allowing rapid propagation. Researchers later argued that had the event struck Earth directly, the geomagnetic consequences could have been severe.

This does not make July 2012 a Miyake analogue. It demonstrates a principle: space weather is path dependent.

The solar eruption does not travel through empty geometry. Previous eruptions change density and magnetic structure. Earth may connect magnetically to a shock in ways that greatly alter particle arrival. A nominally similar flare can produce very different radiation at Earth depending on position and connectivity.

This makes the inverse problem nastier.

The isotope scar tells us what reached Earth, not everything the Sun emitted.

For risk, that is arguably the quantity we care about. Satellites and astronauts experience the particles that arrive, not the flare energy left behind at the Sun. Yet engineering scenarios require time profile and spectrum, not merely integrated fluence. A huge dose delivered over ten hours creates different operational challenges than the same fluence spread over days. Warning time matters. Spectral hardness matters. Shielding effectiveness changes with energy.

Ancient carbon cannot yet give us all of that.

Subannual radiocarbon measurements can constrain season but not hour-by-hour flux. Ice nuclide ratios constrain spectrum statistically but not with spacecraft-like resolution. Models can propose reference profiles, but those profiles are reconstructions.

This is why I resist writing “what would happen if the 774 event struck today” as if it were one answer.

Which 774 event?

The particle fluence reconstructed from carbon and ice? The unknown CME that may have accompanied it? A worst-case magnetic orientation? A hard spectrum scaled from 1956? A time profile inferred from modern shock events? A single eruption or a sequence?

Each choice changes the damage.

A better approach is to separate domains.

For aviation and human radiation exposure, the high-energy particle spectrum is central. At commercial flight altitudes, Earth’s atmosphere still provides substantial shielding, but energetic secondary particles increase dose, especially at high latitudes where geomagnetic shielding is lower. A Miyake-scale hard-spectrum event could create operational radiation concerns far beyond typical solar storms.

For astronauts beyond low Earth orbit, the concern is sharper. Spacecraft shielding helps but can generate secondary radiation under very energetic particle bombardment. Mission timing, shelter design, warning, and cumulative dose become critical. The Apollo missions were lucky in relation to the enormous August 1972 solar particle events, which occurred between Apollo 16 and 17. A Miyake-scale event raises the stakes.

For satellites, energetic particles can cause single-event effects, degrade solar cells, charge dielectrics, and disturb electronics. But vulnerability depends on orbit, design, shielding, and fault tolerance.

For power grids, geomagnetic induction is the headline hazard, but it depends mainly on rapid geomagnetic-field changes driven by solar-wind and CME magnetic conditions. A huge proton event does not mathematically guarantee a huge geomagnetic storm.

For radio and navigation, ionospheric disturbance, solar radio bursts, energetic particles, and geomagnetic activity can each affect systems differently.

The ancient scar therefore maps most directly onto radiation hazard and only indirectly onto some of the most cinematic infrastructure risks.

This distinction makes the event more technically interesting because it prevents one-size-fits-all doom.

The other uncertainty is recurrence.

Recent literature sometimes summarizes the strongest known extreme solar particle events as occurring roughly once per 1,500 to 2,000 years over the last fourteen millennia. That is a useful order-of-magnitude statement, not a forecast clock. The number depends on what qualifies as an event, which parts of the Holocene have adequate annual data, and the detection threshold.

Nine events in fourteen thousand years do not make a robust actuarial table.

They do make complacency based solely on the space age irrational.

This is the same inferential move engineers make with paleoseismology. Written history may contain no magnitude-nine earthquake on a particular fault, but coastal subsidence and tsunami deposits can reveal predecessors. The direct human record is not privileged merely because it is direct. It is often short.

Modern civilization has existed in its current electronically dependent form for less than the average interval between some classes of rare natural events.

That sentence applies to more than the Sun.

What Miyake events add is a strangely quantitative warning. They are not legends of a red sky. They are isotope production anomalies measured in physical archives. They can be modelled. They can be compared with known nuclear reaction yields and modern particle spectra.

The warning is therefore not that prophecy says the Sun will attack us. It is that our empirical sample of solar extremes is incomplete.

The Sun has produced harder particle environments than the instrument era has directly measured.

How much harder, how often, and under what source conditions remain active questions.

There is an almost comic mismatch between the importance of that uncertainty and the modesty of the evidence object. A solar event capable of occupying entire risk reports is inferred partly from cellulose dissolved in chemistry labs.

No telescope saw the source.

No person had to believe it happened.

The Sun left enough nuclear bookkeeping in the atmosphere that we can reconstruct its violence after the witnesses are gone.

This is better than legend and worse than telemetry.

It is also what we have.

The temptation, faced with incomplete source physics, is to fill the gap with narrative: a monstrous active region rotating into view, a flare brighter than anything humans have seen, a CME tearing toward Earth. Those scenes may be plausible. They are not in the record.

The tree gives us the consequence.

The rest has to remain an inference.

That restraint is not anti-story. It is the story. We are trying to reconstruct an event large enough to matter to modern technological risk using archives created accidentally by nitrogen, ice, and wood. The fact that we can get as far as we do is astonishing.

The fact that we cannot see the Sun is the price.
