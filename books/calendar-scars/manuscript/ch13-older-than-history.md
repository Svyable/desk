# Older Than History

Once scientists knew what to look for, the past became noisy.

The first two Miyake events sit in a comfortable historical neighborhood. The eighth and tenth centuries contain written records, established tree-ring chronologies, and enough surviving wood that a one-year radiocarbon anomaly can be checked across continents. Move backward five thousand years and the problem changes. Move backward twelve thousand and it changes again.

The Sun may be the same star. The detector is not the same Earth.

That sentence is the key to the older event catalog.

Researchers have now identified or proposed strong annual radiocarbon production events at several points long before written history: around 660 BCE, 5410 BCE, 5259 BCE, 7176 BCE, and deeper still near the end of the last ice age. Some are supported by multiple tree-ring sequences and other cosmogenic proxies. Others remain less secure. Their raw radiocarbon amplitudes are tempting to rank.

Do not.

At least not without correcting for the changing geomagnetic field, carbon cycle, archive quality, and particle spectrum.

Earth’s magnetic field is part of the detector. Charged particles approaching the atmosphere encounter a rigidity cutoff: lower-energy particles are more effectively excluded at low latitudes, while high latitudes are more exposed. The field’s strength and geometry vary over millennia. A weaker geomagnetic field lets more cosmic radiation reach the atmosphere for the same incoming particle environment.

This means a radiocarbon event recorded seven thousand years ago cannot be compared with 775 by taking the percentage increase in a tree ring and declaring one solar event larger.

The planet changed the gain.

The 7176 BCE and 5259 BCE events illustrate the problem. In the 2022 *Nature Communications* study, Nicolas Brehm and colleagues identified strong rapid radiocarbon increases in absolutely dated tree-ring records. In raw terms the 7176 excursion was among the largest yet found. But geomagnetic-field intensity differed from the first millennium CE. Production modelling translates the terrestrial isotope signal into an inferred solar proton fluence only after accounting for that shielding.

The resulting comparison is less sensational and more useful.

The events still appear extreme. They do not need a “biggest ever” badge.

The 5259 BCE event has already earned scientific significance without winning any ranking contest. It is the event that anchored the Dispilio chronology. An isotope anomaly can matter more as a chronological landmark than as a trophy in solar violence.

This is one of the recurring themes of the book: magnitude is only one kind of importance.

The event around 660 BCE provides another lesson. Early annual radiocarbon work detected an abrupt excursion; subsequent research refined its structure and timing. Japanese tree rings showed prolonged production rather than a single perfectly instantaneous pulse. More recent work using multiple sites and careful chronology has narrowed the onset into the mid-660s BCE.

The physical event may have been extended or compound.

This matters because the popular image of a Miyake event is one enormous flash. The proxies do not require that simplicity. A solar particle episode can involve multiple eruptions, sustained shock acceleration, or temporally separated injections. Carbon-cycle smoothing further blurs the profile. A multi-year radiocarbon rise can therefore contain information about source duration, atmospheric transport, or both.

“Miyake event” is an observational category first.

Physics comes after.

The 5410 BCE event sharpens the category issue. Annual tree-ring measurements from geographically separated archives revealed a rapid carbon-14 increase around that date. The event is significant, but researchers continue to distinguish among strong confirmed extreme solar-particle events, likely events, and candidates. Not every abrupt excursion has identical multi-proxy support.

This is how a growing field should look. The catalog is not a sacred list. It is a set of claims with different evidentiary weights.

Deep time makes those weights harder to maintain.

Absolutely dated tree-ring chronologies thin as we move backward. European oak and pine sequences extend astonishingly far, and subfossil trees from river deposits, bogs, and lakes can bridge enormous spans. New Zealand kauri reaches into much older periods. But continuous annual sequences become rarer. Cross-dating uncertainty grows. Independent archives can have age-model disagreements larger than the one-year effects we want to study.

The irony is obvious: the event can be one year wide while the archive containing it is uncertain by decades.

Solving that mismatch is part of the work.

The very cosmogenic events researchers want to study can help synchronize archives if enough independent records overlap. A prominent carbon-14 spike in tree rings can anchor an ice-core age model; a corresponding beryllium peak can support the event identification. But the procedure risks circularity if the alignment is assumed in order to prove itself.

Chronological bootstrapping requires independent footholds.

The Late Glacial event literature lives near this edge. Researchers have reported a huge cosmogenic production event around 12,350 BCE, larger in some proxy measures than the canonical Holocene events. The finding is potentially profound for solar physics because it pushes the record into a time of substantially different geomagnetic conditions and climate.

It is also easy to oversell.

The farther we move from robust annual absolute chronology, the more every comparison depends on models. The event may indeed represent an extraordinary solar particle storm. But converting its isotope amplitude into a precise statement such as “the strongest solar storm in 14,000 years” compresses chronology, geomagnetism, particle spectrum, and production physics into a headline.

Headlines do not have error bars.

The more honest formulation is that deep archives reveal cosmogenic events whose production signatures can exceed those of the canonical first-millennium events, and whose inferred solar-particle intensity remains an active modelling problem.

That sentence is less exciting only if the real thing has not landed.

The real thing is that trees from the end of the ice age may preserve high-energy solar behavior we have never directly observed.

No civilization existed to lose satellites. Humans did.

People living through these events inhabited worlds of hunting, gathering, fishing, migration, kinship, and local environmental knowledge. An extreme solar particle episode probably did not announce itself as a technological disaster because there was almost no technological exposure to space weather in the modern sense. The event’s significance to them, if any, is unknown.

The isotope record therefore separates hazard from consequence with unusual clarity.

The Sun can produce an extreme event without producing an archaeological catastrophe.

That should inoculate us against one of the worst habits in paleocatastrophism: finding a natural anomaly and then searching the archaeological record for something to blame on it.

Human history is full of discontinuities. Settlement abandonment, demographic change, technological shifts, violence, migration, and dietary transitions occur somewhere in almost every broad time window. If the dating uncertainty is a century, an event in the middle can always be made to look relevant.

Miyake events make this worse and better at once.

Worse, because exact dates are irresistible and can attract retrospective causal narratives.

Better, because exact dates allow causal order to be tested rather than gestured at.

If an archaeological transition began fifty years before the particle event, the event did not cause it. If a settlement continued normally for generations afterward, catastrophe narratives weaken. Precision is the enemy of vague coincidence.

This is one reason the older markers are so valuable even when nobody cares about solar hazard. They can function as temporal controls.

The 5259 BCE event already does this. As more Neolithic and Chalcolithic timber chronologies are anchored, researchers can test whether regional cultural transitions were synchronous or merely grouped into the same broad radiocarbon phases. An event found around 660 BCE can constrain Iron Age sequences near periods dense with written chronology in the Mediterranean and Near East.

The marker is scientifically agnostic about the historical argument.

It is a ruler.

The event catalog itself, however, demands more than a ruler. Solar physicists want frequency.

How often does the Sun produce events as strong as 775?

How often something half as strong?

Is there a hard upper limit?

Does the distribution smoothly continue from ordinary modern solar energetic-particle events, or does extreme activity belong to a different population?

These questions sound statistical, but the statistics are archive-dependent.

Suppose nine strong events or candidates appear across roughly fourteen thousand years. A naive estimate divides years by events and produces a recurrence of order one every fifteen hundred years. But the numerator contains intervals with unequal detection sensitivity. The denominator contains events of unequal confidence. The threshold defining “strong” can vary. Geomagnetic shielding changes. Annual data density is patchy.

A recurrence interval built this way is a first approximation, not an insurance table.

The correct statistical problem includes an observation model.

For each year in the past, what size event could existing archives have detected? Were annual tree-ring samples measured? What was geomagnetic shielding? Does a high-resolution ice record cover the interval? What false-positive rate comes from ordinary solar-cycle variability? What event spectra are more visible in carbon-14 than beryllium-10?

Without that, the catalog confounds solar inactivity with human failure to measure.

This is not unique to space weather. Astronomers correct exoplanet occurrence rates for detection probability. Epidemiologists correct for testing. Paleontologists model fossil preservation. Intelligence analysts distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence when sensor coverage is incomplete.

Miyake-event statistics need the same maturity.

The 2026 Usoskin study is useful because it attacks the problem from the opposite side: false positives. Even if measurement were continuous, natural cyclic variation in cosmogenic production can create annual structures that resemble weak events. That means the catalog needs both detection efficiency and classification specificity.

A rare-event science can be fooled in both directions.

Miss real storms.

Invent storms from background.

The canonical events survive because they are far from the boundary.

Deep-time events also force us to think about solar stability. The Sun’s luminosity evolves on billion-year timescales, but across the Holocene its basic stellar state is stable. Yet magnetic activity varies enormously on shorter scales. The absence of instrumentally observed Miyake-scale events does not imply the Sun has changed fundamentally since 775. The proxy record suggests rare extremes are part of the behavior of a broadly familiar star.

This is more unsettling than an exotic ancient Sun.

An exotic ancient Sun can be dismissed as history.

Our Sun is still here.

There are physical limits. Solar active regions contain finite magnetic energy. Particle acceleration cannot scale without bound. Stellar observations, solar magnetic-field measurements, and theoretical modelling constrain plausible flare energies. Some of the most extreme stellar superflares seen on younger or more active Sun-like stars may not be plausible for the contemporary Sun at the same frequency.

But “there is an upper bound” is not the same as “the modern direct record reaches it.”

The isotope scars demonstrate that it does not.

They also show why a long proxy record matters more than a spectacular modern near miss. Engineers sometimes focus on July 2012 because the CME was measured and narrowly missed Earth. It is a vivid benchmark. Solar physicists focus on 1956 because its hard spectrum is directly reconstructed from neutron monitors. Radiation planners care about August 1972.

Miyake events sit beyond all three in particle-fluence space.

The comparison should not collapse them into one worst-case monster. It should expand the scenario library.

The older events expand it further.

They tell us that 774 was not singular.

They tell us the Sun has produced multiple extreme particle episodes across millennia.

They tell us event shapes differ.

They tell us geomagnetic shielding must be part of reconstruction.

And they tell us the archive is still incomplete.

There is a strange humility in that last point. We began with a spectacular discovery: an anomalous ring in Japanese cedar. A decade later, the field has enough events to talk about populations but not enough to know the population cleanly.

The more we read, the more obvious the unread years become.

This is good news for science and bad news for anyone selling certainty.

The ancient record is not a finished warning label.

It is a survey still under way.

The trees older than history are not telling us when the next event comes.

They are telling us our memory is too short.
