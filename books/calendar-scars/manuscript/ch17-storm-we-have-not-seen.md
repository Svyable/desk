# Storm We Have Not Seen

Modern civilization has not experienced a Miyake event under modern instrumentation.

That sentence is both more useful and less dramatic than “we are unprepared for the next solar apocalypse.”

The direct record is short. Satellites have measured energetic particles for decades. Neutron monitors extend ground-level enhancement records through the second half of the twentieth century. Magnetometers reach much further back. Telegraph disturbances and auroral observations carry geomagnetic history into the nineteenth century.

The radiocarbon scars extend one dimension of the hazard thousands of years further.

They do not tell us exactly what a repeat would do.

Start with astronauts, because their vulnerability maps most directly onto the particle evidence. High-energy solar protons can deliver substantial radiation dose outside the shielding of Earth’s atmosphere. Low Earth orbit still receives protection from geomagnetic field and spacecraft structure, though not complete protection. Crews on lunar or deep-space missions have less environmental shielding.

Mission planners already design around solar particle events. Spacecraft can include storm shelters using water, supplies, or denser structure around a small volume. Operational procedures can move crews away from lightly shielded areas. Forecasting can provide warning after solar eruption signatures, although the highest-energy particles can arrive quickly.

A Miyake-scale hard-spectrum event raises the question of how far existing assumptions extend.

The answer is not one dose number.

Dose depends on particle spectrum, duration, spacecraft shielding, tissue depth, mission location, and orientation. Scaling a modern event by total fluence can misrepresent penetrating high-energy particles. Secondary neutrons and fragmentation complicate shielding. A reconstructed ancient event is therefore best used as an envelope for scenario studies, not as a known exposure profile.

Aviation sits one atmosphere lower.

Commercial aircraft fly above much of the atmosphere’s shielding, and polar routes experience weaker geomagnetic cutoff. Solar energetic particles can increase radiation dose at flight altitude, especially during hard-spectrum ground-level enhancement events. Modern radiation models can estimate route-specific exposure from measured particle spectra.

For an ancient event, the spectrum is reconstructed indirectly.

A 774-like scenario could therefore be operationally important for high-latitude aviation even if passengers on the ground noticed nothing. Airlines might reroute polar flights, lower altitude, or respond to radiation alerts depending on event characteristics and warning systems.

This is a useful illustration of technological exposure. The same particles that passed over eighth-century travelers without creating a recognized hazard category become an occupational concern when humans place pressurized metal tubes near the top of the troposphere.

Satellites create more surfaces for failure.

Energetic particles can upset memory, damage electronics, degrade solar arrays, charge materials, and create sensor anomalies. Radiation-hardened systems are designed with such effects in mind; commercial constellations vary in tolerance and redundancy. A severe event could produce increased anomalies across many spacecraft, but the result would depend on orbit and design.

The phrase “knock out satellites” is too crude.

A heterogeneous fleet does not fail as one machine.

Some spacecraft would weather an event. Some would enter safe mode. Some might suffer permanent degradation. A large low-Earth-orbit constellation has redundancy unavailable to a single strategic satellite, but large constellations also create operational complexity during widespread anomalies.

Navigation systems add ionospheric sensitivity. GPS and other GNSS signals travel through the ionosphere, where solar and geomagnetic disturbances alter electron density. Positioning errors and signal scintillation can increase during severe space weather. Again, high-energy particle fluence is not the only driver; associated solar radiation and geomagnetic conditions matter.

Then there is the power grid, where almost every popular account goes off the rails.

Geomagnetically induced currents are driven by changes in Earth’s magnetic field, which induce electric fields in the conducting ground and connected infrastructure. Long transmission lines and transformers can be vulnerable during intense geomagnetic storms. The 1989 Quebec blackout is the modern canonical example. Carrington-era telegraph effects show the mechanism can be severe.

A Miyake carbon-14 spike does not directly measure this hazard.

It measures atmospheric cosmogenic production from energetic particles.

The solar event producing those particles may have accompanied a powerful CME capable of generating an extreme geomagnetic storm. It may not have produced the maximum conceivable magnetic coupling at Earth. The interplanetary magnetic field orientation, solar wind speed, magnetospheric state, ground conductivity, and grid topology all influence the outcome.

Therefore a sentence such as “a Miyake event would destroy the electrical grid” outruns the evidence twice: first by assigning unknown CME properties to the ancient event, then by assigning uniform failure to a modern engineered system.

The correct conclusion is less cinematic and more actionable.

The proxy record establishes that the high-energy particle hazard has a tail beyond direct observations. Infrastructure whose risk depends directly or indirectly on extreme solar conditions should test whether its design basis spans that tail.

This is what engineering does with other rare natural hazards.

Nuclear plants use seismic hazard curves extending beyond instrumentally observed earthquakes. Flood defenses use paleoflood evidence where possible. Tsunami planning incorporates geological deposits from prehistoric events. Space infrastructure should not treat the satellite era as the full empirical distribution merely because the instruments are better.

The proxies are worse instruments over a much longer time.

That trade is worth making.

How often should the ancient events enter design standards?

Here the statistical uncertainty bites. If the largest class occurs roughly every millennium or two, a satellite with a fifteen-year life has low individual probability of exposure. A civilization operating continuous space infrastructure over centuries has a different cumulative probability. A lunar settlement intended to persist indefinitely has another.

Risk horizon changes the question.

So does consequence. An event with a one-in-a-thousand annual probability can deserve attention if consequences are large and mitigation cheap. The same probability can be irrelevant if consequences are minor or adaptation automatic.

Recurrence alone cannot make policy.

There is also a temptation to convert the 774 event into an economic-loss estimate. Give every satellite a failure probability, simulate grid outages, price aviation disruption, total the number, and announce trillions.

Scenario modelling can be useful. False precision can be worse than no number.

An ancient event does not give enough source detail to support a single loss figure. Better work would construct a family of physically consistent scenarios: hard-spectrum high-fluence SEP with modest geomagnetic storm; hard SEP plus severe Earth-directed CME; long-duration compound eruptions; different seasons and geomagnetic field states. Infrastructure models could then explore sensitivity.

The output should be a range tied to assumptions.

This is where the archaeology half of the book offers an unexpected lesson to engineering.

Archaeologists learned not to let an exact date carry claims beyond its context. Space-weather planners should not let an exact radiocarbon amplitude carry source properties beyond the proxy.

Evidence posture transfers across fields.

There is another modern exposure whose importance has changed rapidly: electronics at scale.

A medieval society could lose a spectacular auroral night and suffer almost nothing material from the electromagnetic environment. A modern society coordinates transportation, finance, communication, agriculture, logistics, emergency response, and defense through electronic systems. Many are backed up. Many are geographically distributed. Some have failure modes correlated through common space-weather exposure.

The systemic question is not whether one component fails.

It is whether common-mode stress defeats redundancy.

A navigation constellation degraded while high-frequency polar radio fails and satellite operators enter safe modes creates a different problem from any single outage. Grid stress during communication degradation creates another. Aviation rerouting increases traffic load elsewhere. Timing services support financial and telecommunications infrastructure.

Complexity can amplify or absorb shocks depending on architecture.

This is where doomsday writing usually assumes amplification and resilience writing assumes absorption. Reality will contain both.

Modern systems have advantages ancient telegraph networks did not. Space-weather observatories watch the Sun continuously. Forecast centers issue alerts. Grid operators have geomagnetic-disturbance procedures. Satellites can enter safe modes. Aircraft can reroute. Radiation monitors provide real-time data. Engineers know the hazard exists.

Preparedness is not zero.

The unknown is whether planning envelopes are wide enough for an event outside direct experience.

Miyake events are therefore less a prophecy than a stress test for assumptions.

If a model has been validated only against space-age particle events, what happens when fluence is increased into the proxy-inferred regime?

Which components scale linearly?

Which saturate?

Which failure modes appear only above thresholds?

Where does warning still help?

Where do the first particles arrive too quickly?

Which systems recover automatically and which require human intervention?

These are engineering questions, not apocalypse questions.

The distinction matters because fear is a poor maintenance schedule.

There is one domain where the ancient record may be particularly valuable: human expansion beyond Earth.

A permanent lunar presence, Mars transit missions, and long-duration deep-space operations increase the number of people outside the atmosphere’s radiation shielding. The chance that any one short mission encounters an extreme solar particle event is low. The chance that a permanent human spacefaring civilization eventually encounters one approaches certainty if the hazard persists over millennia.

Civilization duration converts rare events into expected events.

The relevant question becomes not “will this happen during Artemis mission X?” but “does a sustainable off-Earth architecture remain safe across the solar environment the Holocene record demonstrates?”

That is a different design philosophy.

It favors shelter mass, distributed sensing, operational procedures, medical planning, and perhaps mission timing strategies robust to uncertainty in event recurrence. It also argues for treating cosmogenic isotope science as part of human-spaceflight history, not a remote paleophysics niche.

The trees are telling spacecraft designers something.

Not exactly what to build.

What range of nature they have to respect.

The same principle applies to terrestrial technology. The exact future event is unknowable. The existence of a longer tail is not.

This is an awkward category of knowledge. Engineers prefer specified loads. Policymakers prefer probability. The proxy gives us a distribution with sparse tail observations and incomplete source reconstruction.

We know enough to reject complacency and not enough to justify certainty.

That is often the real frontier.

There will eventually be another extreme solar particle event. “Eventually” may mean next year or centuries. The probability distribution does not owe us drama on schedule.

If it comes during a technological era, we will have instruments everywhere. The event that ancient trees recorded silently will be measured from the solar surface to the ground. Spacecraft will see the particles. Neutron monitors will record secondary cascades. Atmospheric chemistry instruments will follow ionization. Power grids will log induced currents. Aviation dosimeters will respond.

For the first time, a Miyake-class event would have witnesses capable of understanding it while it happened.

That event would become the calibration point future researchers currently wish 774 had been.

I hope the instruments survive well enough to be useful.

That is as close to a warning as this book needs.
