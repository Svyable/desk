# Second Scar

One anomaly can found a mystery. Two begin a population.

The 774/775 radiocarbon jump was strange enough that almost any explanation could still be made to orbit it. A unique astrophysical accident has enormous explanatory freedom. Nearby stellar catastrophe, rare gamma-ray event, an unprecedented solar outburst: when there is one point in a distribution, the distribution can be almost anything.

Then Fusa Miyake’s group found another.

After the 2012 paper, the obvious next move was to search more annual tree rings. The researchers measured Japanese cedar material spanning the ninth through eleventh centuries. In 2013 they reported a second rapid radiocarbon increase near the end of the tenth century. It was smaller than the 774 event but still abrupt enough to stand out sharply above ordinary annual variation.

This changed the problem.

If two large excursions occurred within roughly two centuries, the first was less likely to be a once-in-geological-time accident from an unrelated stellar source. A recurring solar-terrestrial process became more plausible. The second event also supplied another potential chronological marker, one that happened to sit immediately before one of the most historically dense periods in North Atlantic archaeology.

Before any of that, however, a ring went missing.

The original 2013 article placed the new increase between AD 992 and 993. During reanalysis, the researchers discovered that an annual layer around AD 956 had been omitted in counting. Every date beyond that point in the sequence had to move one year forward. The journal published a corrigendum in November 2013. The event became 993–994.

It is difficult to design a better parable about precision.

A field had just discovered a method capable of identifying one-year atmospheric events, and one of its foundational papers was off by one year because of a missing ring. The cheap reading is obvious: so much for exact dating.

The more useful reading is the opposite.

If your method claims annual precision, a one-year error is not rounding. It has to be found, explained, published, and propagated through the literature. That is what happened. The correction did not make the radiocarbon jump disappear. It changed its calendar placement. The observational phenomenon survived; the chronology improved.

This difference between observation and indexing is crucial.

Imagine a sequence of one hundred numbered photographs. The photographs themselves are real. If image 37 is accidentally skipped when the folder is numbered, every later caption will be off by one. Discovering the skipped image does not invalidate the photographs. It repairs the mapping between sequence position and label.

Tree rings are not photographs, and finding a missing ring can be much harder than finding a missing file. A tree near its ecological limit may fail to form visible wood around part of its circumference during a terrible year. Another tree may form the ring clearly. Cross-dating is designed to catch exactly this problem by comparing patterns among individuals and sites.

The corrigendum therefore became an argument for replication without anybody intending it as such.

The second scar was subsequently found elsewhere. Global work confirmed a coherent radiocarbon increase around 993 across multiple tree records. Ice-core cosmogenic radionuclides supported the broader class of solar-particle events. Historical auroral reports from around 992–993 added intriguing context, though, as always, texts remain secondary to the isotope evidence.

The event was real enough to become a ruler.

At L’Anse aux Meadows, it would eventually supply the fixed internal ring from which researchers counted to 1021. At European sites it helped test long chronologies. In living ancient trees, the marker can independently validate ring counts. The second event is less dramatic in magnitude than 774 but in some ways more useful historically because of where it falls.

A timestamp does not care how spectacular the event was. It cares whether the pattern is recognizable.

The 993 event also helps expose a category problem hidden by the name *Miyake event*. What exactly belongs in the category?

At first, the canonical pair—774/775 and 993/994—defined the phenomenon. Both showed abrupt annual radiocarbon increases far larger than ordinary variation. Both later received independent support from other cosmogenic nuclides. Both were consistent with extreme solar energetic-particle events. As researchers searched more tree-ring series, additional excursions appeared at other times and at different amplitudes.

Some were strong. Some were subtle. Some replicated. Some remained candidates. Some may represent the tail of ordinary solar-particle physics. Others could be combinations of production changes and background cosmic-ray variability. The category widened faster than the mechanism became certain.

Scientific vocabulary often does this. A name begins as a handle for a striking observation and becomes, quietly, an ontological claim. If we are not careful, saying “Miyake event” begins to imply that every named excursion was produced by the same physical process, with the same spectral family and a common recurrence law.

That has not been proved.

A safer hierarchy is useful.

At the hard center are abrupt annual carbon-14 excursions replicated in independent tree-ring records and supported by other cosmogenic radionuclides in compatible chronologies. These are strong extreme solar-particle events.

Around them are replicated radiocarbon excursions whose solar interpretation is plausible or likely but whose multi-proxy confirmation is weaker.

Further out are candidate events: bumps that look interesting in one or more records but remain vulnerable to background variation, chronology error, measurement scatter, physiology, contamination, or regional effects.

The distinction is not bureaucratic. It changes what we can do with the event.

An archaeological calendar pin needs recognizability. A space-weather recurrence estimate needs a well-defined event catalog. If weak candidates are promoted too easily, the estimated frequency of extreme events rises. If detection thresholds are too conservative, the catalog misses the lower tail and makes the distribution look artificially discontinuous. The shape of the hazard curve depends on classification decisions made in laboratories measuring wood thousands of years old.

The 2026 paper by Usoskin and colleagues makes this problem especially current. Their modelling asks whether ordinary cyclic variability in galactic cosmic rays—modulated by the Sun—can distort or mimic the radiocarbon signature of weaker extreme solar particle events. The answer is uncomfortable in the productive way: yes, background structure matters.

Imagine listening for gunshots during a thunderstorm. Better microphones help, but they do not make the thunder disappear. At some point the problem becomes classification, not measurement precision.

For the giant 774 event, the signal-to-background ratio is generous. For weak events, the same confidence is unavailable. That means the field’s next phase may be less about finding spectacular spikes and more about defining detection thresholds honestly.

This is where the second event becomes methodologically important. It was not simply another point. It proved recurrence at a scale large enough to identify, but it also came with a one-year chronology correction and a smaller amplitude. It forced researchers to think about what features of the first event were essential and what were incidental.

The 774 event rises roughly twice as strongly as 993 in some global reconstructions. Yet both appear to share similar broad morphology: rapid production followed by slower decay as carbon redistributes through the global cycle. The 993 event’s inferred production timing has been placed around boreal spring in modelling, distinct from the summer timing inferred for 774. The hemispheric expression again reflects seasonality and transport.

Two events therefore show sameness and difference at once.

That is what a real population should do.

The next question was whether older wood carried more.

Researchers searched around 660 BCE, a period where previous radiocarbon data suggested an excursion. Annual measurements from multiple regions and later ice-core comparisons strengthened the case for another major solar proton event. More recent work has tried to pin its timing within the mid-660s BCE, demonstrating again how year labels can shift with region, sample convention, and event onset.

Then came events much deeper in the Holocene: 5410 BCE, 5259 BCE, 7176 BCE, and an especially large Late Glacial event reported around 12,350 BCE. The list is still being refined.

Each additional event changes two fields at once.

For solar physics, it lengthens the record of extreme particle behavior.

For chronology, it adds another potential synchronization point.

This creates an odd incentive. Solar physicists want a complete event catalog because recurrence statistics improve with every verified point. Archaeologists want events distributed through time because every marker opens new chronological territory. Laboratories have every reason to search.

Search intensity, however, can distort apparent frequency.

The best-known periods are not necessarily the most active solar periods. They are the periods where long, annually resolved, absolutely dated wood exists and researchers have measured it. Data density depends on tree preservation, archive history, species longevity, laboratory budgets, and scholarly interest. A thousand-year stretch with no detected event may contain no event—or simply no adequate annual measurements.

Absence in the catalog is a mixture of solar behavior and archival opportunity.

This is a familiar problem in paleontology. The fossil record is shaped by life and by the probability of becoming a fossil. A species that lived in conditions hostile to preservation can look rare or absent. The Miyake catalog has a similar observation function. An extreme particle event must occur, produce a detectable cosmogenic signal, be stored in surviving wood or other archives, have its sequence preserved, and be measured at appropriate resolution.

The Sun writes more than we have read.

The second scar taught the field to read deliberately.

It also gave researchers a way to test whether a chronology that looked annually continuous was actually correct. If the 993 signal should appear in a known-age sequence and does not occur where expected, several possibilities open: the sample may have a missing or false ring, the radiocarbon measurement may be wrong, the species may carry physiological effects, the chronology may be misassigned, or the event’s expression may differ locally.

The marker becomes a checksum.

This is an underappreciated use. Chronology usually uses dendrochronology to calibrate radiocarbon. With a global radiocarbon event, radiocarbon can independently audit dendrochronology. The relationship becomes reciprocal.

The 2018 global study did this at scale. It found sporadic one-year irregularities in individual samples, including missing rings in some records, even while the composite chronologies remained well dated. That result is exactly what we should expect from biology. A forest chronology can be accurate even when one tree refuses to cooperate in one year.

Consensus emerges from overlapping imperfection.

The phrase sounds philosophical but it is practical laboratory procedure. You do not demand that every tree be perfect. You demand that enough independent records converge to expose the imperfect ones.

This principle will matter later when we turn to archaeology. A single beam with a detected event can provide a powerful date, but confidence rises when multiple timbers agree. A single historical chronicle mentioning red sky is interesting; multiple independent observations with compatible timing are better. A single isotope spike is a candidate; carbon-14 plus beryllium-10 plus chlorine-36 is a different category of argument.

The second scar also changes our emotional relationship to the Sun.

One extreme event in 774 could be treated as a freak. Two within roughly 220 years make freakness harder to use as comfort. Add older events and the instrumental era begins to look obviously inadequate as a complete sample of solar extremes.

This does not mean an event is “overdue.”

That word should be banned from low-frequency natural hazards unless the process is genuinely periodic. Earthquakes are not library books. Asteroid impacts are not late. Solar particle extremes do not obey a human appointment schedule. If the average recurrence were, say, one event per millennium or two, that would not mean a millennium without one creates a debt.

Rare-event statistics are lumpy.

The known events may also represent different portions of a continuum rather than a separate class. A 2021 analysis argued that newer moderate events begin to fill the apparent gap between modern instrumentally observed solar particle events and the much larger proxy events, favoring the possibility that Miyake-scale episodes are the extreme tail of ordinary solar eruptive behavior rather than a wholly separate phenomenon.

That idea is comforting only until you think about it.

If the events are ordinary physics pushed into the tail, we do not need an exotic mechanism to get one. The same Sun we observe now may be capable, under rare combinations of active-region size, magnetic topology, shock geometry, and Earth connection, of producing particle fluences far beyond our direct record.

The unknown becomes less exotic and more relevant.

The 2026 work complicates this again by asking whether the lower end of the inferred event population is partly an artifact of background variability. The field is therefore trying to determine whether extreme solar particle events form a smooth statistical tail, a special population, or a mixture that current proxies cannot yet cleanly separate.

A tree-ring spike becomes a question about the mathematics of extremes.

The irony is that archaeology does not need that question solved.

If 993 is a globally replicated marker, an archaeologist can use it regardless of whether solar physicists eventually call the source a black-swan SEP, a dragon-king event, a compound eruption, or something else. The marker’s chronological usefulness depends on its recognizability and dating, not on a final theory of its production.

This is one of the book’s central asymmetries.

We can use a phenomenon more precisely than we understand its cause.

Science is full of this. Sailors navigated with magnetism before Maxwell. Doctors used quinine before parasite biochemistry was understood. Engineers exploited steam before thermodynamics was formalized. An event can be operationally useful while its mechanism remains partly open.

The danger comes when operational success is mistaken for explanatory completion.

Because the 993 scar can date Norse wood, it is tempting to treat “Miyake event” as a solved solar category. It is not. Because the solar origin is strong, it is tempting to convert a radiocarbon production estimate into a precise future grid-loss scenario. We cannot. Because a missing ring was corrected, it is tempting to call the method fragile. It is not fragile in that simple way either.

The second event sits exactly where mature science often sits: between confidence levels.

High confidence: a rapid global radiocarbon production event occurred.

High confidence: an extreme solar-particle source best explains the canonical isotope stack.

High confidence: the event can function as a calendar marker in appropriate tree-ring sequences.

Lower confidence: the exact solar eruption sequence, particle acceleration mechanism, and associated geomagnetic storm.

Developing confidence: recurrence distribution of events of different sizes.

Case-specific confidence: what any one archaeological date means historically.

This layered posture is more cumbersome than “solar superstorm hit Earth,” but it survives contact with the evidence.

And the missing ring survives inside it as a permanent warning.

In a field obsessed with one-year resolution, one year can be lost in wood anatomy. The correction did not diminish the discovery. It revealed the standard the discovery would have to meet.

The event had to be found again.

It was.

Then people started counting from it.
