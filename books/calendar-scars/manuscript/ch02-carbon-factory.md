# Carbon Factory

Carbon-14 begins as an inconvenience in nitrogen.

The atmosphere is mostly nitrogen. A high-energy particle enters from space, strikes nuclei high above us, and starts a cascade. Secondary particles multiply. Among them are neutrons energetic enough to participate in nuclear reactions but, after losing energy through collisions, slow enough to be captured. Nitrogen-14 takes in one of those neutrons and ejects a proton. What remains is carbon-14.

A dating method is hiding inside that reaction, but nothing about the reaction cares about dating.

Carbon-14 is radioactive. Its half-life is roughly 5,730 years, which means a population of carbon-14 atoms declines predictably enough that organic material can serve as a clock after it stops freely exchanging carbon with the environment. This is the famous part. Willard Libby’s great mid-twentieth-century achievement was recognizing that cosmic rays continually manufacture radiocarbon in the atmosphere and that living organisms, through the carbon cycle, participate in a changing atmospheric ratio. Once an organism dies, its radiocarbon inventory begins to fall away by radioactive decay without being replenished through ordinary metabolism. Measure what remains, compare it with what should have been there, and time enters matter.

That summary is so clean that it almost conceals the century of complications packed inside the words “what should have been there.”

Atmospheric radiocarbon concentration is not constant. That is not a minor correction around the edges of the method. It is one of the central facts of radiocarbon dating. The production rate varies as the flux of cosmic rays changes. Solar magnetic activity modulates the galactic cosmic rays reaching Earth. Earth’s own magnetic field changes its shielding. Newly made carbon-14 moves through atmosphere, plants, soil, oceans, and living organisms. The oceans are not instant mixers. Hemispheres are not interchangeable. Marine organisms can look artificially old because they draw from carbon reservoirs with different histories. Freshwater systems can have their own reservoir problems. Even a sheep can inherit a local isotopic complication if the plants it eats grew over geologically old carbon dioxide.

Radiocarbon dating therefore became a two-clock problem. There is the radioactive clock inside the sample, and there is the historical atmosphere against which that clock must be interpreted.

Tree rings helped solve the second problem.

A tree with independently known annual rings gives us material whose calendar age can be known without radiocarbon. Measure the carbon-14 content of those rings and we learn what the atmosphere looked like in those years. Stitch enough known-age material together and we build a calibration curve: a map from radiocarbon age to calendar age.

The tree does not merely become an object to date. It becomes the instrument by which dates are corrected.

This inversion is worth sitting with because Miyake events emerge from it. When we say a tree ring “contains” the event, we do not mean a mysterious foreign isotope arrived from the Sun and lodged in the wood. The Sun did not throw carbon-14 atoms at a cedar. Energetic particles triggered nuclear reactions in Earth’s atmosphere. The atmosphere made the radiocarbon. The carbon oxidized and entered the carbon cycle. The tree took in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis. The event was translated from particle physics into plant tissue.

That translation has a delay and a shape.

If an extreme solar particle event occurred in a few hours, a tree would not record a needle-thin hourly pulse. Carbon-14 production occurs in the atmosphere; the new radiocarbon must mix and move. Stratosphere-troposphere exchange matters. Growing seasons matter. A conifer at high latitude fixes carbon during a different annual window than a tree in subtropical conditions. Southern Hemisphere growing seasons cross the human calendar boundary differently from Northern Hemisphere seasons. Stored carbohydrates can sometimes be remobilized. Earlywood and latewood can contain carbon fixed at different times.

The event arrives in wood through a biological low-pass filter.

Yet the 774 scar remained abrupt enough to see.

This is one reason the event was so startling. Natural radiocarbon variation was familiar. The eleven-year solar cycle leaves structure. Grand solar minima leave structure. Changes in geomagnetic shielding leave structure. But an abrupt rise of roughly twelve per mil from one annual ring to the next was not ordinary modulation. The production pulse had to be substantial.

The size of the pulse immediately created a modelling problem.

Early discussion produced estimates that seemed to differ dramatically. Some calculations implied an astonishingly large global production of carbon-14; later work using different carbon-cycle assumptions produced smaller figures. Even the 2012 paper eventually carried an author comment explaining a factor-of-four definitional issue in comparing production per unit area. This is the kind of technical dispute that popular accounts often flatten into “scientists were wrong at first.” That is usually a lazy description of model refinement.

If you are reconstructing an event that occurred before particle detectors, you cannot place a Geiger counter in 774. You have a concentration change in wood. To infer the particle fluence that produced it, you need a model of atmospheric nuclear production and a model of the carbon cycle. You need to know how much newly produced radiocarbon is diluted among reservoirs before a tree fixes it. You need a plausible particle energy spectrum, because particles of different energies produce cosmogenic nuclides differently. You need geomagnetic conditions, because Earth’s magnetic field filters charged particles by rigidity and latitude.

Each layer adds physics. Each layer adds uncertainty.

The mistake would be to treat that uncertainty as if it erases the observation. It does not. The measured step in radiocarbon is the hard object. The inferred solar particle spectrum is one level further away. The exact flare or coronal mass ejection configuration is further still.

This book will keep those levels separate.

The word *cosmic ray* also causes trouble because it sounds like one thing. It is a historical umbrella. Galactic cosmic rays are mostly high-energy charged particles arriving from beyond the solar system, their flux at Earth modulated by solar magnetic activity and Earth’s magnetic field. Solar energetic particles are accelerated in eruptive solar events and associated shocks. Both can produce atmospheric nuclear cascades. Their energy spectra and time behavior differ. The phrase “cosmic-ray event” can therefore describe the terrestrial signature without settling which population was responsible.

For 774 and 993, the evidence moved strongly toward the Sun as multiple nuclides were brought together.

Carbon-14 is only one cosmogenic isotope. Beryllium-10 is produced in the atmosphere and can be deposited into polar ice. Chlorine-36 is another useful product. Their production responds differently to the energy spectrum of incoming particles. If a candidate event appears in carbon-14 and appropriate peaks appear in beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 around the same time, a solar-particle interpretation becomes much harder to replace with an explanation that only fits one isotope.

In 2015, Mekhaldi and colleagues used multi-radionuclide records to argue that the 774/775 and 993/994 events were most likely produced by extreme solar events with a hard proton spectrum. That word *hard* has nothing to do with difficulty. In particle physics it means relatively enriched in high-energy particles. High-energy protons matter because they penetrate deeper and produce particular cosmogenic signatures. The ratio among nuclides becomes evidence about the shape of a particle population that no instrument recorded directly.

It is an archaeological reconstruction of radiation.

The analogy is not perfect, but it is useful. A historical archaeologist may infer how a building burned from char patterns, collapsed walls, melted glass, and the distribution of debris. A space physicist working on a Miyake event infers the ancient particle environment from carbon in trees and isotopes in ice. Neither has the event itself. Both have residues shaped by transport.

Trees have an advantage that ice does not always share: annual architecture can be startlingly exact.

Dendrochronology is built on the fact that ring widths respond to environmental conditions in patterns that can be cross-matched among trees in a region. One drought year may make a narrow ring across many individuals. A favorable season may produce a wider ring. Overlapping living, dead, archaeological, and subfossil wood can extend a chronology backward. When the overlaps are strong and replicated, every ring can receive a calendar year.

But the chemistry inside those known rings adds an independent dimension. The ring-width chronology says *this is 775*. The radiocarbon measurement says *this year contains a global atmospheric jump*. Once that jump is replicated, it can be carried elsewhere as a marker.

The event therefore sits at the intersection of two very different kinds of pattern recognition.

Dendrochronology compares morphology through time: widths, anatomical features, sometimes densities or isotopes. Miyake-event dating compares a chemical excursion. The first can build an annual sequence from shared climate response. The second can pin a sequence to a global atmospheric event even when local ring-width cross-dating is unavailable.

The combination is stronger than either alone.

There is an important catch. Trees do not all record atmospheric carbon identically.

A leaf fixes CO2 during photosynthesis. The carbon incorporated into structural wood can include newly fixed carbon and, depending on species and conditions, some stored carbohydrates from previous seasons. Different tissues form at different times. A tree growing near its climatic limit can produce a ring so narrow that sampling without contamination becomes hard. Severe stress can cause a locally absent ring. Some species make false rings when growth stops and restarts within a season. Fire, insects, frost, or defoliation can alter anatomy. The ring boundary that looks obvious at normal scale may become ambiguous when a scientist needs thirty milligrams of uncontaminated cellulose from a strip narrower than a fingernail scratch.

This is why the global replication work was not merely a victory lap after the 2012 discovery. It was a stress test of whether the scar behaved like a global atmospheric marker when biology was allowed to be biology.

The COSMIC collaboration gathered material from long tree-ring records around the world. Researchers rechecked ring boundaries, isolated individual annual material, chemically prepared it, and measured hundreds of samples using accelerator mass spectrometry. The 774 and 993 excursions appeared coherently across the network, with hemispheric differences that carbon-cycle modelling could explain rather than ignore.

The details are almost comically physical. Rings had to be cut with scalpels. Very narrow growth meant tiny samples. Contamination by neighboring rings could blur the annual transition. Samples traveled from tree-ring archives to preparation laboratories to accelerator facilities. The global cosmic event, in practice, was reconstructed by people labeling slivers of old wood.

Scientific grandeur often arrives through logistics.

Accelerator mass spectrometry deserves more space than it usually gets in popular treatments because it changed what radiocarbon could ask. Traditional radiometric counting waits for radioactive carbon-14 atoms to decay and detects the emissions. AMS instead counts isotope ratios by accelerating ions and separating them by mass and charge through electromagnetic fields and detectors. The method can work with dramatically smaller samples and shorter measurement times. It does not wait patiently for a rare atom to die. It sorts atoms.

That distinction opened annual tree rings to high-precision campaigns that would once have been impractical or destructive.

A mass spectrometer has no idea that it is dating Vikings. It measures ratios. The narrative enters later.

This separation between measurement and story is one of the healthiest features of the field. At L’Anse aux Meadows, the instrument did not say “Norse.” Archaeological context established that pieces of wood had been modified with metal tools in a site already understood as Norse. Ring sequences and the 993 marker established when the trees had been cut. The historical conclusion emerged from joining independent arguments.

At Por-Bajin, the accelerator did not know Manichaeism. It located a radiocarbon pattern. Tree anatomy and bark established felling. Archaeology connected the beam to the foundation. Political and religious history then became newly constrained by the resulting date.

The same atom can therefore live several intellectual lives.

For a nuclear physicist, carbon-14 is a reaction product. For an atmospheric scientist, it is a tracer moving through reservoirs. For a tree physiologist, it is carbon incorporated into tissue. For a geochronologist, it is a calibration signal. For an archaeologist, it is a date. For a historian, it may decide between political narratives. For a space-weather engineer, it is evidence that the Sun has produced particle environments outside the instrument record.

The most interesting science often happens when nobody owns the object completely.

There is another reason to begin with production rather than archaeology. The phrase *solar storm* is too loose for the later hazard discussion unless we build better vocabulary now.

A solar flare is a rapid release of electromagnetic radiation from the Sun’s atmosphere. A coronal mass ejection is a large expulsion of plasma and magnetic field into space. Solar energetic particles can be accelerated in association with eruptions and shocks. A geomagnetic storm is Earth’s magnetospheric response to solar-wind and magnetic-field conditions, especially sustained southward interplanetary magnetic field that couples efficiently with Earth’s field. These phenomena can occur together, but their amplitudes do not scale in a simple one-to-one way.

The Carrington event of 1859 is famous because of its geomagnetic effects: aurorae at unusual latitudes, telegraph disruptions, and magnetic observations. A Miyake event is identified primarily through cosmogenic nuclide production, which tells us about high-energy particle fluence. Saying that 774 was “bigger than Carrington” without specifying *bigger in what quantity* is scientifically sloppy.

Was the proton fluence larger? Almost certainly, according to many reconstructions. Was the geomagnetic disturbance larger? We do not know from the radiocarbon scar alone. Did it involve a stronger white-light flare? Unknown. Would it induce larger currents in modern power grids? That depends on the CME and magnetic configuration, not merely on the particles that made carbon-14. Would it pose a greater radiation hazard to astronauts or high-altitude aviation? The particle spectrum becomes directly relevant.

One ancient event can therefore be simultaneously a superb archaeological timestamp and an incomplete engineering scenario.

That is not a contradiction. It is a reminder that evidence can be strong for one question and weak for another.

The carbon factory in the atmosphere gives us the hard core. Energetic particles entered. Nuclear cascades occurred. Carbon-14 production rose abruptly. Atmospheric transport distributed the signal. Trees fixed the carbon. Later scientists measured it. The event was large enough to rise above ordinary variability and coherent enough to replicate across distant records.

Everything after that requires interpretation.

Even the word *event* contains an assumption about duration. Annual tree rings compress whatever happened within the year. Subannual sampling can narrow timing, and modelling of Northern and Southern Hemisphere uptake can constrain seasons. But the physical particle episode might have lasted hours, days, or consisted of multiple injections. Ice deposition can smear production differently. The carbon cycle smooths the pulse. A sharp annual step is not a direct photograph of the Sun’s time profile.

The atmosphere is both recorder and editor.

That makes it a useful teacher for the rest of the book. We are going to encounter many records that feel direct because they are precise: a ring count, a date, a year in a chronicle, a radiocarbon value. None is direct in the philosophical sense. Every record has a transfer function. Trees turn atmospheric CO2 into cellulose. Ice turns atmospheric aerosols into layers. Chroniclers turn witnessed or inherited events into language. Archaeological deposits turn behavior into stratigraphy. Instruments turn isotope ratios into numbers through calibration standards and corrections.

Precision comes from understanding those transformations, not pretending they do not exist.

The 774/775 scar is powerful precisely because so many transformations can be modelled and cross-checked. Tree rings in distant regions show the rise. Other cosmogenic isotopes respond. Carbon-cycle models reproduce the broad timing. Solar-particle spectra can explain the nuclide ratios. No medieval observer had to name the event correctly. No government had to record it. No archive had to preserve a parchment.

The atmosphere made its own archive.

And because living things breathe the atmosphere into structure, the archive was copied into forests.

That copying is what turned nuclear physics into chronology.

A burst of particles becomes neutrons. Nitrogen becomes carbon-14. Carbon becomes carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide becomes wood. Wood becomes a beam. A beam becomes a ruin. A ruin becomes a sample. A sample becomes graphite in an accelerator. A ratio becomes a spike. A spike becomes 775. A counted sequence becomes 777 or 1021 or 5140 BCE.

Nothing in that chain is magic. Every link can fail.

The astonishing part is that sometimes the chain survives.
