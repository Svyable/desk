# A Clock That Smears

Radiocarbon dating has the peculiar reputation of being both miraculous and disappointingly vague.

Ask somebody outside archaeology what carbon dating does and the answer is usually something like this: you send an ancient object to a laboratory and the laboratory tells you how old it is. The imagined output looks like a receipt. *This bone: 3,417 years old.* *This basket: 842 BC.* *This timber: Thursday.*

The real output is a probability distribution built from a measurement, a calibration curve, assumptions about reservoirs and sample history, laboratory uncertainty, and whatever archaeological information is allowed into the model. The method can be extraordinarily powerful while refusing to behave like a birthday certificate.

That refusal is not a defect. It comes from the atmosphere.

If atmospheric carbon-14 had always been constant, a measured radiocarbon age could be translated to calendar age through radioactive decay with relative simplicity. It has not been constant. Solar modulation, geomagnetic variation, carbon-cycle changes, and other effects make the atmospheric ratio wander. A sample whose measured radiocarbon age looks like 2,500 radiocarbon years can correspond to more than one possible calendar interval because the calibration curve sometimes bends backward, flattens, or revisits similar values.

Archaeologists learned to live inside those probability shapes.

A calibrated result might occupy a range of decades or centuries. Multiple disjoint calendar intervals can carry significant probability. A plateau in the calibration curve can make additional laboratory precision buy surprisingly little calendar precision. If the curve is flat, measuring the vertical coordinate more exactly does not tell you where you are along the horizontal axis.

This is one of those ideas that becomes clearer with a physical analogy. Imagine hiking along a mountain trail with an altimeter but no map. If altitude increases steadily with distance, knowing your elevation tells you roughly where you are. If the trail traverses a long level bench, the same elevation persists for kilometers. A perfect altimeter cannot decide which point on the bench you occupy.

The calibration curve contains benches.

It also contains wiggles. Those wiggles can be useful if you have a sequence.

Suppose a beam contains fifty annual rings. You know ring 10 is exactly twenty years before ring 30. If you measure several rings at known separations, you are no longer asking where one radiocarbon value fits the calibration curve. You are asking where an entire pattern of values, spaced by known intervals, best matches the curve. This is wiggle matching. The internal clock of the tree supplies relative time; the calibration curve supplies the possible absolute positions.

Long sequences can constrain dates dramatically.

Miyake events turn a wiggle into a cliff.

The difference matters because a sudden radiocarbon production event creates a distinctive local pattern that can be matched at annual scale. If a sequence crosses the 774/775 jump, and if the rise is large enough relative to measurement uncertainty and background variation, the sequence can be locked to that year. Then ring counting outward may yield an exact calendar date for the outermost preserved ring.

The method feels like cheating because the conventional calibration problem has been reversed. Ordinarily, atmospheric variation is the nuisance that forces radiocarbon ages to be calibrated. Here, an extreme atmospheric variation becomes the solution.

The noise becomes a barcode.

That is the phrase I wrote in the margin the first time the logic fully landed for me. It is too neat to carry the whole argument, but it captures the inversion. Radiocarbon pioneers wanted to correct for atmospheric instability. High-resolution researchers discovered that some instability was so abrupt and globally coherent that it could function as an identifier.

The history of calibration is full of these reversals. Tree rings were initially among the materials used to test radiocarbon dating because their calendar age could be known independently. Disagreement between radiocarbon ages and tree-ring ages revealed that the assumption of constant atmospheric carbon-14 was inadequate. Dendrochronology then became the backbone of calibration. Decades later, radiocarbon returned the favor: a sharp isotopic event can now anchor a tree-ring chronology that is internally ordered but not connected to an absolute regional master.

The clock calibrates the clock that calibrates the clock.

This recursive relationship is not a philosophical flourish. It explains why chronology science is so obsessed with independent anchors. A tree-ring sequence can be internally perfect and absolutely wrong. A calibration curve can be statistically elegant and locally inappropriate. A historical text can contain an exact regnal year that rests on a copied king list with a missing co-regency. A volcanic layer can be chemically distinctive but miscorrelated to the wrong eruption. Every clock benefits from another clock built on different failure modes.

Miyake events offer one of those differently built clocks.

Their strength is not that radiocarbon suddenly becomes exact. Their strength is that a rare atmospheric production pulse can create a recognizable shape at a known time. The exactness comes from finding the shape in sequential material.

This distinction is essential because it prevents a common misunderstanding: you cannot take any piece of eighth-century charcoal, measure its carbon-14 once, and expect the machine to say 775. A single measurement of mixed or unknown-age wood will not reveal an annual spike in a useful way. You need a sequence of rings. You often need preliminary dating good enough to know where in the sequence to spend expensive annual measurements. You need enough samples around the suspected event to distinguish a genuine rise from laboratory noise and ordinary variation. You need the ring positions relative to the bark or another archaeological horizon if you want the marker to date human activity rather than merely date part of a tree.

The extraordinary precision is conditional.

Por-Bajin illustrates the conditions beautifully because the sample was nearly ideal. Researchers had a foundation beam with dozens of preserved rings and bark. Conventional or wiggle-match dating placed the sequence broadly enough that the 775 event was plausible. They measured selected annual rings near the expected position. The radiocarbon pattern identified the event in ring 43. Two rings remained to the outer edge. Anatomical inspection showed the final ring was incomplete. This allowed not only the year of felling, 777, but the season to be inferred.

The method did not turn a random timber into a calendar. The timber already contained a relative calendar. The event supplied a name for one page.

At L’Anse aux Meadows, the logic was similarly disciplined. Researchers selected pieces of wood from archaeological contexts associated with Norse activity and showing metal-tool modification. The wood retained bark edges or waney edges. Annual ring sequences reached back across the 993/994 event. The team identified the cosmogenic signature and counted outward to the last ring. Three pieces from three trees converged on 1021 CE.

That convergence matters more than the romance of the number.

One sample can be odd. Three independent trees with the same inferred cutting year are harder to dismiss as reused old wood or a counting accident. The archaeological context already told researchers Europeans had been present; the isotope method sharpened *when* at least some wood-working occurred.

A good exact date does not arrive alone. It arrives in a web.

The same is true at Dispilio, where the challenge was deeper in time. The Neolithic juniper chronology contained 303 annual rings but floated in calendar time. Conventional wiggle matching narrowed its placement enough to search for the 5259 BCE event. Researchers then performed 115 single-ring carbon-14 measurements across four selected wood samples to locate the excursion. Once identified, the master chronology could be fixed to absolute time, with its final ring at 5140 BCE.

The number of measurements is worth noticing. “They found a Miyake event” compresses an enormous amount of laboratory work. More than a hundred individual rings were chemically prepared and measured to lock one chronology. Exactness is expensive.

This is part of why Miyake-event dating will not instantly replace ordinary archaeology. The method is high-value where the question justifies the effort and the samples preserve the right structure. Most excavation deposits contain organic material with messy relationships to human events. Charcoal from a hearth may come from the heartwood of a centuries-old tree. A seed dates the season it grew but offers no long ring sequence. Bone dates an organism but not necessarily a construction. Reused timber can predate a wall. Old wood can sit in a landscape before being burned. A beautiful laboratory age cannot repair a bad sample-to-event relationship.

Chronologists call versions of this the old-wood problem, but the deeper issue is association.

What exactly is being dated?

The question sounds elementary and is often where the entire historical argument lives. At Por-Bajin, the foundation beam matters because it is physically tied to initial construction and because local timber availability makes long-distance reuse less plausible. At L’Anse aux Meadows, tool marks and context tie the wood to Norse activity, but even then the date is properly a date for wood cutting, not a complete occupational history. At Dispilio, the final ring anchors a floating chronology, which can then organize multiple structures and phases, but the archaeological interpretation still depends on how timbers were used.

A calendar year can be precise and still be semantically modest.

This may sound like academic caution for its own sake. It is not. Exact dates have unusual rhetorical power. A range such as 990–1050 invites interpretation. A date like 1021 feels like fact in a harder sense. The human mind treats sharp numbers as if the sharpness propagates into every nearby claim. If the wood was cut in 1021, perhaps the settlement began in 1021. Perhaps a voyage happened in 1021. Perhaps a saga episode belongs to 1021. Perhaps a named historical person was present.

None follows automatically.

Precision in measurement can create overconfidence in narrative.

I suspect this will become one of the recurring hazards as single-year radiocarbon work spreads. Archaeology has spent decades developing statistical habits around uncertainty. Bayesian chronological models explicitly combine radiocarbon likelihoods with stratigraphic information and prior knowledge. Researchers have learned, often painfully, not to make a single date carry an entire cultural transition. Then a method arrives that can sometimes produce an exact year. The temptation is to return to the old heroic style of chronology: pin a date, attach a king, declare the mystery solved.

The better use is more interesting.

Exact anchors can strengthen probabilistic models rather than replace them. Once one point in a sequence is fixed, relative dating on either side becomes more informative. A floating dendrochronology can become absolute. Stratigraphic phases can be measured in actual years. Trade goods can be placed before or after a narrow threshold. Construction episodes can be compared with political events whose chronology is independently known. Environmental proxies can be synchronized with human activity.

A single pin can tighten an entire net.

Ribe shows a different version of this. The early medieval trading settlement in Denmark contains stratified urban archaeology where historical change can happen quickly. Conventional radiocarbon ranges are often too broad to resolve commercial phases that archaeologists can see in artifact distributions. Researchers built annual radiocarbon data and combined it with stratigraphy to anchor the sequence tightly enough to trace changes in long-distance trade around the beginning of the Viking Age. Here the point was not a cinematic exact day. It was making radiocarbon useful at the temporal scale of an urban marketplace.

The calibration curve had once been too blunt for that job.

IntCal20 represents part of the broader transformation. The international calibration curve incorporates much higher-resolution tree-ring data and methods designed to preserve real annual structure rather than smoothing everything into decadal blocks. This matters even when no dramatic Miyake event is involved. Annual measurements reveal fine structure that can improve calibration and expose regional or short-term differences.

But the curve itself should not be imagined as a single sacred line descending from a standards committee. It is a statistical construction from many datasets, laboratories, tree species, regions, archives, and measurement campaigns. Data have uncertainty. Some samples represent single rings; others combine multiple years. Marine and Southern Hemisphere curves differ from Northern Hemisphere terrestrial calibration. Methodology evolves.

Calibration is a living argument about the past atmosphere.

Miyake events are conspicuous punctuation marks in that argument.

They also expose a philosophical tension in chronology. Historians often want an absolute answer: when did this happen? Physical dating methods usually return distributions. Dendrochronology can return annual sequences but only when cross-dating succeeds. Textual chronologies can return exact dates but may rest on uncertain transmission. Astronomical retrocalculation can be exact if the ancient observation is identified correctly. Every method trades one kind of confidence for another.

A Miyake marker looks unusually satisfying because it combines physical measurement with annual sequence. It feels like an independent cosmic stamp.

Yet even the stamp has typography.

Different papers label the canonical events differently. The first is often called the 774/775 CE event because the dramatic increase is measured between annual rings assigned to those years. Some authors call it the 775 event. Southern Hemisphere wood can fix carbon on a growth-year convention that crosses calendar years. Subannual measurements and atmospheric modelling can place production in a season that does not map neatly onto the ring label used in every region. The second event appears as 993/994 or 992/993 in older literature, complicated further by the published correction after the missing ring was found.

Exact-year science has an ironic amount of argument over which year to print in the title.

This is not evidence of failure. It is evidence that “year” is itself an interface between natural cycles and human numbering systems.

The tree ring spans a biological growing season. A calendar year spans January through December because we say so. A southern tree may begin growth late in one numbered year and finish early in the next. Carbon produced in the stratosphere may enter the troposphere with delay. The physical event may occur late enough in one growing season that some trees record most of the increase in the next ring.

Calendar precision requires calendar humility.

The methodological future is therefore not simply “find more spikes.” It is to build annual radiocarbon maps dense enough that many kinds of short-term atmospheric structure become usable. Strong Miyake events are the easiest landmarks. Smaller excursions may also become informative if measurement precision, replication, and models improve. But the smaller the feature, the more dangerous natural background variation becomes.

A 2026 study by Ilya Usoskin and colleagues sharpened that warning. Solar-cycle modulation of galactic cosmic rays creates real annual radiocarbon structure. In realistic data, background variability can distort the apparent shape of a weak extreme solar-particle event or potentially mimic one. This creates a floor that cannot be beaten simply by buying a more precise accelerator. If nature supplies structured noise, measurement precision alone does not make classification perfect.

The implication is healthy: a candidate event should earn its status.

Find it in more than one tree. Prefer more than one region. Look for independent cosmogenic isotopes where possible. Model expected production and transport. Do not turn every bump into a named catastrophe because catastrophes publish well.

The strongest events are valuable precisely because they stand far enough above that ambiguity.

This returns us to the strange double life of calibration. Radiocarbon dating was once forced to become less naive because tree rings showed that atmospheric carbon-14 varied. Now the most violent variations allow radiocarbon to become more exact than the smoothed curve once permitted.

A nuisance became a calibration curve.

A calibration anomaly became a timestamp.

A timestamp became 777, 1021, 5140 BCE.

The clock smears because the atmosphere moves. It sharpens, occasionally, because the atmosphere jumps.
