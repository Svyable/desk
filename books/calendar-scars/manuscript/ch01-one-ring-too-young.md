# One Ring Too Young

A tree does not know what year it is.

This sounds obvious until you spend enough time around chronology. We draw lines through history and label them 774, 775, 776. The tree has no use for any of that. It responds to light, temperature, water, soil, injury, insects, competition. In a temperate place it lays down wood in a seasonal rhythm, and if the rhythm is kind and the anatomy legible, a human can later count the result. One ring. Another ring. Another. The calendar is ours. The sequence is the tree’s.

What happened in Japan around the middle of the eighth century entered that sequence as chemistry.

Fusa Miyake and colleagues were not looking for a lost emperor or a secret city. They were measuring carbon-14 in annual rings from Japanese cedar. Radiocarbon scientists already knew that atmospheric carbon-14 changes through time. That fact is why radiocarbon ages have to be calibrated in the first place. The atmosphere is not a vat into which carbon-14 is poured at a perfectly constant rate. Solar activity changes the cosmic-ray environment. Earth’s magnetic field changes. The carbon cycle moves material among atmosphere, biosphere, and ocean. Those effects are the reason calibration curves exist.

But calibration curves, especially the older ones available when Miyake’s group began looking closely, were built at a resolution that could make a violent one-year event look like a gentle decade. Imagine averaging the height of ocean water over ten years and then asking whether a tsunami occurred on a Tuesday. The average is real. It is simply answering another question.

The Japanese team went back to individual rings.

Their 2012 paper in *Nature* reported an abrupt increase in atmospheric radiocarbon between the rings assigned to AD 774 and AD 775. The rise was about twelve parts per thousand in Δ14C, far larger than ordinary year-to-year behavior expected from normal solar modulation. The result was not a long hill. It was a step. Something had changed the production of cosmogenic radiocarbon quickly enough that a tree could write the transition into adjacent years.

That is the moment this book begins, because it is difficult to overstate how odd the result was without overstating what it meant.

The first temptation was astronomical drama. A supernova? A gamma-ray burst? Some kind of solar event beyond the range of modern experience? The original paper considered possible causes and found problems with the obvious versions. There was no famous supernova blazing in the eighth-century sky with the sort of historical record one would expect. A nearby stellar explosion energetic enough to make the isotope anomaly ought to leave other consequences. A sufficiently exotic gamma-ray burst could be made to fit some parts of the problem on paper, but “could” and “did” are different verbs.

The discovery did something more important than produce a mystery. It created a target.

Once a one-year chemical scar exists in known-age wood, every other archive can be asked the same question. Do German trees show it? Russian? North American? New Zealand? Does ice from Greenland or Antarctica contain an independent cosmogenic signal near the same time? Does chlorine-36 behave as it should under a shower of energetic particles? Does beryllium-10 rise? Does the timing fit the way radiocarbon moves from the upper atmosphere into the troposphere and then into cellulose?

A strange result becomes science when it begins surviving people who have reasons to break it.

The scar survived.

By 2018, an international collaboration had measured 484 individual tree rings from records spanning five continents around the two best-known events, the one in the 770s and another in the 990s. The 774 signal was coherent across hemispheres, though not identical in amplitude because the atmosphere, oceans, growing seasons, and carbon reservoirs are not identical across hemispheres. The researchers could model the 774 production pulse as occurring within the boreal growing season, probably around summer. An atmospheric event with no interest in human calendars had left a globally recoverable calendar marker.

The phrase *Miyake event* came to describe this class of abrupt radiocarbon production excursions. Like many scientific names that sound tidy after the fact, it risks hiding the disorder that made the discovery interesting. We do not yet have a direct instrumental observation of an event equal to the largest of these ancient spikes. We have proxies. We have models. We have multiple isotopes with different production and transport physics. We have tree physiology. We have ice chronology. We have a Sun that is not obliged to sample the full range of its behavior during the few decades in which humans have watched it with modern particle detectors.

What we have, in other words, is exactly the kind of evidence that deserves both excitement and manners.

The radiocarbon atom itself is the first strange participant. Ordinary carbon is mostly carbon-12, with carbon-13 as a stable minority isotope. Carbon-14 is radioactive. High-energy particles entering the atmosphere produce secondary neutrons, which can convert nitrogen-14 into carbon-14. The new carbon eventually becomes carbon dioxide. Plants pull that carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. In a tree, carbon fixed during a growing season becomes part of that year’s wood.

A burst of sufficiently energetic particles therefore can leave something like a timestamp distributed across forests.

Not a visible burn. Not a ring wider than its neighbors in some universal way. Not a mark that a medieval carpenter would have noticed when he cut the timber. The signal sits in isotope ratios, accessible only after a chain of developments that the tree could not anticipate: nuclear physics, radiocarbon dating, accelerator mass spectrometry, dendrochronology, and the willingness to spend expensive machine time on one annual slice after another.

That last part matters. The discovery was partly conceptual and partly technological. Radiocarbon dating was born as a method that could tolerate much larger samples than modern accelerator systems require. As accelerator mass spectrometry improved, laboratories could measure smaller amounts of carbon with useful precision. A tree ring stopped being merely one visual band in a chronology and became a chemically interrogable year.

The old calibration curve was not wrong. It was blurry by design.

That distinction will recur throughout this book because new precision has a bad habit of making old knowledge look foolish. Archaeologists did not wait for Miyake events to discover chronology. Dendrochronologists had already built annual sequences extending thousands of years. Radiocarbon calibration had already transformed prehistoric dating. Historians had texts, coins, regnal lists, stratigraphy, typology, inscriptions, astronomy, volcanic layers, and a thousand other clocks of unequal quality. The new method did not invent time. It supplied a new kind of pin.

Pins matter most where sequences float.

Suppose an archaeological site preserves a beam with one hundred annual growth rings. The internal order is exact: ring 43 precedes ring 44, which precedes ring 45. If the bark edge survives, the last ring may be the year the tree was felled or nearly so. Yet unless that one-hundred-year pattern can be cross-dated against an established regional tree-ring chronology, the sequence can remain detached from the absolute calendar. We know the years relative to one another but not their names.

Conventional radiocarbon can narrow the possibilities. Wiggle matching can use multiple samples from known positions in the sequence to find where the shape best fits the calibration curve. But calibration has plateaus and wiggles. Probability distributions can leave decades of room.

Then imagine that ring 43 contains the unmistakable 775 scar.

The sequence snaps into place.

Ring 43 is 775. Ring 44 is 776. Ring 45 is 777. If ring 45 ends in earlywood and the bark is still there, a dendrochronologist may be able to say not merely that the tree died in 777 but that it was felled during a particular part of the growing season.

That is not a hypothetical trick invented for an explanatory paragraph. It is close to what happened at Por-Bajin, a walled eighth-century complex on an island in a lake in southern Siberia. A timber from the foundation preserved the relevant sequence. Researchers found the 775 radiocarbon signal in the forty-third ring and counted outward. The tree had been felled in 777. The anatomy of the terminal ring narrowed the season. The construction date, previously uncertain enough to sustain competing historical interpretations, became summer 777.

A storm at the Sun had become evidence about an Uyghur building project.

This is the collision I care about. The physics did not merely stay in physics. The isotope anomaly moved sideways into archaeology. At L’Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, the 993 event became an anchor from which researchers counted outward to bark edges in pieces of wood cut by metal tools. The answer was 1021 CE. Norse people were working wood in North America exactly one thousand years before the paper appeared in 2021.

Again, the method did not prove the entire saga tradition. It did not tell us the size of the settlement, the duration of occupation, who held the axe, what that person called the place, or how many voyages crossed the Atlantic. It did something narrower and harder: it put human activity there in a specific calendar year.

There is a peculiar authority to an exact year. It can make a reader forget everything it does not say.

This is where the book needs its brakes. A beam can be older than the building it enters. Timber can be reused. Bark can be absent. Heartwood can outlive sapwood. A charred fragment can lose the outer rings that would connect growth to use. A sample can be contaminated. A tree can locally miss a ring. A chronology can contain a counting error. Carbon fixed by a plant can carry regional or seasonal effects. An event can be too small to identify cleanly against background variability. A laboratory result can look beautiful and still be attached to the wrong archaeological question.

The second Miyake discovery provided an almost comic warning against triumphalism. In 2013, Miyake and colleagues reported another abrupt radiocarbon increase near the end of the tenth century. Later that year the journal published a correction. A missing tree-ring layer around AD 956 had been discovered in the sequence. Dates after that point had to move by one year. The event once described as 992–993 became 993–994.

The cosmos had not moved. The tree-ring count had.

I love this detail because it makes the method more trustworthy, not less. A field that publishes its missing ring is telling you something useful about how precision is earned. Exactness is not the absence of error. It is a set of procedures designed to expose error at scales where it matters.

The later global COSMIC collaboration made this explicit. Individual trees can contain missing rings, false rings, damaged boundaries, physiological carry-over, and microscopic ambiguities. The way out is replication. Cross-date many trees. Re-examine ring boundaries. Compare species. Compare hemispheres. Measure the isotope excursion in independent records. Ask whether a single sample is trying to bear more certainty than it can support.

There is a larger philosophical point here, but it is worth delaying because philosophy arrives too easily when the object is strange. Better to stay with the wood.

Take the sample preparation. A researcher needs to isolate a specific ring, sometimes less than a millimeter wide. Neighboring cells cannot casually bleed into the sample if the goal is to resolve an abrupt annual jump. The wood must be chemically prepared to isolate the carbon fraction of interest. The carbon is converted for measurement. An accelerator mass spectrometer separates and counts rare carbon-14 relative to stable isotopes. The resulting number is not read directly as “775.” It enters a chain of calibration, comparison, and modelling.

There is nothing mystical in the method. Its power comes from stacking ordinary disciplines until the intersection becomes extraordinary.

A physicist sees particle production. A dendrochronologist sees ring sequence. A chemist sees cellulose and contamination. An archaeologist sees context. A historian sees a date that may rescue or destroy an interpretation. A space-weather researcher sees an event in a part of the probability distribution that the satellite era has not directly sampled.

The tree sees none of them.

It grew.

That indifference is useful. Human chronicles select. They exaggerate victories, omit humiliations, copy earlier writers, convert weather into omen, and preserve the concerns of literate institutions. A tree ring is not objective in the simplistic sense; biology filters the atmosphere, growth varies, rings can be missing, carbon can be stored and remobilized. But the tree has no reason to flatter Charlemagne or defame a khan. It does not care whether a solar storm fits a theory. Its biases are physiological rather than political.

Different biases can be made to check one another.

Ice cores are valuable for the same reason. Beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 do not solve the problem by themselves. Deposition is messy. Ice chronologies have their own uncertainties. But when annual tree-ring carbon-14 and polar cosmogenic radionuclides tell compatible stories, the causal space narrows. By 2015, multi-radionuclide evidence made an extreme solar event the leading explanation for the 774/775 and 993/994 excursions. The particle spectrum inferred from the isotope ratios was hard, rich in high-energy protons. The ancient event did not need a hidden supernova.

The Sun was enough.

That sentence is scarier than the supernova.

A supernova is someone else’s star dying. An extreme solar particle event belongs to our own system. The same star that grows wheat and powers photosynthesis can occasionally throw high-energy particles into near-Earth space at intensities far beyond the events measured during the modern instrument era. The exact relationship between the isotope events, flares, coronal mass ejections, shocks, and geomagnetic storms remains a live problem. But the existence of the particle events is written into terrestrial archives.

A scar can become a clock because the event was global enough to synchronize distant matter.

That idea reaches beyond radiocarbon. Chronology has always depended on shared events. Volcanic ash can tie archaeological layers together. Eclipses can anchor ancient texts when descriptions are good enough. Ice chemistry can synchronize climate records. Nuclear weapons testing created the bomb pulse that dates modern biological material. The atmosphere is a courier. It carries events into places that never witnessed their source.

Miyake events are unusual because the courier delivers a timestamp into annual growth.

In 2024, researchers used the 5259 BCE event to anchor a 303-year floating juniper chronology from Dispilio, a Neolithic lakeside settlement in northern Greece. The final ring fell in 5140 BCE. That is a startling kind of precision for European Neolithic archaeology: not because every house can now be assigned a birthday, but because a local sequence that once floated can be locked to the absolute calendar. The event occurred thousands of years before anyone at Dispilio could have known what radiocarbon was. Their building timbers nevertheless stored the atmospheric disruption that modern scientists would later use as a ruler.

The ruler is incomplete. Known events are rare. The further back we go, the fewer continuous absolutely dated annual tree-ring chronologies survive. Candidate excursions are debated. Detection thresholds matter. A 2026 modelling study sharpened a problem researchers already understood: normal solar-cycle and galactic-cosmic-ray background variability can distort or even mimic weaker events in radiocarbon. Better measurement alone does not erase natural background structure.

This makes the strongest scars more valuable, not less.

The canonical events are large enough to be recognized across records. The 774 event in particular has become a chemical landmark. Around it, the history of timekeeping has acquired a new trick. Archaeologists can sometimes take an undated sequence, locate the scar, count outward, and arrive at a year. Solar physicists can take the same scar and count inward, asking what kind of event could have made it.

The two groups stand on opposite sides of the same ring.

One asks: what happened to the Sun?

The other asks: what were humans doing when the chemistry arrived?

The answers are not symmetrical. We know more securely that Por-Bajin’s timber was felled in 777 than we know the detailed solar eruption sequence that produced the 775 isotope signal. That asymmetry is delightful. A physical event whose mechanism remains incompletely constrained can still function as a superb chronological marker. You do not need to know the manufacturer of a bullet to match its rifling. You do not need a complete solar model to recognize a globally replicated radiocarbon step.

This book follows that odd division of labor.

There will be solar physics, because the scars need a source. There will be accelerator mass spectrometry, because exact dates do not emerge from wood by staring harder. There will be archaeology, because a dating technique earns its keep when it changes an argument about actual people. There will be error, because a one-year method makes one-year mistakes consequential. There will be space weather, because our infrastructure now reaches into the same near-Earth environment whose ancient extremes we reconstruct from dead trees.

And there will be a recurring rule: precision does not excuse imagination from evidence.

The temptation with a story like this is to make every old mystery surrender to the trees. It will not. You need wood. You need sequence. You need the right years. You need context. You need enough rings around the event to recognize the pattern. You need laboratory measurements. You need a reason to believe the dated growth corresponds to the human action you care about. Many archaeological questions will never meet those requirements.

But some do.

That is enough.

A cedar in Japan carried a discontinuity in carbon that researchers first saw because they measured years one by one. The discontinuity turned out not to belong to Japan. It was global. It was repeated. It was found in other isotopes. It pointed back toward the Sun. Then, almost as a side effect, it became a device for dating human things.

There are technologies invented on purpose and technologies discovered by changing what counts as noise.

The old calibration curve smoothed the scar because smoothing was appropriate to the problem it had been built to solve. Miyake’s work asked what happened when the averaging stopped. A one-year anomaly stepped out of a decadal record. Twelve years later, that style of measurement was fixing a European Neolithic chronology to 5259 BCE.

The tree still does not know what year it is.

We do, sometimes, because something hit the atmosphere hard enough to leave the year behind.
