# Ice Has Another Memory

A tree remembers carbon. Ice remembers fallout.

The two archives are born from the same atmospheric violence but store it differently enough to disagree in useful ways.

This matters because no single proxy deserves to become a religion. Carbon-14 in tree rings is unusually powerful for annual chronology. The ring itself supplies sequence. Photosynthesis ties atmospheric carbon to a growth season. Dendrochronology can connect the ring to a calendar. But carbon-14 is also routed through the carbon cycle, which smooths and redistributes a production pulse. If we infer the source only from carbon, some combinations of particle spectrum and production can mimic one another.

Polar ice gives us different isotopes and different problems.

Beryllium-10 is cosmogenic. High-energy particles generate atmospheric cascades that produce it, after which it attaches to aerosols and is transported and deposited. Chlorine-36 is produced through other atmospheric nuclear pathways and likewise enters ice. Unlike carbon dioxide, neither isotope is incorporated into tree cellulose through photosynthesis. Their production yields respond differently to particle energy.

A strong event appearing in all three systems is therefore harder to fake.

The 2015 multi-radionuclide study of the 774/775 and 993/994 events is one of the most important papers in this story because it moves the causal argument away from literary mystery. The researchers measured annually resolved beryllium-10 in Arctic and Antarctic ice cores and considered chlorine-36 alongside carbon-14. The resulting isotope pattern pointed strongly toward solar proton events with a hard energy spectrum.

This is what independent evidence looks like when nature supplies the experiment.

Carbon in a cedar and beryllium in Antarctic ice do not share the same laboratory history, biological physiology, or deposition pathway. A chronology error can still contaminate comparison, and atmospheric transport matters to both, but their failure modes are not identical. Agreement therefore carries more information than repeating the same carbon measurement in another tree.

The phrase *multi-proxy* is overused in climate and archaeological science to the point that it can sound like a grant-writing tic. Here it earns its keep.

Each cosmogenic nuclide has a production function. An incoming proton of a given energy contributes differently to the probability of producing carbon-14, beryllium-10, or chlorine-36. Lower-energy particles are filtered more strongly by the geomagnetic field and atmosphere. Very high-energy particles penetrate more effectively and generate secondary cascades. If an ancient event produces a particular ratio among nuclides, researchers can compare that ratio with modelled particle spectra.

The ice, in effect, becomes a crude spectrometer.

Crude is not dismissive. A spectrometer that operated twelve centuries before electronics deserves respect. But it is not a modern detector measuring particle energy event by event. Production models, geomagnetic field reconstructions, atmospheric transport, ice accumulation, and dating all stand between the source and the final inferred spectrum.

The transfer function is long.

This is where a useful scientific habit appears: separate *production* from *deposition*.

An extreme solar particle event can create beryllium-10 in the atmosphere quickly. The beryllium then has to attach to aerosols, move through the atmosphere, and be removed by precipitation or dry deposition. Weather therefore affects the ice-core signal. One ice core may show a sharper or larger peak than another even if the atmospheric production was global. Snow accumulation can vary. Layers can thin with depth. Diffusion and post-depositional processes can matter depending on the chemical species and site.

A peak in ice is not a direct histogram of incoming protons.

The same caution applies to carbon-14, but the mechanisms differ. That difference is the point.

Suppose a large carbon-14 rise appeared in trees but no corresponding beryllium-10 or chlorine-36 signal could be found in adequately dated, high-quality ice records. A solar proton interpretation would become less comfortable. Perhaps the carbon chronology is wrong. Perhaps the event is local to carbon-cycle behavior. Perhaps the ice chronology is offset. Perhaps the event spectrum is peculiar. The discrepancy would force work.

For 774 and 993, the work largely converged.

The beryllium and chlorine evidence supported high-energy solar particles, making nearby-supernova and gamma-ray scenarios increasingly unnecessary. An exotic explanation can survive almost anything if nobody demands comparative predictions. Multi-isotope data demand them.

If a gamma-ray burst produced the radiocarbon, what should the other nuclides do? If a solar proton event did it, what ratio should appear? Which explanation matches the sign, timing, and relative production without adding invisible machinery?

This is how mystery shrinks.

The remaining mystery becomes narrower: what solar conditions can generate such an extreme hard-spectrum particle event?

That is a much better question than “what cosmic thing happened?”

Ice also creates a chronological challenge that tree rings can help solve. Ice cores are dated by counting annual layers where possible, by identifying volcanic horizons, by modelling accumulation and flow, and by synchronizing chemical markers. At depth, annual layers thin. Counting uncertainty accumulates. Different cores can disagree by years around ancient events.

A globally synchronous cosmogenic production event can act as a tie point between ice and tree chronologies.

The flow goes both ways again. Trees give annual calendar precision. Ice gives independent radionuclides. The event lets one archive test the other.

This has consequences beyond solar physics. Paleoclimate research lives or dies on synchronization. If a volcanic sulfate layer in ice is dated a few years incorrectly, comparisons with tree-ring cooling, historical famine, or archaeological change can acquire false causal order. A global cosmogenic event provides a marker that is physically unrelated to volcanic deposition and therefore useful for checking age models.

The atmosphere accidentally manufactures synchronization pulses.

This is the broader idea behind *Calendar Scars*. A rare global event can become an interface among archives that otherwise speak different languages.

Trees speak annual growth.

Ice speaks deposited chemistry.

Lake sediments speak layers that may or may not be annual.

Corals speak growth bands and uranium-series clocks.

Historical documents speak human calendars.

Archaeological strata speak sequence without necessarily speaking years.

A globally distributed isotope pulse can sometimes translate.

But translation fails if chronology is forced. The temptation in cross-proxy science is to slide one record until the peaks line up. Once a researcher expects two features to correspond, visual alignment can become self-confirming. The correct use of a Miyake event is stronger: locate a distinctive independently measured production feature with a physically justified relationship among nuclides, then use it as a tie point with stated dating uncertainty.

The marker should constrain the chronology, not be created by the desire to synchronize it.

This seems obvious until you look at how much of historical science is built from wiggle matching of one kind or another.

The 774 event has even been used to interrogate seasonal timing. The global tree data show different uptake between hemispheres because seasons reverse. Carbon-cycle models can ask which month of production best reproduces the annual pattern. Subannual tree-ring samples divide earlywood from latewood. Ice deposition adds another temporal response.

When these systems agree, the production pulse can be narrowed to a season.

The result is a remarkable inversion of archive resolution. We do not have a dated medieval observatory report saying, “On July 14, the proton flux rose.” We have forests and glaciers. Yet by combining them, researchers can place the event roughly in boreal summer 774.

This precision should feel earned, not magical.

It also explains why candidate events are hard.

A strong event can rise above transport noise in multiple proxies. A weak one may vanish in ice because beryllium deposition variability is large relative to production. A modest carbon-14 excursion can be confused with ordinary solar-cycle modulation. Chronology uncertainty can spread a peak across neighboring years. Different archives have different detection thresholds.

Absence of a beryllium peak does not automatically mean absence of a solar event.

This is an important nuance in the current literature. Studies of modern solar proton events suggest many would be essentially undetectable in ice-core beryllium against natural variability. The proxy method is biased toward the spectacular end of the distribution. We are reconstructing the dinosaurs of space weather, not every mouse.

That bias shapes recurrence statistics.

If only the largest events reliably leave multi-isotope scars, the known catalog is a thresholded sample. Smaller events occur more often but blend into background. As measurement precision improves, some of that gap can be filled. Yet natural variability sets a limit: a perfect measurement of an ambiguous natural signal is still ambiguous.

The 2026 modelling work on radiocarbon background makes the same point from the carbon side. Solar-cycle variability can mimic or distort weak event profiles. The challenge is not just machine noise. Nature itself has structure.

The future event catalog therefore needs explicit detection probabilities.

A mature hazard estimate should not simply count named Miyake events and divide by years. It should ask, for an event of a given particle spectrum and fluence, what is the probability that existing tree-ring and ice archives would detect it at a given time? How much annual material has actually been measured? Where are the gaps? How does geomagnetic field strength alter production? How much does event season affect tree uptake?

This is harder than a headline recurrence interval and far more honest.

Ice introduces another variable: Earth’s magnetic field.

Charged particles approaching Earth are filtered by geomagnetic rigidity. When the magnetic field is weaker, lower-rigidity particles can penetrate regions they would otherwise be excluded from. Over millennia, field intensity changes. During major geomagnetic excursions such as Laschamps tens of thousands of years ago, shielding was dramatically reduced.

This complicates comparisons of event amplitude across deep time. A given solar particle fluence can produce a different terrestrial cosmogenic response under a different geomagnetic configuration.

A larger radiocarbon spike does not automatically mean a proportionally larger solar eruption.

The 7176 and 5259 BCE events produced annual carbon-14 rises that exceeded the 775 increase in raw atmospheric amplitude in some reconstructions. After correcting for geomagnetic field differences, their inferred particle strengths become more comparable to the largest canonical event. This is precisely why a leaderboard of “biggest storms in history” is mostly a way to advertise misunderstanding.

The Earth participates in the measurement.

The same event seen under a strong geomagnetic field and a weak one is not the same terrestrial experiment.

That becomes even more dramatic in the Late Glacial event literature. As researchers push the cosmogenic record toward times with different magnetic conditions and less secure annual chronologies, uncertainties expand. The deep record is invaluable, but it is not simply more of the same.

This is where ice earns another role: it extends beyond the reach of continuous living-tree chronologies.

Ancient ice can preserve cosmogenic isotopes far back in time. Tree-ring chronologies also extend deep through subfossil material, but assembling annual absolute sequences becomes increasingly difficult. The two archives overlap imperfectly, creating opportunities for cross-synchronization when distinctive global events appear.

The dream is a network of cosmic tie points through the Holocene and beyond.

That dream should not be confused with current completeness.

We have a handful of strong markers and a growing set of candidates. Some are disputed. Some have uncertain placement. Some appear clearly in carbon but ambiguously in beryllium. The event catalog is still research, not a finished table handed down by nature.

The fact that the catalog is unfinished is part of its excitement. Every long tree-ring archive contains years that may never have been measured individually for radiocarbon. Every high-quality ice core contains cosmogenic chemistry that can be revisited as age models improve. A signal discovered in one archive can tell researchers where to look in another.

Discovery becomes iterative.

The 774 scar sent scientists to ice.

The ice strengthened the solar interpretation.

The solar interpretation sharpened searches for older events.

Older events created new archaeological anchors.

Archaeological successes justified more annual radiocarbon work.

More annual radiocarbon data improved calibration and event detection.

This is how a niche anomaly becomes infrastructure.

There is a final reason I like the ice in this story. Trees feel alive and legible. They give us rings we can touch. Ice is more alien. It is accumulated weather compressed into a vertical archive, bubbles of ancient atmosphere and chemicals laid down by snowfall. Yet the same burst of particles reached both.

A larch in Siberia and snow falling in Antarctica had no ecological relationship. They shared a sky.

That shared sky is what lets us trust the event.

No archive is perfect. That is exactly why two are better.
