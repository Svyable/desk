# Floating Time

A chronology can be exact and homeless.

This is one of the stranger conditions in archaeology. You can possess a wooden sequence two hundred years long and know, with annual confidence, which ring came before which. You can say that one house timber grew thirty-seven years after another. You can identify drought years shared across beams. You can build a master sequence from overlapping timbers until centuries of relative time are locked together.

And still not know where those centuries sit on the calendar.

Dendrochronologists call such sequences floating chronologies.

The word *floating* makes them sound uncertain. Internally, they can be extremely precise. Their problem is not order but address.

Imagine receiving a long section of film cut from a movie whose beginning and ending you know well, but the frames in your hand do not overlap either. Every frame is in the right order. You can reconstruct the sequence perfectly. You simply cannot say whether it belongs twenty minutes or forty minutes into the film.

Archaeological wood does this all the time.

Regional tree-ring chronologies are built through overlap. A living tree provides recent rings with known calendar years. An older timber overlaps enough of the living sequence to extend backward. Another archaeological or subfossil sample overlaps that one. If the chain remains strong, annual dating can reach centuries or millennia into the past.

But gaps happen.

Species differ. Regions differ. Wood is not preserved continuously. A wet archaeological site may protect timbers beautifully while the centuries between that site and the established master chronology leave no surviving bridge. A mountain tree chronology may not cross-date reliably with lowland timber because the climatic limiting factors differ. The ring-width pattern in one ecological zone may not supply enough shared signal to connect to another.

The result is an island of annual time.

Radiocarbon wiggle matching can pull the island toward the mainland.

Measure a series of known ring positions across the floating sequence. Compare the pattern of radiocarbon ages with the calibration curve. Because the ring spacings are fixed, some possible calendar placements fit better than others. The longer and more structured the sequence, the stronger the constraint can become.

But the calibration curve has plateaus. Multiple placements can fit. Laboratory uncertainty broadens the solution. A floating chronology may narrow from centuries to decades and still refuse a single year.

A Miyake event is a bridge dropped from the sky.

If the floating sequence crosses a known abrupt radiocarbon excursion, annual measurements can identify the marker. The event assigns a calendar year to one ring. Every other ring inherits its year by counting.

This sounds almost embarrassingly simple after the laboratory work has been done.

The difficulty is finding the event without spending a fortune measuring every ring.

Researchers usually begin with coarse dating. Conventional radiocarbon or wiggle matching establishes a probable calendar range. If that range includes 775, 994, 5259 BCE, or another verified marker, annual samples are selected around the expected position. The measurements look for the sharp rise. Once the event is located, the entire sequence locks.

The approach resembles searching a long audio recording for a distinctive alarm tone. You do not need to identify every sound. You need enough approximate timing to know which section to scan, then a tone unique enough to recognize.

The atmosphere supplied the tone.

There is a second route. If a sequence is already approximately placed by archaeological or historical information, the Miyake event can test the proposed chronology. A marker appearing a year or two away from expectation may expose a ring-counting problem. A marker failing entirely can challenge the placement.

The method therefore anchors and audits.

This matters because ancient chronology contains many debates that look historical but are really about floating sequences.

Aegean and Near Eastern archaeology is full of them. Tree-ring sequences from Anatolia, the Levant, and the Mediterranean can preserve exquisite relative chronology yet struggle to connect absolutely across gaps. Radiocarbon wiggle matching has narrowed many sequences. Volcanic eruptions such as Thera provide potential marker horizons but come with their own dating controversies. Historical king lists and textual synchronisms can conflict with physical dating.

An exact cosmogenic marker inside the right wood can change the balance of evidence overnight.

That promise should be handled carefully. Miyake events are sparse. A floating chronology has to cross one. The wood must preserve annual rings sufficiently well. The event must be strong and independently known in that period. The sequence cannot be so short that multiple noisy variations resemble the target. And, most importantly, attaching the tree sequence to an archaeological culture still requires context.

Chronology is not a scavenger hunt where every spike wins a kingdom.

But the potential is enormous because absolute dates have nonlinear value.

Suppose an archaeological region contains several floating oak chronologies, each spanning a few centuries, with overlaps among sites. Fix one chronology to an exact year and the connected network may move with it. Suddenly the construction of houses, palisades, wells, boats, and roads can be compared year by year. Cultural phases once understood as broad centuries can become generations.

The archaeology changes scale.

This can reveal tempo.

A transition thought to take a century may occur within twenty years. Two innovations assumed simultaneous may separate by a generation. A settlement abandonment may precede a climatic event rather than follow it. Imported goods may arrive earlier than a historical narrative allows. A political regime may have inherited a building rather than founded it.

Causation often hides in order.

If event A and event B fall within the same hundred-year radiocarbon range, writers can tell almost any causal story. Put them five years apart in the wrong order and half the stories die.

This is why exact chronology can be intellectually violent.

It does not merely make dates prettier. It destroys narratives that depended on blur.

Por-Bajin is a perfect example, which is why it deserves its own chapter. Before the exact date, competing interpretations of the complex could attach it to different rulers and religious moments. Summer 777 did not answer every question, but it moved the building into a political window where one origin story became much stronger than others.

Dispilio may have even broader consequences because the site sits in the European Neolithic, where absolute annual anchors are rare. The 5259 BCE event fixed a 303-year juniper chronology. That one sequence can now serve as a regional chronological spine against which neighboring material can be compared.

The event is not merely dating one house. It is creating infrastructure for other dates.

Chronological infrastructure is an underappreciated scientific asset because it tends to disappear into later scholarship. Once a master chronology exists, future papers cite the dates without reliving the decades of work that built the sequence. A ring labeled 5140 BCE begins to look like a natural property of the timber.

It is not.

That label rests on measurement chains, archive curation, interlaboratory standards, cross-dating, calibration, statistical models, and in this case a cosmic production event.

The date is a technological achievement.

This is why old wood archives suddenly look more valuable in the Miyake era. A box of tree-ring samples collected in the 1970s may contain sequences spanning a cosmogenic event that nobody then had reason to measure annually for carbon-14. Archaeological timbers already excavated and stored in museum depots may be chronologically richer than when they were cataloged.

The new instrument changes the value of the old sample.

This happens across science. Ancient DNA transformed skeletal collections. Stable-isotope methods turned teeth into migration records. Proteomics extracted taxonomic information from fragments that could not preserve DNA. CT scanning revealed internal structures in fossils without destroying them. A new analytical technique does not merely generate new specimens. It reopens archives.

Miyake-event dating reopens wood.

That makes preservation policy unexpectedly important. Wet archaeological timber can degrade rapidly after excavation if not conserved. Subfossil wood can be discarded if it lacks obvious display value. Dendrochronology laboratories hold collections whose long-term funding may be precarious. A sample’s future scientific value is impossible to predict from the questions available when it is collected.

The cedar that answered a solar-physics question had no label saying it would later help archaeologists date Vikings.

Archive stewardship is therefore part of chronology.

There is also a geographic inequality in floating time. Europe and parts of North America have dense dendrochronological infrastructure, long research histories, and large wood archives. Other regions possess rich timber archaeology but far less laboratory investment. Exact dating methods can reproduce those disparities if access to accelerator mass spectrometry, conservation, and regional chronologies remains concentrated.

The physics is global. The ability to exploit it is not.

This matters because cosmogenic events could become tools for regions where written chronologies are sparse or externally imposed. A globally synchronous atmospheric marker does not privilege Roman regnal lists or European documentary archives. In principle, any suitable annual-growth material crossing the event can participate.

That could be scientifically liberating.

It could also invite extractive research if laboratories in wealthy countries treat archaeological wood elsewhere as isotope supply. The method does not solve the politics of archaeology. It inherits them.

The ethical question is not peripheral. Exact dates can affect cultural heritage claims, Indigenous histories, narratives of migration, and ownership of the past. Researchers should not confuse technical capacity with authority to interpret.

A ring can tell you when a tree grew. It cannot tell you whose story matters.

Floating chronologies also remind us that “absolute” dating is a term of art, not metaphysical certainty. An absolute chronology is one tied to the calendar rather than merely to relative sequence. It still carries uncertainty in sample association, year convention, laboratory measurement, and event identification.

The calendar itself is historical.

When we say a ring is 775 CE, we are projecting the proleptic Common Era numbering system onto a tree that grew before most people used anything like that system. That projection is useful and standardized, but it is ours.

The tree had seasons.

Humans later gave them numbers.

This becomes especially awkward in BCE chronology because there is no year zero in the traditional BC/AD system. Astronomical year numbering includes one. Software packages may differ. Papers can label an event by the year in which production began or the subsequent ring in which the concentration jump becomes most visible. A one-year error in a popular summary can arise from calendar convention rather than bad physics.

Exact chronology increases the need for boring metadata.

Which calendar convention?

Which ring-label convention?

Northern or Southern Hemisphere growth year?

Was bark present?

Was the terminal ring complete?

Did the wood come from heartwood, sapwood, or a combined sample?

Was the event identified in one tree or several?

Which reference curve?

These details are not footnote clutter. They are the machinery behind “1021.”

There is a temptation to think that a future annual radiocarbon atlas will make chronology automatic. Feed in a sequence, match the isotope pattern, receive a calendar.

Perhaps parts of the workflow will become automated. High-throughput AMS and statistical pattern matching could search for known markers. Machine-learning methods may help classify ring sequences or optimize which rings to measure. But archaeological dating will remain interpretive because the hardest question is rarely the isotope ratio.

The hardest question is what the wood means in context.

Was the tree felled for this structure?

Was the beam reused?

Did the outer rings survive?

Does the final growth year equal the construction year?

Does a burnt timber date destruction or merely the age of old lumber?

Was a ship repaired with older planks?

A perfect calendar attached to the wrong event is precisely wrong.

That sentence belongs on laboratory walls.

The promise of Miyake dating is not that it removes archaeology. It makes archaeological reasoning more consequential. Once the laboratory can give you a year, sample selection becomes even more important because the seduction of precision becomes stronger.

The best projects therefore begin before measurement. Archaeologists, dendrochronologists, and radiocarbon scientists choose contexts where the sequence can answer a meaningful question. Preliminary dates identify possible event windows. Wood anatomy establishes whether bark or waney edge is present. Multiple samples provide replication. Only then does the accelerator enter.

This collaboration is why the method feels more mature than a gimmick.

It was born from one field and became useful in another because nobody pretended one discipline could carry the inference alone.

Floating time is a problem of disconnected sequences. The solution is often another connection.

A tree in Siberia connects to a Japanese cedar through atmospheric carbon.

A Neolithic lake village connects to Alpine and Irish tree chronologies through a solar particle event more than seven thousand years ago.

A Norse-cut branch in Newfoundland connects to global atmospheric chemistry in 993.

The remarkable thing is not that time was floating.

It is that the sky left something sturdy enough to tie it down.
