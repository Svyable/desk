# Summer of 774

The year 774 is crowded with things people thought mattered.

Charlemagne was fighting Saxons. The Abbasid caliphate was governing an enormous territory from the Islamic world. Tang China was still recovering from the political and demographic trauma of the An Lushan rebellion. Monasteries copied texts, armies moved, taxes were collected, children were born, harvests came in or failed. Across continents, people lived inside calendars that did not agree with one another and usually had no reason to.

Somewhere in that human year, the near-Earth radiation environment changed violently.

Nobody left us a particle count.

That absence is the beginning of the scientific problem. A modern solar energetic-particle event arrives in a world of spacecraft, neutron monitors, radio instruments, coronagraphs, magnetometers, and operational warnings. The 774 event arrived in a world with eyes. Eyes are excellent instruments for some things and useless for others. A proton with hundreds of megaelectronvolts does not announce itself to a monk.

The trees took the measurement anyway.

The original Japanese cedar data showed the abrupt radiocarbon increase. Then the event began to spread geographically as other groups looked. Bristlecone pine in North America. European trees. Siberian material. New Zealand kauri. The exact details of amplitude and year labeling varied, but the central fact survived: this was not local contamination or a peculiar Japanese tree.

By the time the COSMIC collaboration assembled its global dataset, the event had become a planetary marker.

The project’s scale matters because extraordinary events are easy to romanticize when only one archive carries them. One tree can be wrong. One laboratory can carry a systematic offset. One local carbon reservoir can do something unexpected. One chronology can contain a missing ring. The way to turn an anomaly into an atmospheric event is to make it travel.

The 2018 *Nature Communications* study did that with 374 tree rings around the 770s from records at dozens of sites in both hemispheres. The trees belonged to multiple genera, grew across a wide latitudinal span, and came from different elevations and climates. Researchers did not simply collect published radiocarbon numbers. They went back to physical wood, rechecked ring boundaries, and prepared annual samples under a shared analytical program.

The scar crossed oceans.

This global coherence is one of the reasons I resist the phrase “mysterious spike.” The cause was mysterious in 2012. The existence of the production event is no longer mysterious in the same sense. The data have accumulated. Whatever else we argue about, a substantial cosmogenic radiocarbon production event occurred and left a globally distributed signal.

The more interesting mystery is its machinery.

Early speculation had range because the observation was genuinely strange. A nearby supernova could in principle increase cosmic radiation. A gamma-ray burst could produce atmospheric ionization. Extreme solar proton activity could do it. Each candidate came with energetic and observational constraints.

A supernova close enough to generate the required signal should probably have been spectacular and persistent in the sky and should leave a remnant. Medieval astronomical records are incomplete, but they are not empty. Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Arabic, and European observers recorded transient celestial phenomena under some circumstances. No obvious eighth-century supernova of the required kind sits waiting in the catalog.

Gamma-ray-burst ideas had their own problem: possible does not mean probable, and other isotope ratios supplied a way to discriminate.

The solar explanation gained weight because of beryllium-10 and chlorine-36.

Cosmogenic radionuclides are not interchangeable. Their production yields depend on particle energy. The relative response of carbon-14, beryllium-10, and chlorine-36 carries information about the incoming spectrum. Ice cores from polar regions preserve beryllium and chlorine deposited from the atmosphere. When researchers aligned these records with the tree-ring event, they found evidence consistent with a hard solar proton spectrum.

By 2015, the multi-radionuclide case had become strong enough that the Sun ceased to be merely one exotic candidate among several. It became the explanation to beat.

This did not mean somebody could draw the exact solar flare in a diagram.

An extreme solar energetic-particle event is not the same object as a flare. Solar flares are electromagnetic eruptions. Coronal mass ejections throw plasma and magnetic field outward. Shocks driven through the corona and heliosphere can accelerate protons and heavier ions to high energies. The particle event measured at Earth depends on magnetic connectivity, shock geometry, seed populations, transport, and the evolving heliospheric field.

The ancient isotope record gives us the particles after all of that.

We infer the source backward.

The 774 event appears to have produced a proton fluence far above any directly observed in the space age, especially at energies high enough to create ground-level and atmospheric cosmogenic effects. Different reconstructions yield different numerical comparisons because they assume different spectra and production models. Statements such as “ten times stronger” or “a hundred times stronger” float around popular accounts with insufficient attention to energy threshold.

Stronger above 30 MeV is not the same comparison as stronger above 200 MeV. A soft spectrum can contain many lower-energy protons while producing relatively fewer highly penetrating particles. A hard spectrum places more weight at high energies. The ratio matters to isotope production, aviation radiation, astronaut exposure, and detector response.

If we want to understand 774, we have to stop asking for one number called “size.”

Events have shapes.

This is true even before we discuss time. The radiocarbon signal rises across annual rings, but the production event itself may have been much shorter. Carbon-cycle modelling tries to work backward from the smeared biological record. The global tree-ring data allowed researchers to exploit Northern-Southern Hemisphere differences and seasonal growth windows. Their modelling suggested the main production pulse occurred in boreal summer 774, roughly July with uncertainty of about a month in one reconstruction.

A summer storm, if the model is right.

The phrase sounds like weather. It was nothing of the sort.

High-energy protons would have struck the atmosphere, producing cascades and secondary particles. At high altitude, radiation dose would rise. Ionization in the middle atmosphere would increase. Chemistry could be perturbed. A modern society would care intensely about spacecraft electronics, astronauts, polar aviation, high-frequency radio, and perhaps other systems depending on the associated solar and geomagnetic conditions.

What did people in 774 experience?

This is where historical imagination tends to outrun the data.

A large solar particle event does not necessarily produce a globally obvious visible sky display. Aurorae are driven by magnetospheric processes and particle precipitation associated with geomagnetic activity, which is related to but not identical with the high-energy proton fluence that creates cosmogenic isotopes. A spectacular proton event could accompany a major geomagnetic storm. It could also have a less dramatic magnetic consequence depending on the coronal mass ejection’s magnetic orientation and trajectory.

The absence of a universally recorded aurora is therefore not a refutation.

Conversely, the presence of a strange light in a chronicle is not proof.

The eighth century is full of textual traps. Chronicles were copied long after the events they describe. Dating systems differ. Biblical and symbolic language shaped descriptions. A “red sign of the cross” could refer to atmospheric optics, aurora, halo phenomena, editorial theology, or a later textual corruption. A record written in one place cannot be casually synchronized to a particle event whose production timing carries seasonal uncertainty.

Researchers have searched historical texts anyway, because they should. Some auroral reports around the 770s and around the 990s may be relevant. The correct posture is asymmetrical: historical observations can support a solar-disturbance context, but the isotope evidence does not need them to establish that an extreme cosmogenic event occurred.

This is a rare case where the natural archive is more legible than the human one.

There is another historical temptation worth resisting. Once an extreme solar event has a year, people begin looking for catastrophe in that year. Crop failure? Epidemic? Political collapse? Strange weather? Death of a ruler? If enough chronicles are searched, something unpleasant will always be found.

The causal chain from solar energetic particles to surface climate is not a free pass to connect every bad harvest to the Sun.

The 774 event was enormous in particle terms, but that does not mean it delivered a massive change in total solar irradiance. The energy carried by high-energy particles is small compared with the Sun’s continuous electromagnetic output. Atmospheric ionization can change chemical species, especially in the upper atmosphere, but converting that into a large, rapid, globally coherent climate anomaly requires mechanisms and evidence. There is no basis for treating 774 as a hidden volcanic eruption from the Sun.

The tree ring that records radiocarbon production may show no dramatic growth response at all.

This creates a wonderful contrast. The event was physically extraordinary in one dimension and possibly mundane at ground level to the unaided human senses. A medieval farmer could pass through the most extreme solar particle event in the Holocene catalog without knowing that anything unusual had happened above him.

His oak knew only because the atmosphere changed isotopically.

The event’s modern significance therefore comes from systems medieval people did not possess.

A proton with enough energy to matter to a satellite is irrelevant to a plow. A geomagnetically induced current cannot burn out a transformer that does not exist. A GPS receiver cannot lose lock in a monastery. Aircrew dose is not an occupational category before powered flight. An astronaut outside Earth’s magnetic protection is a problem created by having astronauts.

Technological vulnerability is historically contingent.

This point cuts in both directions. Medieval survival of the 774 event does not prove a repeat would be harmless now. Modern dependence on electronics does not prove a repeat would end civilization. Risk is the interaction between hazard and exposure. The Sun supplies the first. We design the second.

The ancient event is valuable because it widens the hazard distribution.

Instrumental records are short. Ground-level enhancements from solar particles have been observed for decades, not millennia. Extreme-value statistics are fragile when the tail contains few points. If the Sun can produce events like 774 once in a thousand or several thousand years, a space-age sample of less than a century has little chance of containing one.

This is the same problem faced in flood engineering, seismology, volcanology, and asteroid risk. Human observation is often shortest where the return periods are longest. We compensate by reading proxies.

Trees are solar particle detectors with lifetimes measured in centuries.

Forests become detector arrays across millennia.

The phrase is technically unfair to trees—they are not designed detectors, and their response is mediated by the carbon cycle—but it captures the scale advantage. A living bristlecone pine can carry thousands of annual samples in one trunk. Dead subfossil wood extends the record further. European oaks, Alpine conifers, Siberian larches, Japanese cedars, New Zealand kauri: species separated by oceans can participate in one atmospheric experiment that nobody planned.

This is why replication across hemispheres matters beyond proving the event was global. The spatial pattern contains physics. Atmospheric mixing between hemispheres takes time. Land-ocean distribution differs. Growing seasons are opposite. If a radiocarbon event appears with expected offsets and amplitudes, those differences become additional constraints on timing and transport.

The world does not merely repeat the signal. It distorts it in informative ways.

Subannual tree-ring work pushes this further. Earlywood and latewood form at different times. In some species, researchers can separate portions of a single ring and measure their carbon-14. The 774 event has been studied at this resolution in Arctic trees. The production pulse can be bracketed more tightly because the tree’s anatomy divides the growing season.

The calendar scar becomes a seasonal scar.

There is a danger here too. Fine resolution creates the illusion that we can reconstruct a day. We cannot, at least not from current proxies with confidence. Carbon transport and stored photosynthate blur the mapping between atmospheric production and cell formation. Different species allocate carbon differently. The production event itself may contain multiple particle injections. The atmosphere mixes.

Precision should approach the transfer function, not outrun it.

The summer-of-774 inference is therefore a model-constrained window, not an eyewitness appointment.

Still, think about what that means. A high-energy solar episode that occurred roughly twelve and a half centuries ago can be placed within a season from chemistry fixed by trees on different continents. That alone would justify a book.

Then archaeology borrowed the marker.

Por-Bajin’s builders arrived two years after the scar. The trees they felled in 777 had already incorporated the 775 radiocarbon transition in inner rings. When those timbers became foundations, the cosmic event became physically embedded inside human architecture. The building did not need to survive intact. Enough wood had to survive.

At L’Anse aux Meadows the second canonical event played the same trick. The 993 scar sat inside trees later cut by Norse tools. A solar particle storm in the late tenth century became the ruler for a transatlantic human event in the early eleventh.

This is why the date 774 keeps reappearing in fields whose practitioners would once have had little reason to speak.

Solar physicists want to know how extreme the Sun can be.

Atmospheric chemists want to know how cosmogenic nuclides move.

Dendrochronologists want independent tie points.

Archaeologists want exact years.

Historians want chronologies that can discriminate among narratives.

Infrastructure planners want credible upper-tail hazards.

All of them meet in a sliver of cellulose.

The event itself remains incomplete in one important sense. We do not know what the solar surface looked like. There is no sunspot drawing. No magnetogram. No coronagraph movie. No spacecraft measured the proton spectrum. The solar mechanism has to be inferred from theory, modern analogues, and the isotope fingerprint.

This prevents 774 from becoming a calibration target for models in the ordinary way. We can ask whether a model can produce a particle event with the inferred fluence and hardness, but we cannot compare the model against a full set of source observations.

The Sun has left us the effect without the crime scene.

That asymmetry helps explain why terms such as *superflare* should be used carefully. Stellar astronomers observe white-light superflares on other stars, including Sun-like stars. Solar physicists can estimate magnetic free energy available in active regions. But mapping an isotope-derived terrestrial particle event directly onto a stellar flare-energy category requires assumptions about how efficiently an eruption accelerates particles and connects to Earth.

The particle scar is not a flare photometer.

What can we say safely?

Something happened in the solar-terrestrial environment around boreal summer 774 that caused a very large, rapid increase in cosmogenic radionuclide production. The event was global in its atmospheric radiocarbon signature. Independent isotopes support an extreme solar energetic-particle origin. The particle fluence and spectrum were outside the range directly measured in the modern era. The exact eruptive sequence at the Sun and the associated geomagnetic-storm severity are not recoverable from carbon-14 alone.

That is already extraordinary.

The temptation to add apocalypse is a failure of nerve.

The real story is stranger because it is quieter. In the year of wars, dynasties, prayers, trade, hunger, marriages, and ordinary death, the Sun produced a particle environment that left a chemical punctuation mark around the planet. Most people may have noticed nothing. The event passed. Trees kept growing.

Twelve centuries later, the punctuation mark became a date.
