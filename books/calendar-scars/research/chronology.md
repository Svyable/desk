# Chronology

## Deep-time event markers

- **12,350 BCE range** — a very large cosmogenic event has been reported in Late Glacial records; its exact comparison with later canonical events depends on chronology and production modelling. Treat as deep-event literature, not as a simple ranked leaderboard.
- **7176 BCE** — strong annual 14C excursion identified across tree-ring records and interpreted as an extreme solar proton event.
- **~5410 BCE** — rapid annual 14C increase reported in trees from California, Switzerland, and Finland; an important event/candidate in the developing catalog.
- **5259 BCE** — strong event recorded in multiple tree-ring chronologies. In 2024 it became the calendar pin used to anchor the Neolithic Dispilio sequence in northern Greece.
- **~660 BCE** — established extreme solar-particle episode recorded in annual tree rings and other cosmogenic proxies. Recent work refines the onset to the 664–663 BCE interval depending on sampling and year convention.

## Common Era events

- **774/775 CE** — the canonical large annual 14C increase first reported by Fusa Miyake and colleagues in 2012 from Japanese cedar. Later replicated globally and supported by 10Be/36Cl evidence consistent with an extreme solar energetic-particle event.
- **777 CE** — construction timber at Por-Bajin was felled in summer 777 according to the 775 radiocarbon marker and terminal-ring anatomy.
- **993/994 CE** — second canonical rapid 14C event published by Miyake and colleagues in 2013; a missing ring later required a one-year correction in the paper’s chronology.
- **1021 CE** — wood at L’Anse aux Meadows was cut in this year, established by locating the 993 event in tree-ring sequences and counting to the waney edge.
- **1052 CE / 1279 CE and other intervals** — smaller or additional production events have been reported in the literature. Their status, size, and independent confirmation should be stated event by event.

## Scientific development

- **1940s onward** — accelerator and nuclear-era understanding of cosmogenic radionuclide production develops alongside radiocarbon dating.
- **1949** — Willard Libby and collaborators publish foundational radiocarbon-dating work; radiocarbon chronology subsequently transforms archaeology.
- **Late 20th century** — dendrochronology and calibration curves increasingly tie radiocarbon ages to calendar ages using known-age wood.
- **2012** — Miyake et al. publish the 774/775 annual spike in *Nature*.
- **2013** — Miyake et al. publish the second event near 993/994 in *Nature Communications*.
- **November 2013** — corrigendum moves the second event by one year after discovery of a missing ring in the counted sequence.
- **2015** — multi-radionuclide 10Be/36Cl evidence strengthens the solar interpretation of the 774/775 and 993/994 events.
- **2018** — COSMIC collaboration reports globally coherent signatures across 44 tree-ring records from five continents and refines seasonal timing of the 774 and 993 events.
- **2020** — Por-Bajin paper demonstrates subannual archaeological dating, placing construction in summer 777.
- **2020** — IntCal20 incorporates far more annual-resolution information and methodology capable of preserving abrupt radiocarbon structure better than older smoothed curves.
- **2021–2022** — L’Anse aux Meadows and Ribe studies demonstrate exact/single-year radiocarbon applications to Viking Age chronology.
- **2022** — strong events at 7176 and 5259 BCE reported from multiple tree-ring chronologies.
- **2024** — Dispilio Neolithic chronology anchored using the 5259 BCE event; work on ~660 BCE further refines event timing.
- **2025** — annual radiocarbon work in the first millennium BCE expands the solar-cycle and chronology record.
- **August 29, 2026** — Usoskin and colleagues publish a sensitivity study warning that realistic solar-cycle/background variability can distort or mimic weak-to-moderate event signatures, strengthening the case for precision, replication, and multi-proxy confirmation.

## Historical boundary markers

- **1859** — Carrington-era geomagnetic storm becomes the famous historical benchmark for magnetic-storm effects, but it should not be treated as directly interchangeable with an ancient SEP event inferred from cosmogenic nuclides.
- **Space age** — direct particle measurements and neutron monitors provide detailed records of much smaller solar particle events than the largest proxy-inferred events, leaving a consequential observational gap between instrumental experience and the Holocene proxy tail.
