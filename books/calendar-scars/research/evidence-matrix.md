# Evidence Matrix

## Class 1 — verified / documented

- Annual tree-ring radiocarbon measurements show abrupt, unusually large atmospheric 14C increases associated with the 774/775 CE and 993/994 CE events.
- The 774 and 993 signatures have been replicated across multiple tree-ring records and continents.
- Independent cosmogenic radionuclide records, especially 10Be and 36Cl in polar ice, support an extreme solar-particle origin for the best-established events.
- Strong older events have been measured in annual tree-ring sequences, including events around 7176 BCE and 5259 BCE; the literature also contains verified or strong events around 660 BCE and other periods.
- Accelerator mass spectrometry can measure annual tree-ring samples small enough to locate the abrupt 14C transition within sequences.
- The 775 marker was identified in structural timber from Por-Bajin; ring position and terminal growth anatomy placed felling in 777 CE and construction in summer 777.
- The 993 marker was identified in wood from L’Anse aux Meadows; ring counting to the bark edge established Norse wood cutting in 1021 CE.
- Annual radiocarbon sequences at Ribe constrain phases of Viking Age trade more tightly than conventional calibration alone.
- The 5259 BCE event was used to anchor the floating Neolithic juniper chronology at Dispilio, placing the final ring at 5140 BCE.
- The 2013 paper reporting the 993/994 event required a published one-year chronological correction after a missing tree-ring layer was identified.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- The best-established large events are widely interpreted as extreme solar energetic-particle events, but the precise solar eruption physics that produces the hardest and largest proxy events remains incompletely constrained.
- Estimates of event strength depend on carbon-cycle models, geomagnetic shielding, radionuclide production models, and assumptions about particle spectra.
- The recurrence frequency of the most extreme events can be estimated from the proxy catalog, but the catalog is still sparse, detection thresholds vary through time, and candidate events continue to be added or rejected.
- Historical reports of aurora-like phenomena near some events are useful contextual evidence but have translation, dating, copying, and interpretation problems.
- A larger annual-resolution radiocarbon map of the Holocene could reveal additional tie points and improve calibration, but laboratory cost, sample preservation, archive access, and cross-dating quality constrain the pace.

## Class 3 — disputed / not established

- A nearby supernova or gamma-ray burst as the cause of the canonical 774/775 event is not supported by the current multi-isotope evidence and should not be presented as co-equal with the solar interpretation.
- Claims that an individual medieval “red cross,” celestial omen, or chronicle entry identifies the physical source of a Miyake event are not established.
- “Miyake event” should not be used as if every candidate excursion is already a member of one proven physical population.
- No single recurrence interval can presently be treated as a clock predicting the next event.
- It is not established that an event with the same particle fluence as 774/775 would necessarily produce a Carrington-class geomagnetic storm; particle radiation and geomagnetic disturbance are different parts of space weather.
- Exact-year radiocarbon dating does not automatically settle historical causation. Dating a beam to a year can constrain a narrative without proving why the people involved acted.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

- “Calendar scars” may become a distributed global chronological infrastructure: rare atmospheric events acting as chemical synchronization pulses across archives that otherwise cannot be directly connected.
- The strongest practical consequence of Miyake events may ultimately be chronological rather than astrophysical: they could anchor archaeological and environmental sequences whose historical importance exceeds the space-weather event itself.
- A mature annual 14C atlas may expose hidden one- or multi-year errors in established floating chronologies and force revisions that seem historical but originate in laboratory metrology.
- The proxy record suggests that modern technological civilization has direct instrumental experience of a much narrower solar-particle regime than the Holocene has produced. How much risk that difference creates depends on event spectrum, infrastructure, warning, shielding, latitude, and engineering.

## Contradiction / incentive audit

### Solar physicists

Incentive toward identifying extreme-event populations and recurrence statistics can encourage treating heterogeneous proxy anomalies as one family too early. Counterweight: require multi-proxy replication and explicit event-by-event status.

### Archaeologists

Exact dates are professionally valuable and narratively irresistible. Risk: a year can be more precise than the archaeological inference connecting the wood to the event. Counterweight: separate growth year, felling year, construction/use year, and historical interpretation.

### Dendrochronologists

Cross-dated ring sequences are extraordinarily precise but not magical. Missing, false, locally absent, or poorly replicated rings can shift chronologies. Counterweight: replicate trees, sites, species, and isotope markers.

### Space-weather risk community

Ancient extreme events provide a compelling upper-tail warning. Risk: translating isotope production directly into grid collapse, satellite loss, or economic totals creates false precision. Counterweight: separate SEP radiation, ionospheric effects, CME magnetic orientation, geomagnetic induction, and infrastructure exposure.

### Popular-history market

“Solar storm dates Vikings exactly” is a powerful hook. Risk: turning a methodological advance into the claim that earlier scholarship knew nothing. Counterweight: show what archaeology, dendrochronology, texts, and ordinary radiocarbon had already established before the one-year pin was added.

## Defeat conditions

The central thesis weakens materially if future work shows that the canonical abrupt 14C signatures are not globally synchronous enough to serve as reliable tie points; if cross-laboratory replication reveals systematic offsets that prevent year-specific anchoring; if the major archaeological demonstrations cannot be reproduced outside unusually favorable showcase samples; or if annual-resolution radiocarbon data prove too sparse, expensive, physiologically noisy, or regionally variable for broad chronological use.

The space-weather half weakens if improved production and particle-transport models collapse the inferred extreme events into a regime already well represented by direct modern observations. The archaeology half survives even then if the atmospheric scars remain globally recognizable calendar markers.
