# Communication Is a Stronger Claim Than Emission

There is a five-step ladder hidden inside almost every argument about biological light.

Something emits photons.

The photons vary with biological state.

Another biological system is exposed to those photons.

The receiving system changes because of the photons.

The sender and receiver have been shaped, developmentally or evolutionarily, so that the exchange performs a biological function.

The first rung is emission.

The fifth is communication.

The distance between them is where most of the controversy lives.

The vocabulary does not help. Researchers use signal to mean a measured output, a statistical correlate, a causal influence, or a message. A photon-count trace is a signal to an engineer. A rise in emission can signal oxidative stress to a clinician. Neither statement implies that cells themselves use the light as a signal.

This chapter is an attempt to be annoyingly literal.

If two cells communicate optically, what exactly would have to be true?

First, the emitter has to produce photons in a reproducible relationship with some biological state. This is the easiest condition. UPE already does it. Wounds, oxidative stress, metabolic perturbations, ultraviolet exposure, and other conditions change emission.

Second, enough photons have to reach the receiver.

This is not a philosophical issue. It is geometry.

An emitter radiating roughly isotropically sends photons in many directions. The fraction intercepted by a small receiver decreases rapidly with distance. Tissue absorption and scattering reduce it further. If the source is inside a cell, membranes and chromophores compete for the photons before they leave. If the source is outside, environmental background enters the calculation.

The total emission measured outside a sample is not the local flux at a candidate receptor.

That can cut both ways. External measurements may underestimate local photon production because many photons are absorbed before escape. A nearby intracellular receptor could see more than a camera outside the tissue. Conversely, a proposed long-range signal between organisms could be far weaker at the receiver than whole-sample counts make it sound.

Third, the receiver needs a mechanism.

A molecule has to absorb the relevant photons or a physical structure has to transduce them. The absorption spectrum should overlap the emission spectrum. The number of absorption events should be sufficient to alter downstream chemistry above noise.

Biology contains many chromophores. Opsins are obvious examples. Flavins, porphyrins, cytochromes, and other cofactors also absorb light. Photoreceptors can be extraordinarily sensitive because one photochemical event can be amplified through signaling cascades.

This gives optical communication plausibility in the abstract.

It does not identify the receiver.

Fourth, manipulating the optical channel should manipulate the response.

This is the decisive causal requirement. If the emitter affects the receiver through photons, then blocking those photons while preserving other channels should reduce or abolish the effect. Passing the photons should preserve it. Artificially reproducing the photons should restore it.

The experiment should have the logic of a wire cutter.

Cut the wire, lose the signal.

Reconnect the wire, regain it.

Biological optics makes the wire invisible, but the logic survives.

Fifth, if communication rather than incidental photochemical influence is claimed, the relationship should have biological organization.

That could mean temporal modulation, spectral selection, anatomical alignment, regulated emission, receiver-specific sensitivity, or evidence that the pathway affects fitness or coordinated function. A receiver accidentally perturbed by metabolic glow is not necessarily part of a communication system.

Evolution can turn accidents into functions, but the function must be shown.

This distinction is familiar in animal behavior. A cue is information an organism can exploit whether or not the source evolved to provide it. A signal is generally shaped by selection because of its effect on receivers. Smoke is a cue that fire exists. A bird's alarm call is a signal. Another species eavesdropping on the alarm uses it as a cue.

Biophoton research rarely uses that vocabulary carefully enough.

A stressed plant may emit more photons because of oxidation. A neighboring organism could theoretically detect the change. The plant does not have to “intend” the emission for the photons to matter ecologically. Calling the phenomenon communication may therefore be premature even if a receiver response is proven.

This may sound like terminology policing. It matters because different words imply different mechanisms.

The strongest evidence currently lies on the lower rungs.

Emission: strong.

State dependence: strong in many preparations.

Receiver exposure: physically unavoidable at some level, because emitted photons go somewhere.

Causal receiver response at endogenous flux: limited and contested.

Dedicated communication function: unestablished.

Once stated this way, the research program becomes obvious.

Measure photon budgets.

Identify receivers.

Intervene on the channel.

This is more productive than debating whether “biophotons are real.” They are.

The field's historical burden partly comes from failing to separate existence from function. Critics attack extravagant communication claims and are then portrayed as denying the emissions. Enthusiasts point to undeniable emissions and act as though function follows.

Both sides win arguments against positions the other side need not hold.

The photon budget is the fastest way to stop this ritual.

Imagine two cell cultures facing one another across a centimeter of transparent material. Suppose the emitting culture releases one thousand photons per square centimeter per second from the relevant surface. That is on the high end of many reported UPE ranges, not a universal value. Only some of those photons are in the wavelength band the receiver can absorb. Only some travel toward the other culture. Reflection and absorption at interfaces reduce them. The receiver's own geometry limits interception.

After those losses, perhaps hundreds, tens, or fewer relevant photons reach a square centimeter per second.

A cell occupies far less than a square centimeter.

The per-cell arrival rate can become tiny.

That does not kill the hypothesis. It forces it to become local or amplified.

Maybe cells communicate only at micrometer distances. Maybe tissue channels light. Maybe one photon can trigger a cascade. Maybe emission is highly directional rather than isotropic. Maybe short bursts have much higher instantaneous intensity than average measurements show.

Each rescue makes a prediction.

A local mechanism should decay strongly with distance.

A waveguide should show directional transport.

A single-photon-sensitive receptor should be identifiable and should respond in a low-noise state.

Bursts should appear in high-temporal-resolution recordings.

Directional emission should vary with orientation.

A good hypothesis becomes more testable as it gets more specific.

The background-light problem is similarly constraining.

A cell exposed to ordinary room light receives an astronomical number of external photons compared with endogenous UPE. If its biological response depends on the endogenous photons, why is the channel not saturated or drowned by the environment?

There are several possible answers.

The system operates only in darkness.

The relevant photons are internal and external light cannot reach the receptor.

The wavelength is filtered by tissue.

The receptor is physically coupled to the emitter.

The information is encoded in timing or location rather than total intensity.

The receiver adapts to background and detects modulation.

Again, every answer is an experiment.

The ambient-light sensitivity reported in some non-contact experiments makes this especially important. If external light changes the effect, it may support a photochemical mechanism. It may also mean delayed luminescence or light-induced metabolic changes are contaminating the supposedly spontaneous signal.

Darkness is not a neutral condition.

Light can alter biology before the experiment begins.

This is why spontaneous and induced emission have to be separated. A sample illuminated during handling may emit delayed photons for minutes or longer. If a receiver is placed nearby during that decay, one could mistakenly attribute a response to ongoing endogenous signaling rather than afterglow from the experimental procedure.

A rigorous communication experiment should therefore control illumination history as tightly as temperature.

There is another powerful control that is underused: information structure.

If the optical channel truly communicates, the emitter should be able to impose a pattern that the receiver follows. One can perturb the sender in a temporally coded sequence—on, off, on, on, off—while leaving the receiver blinded to the schedule. Measure whether the receiver's physiology contains the same temporal structure at the predicted delay.

This is much harder for diffuse artifacts to mimic.

Even better, use two perturbations known to produce different UPE spectra or temporal signatures and ask whether the receiver distinguishes them.

A channel that only correlates with “stressed versus not stressed” may still be causal, but richer encoding makes communication more plausible.

Information theory can quantify such patterns, but the biology must come first. Mutual information between two noisy traces does not prove causation. Common environmental fluctuations can synchronize both. The emitter manipulation and channel blocking remain essential.

The strongest design resembles telecommunications engineering performed on living chemistry.

Characterize the transmitter.

Characterize the channel.

Characterize the receiver.

Inject a known signal.

Measure transfer function.

Break the channel selectively.

The reason this has not already settled the field is partly technical. UPE is weak. Biological preparations drift. High-sensitivity detectors can interfere with the geometry. Spectral filtering discards precious photons. Simultaneous metabolic measurements add complexity. The more controls are introduced, the smaller the effect may become.

That difficulty is real.

It is not an argument for lowering the evidentiary standard.

A phenomenon claiming to reveal a hidden biological communication layer deserves engineering-grade channel tests precisely because ordinary biological variability can manufacture suggestive correlations.

The payoff would be large.

If endogenous optical communication were demonstrated even in one narrow system, it would change how we think about metabolic by-products. It would show that evolution had recruited ultraweak chemiluminescence into functional signaling. It would open questions about intracellular organization, tissue optics, developmental coordination, and sensory biology.

The discovery would not prove that every UPE phenomenon is communication.

It would establish one case.

Science often advances by making a universal dream smaller until it becomes true.

The field does not need to prove that life is made of light.

It needs one receiver that notices the photons life already leaks.
