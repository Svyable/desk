# The Brain's Weakest Signal

The brain is where this subject becomes most seductive.

A liver emits ultraweak photons and we think about oxidation.

A brain emits ultraweak photons and people start talking about thought.

That jump is understandable. The brain is already an electrical organ, a chemical organ, an oscillatory organ, a networked organ. Adding light seems less like adding a measurement and more like discovering a missing medium. The metaphors arrive instantly: neurons flash; consciousness radiates; microtubules guide photons; mitochondria whisper through axons; the skull leaks a faint optical record of cognition.

The evidence is both more interesting and less permissive.

Neural tissue is metabolically expensive. The brain consumes substantial oxygen and glucose relative to its mass. Mitochondria are dense in active neurons and glia. Synaptic transmission changes energy demand. Excitotoxic stress drives reactive oxygen chemistry. Ischemia and reperfusion alter redox pathways violently. If ultraweak photon emission is coupled to oxidative metabolism, the brain should emit.

It does.

Experimental work has reported UPE from brain tissue under different metabolic and pharmacological conditions. Studies in animal models have associated photon emission with glutamatergic activity, oxidative stress, injury, and drug effects. A 2026 Neurology conference review summarized an emerging literature in which neural UPE changes with metabolic state, ischemia, neurodegenerative models, and pharmacological manipulation. The review also noted reports of correlations between transcranial UPE and EEG activity.

A conference review is not a settled field.

It is a map of where the claims currently cluster.

The strongest underlying proposition is straightforward: neural oxidative metabolism can generate ultraweak photons, and changing neural metabolic state can change emission.

That proposition fits everything we already know about UPE chemistry.

The next proposition is that UPE can serve as a noninvasive biomarker of neural metabolism. That is plausible but technically difficult. Photons generated deep in the brain have to pass through tissue, blood, bone, scalp, and hair. Visible wavelengths scatter strongly. A signal measured outside the skull may be dominated by superficial tissue unless the experiment demonstrates depth sensitivity.

This is a familiar problem in optical neuroimaging.

Near-infrared spectroscopy works partly because particular wavelengths penetrate tissue better and because the target chromophores, oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin, have useful absorption differences. Even then, separating cerebral from scalp blood flow is difficult.

Spontaneous UPE begins with vastly fewer photons.

A transcranial signal therefore demands careful geometry and controls. Shaved scalp is not enough. One wants comparisons across skull regions, dead tissue, isolated brain preparations, superficial oxidative manipulations, spectral bands, and perhaps simultaneous intracranial or ex vivo measurements.

The third proposition is stronger: endogenous photons participate in neural signaling.

This is where the brain's glamour can overpower arithmetic.

Neurons already communicate through action potentials, neurotransmitters, gap junctions, neuromodulators, ions, and extracellular fields. An optical signaling layer would need to explain something these mechanisms do not, or provide an efficiency or geometry advantage.

What would light be good at?

Photons can travel quickly. They can carry wavelength-specific information. They can cross spaces without molecular diffusion. In a transparent medium, they can connect emitter and receiver with little delay. Biology already uses photons externally in vision and photoreception.

But brain tissue is not transparent fiber.

It is scattering, absorbing, structurally heterogeneous material. A photon in visible wavelengths may travel only a complicated short path before absorption or scattering. Speed becomes irrelevant when the mean free path is short and the photon count tiny. A chemical synapse taking milliseconds does not need help from a photon that never reaches another neuron.

Some theorists therefore propose waveguiding structures—myelinated axons, microtubules, mitochondrial arrangements, or other cellular components that might channel light. These ideas are physically interesting and experimentally underconstrained. Demonstrating that a structure can guide externally injected photons under idealized conditions is not the same as showing that endogenous UPE is launched into, preserved by, and functionally received from that structure in vivo.

The entire chain matters.

Emitter.

Coupling into the guide.

Propagation loss.

Receiver.

Biological effect.

Evolutionary function.

Skipping any link turns a mechanism into a diagram.

The 2016 Scientific Reports paper showing photon entanglement through brain tissue is a useful example of a result that sounds much more biological than it is. The researchers sent externally prepared entangled photons through slices of brain tissue and examined whether quantum correlations survived. The study was about optical transmission through biological material. It did not demonstrate that the brain generates entangled photons for communication.

Yet the title alone can be pulled into a larger quantum-brain narrative.

This is why source discipline matters in frontier subjects. A paper can be excellent evidence for exactly one link and no evidence at all for the links around it.

Neural UPE is also entangled culturally with phosphene phenomena. People can perceive light without external photons when the visual system is stimulated mechanically, electrically, magnetically, or chemically. Oxidative changes in retinal or cortical tissue can accompany some conditions that produce visual phenomena. A 2023 rat study measuring methamphetamine effects reported increases in both ROS and UPE across several brain regions and speculated that excess photon generation might be relevant to phosphenes or visual hallucinations.

That speculation should remain speculation.

An internally generated photon could in principle activate retinal photochemistry if produced in the right place and quantity. But hallucinations do not require photons. Neural circuits can generate visual percepts without optical intermediates. The burden lies with showing that suppressing or filtering endogenous photons changes the percept while leaving the underlying neurochemistry intact.

That is a very hard experiment.

The more immediate use of brain UPE is as a redox readout.

Consider ischemic stroke. Loss of blood flow collapses energy metabolism. Reperfusion can produce oxidative bursts. Mitochondrial dysfunction persists in injured tissue. A label-free optical signal sensitive to these transitions could complement other monitoring methods, especially in experimental models.

The same logic applies to neurodegeneration. Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and other diseases involve altered mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic changes. If UPE profiles shift reliably before gross structural damage, they might become research biomarkers.

Again, depth is the problem.

Ex vivo slices and exposed cortex are easier than intact human brain. A noninvasive scalp detector would need to distinguish brain photons from skin and muscle photons. A high forehead signal during cognition could arise from facial muscle activity, blood flow, skin metabolism, or environmental artifacts.

This is where simultaneous multimodal recording becomes essential.

EEG, functional near-infrared spectroscopy, thermal imaging, electromyography, cerebral blood-flow measures, and UPE could be recorded together. If UPE contains unique variance linked to neural state after controlling for superficial physiology, the case strengthens.

The signal should earn independence.

The communication hypothesis can also be attacked at the receptor side. Are there known light-sensitive molecules in neural tissue that respond at the endogenous wavelengths and fluxes?

Flavins, cytochromes, porphyrins, opsins, and other chromophores absorb light. Photobiomodulation research suggests external red or near-infrared light can affect mitochondrial and cellular physiology under certain exposure conditions. That demonstrates photosensitivity, but the doses are typically many orders of magnitude larger than spontaneous UPE.

A stadium loudspeaker proves an ear can respond to sound. It does not prove two bacteria communicate by whispers across a highway.

Dose matters.

One could make the communication hypothesis quantitative. Estimate photons generated per neuron or mitochondrion. Estimate local absorption and scattering. Calculate how many photons reach a proposed receptor per second. Measure the receptor's response threshold under physiological conditions. Then compare.

If the budget misses by six orders of magnitude, the theory needs a specific concentrating or amplification mechanism.

If it closes, the idea becomes much more interesting.

The field often treats photon counting as a descriptive exercise rather than a budget constraint. That has allowed neural biophoton theories to proliferate faster than decisive experiments.

There is another possibility: UPE may matter not as a point-to-point signal but as a side effect that feeds back locally on redox chemistry.

A photon emitted inside a cell can be reabsorbed nearby. If a chromophore absorbs it, the energy is not lost from biology even if no message is encoded. This would be photochemical recycling rather than communication. At very low flux, the effect may be negligible, but it is a distinct hypothesis worth separating from language-like signaling.

Biology does not need semantics to use physics.

The brain's UPE therefore occupies several evidence classes at once.

Verified: neural tissue can produce ultraweak photons associated with oxidative chemistry.

Credible but incomplete: UPE may provide useful information about neural metabolic state, injury, and disease.

Disputed: externally measured UPE may track specific cognitive or electrophysiological processes strongly enough for practical brain monitoring.

Speculative: endogenous photons form a functional communication layer involved in neural computation or consciousness.

The order matters.

A book about frontier science should not flatten them into one paragraph.

The weakest signal in the brain may turn out to be a valuable measurement precisely because it is not a new neural code.

Or it may contain a functional component we have not yet learned to isolate.

The experiment that distinguishes those futures has not been done.
