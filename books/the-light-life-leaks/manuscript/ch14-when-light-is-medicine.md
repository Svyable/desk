# When Light Is Medicine

There is one fact that makes endogenous optical signaling impossible to dismiss with a shrug: cells can respond to light.

That much is not controversial.

Photobiomodulation uses red and near-infrared light to alter biological processes without the destructive heating associated with high-power lasers. The literature is uneven and some clinical claims outrun evidence, but the core photochemistry is serious enough that mitochondria, cytochromes, nitric oxide, reactive oxygen species, membrane channels, and downstream transcriptional responses are all active research areas.

External photons can change cells.

The mistake is to assume that endogenous photons therefore do.

The difference is dose.

A photobiomodulation device can deliver milliwatts per square centimeter for seconds or minutes. Even after losses, the number of incident photons is enormous compared with typical measured UPE fluxes of tens to thousands of photons per square centimeter per second.

Orders of magnitude are not details in photochemistry.

They are often the mechanism.

If a cultured cell responds to an externally applied red-light dose containing trillions of photons, that does not show that a handful of spontaneous photons from a neighboring cell can trigger the same response. The receiver may be light sensitive but not remotely sensitive enough.

This chapter is where the communication hypothesis faces its hardest arithmetic.

Take a red photon around 650 nanometers. Its energy is roughly three times ten to the minus nineteenth joules. One milliwatt of red light therefore corresponds to roughly quadrillions of photons per second. Photobiomodulation studies may use irradiances and durations that deliver joules per square centimeter.

Compare that with one thousand spontaneous photons per square centimeter per second.

The energy difference is colossal.

A direct equivalence is implausible.

There are ways around it, but each way changes the theory.

The first is proximity. Whole-surface UPE measurements may underestimate photon generation inside a tiny intracellular compartment because most photons are absorbed before escaping. A mitochondrion sitting nanometers from another absorbing structure could experience a much higher local flux than an external camera sees.

The second is focusing or guidance. If cellular structures channel photons instead of letting them radiate isotropically, local delivery could be enhanced.

The third is amplification. One absorbed photon can trigger a cascade in systems evolved for single-photon sensitivity.

The fourth is resonance or nonlinear response. A particular molecular transition might respond to a narrow wavelength even at low average power if the timing and state are right.

The fifth is that photobiomodulation and endogenous signaling involve entirely different receptors.

All are possible.

None can be assumed.

Photobiomodulation is most useful to this book as a set of constraints rather than a precedent that proves the case.

Cytochrome c oxidase, complex IV of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, is often proposed as a photoacceptor in red and near-infrared light therapy. Absorption may alter enzyme state, nitric oxide binding, mitochondrial membrane potential, or downstream redox signaling. Other work points to additional chromophores, water-associated effects, transient receptor channels, flavins, and light-sensitive pathways outside mitochondria.

The mechanistic picture remains contested in detail.

That should make us cautious about borrowing it wholesale for biophoton signaling.

There is a methodological lesson too. Photobiomodulation experiments can control wavelength, irradiance, pulse pattern, exposure time, and total dose. Endogenous UPE studies often measure whatever spectrum the biology happens to emit. If researchers want to show that spontaneous photons have function, they can use the external-light toolbox in reverse.

First measure the endogenous emission.

Then reproduce it artificially.

Not approximately bright.

Actually weak.

Match the wavelength distribution as closely as possible. Match the temporal pattern. Match the geometry. Deliver the estimated flux to the proposed receiver. Ask whether the biological effect appears.

This is substitution again, but photobiomodulation technology makes it feasible.

The experiment may be humiliatingly difficult because standard LEDs are too bright. Neutral-density filters, calibrated attenuators, single-photon sources, or heavily reduced duty cycles may be needed to reach UPE-level fluxes. The resulting apparatus would look less like therapy and more like quantum optics.

That is exactly why it would be valuable.

If a receiver responds at realistic endogenous photon counts, the communication hypothesis moves from speculative to mechanistically plausible in one step.

If it only responds when the light is increased a million-fold, then photobiomodulation cannot be used as evidence for UPE signaling without an additional amplification mechanism.

A null result at endogenous flux would not prove that photons are never used biologically. The true local flux might be higher than the external estimate, or the wrong receiver might have been chosen. But it would eliminate a large class of casual analogies.

The medical literature also teaches another caution: biphasic dose responses.

Many photobiomodulation studies report windows in which a moderate dose produces an effect while higher or lower doses do less, sometimes summarized by hormetic or biphasic response models. The reality varies by system, and poorly standardized dosimetry has contributed to inconsistent results.

This is relevant because enthusiasts can use nonlinearity as an escape hatch.

If high light works and low light does not, maybe lower still would work through a different regime.

If one dose fails, perhaps it missed the window.

A hypothesis with an unknown dose window can survive indefinitely.

The solution is a dose-response curve over many orders of magnitude, including the spontaneous biological range.

Plot it.

Do not narrate around it.

Another useful comparison is vision. The human visual system under dark adaptation can detect remarkably small numbers of photons. Rod photoreceptors contain rhodopsin, a molecule evolved for photon capture. Absorption changes retinal configuration and initiates a biochemical amplification cascade. The signal is then processed by neural circuits that pool across receptors and time.

Single-photon sensitivity is therefore biologically possible.

But it is not generic.

A liver cell is not a rod cell merely because it contains chromophores.

The visual system pays enormous anatomical and metabolic costs to achieve sensitivity: specialized receptors, pigment regeneration, dark adaptation, neural convergence, noise control, and a protected geometry.

If mitochondria or other organelles operate at similar sensitivity, one should expect specialized machinery or at least measurable molecular adaptation.

This gives evolutionary biology a role in the test.

Search for photoreceptor-like proteins localized near proposed emitters. Compare species living in different optical environments. Examine whether knocking out candidate chromophores changes responses to UPE-level irradiation. Look for conserved structures that would be inexplicable if light were irrelevant.

The strongest endogenous-light theory should leave fingerprints in anatomy and genetics.

Photobiomodulation also complicates UPE experiments because the act of observing or preparing samples can change them through light. A specimen exposed to bright illumination before dark measurement may have altered mitochondrial state, generated reactive oxygen species, or entered a delayed-luminescence period. The measurement protocol becomes an intervention.

Dark adaptation is not only about removing optical afterglow.

It may be allowing biology to recover from illumination.

This is especially important for neural and mitochondrial preparations, where external light used for microscopy can be biologically active. Fluorescent imaging can generate phototoxicity and ROS. A sample studied under a microscope before UPE recording may not be in its native state.

The field's best feature—label-free dark measurement—should therefore be protected from upstream optical contamination.

There is a broader conceptual possibility worth preserving. Endogenous photons may have no evolved signaling function but still exert tiny local photochemical effects. Metabolism generates them; nearby chromophores occasionally absorb them; the resulting reactions slightly alter redox pathways. This would be a feedback loop without communication in the semantic sense.

Think of heat generated by metabolism. Organisms did not invent every molecule of heat as a message, but temperature affects enzyme kinetics and physiology. Evolution can later exploit thermal gradients or thermosensation.

Photons could occupy a similar category.

The distinction between by-product and signal may be evolutionary rather than physical.

This is why “does light affect cells?” is too blunt a question.

Of course it can.

The frontier question is whether the light cells naturally generate affects other biological processes at the actual dose, location, spectrum, and timing in which it exists.

Photobiomodulation gives us the tools to ask.

It does not give us permission to skip the answer.
