# The Human Body at Four in the Afternoon

The human body emits light.

That sentence sounds much more important than it is until one adds the units.

The light is far too faint to see. It does not outline the body in an aura. It does not make a person luminous in ordinary darkness. A sensitive photon-counting camera needs long exposures and controlled conditions to image it. Yet the emission is not flat. It changes across the body and across the day.

In 2009, Masaki Kobayashi, Daisuke Kikuchi, and Hitoshi Okamura published a small but enduring experiment in PLOS ONE. They imaged spontaneous ultraweak photon emission from healthy human subjects at different times of day and found a diurnal pattern. Emission intensity rose and fell on a schedule, with a peak in the late afternoon. The spatial distribution across the body was also uneven. The face, in particular, showed relatively strong emission.

The result is often cited as proof that humans “glow.”

A more useful reading is that oxidative metabolism has a clock.

The study sits at the intersection of two rhythms that are easy to confuse. Body temperature follows a circadian pattern. Metabolism follows circadian regulation. Hormones, feeding, activity, sleep pressure, mitochondrial function, redox state, and skin physiology all vary over the day. Ultraweak photon emission can therefore change with time even if no dedicated optical signaling system exists.

The researchers compared the photon rhythm with body temperature and found that the patterns were not identical. That mattered because it pushed against the simplest thermal explanation. Visible-range UPE is not ordinary blackbody radiation, and the timing of its peak need not follow the body's temperature peak exactly. The optical signal appears to reflect biochemical state rather than merely warmth.

This is a good example of what UPE can do without becoming exotic.

A blood test samples molecules. A thermometer samples heat. An electrocardiogram samples voltage differences generated by organized electrical activity. UPE samples rare radiative outcomes of biochemical reactions. Each measurement is indirect. Each becomes useful when the mapping from signal to physiology is stable enough.

The human experiment also reveals why the field should be wary of single-time-point studies.

If photon emission varies naturally across the day, then comparing patients with controls at different times can manufacture a difference. Comparing a treatment group measured in the morning with a control group measured in the afternoon can confound treatment with circadian phase. Feeding, caffeine, exercise, sleep deprivation, skin products, ultraviolet exposure, room temperature, and recent light exposure may all matter.

Weak signals make mundane protocol differences powerful.

This is one reason “biophoton diagnostics” has remained more promise than clinic. It is not enough for disease states to produce different average emissions in one laboratory. The signal must be standardized against normal variation.

The circadian result raises a deeper possibility. If UPE is strongly tied to redox metabolism, it might provide a noninvasive window onto mitochondrial rhythms. Mitochondria are not static power plants. Their morphology, substrate use, respiratory activity, reactive oxygen production, and interaction with cellular clocks change with physiological state. A whole-body optical signal cannot resolve those processes directly, but its temporal pattern may encode their aggregate consequences.

There is a useful distinction here between information for us and information for the organism.

A thermometer contains information about body temperature for a clinician. The mercury is not communicating with the patient. A pulse oximeter extracts information from light absorption by blood. The photons used by the instrument are not necessarily part of an endogenous biological message. Likewise, UPE can carry information about oxidative state to an external detector without serving any signaling function inside the body.

This distinction is frequently lost because information is an overloaded word.

If two variables are statistically correlated, one contains information about the other in a mathematical sense. That does not mean one was sent as a message. Tree rings contain information about past climate. Sedimentary isotopes contain information about ancient oceans. Neither was produced for the paleoclimatologist.

Ultraweak photons may be biological tree rings arriving in real time.

The human body experiment is especially good at demonstrating this modest form of information. The emission pattern changed reproducibly enough across time to reflect physiological rhythm. One can imagine using such a signal to infer metabolic state. That is meaningful even if no cell evolved to read it.

But could cells read it?

Circadian biology makes that question tempting because light is already the dominant environmental synchronizer of the human clock. Photons reaching the retina reset the suprachiasmatic nucleus through well-characterized photoreceptive pathways. Peripheral clocks respond to hormonal, feeding, temperature, and metabolic cues. If tissues emit photons rhythmically, one might wonder whether endogenous light contributes to synchronization.

There is currently no need for that mechanism.

The known circadian system has robust chemical and neural coupling. The endogenous photon flux is tiny compared with environmental light in exposed tissues and compared with established intracellular signaling. A hypothesis of photon-mediated circadian coordination would therefore carry a heavy burden: it would have to explain what optical communication adds that existing pathways cannot.

This is an important rule for frontier biology. A new mechanism should not be rewarded simply for being possible. It should solve an explanatory problem.

What observation is otherwise hard to explain?

What prediction differs from the chemical model?

What intervention uniquely tests the optical route?

Without those questions, endogenous-light hypotheses become decorations on already functioning biology.

The external measurement opportunity is more immediate.

Consider oxidative stress in skin. The skin is accessible, broad, and continuously exposed to environmental insults. Ultraviolet radiation drives reactive oxygen chemistry. Inflammation changes redox state. Wound healing changes metabolism. Aging alters antioxidant capacity and mitochondrial function. UPE could potentially provide a contactless signal related to those processes.

The face's relatively strong emission in the 2009 study may reflect anatomy, metabolism, blood supply, skin chemistry, exposure history, or optical escape. It would be a mistake to assign one cause without targeted experiments. But spatial heterogeneity is exactly what makes imaging potentially useful. A photon counter that gives one whole-body number loses the map.

The map, however, comes with optical bias.

Dark skin and light skin do not transmit visible photons identically. Hemoglobin concentration alters absorption. Hair blocks and scatters. Subcutaneous fat changes geometry. A bright region may represent higher photon production or simply easier escape. Clinical imaging would need correction models much as near-infrared spectroscopy and optical tomography account for tissue properties.

This is where the phrase “label free” can mislead.

A technique can be label free and still require a great deal of modeling.

No fluorescent dye needs to be injected for UPE imaging. That is an advantage. But the tissue labels itself with pigments, blood, water, structure, and metabolism. The detector receives a mixture.

The circadian study also hints at a practical design for replication. Instead of asking only whether people emit photons, a stronger multicenter experiment could ask whether within-person temporal patterns reproduce across instruments and laboratories. Each participant could be measured repeatedly over several days under controlled feeding, sleep, activity, and light exposure. Body temperature, heart rate, glucose, cortisol, melatonin, oxygen consumption, and perhaps blood markers of oxidative stress could be sampled alongside UPE. Skin reflectance and pigmentation could be measured to correct optical escape.

The result would either stabilize UPE as a physiological biomarker or reveal how much of the original pattern depended on uncontrolled conditions.

That is a worthwhile outcome either way.

The field has a habit of treating replication as an act of belief or disbelief. A better replication asks what parameter space the phenomenon occupies.

Maybe the late-afternoon peak is robust across people.

Maybe it shifts with chronotype.

Maybe it tracks feeding more than circadian phase.

Maybe sleep deprivation flattens it.

Maybe exercise produces a transient increase followed by antioxidant compensation.

Maybe skin exposure dominates the camera while deeper metabolism remains invisible.

Each possibility turns a vague glow into physiology.

There is also a philosophical inversion worth noticing. For most of human history, people used visible appearance to infer internal state: pallor, flushing, jaundice, cyanosis, sweating. Modern medicine gradually replaced the unaided eye with instruments that detect signals outside ordinary sensation: X-rays, ultrasound echoes, magnetic resonance, electrical potentials, infrared radiation.

UPE belongs to that tradition more than to mysticism.

The body has always been producing physical traces too weak or too ambiguous for ordinary senses. Technology expands the range of traces we can use.

The fact that one of them is visible-spectrum light creates cultural baggage because light has carried metaphysical meaning for millennia. Halos, souls, enlightenment, radiance, and aura are already waiting in language before the detector arrives.

A false-color UPE image can therefore be recruited into an ancient story faster than a spectroscopy lab can publish its controls.

That is not the instrument's fault.

It does mean the scientist has to be unusually precise.

The human body emits ultraweak photons associated with biochemical processes.

The emission can show diurnal variation.

Sensitive cameras can image it.

Those statements are remarkable and defensible.

They do not imply that visible auras can be perceived by humans, that emotional states have unique optical signatures, that healers exchange measurable biophotons, or that consciousness leaves the body as light.

If any of those stronger claims are true, they need their own experiments.

The late-afternoon body does not need mythology.

It is already doing something beautiful enough: leaking a tiny optical shadow of its chemistry into a room dark enough to notice.
