# The Coherence Problem

Coherence is one of those scientific words that becomes less precise as it travels.

In optics, it has technical meanings tied to stable phase relationships, frequency, time, and space. A laser is highly coherent in ways an incandescent bulb is not. Interference experiments can quantify coherence. The concept belongs to measurement.

In biophoton literature, coherence sometimes becomes a synonym for organization.

The light is coherent, therefore life is coherent.

The photons are coherent, therefore cells share information.

Biological order exists, therefore the radiation must be laser-like.

The word can do several pages of argumentative work without a single interferometer appearing.

Fritz-Albert Popp became the name most associated with this stronger tradition. In the 1970s and 1980s, Popp and collaborators argued that ultraweak photon emission from living systems exhibited properties consistent with a high degree of coherence and that DNA might be an important source. A 1984 paper described photon-count statistics, spectral behavior, decay after illumination, and other observations as evidence for coherence, while proposing DNA as a kind of exciplex laser system far from equilibrium.

This is one of the major forks in the field.

One branch developed UPE as chemiluminescence associated with oxidative metabolism. The other treated biological light as evidence of a deeper organizing electromagnetic field.

The branches sometimes cite the same measurements.

That is why the coherence claim has to be reconstructed rather than mocked.

A biological system is unquestionably far from thermodynamic equilibrium. Cells maintain gradients, repair themselves, cycle energy, build ordered structures, and coordinate reactions over many scales. None of that requires optical coherence. A hurricane is organized and dissipative without being a laser. A flock of birds is coordinated without phase-coherent radiation. Biological order does not automatically migrate into every emitted photon.

To claim optical coherence, one needs optical evidence.

Temporal coherence asks how stable the phase relationship remains over time. It is related to spectral linewidth: a narrow frequency distribution can maintain phase correlation longer than a broad one. Spatial coherence asks whether different points across a wavefront maintain predictable phase relationships. Interference measurements are classic tests.

Ultraweak biological emission is challenging because the photon rate is so low that conventional coherence measurements become difficult. Long collection times can average over changing biological states. The source may consist of many independent emitters. Tissue scattering destroys directional information. Broad spectra reduce coherence length.

These difficulties do not make coherence impossible.

They make indirect inference risky.

Photon-count statistics are one route. Different light sources can exhibit different statistical distributions. Ideal coherent light has Poissonian photon number statistics. Thermal light has bunching and super-Poissonian behavior. Quantum states can show sub-Poissonian statistics. But real detectors add dark counts, dead time, afterpulsing, gain variation, binning choices, and background. A biological source that changes intensity during measurement can generate count distributions that mimic nontrivial statistics.

A Poisson-like count distribution is not enough to declare a biological laser.

Many independent random emitters can also approach Poisson statistics under particular conditions.

Delayed luminescence adds another layer. After biological material is illuminated, its photon emission can decay over time. Some researchers have fit those decay curves with hyperbolic rather than simple exponential functions and interpreted the behavior as evidence of cooperative or coherent processes.

But a non-exponential decay can arise from a mixture of many ordinary relaxation processes with different lifetimes.

Complexity is not coherence by default.

The strongest version of Popp's program proposed that living systems maintain a coherent photon field involved in regulation. The attraction is obvious. It offers a physical medium that could coordinate vast numbers of biochemical reactions without relying only on diffusion and local contact. The organism becomes not merely a chemical network but an electromagnetic whole.

The problem is not that this sounds grand.

The problem is that a coordination theory needs causal interventions.

If coherent photons regulate cells, disrupting coherence while preserving average photon energy should alter function. If DNA is a major coherent source, changing DNA state should produce predictable optical effects that cannot be explained by associated chemical changes. If the field coordinates growth, externally imposing matched coherent light at endogenous intensities should influence the relevant process more strongly than incoherent light with the same spectrum and photon count.

These are difficult experiments, but they are the ones the claim demands.

A field can remain controversial for decades when its evidence is mostly compatible with both an ordinary mechanism and an extraordinary mechanism.

UPE intensity rises under oxidative stress.

Ordinary account: more reactive excited species produce more chemiluminescence.

Coherent-field account: stress perturbs the organizing photon field.

If both predict “light changes,” the observation does not decide between them.

The theories need divergent predictions.

A modern 2024 paper by Nathan Babcock revisited Gurwitsch's onion experiment using an open quantum systems framework. It explored whether ultraweak ultraviolet emissions and biological responses could be modeled within quantum biology. The paper is intellectually serious and mathematically ambitious. It is not an experimental demonstration that Gurwitsch's original causal claims were correct.

Theory can show that a mechanism is physically conceivable.

It cannot promote conceivability into observation.

This distinction is especially important in quantum biology, where the word quantum attracts both genuine frontier science and metaphysical overreach. Photosynthesis involves quantum mechanics because all molecular photochemistry does. Radical-pair magnetoreception models invoke quantum spin dynamics in a specific experimentally constrained way. Enzyme tunneling and excitonic energy transfer can be studied quantitatively.

“Quantum” is productive when it identifies a mechanism with measurable parameters.

It is decorative when it merely means subtle, holistic, or mysterious.

Biophoton coherence claims often drift between those uses.

There is a straightforward experiment modern technology makes increasingly possible. Collect UPE from a stable biological preparation through a high-efficiency optical system and perform Hanbury Brown and Twiss-type intensity correlation measurements. Measure second-order coherence functions under controlled conditions. Compare living tissue with matched chemiluminescent reactions known to arise from oxidative chemistry. Vary metabolic state. Measure the detector's afterpulsing and dead-time artifacts. Repeat across laboratories.

If biological emission shows reproducible photon statistics inconsistent with known chemical luminescence and instrument noise, the coherence case gains substance.

Interferometric tests are harder but not unimaginable with sufficient collection efficiency. One can also search for narrow spectral components that would be unexpected from broad oxidative emission.

The important phrase is unexpected from broad oxidative emission.

A coherent component, if it exists, may be a tiny fraction of total UPE. Whole-body images would drown it in incoherent background. This possibility is often invoked to explain why direct evidence is sparse.

That move is legitimate only if it leads to a targeted measurement.

Otherwise the theory becomes immune to negative results: when coherence is not seen, the coherent photons were hidden; when unusual statistics appear, they are evidence.

A scientific hypothesis must risk disappointment.

There is another way to ask the coherence question without fetishizing photons. Does biological organization generate correlations in emission beyond what one would expect from independent local chemistry?

Simultaneously image distant regions of a tissue with high temporal resolution. Control for shared changes in oxygen, temperature, and systemic metabolism. Ask whether photon bursts correlate across space at delays inconsistent with common chemical drivers. Perturb one region locally and measure whether optical correlations propagate.

Even a positive result would not prove phase coherence.

But it could reveal organized emission dynamics worth explaining.

This is a better route than beginning with the word laser.

The DNA-laser metaphor is particularly sticky because DNA already occupies too much conceptual territory. It is genetic material, an information-bearing polymer, a structural molecule, a substrate for repair and transcription. Calling it an antenna, photon store, or laser source gives it another role that feels symbolically inevitable.

Nature does not care about symbolism.

DNA and associated chromatin can participate in oxidation. Nucleic acids can exhibit photophysical behavior. Their bases absorb ultraviolet light. Excited states and energy transfer in nucleic acids are legitimate subjects of physical chemistry.

The question is not whether DNA interacts with photons.

Of course it does.

The question is whether spontaneous biological photon emission from DNA has the intensity, coherence, and functional role attributed to it in stronger biophoton theories.

A 2024 Scientific Reports paper claimed ultraweak photon emission from isolated barley genomic DNA and interpreted several observations in terms of non-equilibrium phase transitions and coherent emission at a DNA-water interface. The work is provocative. It is also a reminder of how far interpretation can outrun consensus. Extraordinary language appears quickly around statistical and optical measurements that need independent replication.

Replication should focus on the parts that can kill the theory.

Does purified DNA emit above matched buffer controls across independent laboratories?

Does emission scale predictably with DNA mass and conformation?

Do contaminants, oxidation products, or residual proteins explain it?

Can phase coherence be measured directly?

Does sequence matter?

Does dead synthetic DNA behave differently from biologically extracted DNA?

If the source is a generic photochemical property of nucleic acids, the result may be interesting without supporting a living coherent field.

The coherence debate is therefore not a sideshow.

It teaches the central discipline of this book: nearby truths do not stack automatically.

Living systems are organized.

Living systems emit photons.

Some biological photochemistry exhibits quantum behavior.

DNA interacts with light.

None of those statements, alone or together, proves that organisms are regulated by a coherent biophoton field.

The stronger claim may still be true.

It simply has to arrive by experiment rather than grammar.
