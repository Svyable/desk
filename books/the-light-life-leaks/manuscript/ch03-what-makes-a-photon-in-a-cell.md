# What Makes a Photon in a Cell

A biological photon does not begin as light.

It begins as chemistry under strain.

A molecule is oxidized. An electron moves where it should not remain. Oxygen, indispensable and dangerous, accepts energy into reactive forms. A lipid chain breaks into a cascade. A carbonyl reaches an electronically excited state. Singlet oxygen forms and decays. Energy that might have been dissipated through collisions, heat, or chemical rearrangement occasionally leaves as a photon.

That is the prosaic engine under most modern accounts of ultraweak photon emission.

Prosaic is the wrong word.

The fact that a living cell occasionally converts the violence of oxidation into visible or near-visible light is not ordinary in the sense of being uninteresting. It is ordinary only in the scientific sense that no new force has to be invoked. The same chemistry that makes oxidative stress biologically consequential also creates an optical side channel. The light is a receipt from redox metabolism.

Reactive oxygen species are often spoken of as though they were one substance. They are a family. Superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, peroxyl radicals, and related reactive intermediates differ in lifetimes, reactivities, locations, and biological consequences. Some are produced deliberately as signals. Some arise as unavoidable by-products. Some are rapidly detoxified. Some initiate chain reactions that damage membranes or proteins. The phrase oxidative stress compresses this chemistry into a condition: production and handling have moved out of balance enough to alter function or damage.

Photon emission can emerge during that imbalance because electronically excited products are generated in oxidative reactions.

Lipid peroxidation is a particularly important route. Polyunsaturated fatty acids in membranes are chemically vulnerable. A radical can abstract a hydrogen, creating a lipid radical that reacts with oxygen and propagates a chain. Decomposition of lipid hydroperoxides can yield excited carbonyl species. When those excited states relax, photons can be emitted. Singlet oxygen can also produce characteristic emission either directly or through reactions that generate excited products.

The cell does not need a luciferase gene. It needs chemistry energetic enough to populate an excited electronic state.

This distinction separates UPE from familiar bioluminescence. A firefly has evolved an efficient light-producing biochemical system in which emitted photons are the point. Ultraweak emission is generally many orders of magnitude dimmer and is not known, in the basic case, to be an evolved luminous display. Its inefficiency is part of the phenomenon. Vast numbers of molecular events occur for every photon that escapes.

That inefficiency makes UPE useful as a probe.

A detector is not watching metabolism directly. It is sampling rare radiative exits from chemical pathways entangled with metabolism. If those pathways become more active under oxidative stress, photon counts can rise. If antioxidant systems change the chemistry, emission can change. If mitochondrial respiration alters reactive oxygen production, the optical output can shift. If tissue dies and organized metabolism collapses, the pattern changes again.

The temptation is to infer too much from intensity.

More photons does not simply mean more metabolism.

A tissue can have high metabolic flux and efficient redox control. Another can have lower overall energy turnover but severe oxidative imbalance. Temperature can accelerate reaction rates. Oxygen availability can alter pathways. Pigments and tissue structure can absorb emitted photons before they reach a detector. Skin thickness, fur, blood, melanin, water, lipid content, and geometry all affect what escapes. An observed count is the result of production minus losses, filtered by anatomy and instrumentation.

This is why the mechanistic literature matters more than dramatic images.

In 1991, B. Devaraj and colleagues measured ultraweak light emission from isolated rat liver nuclei using a sensitive single-photon counting system. The emission was oxygen dependent. The investigators interpreted it as arising from oxidative or peroxidative processes. Their perturbations implicated singlet oxygen as a possible contributor. The significance was not that nuclei had been discovered to glow in some mystical sense. It was that a physical signal could be tied experimentally to known redox chemistry.

Three decades later, spectral studies of human skin pushed the same question toward molecular discrimination. Work published in 2020 examined ultraviolet-A-induced UPE from biomolecules relevant to skin and from human skin tissue. Different molecular preparations produced different spectral patterns. The tissue spectrum looked like a mixture rather than a single line from one exotic emitter. That is what a crowded oxidative system should look like.

A photon counter can therefore become a biochemical instrument without requiring a complete map from photon wavelength to molecular source.

The important phrase is without requiring complete.

Biology rarely gives one-to-one correspondences. A spectral band can have multiple contributors. One reactive species can initiate several pathways. The same molecule can behave differently in different environments. Photon emission is attractive because it is noninvasive and label free, but those advantages come with an inverse problem: the detector sees escaping photons, then researchers infer which chemistry produced them.

Inverse problems are where scientific modesty earns its salary.

Suppose a stressed plant leaf gets brighter in UPE. Several things may be happening at once. Mechanical injury disrupts membranes. Calcium waves and electrical signals propagate. Reactive oxygen species rise locally and systemically. Defense hormones change. Enzymes turn on. Lipoxygenase pathways engage. Cell walls are modified. Microbes encounter newly exposed tissue. The wound edge becomes a chemical construction site.

Photon emission is one trace among many.

The trace is valuable because it can be imaged over time without adding a fluorescent reporter that itself perturbs the system. But one should resist the desire to make the light the master variable simply because it is visually seductive.

This is a recurring problem in measurement science. The channel that becomes easiest to visualize begins to dominate the explanation. Functional MRI did this to neuroscience for a time; gene expression heat maps do it in molecular biology; false-color satellite imagery does it in environmental science. Once invisible data are converted into pictures, the picture acquires a psychological realism larger than the underlying measurement.

UPE images are especially vulnerable because the published colors are often arbitrary. The animal is not green, red, or blue with metabolic fire. The colors map count intensity. A dark pixel may mean fewer detected photons, not absence. A bright pixel may represent integration over minutes. Spatial smoothing or binning can make sparse events look continuous.

The chemistry itself is temporally sparse at the level of individual detected photons and continuous at the scale of the underlying reactions.

That distinction opens an interesting analytical possibility. Intensity is only one property of emission. Temporal statistics may contain information about reaction dynamics. Spectral composition may distinguish pathways. Spatial patterns may localize oxidative processes. Correlations with oxygen consumption, redox state, membrane potential, enzyme activity, or electrophysiology can identify which biological transitions alter the optical trace.

Researchers interested in coherence have sometimes treated photon statistics as evidence that the emission is more organized than ordinary chemical luminescence. We will come to those claims later because the word coherence carries several meanings and often arrives before the instrument model. For now, the conservative mechanistic picture is enough: oxidative reactions generate electronically excited species; some decay radiatively; the emitted photons can be counted.

Mitochondria sit near the center of the story because they are major sites of cellular redox metabolism.

The mitochondrial electron transport chain moves electrons through complexes in the inner membrane, couples that transfer to proton pumping, and uses the resulting electrochemical gradient to make ATP. The system is efficient but not perfect. Electron leakage can reduce oxygen prematurely, generating superoxide and downstream reactive species. The rates depend on substrate, membrane potential, respiratory state, oxygen, enzyme condition, and stress.

Mitochondria also contain dense assemblies of chromophores and redox cofactors. Cytochromes absorb light. Flavins absorb light. Porphyrin-containing molecules absorb and emit. None of that proves that mitochondria have an endogenous optical language. It does mean that living cells contain both plausible sources and plausible absorbers of photons.

That is where the communication hypothesis gets its first foothold.

If mitochondria emit photons as a consequence of redox activity, and if other mitochondrial molecules are photoresponsive, perhaps the emission is not entirely wasted. Evolution could exploit a channel that already exists.

The hypothesis is conceptually reasonable.

Its difficulty is quantitative.

A molecule capable of absorbing a photon is not automatically a receiver for an ultraweak signal. The number of photons reaching it matters. So does the absorption cross-section. So do competing photons from the environment, thermal noise in downstream chemistry, stochastic variation in redox state, and tissue attenuation. A photoreceptor exposed to sunlight lives in a different photon economy from an enzyme buried in tissue receiving a handful of endogenous photons.

Biology has solved extreme sensitivity before. Rod photoreceptors can respond to very small numbers of photons under dark-adapted conditions. Some photochemical reactions achieve amplification because one absorbed photon changes a molecule that triggers a large downstream cascade. Magnetoreception hypotheses based on radical pairs similarly explore how weak physical influences might bias biochemical reactions that are then amplified.

But invoking amplification after the fact is not enough.

A proposed endogenous photoreceptor should be identified, its spectral sensitivity measured, its location matched to the emitting source, and its response demonstrated at realistic UPE intensities. Without that sequence, the argument remains possibility rather than mechanism.

The mechanistic account also clarifies why death does not produce a binary optical switch.

Cells do not stop all chemistry at the instant an organism dies. Oxygen remains in tissue. Enzymes continue temporarily. Reactive species persist. Membranes undergo peroxidation. Ischemia and reperfusion-like transitions can alter redox chemistry. Some postmortem processes may transiently increase oxidative reactions even as organized metabolism collapses. A detector can therefore see residual or changing emission after death.

This matters when popular accounts describe the 2025 mouse study as though light simply vanished at a metaphysical boundary. The published experiment showed a strong contrast between live and recently euthanized mice under its conditions. The chemically sensible interpretation is a major change in ongoing physiological and metabolic processes, not an instantaneous universal law that living matter emits and dead matter does not.

Plants make the mechanistic connection even clearer because stress can be applied locally and observed over time.

Mechanical damage triggers ROS production as part of wound signaling and defense. Herbivore-associated molecules can modify the response. Heat changes reaction rates and stress pathways. Hydrogen peroxide application directly alters the oxidative environment. In the 2025 imaging study, wounded leaf regions were brighter than unwounded regions, and chemical treatments changed the pattern. Benzocaine produced a surprisingly strong effect under the tested conditions, a result that remains mechanistically open.

The bright wound is not a message simply because it is spatially localized.

It is a map of chemistry escaping as light.

There is a useful humility in that phrase. A map is already extraordinary. If UPE can noninvasively visualize oxidative dynamics without dyes, transgenes, or ionizing radiation, it could become useful in plant phenotyping, tissue monitoring, drug screening, dermatology, neurology, oncology, and aging research. Those applications depend on calibration and specificity, not on proving a secret optical communication network.

The field sometimes seems disappointed by its own strongest result.

“Only oxidative chemistry” is said with the tone of someone learning that the haunted house has wiring behind the walls.

But oxidative chemistry is where aerobic life negotiates energy and damage. It is where mitochondria make useful work and generate dangerous by-products. It is where immune cells weaponize reactive species. It is where ischemic tissue fails, where ultraviolet radiation injures skin, where plants signal wounds, where aging pathways accumulate molecular scars.

If those processes leak measurable photons, the leak is a window.

Whether it is also a language is a separate experiment.
