## Thesis

Before “disclosure” became a political demand and a media category, anomalous claims already moved through American power by several very different routes. Christopher “Kit” Green encountered parapsychology and remote-viewing work through the scientific-intelligence world. Alonzo L. McDonald entered Jimmy Carter's White House as a management operator responsible for how the institution itself ran. James A. Baker III became Ronald Reagan's chief of staff and one of the most consequential gatekeepers of the modern presidency.

Later UFO literature, Jacques Vallée's journals, testimony, and reporting about a supposed Collins Elite place these kinds of figures near a much larger story about classified anomalous phenomena, elite prayer networks, and competing extraterrestrial, psychological, national-security, and theological interpretations. The public record does not presently establish that Green, McDonald, and Baker formed one secret group or shared one conclusion.

That gap is the book.

*Before Disclosure* asks how unusual information reaches power before government has a public office, vocabulary, or consensus for it—and how later generations turn partial records, private relationships, compartmented programs, participant memories, and genuine secrecy into apparently coherent hidden histories.

## Evidence rule

People, programs, meetings, beliefs, and conclusions are separate propositions. A documented government role does not prove participation in an anomalous program. A diary entry proves what the diarist recorded, not the truth of every underlying claim. A later interview is not contemporaneous documentation. A classified program's existence is not evidence that a separate alleged program existed. The book will mark verified/declassified facts, credible but incomplete evidence, disputed allegations, and speculative synthesis explicitly.
