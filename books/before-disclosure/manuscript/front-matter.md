# Before Disclosure

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

The word disclosure assumes there is a thing to disclose.

That sounds obvious until you try to draw it.

A program? A set of programs? A body of evidence? A crash? A belief held by a few officials? A collection of unresolved reports? A counterintelligence story? A scientific anomaly? A theological interpretation? A bureaucracy that never agreed with itself?

The modern conversation often begins at the end. It imagines a secret already assembled somewhere inside government and asks when the doors will open.

This book begins earlier.

It follows three men who entered American power through different doors: Christopher “Kit” Green through scientific intelligence and the CIA's encounter with remote-viewing research; Alonzo L. McDonald through management and the Carter White House; James A. Baker III through presidential politics and the Reagan White House.

Their documented careers are not a conspiracy diagram.

That is precisely why they are useful.

Around those careers grew a second archive: Jacques Vallée's journals, participant recollections, UFO literature, stories about private prayer networks, and claims that some officials interpreted anomalous phenomena in theological rather than extraterrestrial terms. Some of that material may preserve information the formal record missed. Some may be accurate but incomplete. Some may be wrong. Some may have become persuasive only because the documents that would settle the question remain unavailable.

Before deciding what government concealed, we need to understand how government encountered the unknown.

Who was allowed to ask a strange question without losing standing?

Who could route it to a scientist?

Who could put it in front of a president?

Who could stop it?

Who could rename it?

And who, decades later, could make a missing file look like an answer?

This is a book about the machinery before the announcement.
