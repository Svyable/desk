# Chapter 1 — Three Doors

Christopher Green's door opened onto a laboratory.

Alonzo McDonald's opened onto an organization chart.

James Baker's opened onto the Oval Office.

The temptation is to connect the three before we have earned the lines.

That temptation is especially strong in the history of unidentified flying objects, remote viewing, psychic research, presidential briefings, and what came to be called disclosure. The subject has spent decades accumulating names. Once a name appears twice—in a diary, a memoir, a declassified file, a conference, an address book, a later interview—it begins to behave like a node. Put enough nodes on a page and a network appears whether the historical actors knew they were in one or not.

So let us begin with the doors.

In the early 1970s, Stanford Research Institute conducted work on what its researchers called remote viewing: the claimed ability to describe a distant target under conditions intended to block ordinary sensory access. The Central Intelligence Agency's released files contain reports of the work. They include the famous West Virginia target experiments, client-supplied coordinates, efforts at double-blind procedure, and claims that some descriptions corresponded with information known to the sponsor.

The released record also contains something less cinematic and more important: government people were not merely reading newspaper stories about psychics. Intelligence organizations had contracted, monitored, challenged, and evaluated research they considered uncertain enough to test and potentially consequential enough not to ignore.

Christopher “Kit” Green appears in participant histories of that work as an early CIA contract monitor and scientific interlocutor. Russell Targ's retrospective account names him directly. CIA-released documents independently establish the agency's sponsorship, the SRI work, and the use of government contract monitors, although recovering the cleanest declassified chain that identifies Green by name in each relevant role remains a research task.

That distinction matters.

The sentence “the CIA investigated remote viewing” is documented.

The sentence “Kit Green was involved” is strongly supported by participant accounts and surrounding archival material.

The sentence “therefore Green knew the truth about UFOs” is not a sentence the first two facts are capable of producing.

Green is valuable because he occupied a difficult professional position: somebody inside an intelligence institution had to decide what to do with claims that sounded ridiculous, might be fraudulent, might be artifacts of experimental design, might be psychologically interesting, or—under the most consequential hypothesis—might reveal a capability an adversary could exploit.

The job was not to believe.

The job was not to disbelieve.

The job was to prevent ridicule from becoming an analytic method.

That is one door through which the anomalous enters government. A scientist or analyst is told: this is improbable, but the cost of being wrong might be high. Look.

Alonzo L. McDonald entered through a different door.

McDonald had been a senior business executive and management consultant before joining the Carter administration. The Miller Center's oral history identifies him as Special Representative for Trade Negotiations and later Director of the White House Staff. He moved into the White House in 1979 after a staff review and described his job in managerial terms: operations, organization, communications, personnel, the machinery through which a presidency tries to become a functioning institution.

A White House staff director does not need to know every secret to matter to secrecy.

That is the organizational paradox worth holding.

Presidential power is often imagined as a pyramid. Information rises. Decisions descend. The president sits at the apex, so the apex must contain the complete picture.

Real institutions do not work that way.

They route.

The person who controls a calendar may not control a policy, but controls access to time. The person who designs staff process may not possess every intelligence compartment, but shapes which offices meet, how papers move, and what kinds of questions acquire a place in the formal flow. The person who acts as an honest broker may become important precisely because he is not supposed to own the substance.

McDonald, in his own oral history, emphasized that kind of role.

Later literature makes more ambitious claims about McDonald: links to elite Christian networks, anomalous briefings, and stories that would connect White House management to a hidden theological interpretation of UFO phenomena. Those claims are interesting enough to investigate and weak enough, at present, that they cannot be used as the foundation.

That makes McDonald more useful, not less.

If the alleged network was real, we need to find its traces around a man whose ordinary job already placed him near the routing machinery of the presidency.

If the alleged network was not real, we need to understand how a real White House operator became attached to it in later stories.

Both outcomes teach us something.

James Baker's door is easier to see.

After the 1980 election, Ronald Reagan chose Baker as White House chief of staff even though Baker had previously worked against him in Republican politics. Baker's Miller Center oral history is unusually good on what the job meant. The chief of staff was not merely the president's senior employee. The office existed to coordinate an institution full of people who had separate access, separate policy domains, separate constituencies, and separate ways to get a decision.

Baker remembered the danger of what White House staff called “Oh, by the way” decisions: an official could catch the president at the end of a meeting and obtain agreement on something the rest of the process had not examined.

There is no UFO in that anecdote.

There does not need to be.

It tells us something crucial about any claim of a secret presidential briefing. The historical question is never merely whether the president could have been told something without the whole White House knowing. Of course presidential systems contain private conversations and restricted information. The question is what institutional consequences should follow if a particular conversation really occurred.

Was there a tasking afterward?

A meeting?

A security change?

A budget request?

A legal opinion?

A scientific review?

A memo written for somebody outside the room?

A travel decision?

A new compartment?

A follow-up briefing?

Secrecy can narrow the documentary wake. It rarely abolishes causality.

This is where the three doors begin to face one another.

Green represents evaluation: what should intelligence do with evidence that does not fit ordinary categories?

McDonald represents routing: how does an institution decide what reaches the center and in what form?

Baker represents gatekeeping: how does a president receive, sequence, and act on information when many people can claim urgency and only a few can get into the room?

Those roles are documented. The larger network is not.

Yet there is another archive in which the network seems to exist.

Jacques Vallée kept journals.

Vallée is difficult to fit into the usual boxes. Computer scientist, astronomer, venture investor, and one of the most intellectually durable figures in UFO research, he moved through scientific, technical, intelligence-adjacent, and anomalous-research circles for decades. His journals record conversations and names that later readers treat as glimpses behind the curtain.

A journal is a wonderful historical object because it is close to a mind.

That is also its limitation.

A diary can establish that Vallée met someone, heard something, suspected something, or recorded a claim. It can sometimes preserve a detail nowhere else survives. It does not automatically verify what a source told him. When a journal entry says an official was briefed, we need to know whether Vallée witnessed the briefing, heard about it from a participant, heard about it secondhand, or inferred it.

The same rule applies to Nick Redfern's later reporting on a supposed group often called the Collins Elite, whose alleged members are said to have interpreted UFO phenomena through a Christian demonological framework. The story has become influential because it offers an explanation for a particular kind of government resistance: perhaps officials did not suppress UFO information because they thought it false, but because some thought engagement with the phenomenon spiritually dangerous.

That is a startling hypothesis.

It is not yet a documented institution.

The name of a group is not the group.

A set of beliefs reported by unnamed or pseudonymous sources is not an organizational chart.

A theological interpretation held by one official is not government policy.

And an actual Christian network in Washington does not prove that every secretive Christian claim about UFOs belongs to it.

These separations are where the history lives.

There is a broader reason this matters now.

“Disclosure” has become a word with bureaucratic gravity. Congress has held hearings. Government offices have adopted UAP terminology. Official reports distinguish unresolved cases from evidence of extraterrestrial origin. Whistleblower allegations have raised claims about retrieval and reverse-engineering programs. The public debate increasingly asks whether a hidden architecture will be exposed.

But any hidden architecture, if it exists, has a prehistory.

It had to begin before it had a name.

Someone had to decide that an observation was worth routing.

Someone had to determine that the issue belonged to intelligence rather than science, or science rather than counterintelligence, or aerospace rather than theology.

Someone had to decide who needed to know.

Someone else had to decide who did not.

That process can create the appearance of a unitary secret even when the reality is fragmented. Different groups can hold different pieces, use different explanations, and classify information for different reasons. Decades later, a researcher can encounter the pieces in reverse order and mistake adjacency for design.

The opposite error is possible too.

Fragmentation can conceal a coherent program.

Informal relationships can route information around formal structures.

Compartmentation can make people working on the same problem believe they are working on different problems.

A later historian can mistake deliberate partition for bureaucratic coincidence.

We do not get to choose between those possibilities on atmosphere.

We have to reconstruct the traffic.

That is the work of this book.

Green at the scientific edge.

McDonald inside the presidential machine.

Baker at the gate.

Three doors.

The question is not whether we can draw lines between them.

The question is whether the lines were there before we picked up the pen.
