## Thesis

The most interesting fact about the U.S. government's remote-viewing programs is neither that intelligence agencies proved psychic perception nor that credulous bureaucrats wasted money on nonsense. It is that a succession of institutions kept finding a weak, irregular signal interesting enough to test while repeatedly failing to turn it into dependable intelligence. The book treats that gap—between statistical anomaly and operational utility—as the real subject.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified/declassified/documented:** program existence, tasking, protocols, evaluations, termination, and released session records.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** retrospective accounts by participants and officials where records only partly corroborate detail.
3. **Disputed:** celebrated operational hits, claims of unique intelligence value, Soviet-threat narratives, and strong interpretations of laboratory effects.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** whether the persistence of the program reflects a genuine small anomaly, institutional threat psychology, selection effects, or some mixture.

## Defeat condition

The central thesis fails if the archival record shows either a robust, independently repeatable intelligence capability with useful specificity or, at the opposite extreme, no persistent empirical anomaly and no serious internal effort to discriminate signal from coincidence.
