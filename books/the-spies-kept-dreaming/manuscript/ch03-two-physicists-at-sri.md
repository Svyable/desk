# Two Physicists at SRI

Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ made an unusual migration from physics and engineering into a research problem that most physicists preferred to keep outside the building. At Stanford Research Institute, later SRI International, they helped turn clairvoyance from séance vocabulary into experimental vocabulary: targets, protocols, viewers, judges, coordinates.

The change in nouns mattered. Bureaucracies can contract for a protocol more easily than for a miracle.

The early SRI work became famous partly because particular sessions seemed spectacular. It also became controversial because experimental design, sensory leakage, judging procedures, subject selection and retrospective scoring can all enlarge an apparent effect without fraud. A person can be sincere, a researcher can be careful by ordinary standards, and an experiment can still contain enough degrees of freedom to manufacture astonishment after the fact.

This is where the history becomes more interesting than the caricature. Critics forced protocol changes. Researchers responded with tighter procedures. The subject did not simply vanish under better controls, at least according to advocates and some later statistical assessments. Nor did it mature into an effect that ordinary laboratories could summon on demand.

That unstable middle is the book's territory.

A technology can survive weak repeatability if each success is spectacular and failures are cheap. Intelligence collection is less forgiving. A satellite that works one day in twenty is a broken satellite. A human source who is right unpredictably may still be useful if provenance and context allow confidence to be calibrated. Remote viewing offered neither ordinary machine reliability nor ordinary source accountability. The viewer often could not explain why an impression should be trusted.

SRI helped create a method for producing data. It did not solve the harder problem of knowing when the data deserved belief.
