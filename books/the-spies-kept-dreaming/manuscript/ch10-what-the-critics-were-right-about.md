# What the Critics Were Right About

Ray Hyman's skepticism matters because remote viewing is almost designed to exploit human matching machinery.

We are good at finding resemblance after the answer is known. We undercount misses. We remember dramatic correspondences. We tolerate flexible descriptions when a story has already become compelling. Small procedural leaks can matter enormously when the claimed effect is small.

None of this requires fraud.

Critics were also right to insist that extraordinary operational claims cannot be established by a handful of retrospective success stories. If a program ran for years and accepted hundreds of tasks, the denominator is not optional. The glamorous cases have to be placed beside the dead sessions, vague outputs, wrong locations and occasions when ordinary intelligence already pointed toward the same answer.

The harshest skeptical lesson is about utility. A method that cannot tell its user when it is correct can remain statistically interesting and still be practically close to worthless.

But skepticism becomes weaker when it turns these methodological points into a claim that there is nothing left to study. The historical record shows serious institutions repeatedly tested the subject, critics repeatedly identified weaknesses, protocols repeatedly changed, and some statistical controversy survived. That is not proof of an anomalous information channel. It is enough to make dismissal by facial expression intellectually lazy.

The critic's strongest weapon is not ridicule. It is bookkeeping.
