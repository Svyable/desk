# The Customer Could Not Use It

Intelligence is not a contest to say something true. It is a contest to reduce uncertainty before a decision.

This distinction explains the apparent paradox in the final assessments. Evaluators could acknowledge material they considered anomalous or statistically above chance while still concluding that remote viewing had not demonstrated adequate intelligence value.

A customer needs specificity. “Something metallic near water” may be correct and useless. “The vessel is in berth three until 0400” can be checked and acted upon. The second statement is also easier to prove wrong.

Weakly constrained systems often survive by living in the first category. Their outputs accumulate partial truths without accepting enough falsifiable detail to become operationally decisive.

The lesson is broader than remote viewing. Organizations routinely confuse interesting output with decision value. Forecasts, consultants, intelligence products and algorithmic systems can all sound insightful while leaving the user's probability distribution almost unchanged.

The customer test is brutally simple: what did you know after receiving this that you did not know before, how much more confident were you, and what did you do differently?

For STARGATE, the public record does not support a robust affirmative answer across the program. That is why the operational verdict can be negative without settling the underlying scientific question.
