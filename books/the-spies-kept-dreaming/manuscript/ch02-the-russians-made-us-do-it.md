# The Russians Made Us Do It

Cold War research budgets were often built from a peculiar syllogism: the adversary is interested; therefore the subject may matter; therefore ignorance is dangerous.

This did not require belief. It required asymmetry of fear. If Soviet researchers were spending money on parapsychology and the phenomenon was worthless, matching them wasted money. If it worked even weakly and the United States ignored it, the imagined cost could be enormous. Intelligence organizations are designed to dislike the second error more than the first.

That threat logic deserves to be separated from the scientific evidence. Reports about Soviet “psychotronics” were themselves a mixture of documented institutions, translated publications, secondhand claims and mirror-imaging. American officials could observe Soviet interest without knowing whether Soviet scientists had discovered anything. One bureaucracy's investigation became another bureaucracy's justification.

This feedback loop is common in strategic technology. Missile gaps, bomber gaps and exotic weapons rumors all exploit the same difficulty: secret systems are hardest to evaluate precisely when the penalty for underestimating them appears largest. Parapsychology added another complication. The claimed mechanism was unclear, so ordinary engineering estimates could not bound capability. There was no obvious aperture size, fuel requirement or thermal signature to calculate.

The Soviet-threat story therefore belongs in evidence class one only when it concerns what American agencies documented themselves doing in response. Claims about what Soviet programs actually achieved require a separate ledger. The temptation to merge those columns is enormous. It is also how threat assessment becomes folklore.

Remote viewing entered an intelligence ecosystem already primed for low-probability, high-consequence bets. The program's persistence cannot be understood by asking only whether a laboratory p-value crossed a conventional threshold. Somebody also had to decide what it would mean to be wrong in the dangerous direction.

That is not proof of psi. It is proof that uncertainty has a budget.
