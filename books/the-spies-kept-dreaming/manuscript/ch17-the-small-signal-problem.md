# The Small Signal Problem

The hardest version of remote viewing is not the spectacular one. It is the small one.

Suppose future preregistered, multisite experiments repeatedly produced an effect barely above chance, robust to ordinary sensory leakage and analytical flexibility, yet too weak to identify a submarine or hostage reliably. What would we call that?

Science would have a problem worth pursuing. Intelligence still might not have a tool.

This possibility helps explain why the argument has lasted. Advocates and critics often optimize for different thresholds. The advocate asks whether the null model is adequate. The operator asks whether the output changes action. The skeptic may ask whether methodological artifacts can explain the excess. These questions can yield different answers without anyone changing the data.

Small effects are also where publication bias, optional analysis and subtle leakage do the most damage. The weaker the signal, the more methodological cleanliness matters.

The honest frontier is therefore neither “psychic spying was real” nor “nothing happened.” It is a demand for an experiment whose failure would actually disappoint believers and whose success would actually surprise skeptics.

Anything softer merely keeps the argument alive.
