# What Would Count Now?

A modern test does not need another legend. It needs paperwork before the first viewer sits down.

Register the protocol. Fix the target pool. Separate experimenters who know the target from those who interact with participants. Record every session. Define scoring before feedback. Use independent judges. Publish failures. Replicate across laboratories that disagree about the phenomenon. Make the raw data available. Test whether confidence predicts accuracy. Most importantly, require prospective discrimination among targets rather than retrospective resemblance.

For an intelligence claim, add another layer. Give the product to analysts blind to source type and measure whether it improves forecasts or target identification beyond ordinary baselines. Price the false positives. Ask whether the method contributes unique information.

If remote viewing survives that, the world becomes more interesting.

If it does not, the old archive remains historically interesting for a different reason. It shows intelligent institutions confronting an embarrassing possibility under strategic pressure, sometimes carefully, sometimes imperfectly, for much longer than public memory admits.

The archive does not authorize belief. It authorizes a better experiment.

That may be the strangest inheritance of STARGATE: after all the coordinates, sketches, hostages, psychics, physicists, soldiers and secret stamps, the honest ending is a protocol.
