# The Trouble With a Hit

Suppose a viewer says: large structure, water nearby, metallic, vertical elements, cold, activity underground.

Afterward the target is revealed to be an industrial facility beside a river. The description feels good. But how good? Would it have fit a bridge? A shipyard? A dam? A power station? A naval base? What did the viewer say that was wrong? Which details were recorded before feedback? How many sessions produced nothing memorable?

The remote-viewing argument repeatedly crashes into this scoring problem.

Human language is elastic. Intelligence targets are structurally repetitive. Military and industrial sites contain fences, roads, buildings, towers, vehicles, water, earthworks and people. If the judge knows the target, vague descriptors become magnets for correspondence. If the judge receives several possible targets under blind conditions, the task becomes harder and more informative.

This is why anecdotes age badly. A celebrated hit can remain genuinely puzzling while contributing little to an estimate of performance. The relevant unit is not astonishment. It is discrimination.

The same problem appears outside parapsychology. Medical diagnosis, handwriting comparison, intelligence analysis and machine-learning benchmarks all become inflated when evaluators can unconsciously optimize the match after seeing the answer. Remote viewing merely makes the problem impossible to ignore because the proposed channel of information is itself unknown.

A fair book has to preserve the hits without worshiping them. Some descriptions are difficult to shrug off casually. But a difficult anecdote is not yet a calibrated instrument. The denominator is part of the phenomenon.
