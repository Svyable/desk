# Jessica Utts and Ray Hyman

The 1995 evaluation is memorable because the disagreement did not divide neatly into scientist and mystic.

Statistician Jessica Utts argued that the laboratory evidence supported the existence of anomalous cognition strongly enough that continued proof-oriented experiments were unnecessary; the research question should move toward mechanism and conditions. Psychologist Ray Hyman remained unconvinced that the evidence justified that conclusion and emphasized methodological and replicability problems.

Their disagreement is useful because both could look at substantially overlapping material and assign different epistemic weight to it.

This is not a reason to declare truth subjective. It is a reason to inspect where judgment enters: which studies belong in the dataset, how heterogeneity is treated, what counts as independent replication, whether methodological improvements predict effect size, and how much prior improbability should matter.

The operational conclusion was easier. Even a favorable reading of laboratory statistics did not rescue intelligence utility. CIA did not restore the program.

That split verdict is the intellectual center of the book. A phenomenon can remain scientifically disputed after a capability built around it has already failed its customer test.
