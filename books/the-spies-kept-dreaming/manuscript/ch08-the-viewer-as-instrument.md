# The Viewer as Instrument

Machines are allowed to be boring. Human instruments are not.

Remote-viewing programs depended heavily on individual performers, training histories, interviewer relationships and subjective states. That made the viewer simultaneously sensor, signal processor and narrator. Every layer introduced variance.

The program's training records show an effort to discipline that variance. Viewers wrote sensory fragments, drew shapes, separated impressions from interpretations, and learned procedural stages. Whatever one thinks of the claimed channel, the operational problem was recognizably technical: how do you extract a weak signal from a noisy adaptive system?

The answer never became standardized enough.

Exceptional performers create another trap. If an effect exists mainly in selected individuals, population-average replication can wash it out. But selecting people after observing extraordinary runs invites regression to the mean. The better the initial performance, the more room there is to fall on retest even if nothing mysterious is happening.

A mature science would need prospective selection criteria, preregistered scoring and enough repeated trials to distinguish talent from luck. A mature intelligence capability would additionally need to know when the talented person was having a bad day.

The viewer-as-instrument model is therefore not an insult. It is the strongest version of the claim. Instruments require calibration.
