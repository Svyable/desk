# What the Believers Were Right About

Believers were right about one thing that respectable culture often gets wrong: ridicule is not a method.

A claim does not become false because it is embarrassing. History contains enough discarded observations later rehabilitated to make automatic contempt a poor filter. The correct response to an implausible measurable claim is usually to make the measurement harder to fool.

Remote-viewing advocates also understood that repeated weak effects can matter scientifically even when no single trial convinces. Statistics exists partly because nature does not always perform at theatrical scale.

Their mistake, when it occurred, was often in crossing levels of inference too quickly. A laboratory effect becomes evidence for psi; psi becomes evidence that a celebrated operational session was genuine; the session becomes evidence that intelligence agencies possessed a psychic capability. Each step requires new evidence.

Still, the advocates' insistence on keeping the anomaly visible prevented institutional discomfort from becoming a substitute for replication. That service is easy to miss because the surrounding claims were so large.

The right posture is irritatingly expensive: neither belief nor dismissal, but another well-controlled trial.
