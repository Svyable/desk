# The File Cabinet of Impossible Things

The first useful discipline is to stop being impressed by the stamp.

The CIA's released STARGATE collection is visually irresistible: SECRET markings, NOFORN warnings, session numbers, hand drawings, coordinates, memoranda. It looks like the backstage pass to reality. But classification answers a narrower question. It tells us the government once thought disclosure of the material could damage national security. It does not tell us the proposition under investigation was true.

That distinction is the entrance fee for everything that follows.

By the late Cold War, remote viewing had accumulated several institutional lives and several names. Work moved among CIA, Army intelligence and DIA. The labels changed. The basic proposition remained astonishingly compact: a person, denied ordinary access to a target, might nevertheless describe something about it.

A released 1980 GRILL FLAME report concerning the American hostages in Tehran states the epistemic problem with almost comic neatness. The viewer's impressions were supplied as “raw intelligence data”; interpretation and use belonged to the requester. The bureaucracy had created a category for information that could be collected but not trusted in the ordinary way.

The archive also contains its own antidote to cherry-picking. A 1982 training report for session 876 says post-session analysis found “very little correlation.” Another training record says there was “absolutely no target correlation.” These are not skeptical reconstructions written decades later. They sit inside the same collection enthusiasts mine for hits.

That makes the archive valuable. Failure was not wholly erased.

The question, then, is not whether one can find uncanny pages. In a sufficiently large archive of loosely constrained descriptions, one almost certainly can. The question is what denominator those pages belong to, what rules existed before the target was revealed, how misses were scored, whether judges were blind, whether descriptions were specific enough to discriminate among plausible targets, and whether the result changed a decision.

Those questions are less cinematic than psychic espionage. They are also the only ones capable of surviving contact with it.
