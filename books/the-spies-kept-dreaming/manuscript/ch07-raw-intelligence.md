# Raw Intelligence

“Raw intelligence” is a generous category. It is also a dangerous one.

Ordinary collection systems produce uncertain material all the time. A human source lies. A radar return is clutter. An intercepted phrase is mistranslated. An image hides what matters behind cloud. Intelligence practice exists partly to attach provenance and confidence to imperfect observations.

Remote viewing entered that machinery without a stable error model.

A case officer can learn that a source exaggerates numbers but accurately reports meetings. A radar engineer can characterize false alarms. With enough observations, error becomes something one can model. Remote viewing's defenders often described performance as sporadic: a viewer could be impressive on one target and barren on the next, with no agreed internal indicator telling the analyst which was which.

That is catastrophic for use even if average performance exceeds chance.

The distinction matters beyond psi. Modern systems increasingly produce outputs whose aggregate benchmark performance can look excellent while any individual answer may be difficult to trust. Intelligence has always lived inside that calibration problem. Remote viewing was an unusually pure early version: information arrived without an accepted causal chain and often without a confidence measure that tracked correctness.

The archive's phrase “raw intelligence” should therefore be read literally. Raw means unfinished. It does not mean secretly true.
