# More Than Two Hundred Tasks

The 1995 operational review contains a number more important than any famous anecdote: from 1986 into 1995, the Defense Department program received more than two hundred operational tasks.

Now we have a denominator-shaped object.

The same review describes a later effort to obtain numerical customer evaluations. By May 1995, three viewers had responded to forty tasks from five operational organizations, producing roughly one hundred accuracy and value scores. This is where the program stops looking like a paranormal story and starts looking like an underperforming service bureau.

Customers matter because they bear the cost of ambiguity. A laboratory judge can award correspondence. An analyst must decide whether the product reduces uncertainty enough to justify attention.

The surviving evaluation is therefore central to this book's thesis. It suggests the program was not terminated merely because officials became ideologically hostile to weirdness. The operational case was weak. Information could seem accurate in parts without being specific, timely or reliable enough to add value.

This is the place where future research must become granular: every recoverable task, date, viewer, target type, customer score, corroborating source and claimed outcome in one ledger. Until that exists, “hundreds of missions” is evidence of demand, not success.

Duration proves persistence. It does not prove performance.
