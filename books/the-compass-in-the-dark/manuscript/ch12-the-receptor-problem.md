# The Receptor Problem

A sense can be behaviorally obvious and anatomically elusive when the stimulus penetrates the body.

Light enters through eyes. Odorants enter through epithelia. Sound couples efficiently to specialized mechanical structures. Magnetic fields pass through tissue with little regard for biological boundaries. A receptor could be in the eye, beak, inner ear, nose, brain or somewhere nobody has sampled carefully.

That creates an ugly search problem. Histology has no universal stain for magnetoreception. A magnetite receptor may contain particles too small, sparse or easily confused with ordinary iron metabolism. A radical-pair receptor may look like a familiar flavoprotein doing another job.

The 2012 demolition of the pigeon-beak iron clusters showed how seductive anatomy can be. The 2021 CRY4 result showed the opposite problem: excellent molecular function without causal localization in the behaving animal.

The receptor standard should therefore be demanding. A candidate should be present in an anatomically plausible structure, transduce geomagnetic-scale fields, connect to neural pathways, and produce a predictable behavioral deficit when specifically disrupted. Rescue should restore the behavior. Ideally, altered molecular parameters should shift behavioral magnetic sensitivity in quantitatively predicted ways.

No single experiment will probably do all of this. But the chain should eventually close.

Until then, the right sentence is not we have found the magnetic sense. It is we have found pieces of systems capable of explaining parts of magnetic behavior.
