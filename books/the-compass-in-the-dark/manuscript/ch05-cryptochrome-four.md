# Cryptochrome Four

In 2021, a large collaboration reported one of the most concrete pieces of the cryptochrome story. Cryptochrome 4 from the European robin showed magnetic sensitivity in vitro. Its photochemistry responded to magnetic fields, and the robin protein was more magnetically sensitive than homologues from chicken and pigeon in the tested system.

The paper mattered because the argument had finally moved from a protein family to a particular molecule from a migratory species. Site-specific mutations altered the radical chemistry. A chain of tryptophan residues helped generate successive radical pairs. The biophysics looked like something evolution could tune.

It was also easy to overread.

An isolated protein responding to magnetic fields is not a bird navigating. The experiment did not show that CRY4 is necessary for magnetic orientation in vivo, that the relevant radical pair is the one assumed in every behavioral model, or that the protein's effect size in a cell is sufficient for a nervous system to extract directional information under natural noise.

This gap between molecular plausibility and organismal causality is the book's central tension. Popular science often wants one discovery to close the chain. Biology rarely obliges.

Later theoretical work has made the molecular picture more interesting rather than simpler. A 2024 Nature Communications paper examined a tightly bound FAD-superoxide radical pair in cryptochrome and showed a route by which Earth-strength magnetic fields might matter under strongly asymmetric recombination, invoking the quantum Zeno effect. This does not establish that birds use that specific pair. It expands the set of chemically plausible ways weak fields might affect cryptochrome.

The phrase quantum Zeno effect sounds like a gift to anyone trying to make the subject mystical. It should have the opposite effect. The more specific the mechanism becomes, the more brutally testable it should become. Which radicals? Which lifetimes? Which reaction rates? Which field strengths? Which orientations? Which mutations remove the effect?

A good quantum-biological claim should become less magical as the equations improve.
