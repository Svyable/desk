# A Compass Made of Chemistry

The radical-pair proposal begins with chemistry, not navigation. Light excites a molecule. Electron transfer creates two radicals whose unpaired electron spins begin in a correlated quantum state. The interconversion of spin states can alter which chemical products form. A magnetic field can perturb that spin evolution.

None of those sentences requires a bird.

The remarkable move was to ask whether biology could arrange such chemistry so that Earth's field, roughly tens of microtesla, changed reaction yields enough to become a sensory signal. The energy scale looks insulting. Thermal motion at physiological temperature is enormous by comparison. Classical intuition says the field should disappear into biochemical noise.

But the relevant comparison is not a bar magnet lifting a nail. Spin chemistry can be sensitive to field-dependent reaction pathways without the magnetic energy dominating the thermal energy of the molecule as a whole. That distinction is where many popular treatments go wrong. They frame the proposal as an animal detecting an impossibly weak force rather than a chemical reaction whose branching ratios may depend on spin dynamics.

Cryptochromes became central because they are flavoproteins capable of light-driven electron-transfer chemistry and are present in animal tissues including the retina. Their known roles in circadian biology initially made them plausible candidates but not proof. A protein being in the right place with the right cofactors is the beginning of an investigation.

The theory also demands geometry. A compass needs directional information. If magnetically sensitive molecules are ordered within a tissue, orientation-dependent chemical yields could in principle create a pattern across the visual field. The bird would not need to feel north as a tug. It could experience, or neurally compute, a modulation attached to viewing direction.

That visual-overlay idea is attractive enough to be dangerous. It is easy to illustrate and therefore easy to mistake for established phenomenology. We do not know what a bird's magnetic percept is like. The proposed reaction may feed a nonvisual pathway even if it occurs in the retina. Mechanism and subjective experience are separate problems.

Still, the radical-pair model did something rare for a speculative biological hypothesis: it generated perturbations. Change light conditions. Apply weak radiofrequency fields. Alter the protein. Compare species. The story could be attacked.
