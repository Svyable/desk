# Radio Noise as a Scalpel

A conventional lesion is easy to understand. Damage a structure, lose a function. Radiofrequency perturbation is subtler because the intervention can target reaction dynamics rather than anatomy.

For radical-pair magnetoreception, that is potentially powerful. Oscillating magnetic fields at particular frequencies can alter spin transitions. If bird orientation fails under fields predicted to interact with radical pairs, the behavior becomes a kind of spectroscopy performed on an intact animal.

The phrase kind of matters. Behavioral radiofrequency experiments are not electron paramagnetic resonance spectra. The organism is a complex filter. Coils produce electric as well as magnetic environments. Field homogeneity, harmonics, shielding, grounding and ambient noise all matter. A bird can become disoriented for reasons unrelated to spin chemistry.

Still, the program has yielded unusually specific tests. Broadband electromagnetic noise disrupted European robin magnetic orientation in shielded-versus-unshielded conditions. Later frequency-range experiments pushed disruption into tens of megahertz. The results constrain candidate mechanisms because some physical sensors should be far less vulnerable to such weak oscillatory fields.

A proposed 2024 test goes further: expose birds to monochromatic radiofrequency fields in the absence of Earth's static field and ask whether the oscillating field itself can provide orientation information. If successful under the predicted conditions, such a result would be difficult to explain with ordinary magnetite torque.

This is the experimental culture the field needs. Do not merely ask whether a mechanism can fit known behavior. Design a condition under which rival mechanisms make different predictions.

A scalpel is useful because it cuts between explanations.
