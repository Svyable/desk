# The Compass in the Dark

The robin leaves at night whether or not we know how it does it.

That fact places useful pressure on theory. The phenomenon is not waiting for our preferred mechanism. Behavioral magnetoreception survives the collapse of particular receptor candidates. The bird does not care that iron-rich cells in a famous beak model turned out to be macrophages. The turtle does not care that cryptochrome advocates and magnetite advocates occupy different literatures.

What has changed is the quality of the ignorance.

We have proteins whose photochemistry responds to magnetic fields. We have controlled radiofrequency perturbations that alter orientation. We have neural activity carrying magnetic variables. We have conditioning paradigms that separate map recognition from compass heading. We have better tools for localizing receptors without assuming where they must be.

And still, the organ is missing.

There is a tendency in frontier science to treat unresolved mechanism as a defect in the phenomenon. Sometimes that is correct; many alleged effects disappear when measurement improves. Magnetoreception has behaved differently. Better experiments have repeatedly complicated the mechanism while leaving substantial behavioral evidence intact.

That makes it a model for how to live with a real unknown. Do not inflate it. Do not domesticate it too quickly. Keep the contradictions visible.

If cryptochrome radical pairs eventually turn out to be the avian compass, the result will be one of the cleanest examples of quantum mechanics entering an animal's ordinary sensory life. If they do not, the failure will still have forced biophysics to discover why molecules that are magnetically sensitive in vitro were not the receptor everyone hoped they were.

Either outcome is worth having.

The compass is in the dark. The darkness is smaller than it used to be.
