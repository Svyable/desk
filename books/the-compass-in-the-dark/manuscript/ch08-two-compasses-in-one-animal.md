# Two Compasses in One Animal

The temptation to find one magnetic sense may itself be a mistake.

Navigation requires at least two different kinds of information. A compass tells you direction. A map tells you where you are relative to a goal. Animals can in principle derive both from Earth's field, but the computational problems differ.

An inclination compass can use the angle of field lines relative to gravity or the horizon to distinguish poleward from equatorward directions. A magnetic map can exploit geographic gradients in intensity and inclination. The first is about heading. The second is about position.

Different receptor mechanisms may be better suited to each job. Radical-pair chemistry can naturally produce directional sensitivity tied to molecular orientation. Magnetite-based systems could, in principle, provide intensity information. Trigeminal pathways implicated in magnetic anomaly detection need not be the same pathway underlying light-dependent compass orientation.

This dual-system possibility explains why experiments can appear contradictory when they are actually perturbing different channels. Cut one nerve and map-like responses change while compass behavior survives. Change lighting and compass orientation fails while some magnetic discrimination remains.

It also raises the bar for claims of a single master receptor. A bird may carry multiple partially redundant magnetic sensors integrated with stars, sun, odor and landmarks. Navigation is almost certainly a sensor-fusion problem before the phrase became fashionable in engineering.

The more interesting biological question becomes not which sense is true, but how the nervous system arbitrates among cues when they disagree. Calibration may occur during development or at twilight. One cue may dominate under cloud. Another may correct accumulated error over long distances.

A map and a compass can share the same planet without sharing the same molecule.
