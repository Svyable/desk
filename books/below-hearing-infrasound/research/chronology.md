# Chronology

**1883** — Krakatoa eruption produces atmospheric pressure disturbances recorded around the world; waves circle the globe repeatedly in barograph records.

**1940s–1960s** — Atmospheric nuclear testing and long-range acoustic research drive major advances in explosion infrasound, upper-atmosphere propagation and array processing. This period needs a dedicated primary-history pass before detailed institutional claims are added to the manuscript.

**1963** — Partial Test Ban Treaty prohibits nuclear weapon tests in the atmosphere, outer space and underwater, leaving underground testing outside its ban.

**24 September 1996** — The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty opens for signature. Its verification protocol specifies the International Monitoring System and International Data Centre, including a 60-station infrasound network.

**2001** — CTBTO reports its infrasound station IS26 in Germany as the first IMS infrasound station certified.

**9 October 2006** — DPRK announces its first nuclear test. CTBTO later describes the IMS as detecting all six declared DPRK nuclear tests from 2006 through 2017, principally through the network’s explosion-monitoring technologies.

**2005–2010** — Dataset later used by Matoza et al. for automated global search of explosive volcanism in IMS infrasound records; the study detects and locates three of seven reported VEI 4 eruptions in the period.

**December 2011–August 2012** — Mt. Cleveland study period in Alaska. Twenty explosions are detected by regional infrasound at distances up to 1,827 km; some events missed in initial automated satellite analysis are recovered acoustically/seismoacoustically.

**15 February 2013** — Chelyabinsk meteoroid enters over Russia and produces a major airburst. CTBTO’s infrasound network records the event globally; later work uses detections and nondetections to study network performance, source directivity and station noise.

**2013** — Brown et al. and Borovička et al. publish independent Nature reconstructions of Chelyabinsk’s energy, trajectory, fragmentation and hazard implications.

**2015** — Pilger et al. publish station-by-station analysis of CTBT infrasound network performance for Chelyabinsk, giving the book its clearest empirical case that a famous event can be real, enormous and still absent at some plausible stations.

**2017** — Matoza et al. publish automated global explosive-volcanism detection using IMS infrasound data.

**2017–2019** — High-broadband volcano studies at Popocatépetl and continued advances in local/regional seismoacoustics deepen source-physics work that complements global monitoring.

**4 August 2020** — Port of Beirut explosion produces global/regional geophysical observations. CTBTO later lists Beirut among major nonnuclear events observed by the IMS.

**15 January 2022** — Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai eruption excites a broad spectrum of global atmospheric, oceanic, seismic and ionospheric disturbances. CTBTO reports detection at all 53 then-operating IMS infrasound stations; subsequent studies track repeated global propagation.

**2022–2024** — Hunga analyses using ground, satellite, GNSS, seismic and balloon observations turn the eruption into a benchmark for atmospheric-wave physics.

**2024** — U.S. Geological Survey publishes updated technical guidance on infrasound for volcano monitoring, reflecting the transition of infrasound from a specialized research method into a practical component of modern hazard monitoring.

**May 2026** — CTBTO’s current IMS map reports roughly 90 percent of the full 337-facility system operating and lists the treaty network’s intended 60 infrasound stations among its four monitoring technologies.
