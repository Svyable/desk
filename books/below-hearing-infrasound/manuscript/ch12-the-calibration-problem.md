# The Calibration Problem

Calibration is easy when the instrument can be brought to the source.

Put a known voltage into an amplifier. Hang a known mass from a scale. Shine a lamp of known brightness into a detector. Repeat. The appeal of laboratory calibration is control: the input is specified closely enough that error can be assigned mostly to the instrument.

A global infrasound network does not live in that world.

Its most consequential sources are distant, energetic and infrequent. The path between source and station can be thousands of kilometres long. The atmosphere changes while the wave travels. Source geometry may be uncertain. Even when the location and origin time are known, the energy coupled into the relevant acoustic band may not be.

The calibration problem is therefore distributed among source, path and receiver.

This helps explain why so many of the events in this book matter despite being poor replicas of nuclear explosions. Chelyabinsk offers a moving line source with excellent external trajectory information. Volcanoes offer repeated sources at known locations but complex vent physics. Hunga offers a planetary-scale impulse with enormous dynamic range and multiple atmospheric modes. Chemical explosions offer impulsive nonnuclear sources, sometimes with strong contextual constraints. Rockets offer known moving sources. Microbaroms offer persistent distributed sources. None gives the entire answer. Together they constrain different parts of the forward model.

The temptation is to call all of them “calibration events” and move on. That would hide the hard part: calibration is only useful if the parameter being calibrated is identified.

A meteor can calibrate detection probability for a particular frequency band, source altitude and trajectory geometry. It cannot by itself calibrate yield estimation for a buried nuclear explosion. A surface chemical blast may test location algorithms and some amplitude relationships, but its coupling to the ground and atmosphere differs from an airburst. A volcano can test long-range path models but may radiate strongly directionally through a vent. The correct lesson from natural calibration is modularity.

Ask which uncertainty the event constrains.

This is a more general rule for frontier science. Researchers often reach for analogs when direct experiments are impossible. Mars chambers stand in for Mars. impact craters stand in for ancient collisions. animal models stand in for human disease. simulation stands in for rare catastrophes. The analog becomes dangerous when resemblance in one dimension is allowed to imply equivalence in all dimensions.

Infrasound makes the mistake visible because source classes differ so obviously.

There is a second calibration problem: the network changes over time. Stations are certified, upgraded, repaired and occasionally affected by environmental change. Processing algorithms evolve. The number of operating arrays has increased since the early IMS years. Atmospheric models improve. Comparing a 2005 event with a 2026 event therefore requires attention to the observing system as it existed at each time.

Long duration is an asset only if changes are documented.

The treaty architecture helps here because configuration control, authentication and operational manuals are not optional niceties. A verification regime needs traceability. Scientific users benefit from the same discipline. If a station’s sensor response, array geometry or noise-reduction system changes, that is metadata, not clerical trivia.

Calibration also contains an institutional problem. The network’s primary purpose is treaty verification, while many scientifically useful events have nothing to do with compliance. Who decides which events deserve intensive reprocessing? Which data and products are accessible to whom? How quickly can researchers use them? How are methods transferred from academic work into operational processing?

The answers have changed over time and vary by data class, but the conceptual point is stable: a sensor network is calibrated socially as well as physically. People decide which errors matter.

For a volcanologist, failure to detect a moderate eruption in the Aleutians may be operationally important. For a treaty analyst, the same miss matters only insofar as it reveals detection limits relevant to explosion monitoring. For an atmospheric scientist, the event may be valuable because the source time is known and the path crosses an underobserved region of the stratosphere. The waveform is identical. The calibration question changes with the mission.

This plurality can improve the instrument because different communities notice different failure modes.

The ideal arrangement is not that treaty analysts become volcanologists and asteroid dynamicists. It is that the network remains legible enough for expertise to attach. A bolide specialist can constrain source trajectory; an atmospheric acoustician can model propagation; a station engineer can characterize noise; an analyst can test association; a treaty authority can interpret the result in the legal framework.

No single discipline has to own the whole event.

The calibration problem becomes especially difficult near detection thresholds. Large events are forgiving. Hunga was not going to disappear because one atmospheric model was slightly wrong. Small or poorly coupled events can. At threshold, details of station noise, frequency-dependent attenuation and atmospheric ducting determine whether the event is observed at all. The most politically interesting cases may therefore be the least satisfying scientifically.

This is why performance cannot be summarized by a single minimum yield.

A statement such as “the network detects explosions above X kilotons” silently assumes source type, location, altitude or depth, atmospheric state, station availability and acceptable probability of detection. Real performance is a surface over those variables. Operational organizations simplify that surface for communication, but scientific honesty requires remembering it exists.

Nondetections are part of calibration because they define the valleys in that surface. A source with known energy that fails to appear at an expected station can reveal a modeling error, a noise problem or a source-radiation assumption. If only detections are retained, the network learns optimism.

False detections matter too. An association algorithm that repeatedly invents events from unrelated arrivals may look sensitive until the catalog is compared with known sources. Clutter rates, recurring microbaroms and station-specific artifacts need to be measured. A verification system is calibrated not just against what it should see but against what it should refuse to call an event.

This is where the word “calibration” finally becomes larger than instrument response.

The network is calibrated when its claims line up with the world: events are found when they should be, absent when physics predicts absence, located within stated uncertainty, and classified with error rates that survive alternative explanations.

That standard is harder than making a sensor accurate.

It is also why the planet’s unsolicited explosions are so valuable. Nobody built Chelyabinsk for the CTBT. Nobody erupted Hunga on command. The ocean did not agree to emit microbaroms for atmospheric science. These sources arrive with all the inconvenience of nature—and therefore test parts of the system a laboratory would be tempted to hold fixed.

A network designed for distrust is calibrated by events that do not care whether anyone believes them.
