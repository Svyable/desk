# The Missing Detection

The cleanest-looking evidence in monitoring is sometimes the thing that is not there.

No signal. No plume. No track. No response. Absence has rhetorical force because it feels less interpretive than a waveform. Either the instrument saw something or it did not.

Infrasound destroys that simplicity.

A station can fail to detect a real energetic event for ordinary reasons. The source may radiate weakly toward that station. The atmosphere may not return enough energy to the surface along the path. The relevant frequencies may be attenuated. Local noise may raise the threshold. An array element may be unavailable. The event may produce a waveform that falls outside a detector’s processing assumptions. A moving source may direct more acoustic energy elsewhere.

Chelyabinsk supplies the concrete warning. The 2013 meteor was not in doubt. Yet some operating IMS infrasound stations detected it strongly and others did not. Pilger and colleagues used the pattern to study network performance rather than treating the absences as contradictions. Their analysis found a strong role for source directivity and station noise, among other factors.

A missing detection becomes evidence only after a detection model says the event should have been seen.

This principle is foundational in experimental science but often disappears in public arguments. “No evidence” is treated either as decisive disproof or as meaningless. Both positions avoid the harder question: what was the probability of observing the evidence if the hypothesis were true?

If the probability was near one, absence is powerful. If it was near zero, absence tells us little. If it is poorly known, the missing detection mostly exposes our ignorance of sensitivity.

The same logic governs treaty verification. A state accused of a covert explosion might point to the lack of infrasound detections. Analysts would need to ask whether the event type, location, yield and atmospheric conditions implied strong expected signals at available stations. The answer could strengthen or weaken the accusation. It cannot be read directly from silence.

This makes network performance modeling politically consequential.

A monitoring regime needs not only a catalog of what it has detected but a map of what it could have detected under specified conditions. That map is necessarily probabilistic. Station uptime matters. Noise matters. source depth and coupling matter. Atmospheric state matters. Network geometry matters. The result is not one global threshold but a changing sensitivity surface.

Communicating that surface is difficult. Policy prefers crisp statements. The public wants to know whether a test above some yield can be hidden. Scientists answer with caveats about location, environment and probability. Both needs are legitimate. The danger arrives when a probabilistic performance claim is simplified into an absolute one and later used outside its assumptions.

An honest verification system should be able to say two apparently contradictory things at once: the network is highly capable, and particular events may still be missed by particular technologies.

That is not weakness. Redundant sensing exists because no channel is perfect.

The concept of negative evidence becomes even more important when we leave nuclear testing and enter anomalous claims. Consider an alleged high-energy aerospace event. If no infrasound station detected it, does that argue against the claim? Only if the alleged event should have generated infrasound in the network’s band and should have had favorable paths to operating stations. If the claim involves silent propulsion with no strong shocks, infrasound may be irrelevant. If it involves a kilometer-scale fireball, silence could matter more. The physical hypothesis determines the expected observable.

Too many anomaly debates reverse this order. They start with available sensors and infer what the event “must” have been. A better approach starts with candidate mechanisms and predicts the sensor consequences before looking for confirmation.

This is why falsifiability is more laborious than slogans suggest. To falsify a claim with absence, one must specify what observation was expected, where, when, and with what probability.

Infrasound provides a beautiful example because the probability changes with weather aloft.

Suppose two identical explosions occur at the same location six months apart. Seasonal stratospheric winds can alter which directions support long-range ducting. A station that detects one may miss the other despite unchanged source energy. If analysts ignore seasonal atmosphere, the second nondetection may be misread as evidence of a smaller event.

Conversely, if a propagation model predicts a strong path and the station is quiet under low-noise conditions, the absence can become informative. It might challenge the assumed source energy or location. It might reveal an error in the atmospheric model. It might indicate an instrumentation problem. Negative evidence is often diagnostic before it is dispositive.

This matters to the scientific use of the network too. Volcano monitoring can suffer acoustic shadows. A missing array detection does not prove an eruption did not occur when satellite or seismic data show otherwise. Instead the mismatch can test atmospheric models. Bolide research can use nondetections to constrain radiation directivity. Rocket observations can expose path errors. The blank trace becomes a measurement of the system.

There is a historical irony here. Arms-control debates have often worried about evasion: could a state conduct a test in a way that escaped detection? The technical answer involves not only making the source smaller or better contained but understanding the sensors’ blind spots. Monitoring scientists therefore have a professional incentive to study exactly how real events vanish.

That incentive can make the network more scientifically mature than systems optimized for impressive detections alone.

A telescope survey that publishes only discoveries overstates its understanding of selection effects. A medical study that loses negative outcomes distorts treatment estimates. A remote-viewing archive built around celebrated hits cannot establish performance. A UAP database containing only unresolved cases cannot estimate how often ordinary explanations succeed. The structure is the same.

The missing detection belongs in the evidence ledger.

There is one more complication. Sometimes absence results from data access rather than physics. A station may have recorded an event, but the investigator does not have the relevant data. A national sensor may exist outside the public network. A classified collection system may have observed something without public release. In those cases “no public detection” is not “no detection.” The distinction is essential in intelligence-adjacent topics.

The CTBT system partly avoids this confusion by defining authenticated network facilities and data flows. Analysts can at least specify which part of the observing system they are discussing. That institutional clarity should be copied wherever possible.

Silence is seductive because it looks clean. In a changing atmosphere, it is not clean at all.

The empty trace has a source too: geometry, noise, propagation, threshold, failure, or genuine absence. Before using it as evidence, one has to determine which kind of silence one is looking at.
