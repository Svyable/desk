# Volcanoes Behind Weather

A satellite can fail to see a volcano because the weather is bad. An infrasound array can sometimes hear the explosion anyway.

That sentence contains almost the entire case for combining remote-sensing technologies, but the details are better.

Cleveland Volcano rises from Chuginadak Island in Alaska’s Aleutian chain. It is remote, active, and awkward to monitor densely from the ground. Between December 2011 and August 2012, researchers studying Cleveland reported twenty explosions detected by regional infrasound sensors at distances as great as 1,827 kilometres. Some explosions associated with disruption of small lava domes were confirmed by satellite remote sensing. Others had eluded initial automated satellite analysis, in part because cloud and weather complicate optical and infrared observation.

The atmosphere that makes satellite viewing difficult does not necessarily prevent low-frequency acoustic propagation. Different sensors fail for different reasons.

For hazard monitoring this is practical. An explosive eruption can loft ash into aviation routes. A remote volcano may lack a dense local seismic network. Regional infrasound can provide rapid confirmation that an explosion occurred, constrain direction, and sometimes support estimates of source behavior. Modern volcano observatories increasingly use local or regional infrasound alongside seismic, satellite and other measurements.

For the treaty network, volcanoes are unavoidable alternatives.

An explosive eruption can be enormous. Even smaller eruptions can generate coherent long-range signals. If the purpose of an infrasound network is to identify atmospheric explosions, a volcano is exactly the sort of source that must be modeled rather than dismissed. Known volcanic vents simplify location. Repeated eruptions create opportunities to compare observations over changing atmospheric conditions. The same event class that can become clutter for one mission becomes a calibration source for another.

The 2017 study by Robin Matoza and colleagues pushed the idea toward global automation. They searched multiyear IMS infrasound data for sustained explosive volcanic signals, using cross-bearings on a global grid and accounting for a background clutter rate. In data from 2005 through 2010—when the network was less complete than it is now—the method automatically detected, associated and located three of seven globally reported VEI 4 eruptions in the study period.

Three of seven is not a triumphant number if presented as universal volcano detection. It is a useful number because it contains the denominator.

The missed eruptions matter. Network geometry, eruption acoustics, atmospheric paths and noise determine whether a global array system sees an event. The result shows capability without pretending completeness. It also illustrates why a treaty network can become an Earth-science instrument before it becomes perfect at the secondary task.

There is a temptation in discussions of global sensors to equate “detected from far away” with “understood.” Volcanoes resist that shortcut. An eruption’s infrasound depends on vent geometry, gas release, jetting, fragmentation, plume dynamics and directionality. Recent experiments at Stromboli have used infrasound sensors on uncrewed aircraft to measure aspects of vertical radiation that ground sensors cannot sample well. That work underscores a limitation of distant arrays: the source does not radiate equally in every direction, and a ground network sees only certain angles.

Again a natural event reveals the detector’s assumptions.

The United States Geological Survey’s 2024 guidance on infrasound for volcano monitoring is careful about these advantages and limitations. Infrasound can travel long distances and is unaffected by clouds or precipitation in the way optical instruments are, but wind and ocean noise matter, and atmospheric variability changes propagation. Local arrays can detect and characterize eruptive activity; regional arrays can cover volcanoes where local instrumentation is sparse. The strongest observatories combine acoustic and seismic information.

The practical lesson is redundancy with different failure modes.

This sounds like an engineering cliché until one sees what happens when two sensors disagree. A satellite reports nothing because a cloud deck blocks the view. An infrasound array reports a coherent arrival from the volcano. That disagreement is informative. Or the satellite shows a plume while the array is quiet. Perhaps the event was not strongly explosive. Perhaps propagation was unfavorable. Perhaps the acoustic system’s threshold was too high. Each mismatch forces a physical explanation.

A monitoring architecture becomes robust not when its sensors always agree, but when disagreements can be investigated.

Volcanoes also complicate the politics of verification in a useful way. A remote infrasonic detection near a nuclear-capable state can create concern if interpreted without geological context. Catalogued volcanic sources, eruption bulletins and satellite data can prevent ordinary geophysics from being inflated into political suspicion. The inverse is also true: analysts should not label an unexplained signal volcanic merely because a volcano exists in the region. Attribution has to earn its specificity.

This is one reason public scientific collaboration around monitoring data matters. Volcanologists bring source knowledge that treaty analysts may not possess. Atmospheric acousticians bring propagation expertise. Meteorologists improve path models. Seismologists supply complementary event information. A verification problem that looks narrow from the outside turns out to depend on a community that understands the planet’s legitimate explosions.

The phrase “peaceful applications” understates this dependence. Volcano science is not simply allowed to borrow treaty sensors after the national-security work is done. Better knowledge of volcanoes improves the negative class against which suspicious atmospheric events are judged.

And the treaty network gives volcanology something in return: authenticated long-duration sensors in places selected for global coverage rather than volcano proximity alone. Those distant stations can catch unusually energetic eruptions, test long-range propagation and extend catalogs beyond the range of local observatories.

The relationship is asymmetric and imperfect, but real.

Cleveland is a good place to end because nothing about it requires a planetary philosophical flourish. A volcano exploded on a remote island. Clouds made some observations difficult. Pressure waves traveled through the atmosphere. Sensors far away recorded them. People compared channels and recovered events that would otherwise have been easier to miss.

That is enough.

The extraordinary scientific afterlife of the treaty network is built from ordinary moments like this: one instrument sees what another cannot.
