# Sound Below Hearing

Human hearing supplies the wrong intuition for infrasound because hearing is organized around a biological band rather than a physical boundary. Below roughly twenty hertz, pressure waves do not stop being sound. They stop being ordinarily audible to us. The atmosphere does not care about that threshold.

At low frequencies, wavelengths become long. A one-hertz sound in air has a wavelength on the order of hundreds of metres. Drop the frequency by another factor of ten and the scale becomes kilometres. Obstacles that would scatter or block higher-frequency sound can become small compared with the wavelength. Atmospheric absorption is lower than it is for many audible frequencies. Under the right conditions, energy can travel across continents.

This is the useful fact behind global infrasound monitoring. It is also the beginning of the complications.

A distant infrasound station does not measure the explosion directly. It measures a pressure history after the source has radiated energy into a moving, stratified atmosphere. Between source and station, the wave can refract through regions where temperature and wind alter the effective speed of sound. It can travel through tropospheric, stratospheric or thermospheric paths. Different paths can arrive at different times. Some frequencies survive better than others. The amplitude at the sensor reflects the source, but it also reflects the route.

This is one reason yield estimation from infrasound is never as simple as reading a number from a dial. A larger explosion generally produces a larger disturbance, but the relationship between remote amplitude and source energy depends on propagation, source height, geometry, frequency content and atmospheric state. A meteor makes the problem harder because it is not a stationary point explosion. It deposits energy along a trajectory. A volcano can radiate directionally through a vent. A rocket moves. Even a nominally simple chemical explosion interacts with the ground, nearby structures and the atmosphere.

The instrument at the far end is a microbarometer, designed to measure very small changes in air pressure. But a single pressure sensor is not enough for the most interesting work. Wind pushes on sensors and creates local pressure fluctuations. Weather produces low-frequency variations. A truck, surf, machinery or turbulence can contaminate a site. The International Monitoring System therefore uses arrays: several sensor elements separated across a site.

An array can ask a question that a lone sensor cannot. Did a similar waveform reach multiple elements with the small time delays expected for a coherent plane wave crossing the array? If so, from what direction did it appear to arrive? What was its apparent horizontal speed? Those quantities help separate a propagating infrasonic wave from local noise.

The array does not solve source identity. It improves the geometry of the claim.

This distinction between detection and identity is where many popular accounts go wrong. If an array shows a coherent signal arriving from the direction of a known volcano at the right time, source attribution may be strong. If several arrays around the world produce intersecting bearings and timing consistent with a known meteor, stronger still. If an unusual arrival appears at one station with no corroboration and no independent source information, the honest description is narrower: an unusual coherent arrival occurred.

That narrowness is not timidity. It is the difference between measurement and story.

The first instrumentally recorded naturally occurring infrasound is often traced to the 1883 Krakatoa eruption. Barographs around the world registered enormous atmospheric pressure waves that circled the planet repeatedly. The observation predates the modern treaty network by more than a century and is useful for a simple reason: global atmospheric acoustics did not begin as a verification technology. The atmosphere had always been capable of carrying extraordinary low-frequency disturbances. What changed later was the ability to observe them continuously with a deliberately distributed array network.

The scale of a modern station makes this continuity less dramatic to look at than it is conceptually. There may be no giant dish, no long optical tube, no mountain-sized detector. Several microbarometers, spatially arranged and connected to processing and communications, can participate in observations of events on the far side of a continent. Their power comes from correlation.

That word matters. Infrasound monitoring is a science of relationships among traces: one element with another, one station with another, one technology with another, an observed arrival with a modeled atmosphere, a candidate source with external evidence. The pressure waveform by itself is rarely the entire argument.

The physics also explains why low frequency does not mean low energy. Frequency describes repetition rate, not source magnitude. The Chelyabinsk meteor released energy commonly estimated around half a megaton of TNT equivalent as it fragmented through the atmosphere, and its low-frequency pressure waves were detected thousands of kilometres away. Hunga generated an even more complicated spectrum of atmospheric disturbances, from infrasound to much longer-period Lamb and gravity waves. What makes these events legible is not that they fit one clean tone below hearing. They push energy into a range of atmospheric modes.

For verification, however, there is another useful feature. Nuclear explosions in the atmosphere and shallow underground explosions can couple energy into the air. The treaty network’s designers can therefore use infrasound both as a primary technology for atmospheric events and as complementary evidence in some other cases. That complementarity is important because different physical channels fail differently. A seismic signal moves through rock. An infrasonic signal moves through air. A radionuclide plume moves with atmospheric transport and depends on release. Agreement among channels is more informative than forcing one technology to do everything.

The hidden elegance is that no one had to make infrasound mysterious for it to become strategically useful. The waves obey ordinary physics. The difficulty lies in the ordinary physics being complicated enough.

The atmosphere is layered, dynamic, rotating with the planet, heated by the Sun, stirred by weather, bounded below by rough terrain and ocean and above by regions where composition and temperature change dramatically. A signal can propagate through it for hours. During that time our instinct is to imagine a message traveling through a medium. A better instinct is to imagine the medium continually rewriting the message’s route.

This is why the central object in the next chapters is not the microbarometer. It is the path between source and microbarometer. The station records the end of an experiment that began somewhere else. To understand what it heard, we have to reconstruct the atmosphere in between.
