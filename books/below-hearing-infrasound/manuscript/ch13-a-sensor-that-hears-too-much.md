# A Sensor That Hears Too Much

Sensitivity has an obvious sales pitch. A better sensor sees weaker things, farther away, more often. In most technical fields, that sounds like progress without qualification.

Verification systems make the qualification impossible to avoid.

A sensor that hears more of the world also hears more things that are not the target. At some point the engineering problem shifts from detection to triage. If the target is rare and the background is abundant, a tenfold gain in sensitivity can create far more work than confidence.

The infrasound network lives inside this asymmetry. Nuclear explosions are rare. Atmospheric disturbances are not. Microbaroms recur. Volcanoes erupt. Mining blasts happen. Rockets launch. Meteors enter. Aircraft create shocks. Industrial accidents occur. Storm systems and atmospheric processes generate low-frequency pressure fluctuations. The array hardware can reject some local incoherent noise, but a distant coherent nonnuclear source can look beautifully signal-like because it is a signal.

The problem is not false noise. It is true evidence for the wrong hypothesis.

This distinction matters because many automated detection systems are evaluated first on whether they find patterns humans recognize. An algorithm that detects every strong coherent arrival may have excellent recall and terrible operational value. The next step—association, location, source characterization and screening—is not administrative cleanup. It is where the monitoring claim becomes specific enough to matter.

The CTBT Protocol anticipates this by defining an International Data Centre and standard event-screening parameters. States receive data and products, and they retain responsibility for compliance judgments. The architecture separates technical processing from political conclusion.

That separation should not be read as bureaucratic caution. It is a response to base rates.

Imagine a global system that generates thousands of candidate acoustic arrivals over time. Only a tiny fraction could plausibly represent treaty violations. If analysts begin with the assumption that every unexplained arrival is suspicious, the system becomes an engine for manufacturing mystery. If they begin with the assumption that every arrival is ordinary, it becomes an engine for missing novelty. The discipline is to update both ways.

This is why catalogues of ordinary sources are strategically important. Mines produce repeated blasts from known regions. Volcanic vents occupy fixed coordinates. Launch sites and test ranges produce recurring engineered activity. Ocean microbaroms have climatological structure. Aircraft corridors and industrial areas have patterns. Cataloging these sources does not make them irrelevant. It lets the system treat recurrence as evidence.

There is a danger here too. A catalog can become a garbage bin for events that resemble a known class without being demonstrated members of it. “Probably mining” is not the same as matched to a mine blast schedule. “Probably volcanic” is not the same as associated with an eruption bulletin. Screening needs to preserve uncertainty rather than laundering it through labels.

The most useful conceptual tool is not a taxonomy but a claim ladder.

At the bottom: a coherent array signal was detected.

Next: several arrays observed arrivals that can be associated with one source region and origin time.

Next: propagation modeling supports the association.

Next: waveform and contextual features are consistent with an explosion-like source.

Next: seismic, hydroacoustic, radionuclide or external observations constrain the source class.

At the top: a treaty-relevant interpretation survives credible alternatives.

Each rung requires additional evidence. Skipping rungs is how sensors acquire supernatural reputations.

This ladder also helps with scientific discovery. Suppose a truly unusual atmospheric source appears. A system trained to preserve intermediate claims can keep the anomaly alive without assigning it a premature identity. Analysts can say: coherent, multi-station, located, not matched to catalogued sources, poorly fit by current propagation or source models. That is a scientifically meaningful anomaly. It can be revisited.

By contrast, a system that forces every event into an existing class will erase novelty. A system that celebrates every unclassified event as extraordinary will drown in novelty. The ability to remain undecided is a technical capability.

This may be the strongest lesson a verification network offers to UAP research, biosignature detection, SETI and other domains where a rare target sits inside an enormous background. The question is not whether unexplained cases survive. They will. The question is whether the pipeline preserves enough information to distinguish model failure, missing context, sensor artifact and genuinely novel source behavior.

Infrasound analysts have a practical advantage: the physics of the medium is well established. When a signal is odd, the cause is not usually that pressure waves have abandoned acoustics. More often the source, path, noise or geometry is incompletely known. This encourages mechanistic humility.

Frontier fields often lack that advantage. In consciousness research, the causal chain from stimulus to report is contested. In anomalous aerospace, raw sensor access may be limited. In astrobiology, the target life may not share Earth biochemistry. In those settings, classifying anomalies is harder because the forward model is less complete.

The lesson should therefore be transferred carefully. We cannot simply import infrasound screening criteria into another field. We can import the architecture of doubt: multiple sensors, known-source catalogs, explicit propagation or observation models, preserved nondetections, denominator accounting and claim levels that do not collapse into one binary answer.

There is another reason the sensor that hears too much can be useful. False positives reveal the world.

A signal rejected as a nuclear explosion may become a meteor paper. A volcanic arrival screened out of treaty relevance may improve eruption monitoring. A persistent background may become an atmospheric probe. The rejected cases are not necessarily failures. They are failures only relative to one mission.

This creates an unusual economics of information. The network pays the cost of global sensitivity for treaty verification. Science harvests some of the events verification must reject. Science then improves source and propagation models that can reduce verification uncertainty. The false-positive burden is partially converted into research value.

Not all monitoring systems can do this. Spam filters gain little from studying every rejected advertisement as a natural phenomenon. Infrasound can because the background is geophysically meaningful.

The planet is not clutter in any absolute sense.

The treaty wants one thing from the pressure field. Volcanology wants another. planetary defense another. atmospheric science another. An array can serve all of them because the waveform does not come preassigned to a discipline.

A sensor that hears too much becomes dangerous if its operators believe every signal belongs to them. It becomes powerful when they learn how to give the signals away.
