# The Atmosphere Is an Instrument

There is a useful mistake engineers make when they draw a source, a channel and a receiver as three separate boxes. The diagram clarifies responsibility. It also tempts us to believe the boxes are separable in nature.

For infrasound, the atmosphere is not a neutral channel. It is part of the measuring apparatus.

Sound speed in still air depends strongly on temperature. The atmosphere, however, is not still. Wind adds or subtracts from the effective propagation speed depending on direction. Both temperature and wind vary with altitude. The resulting vertical profile determines whether acoustic energy bends upward away from the surface, refracts back downward, or becomes trapped in paths that can carry it over immense distances.

These paths are often described as ducts. The word suggests a pipe. No pipe exists. A duct is a region of the atmosphere where the effective sound-speed structure supports repeated refraction that returns acoustic energy toward lower altitudes rather than letting it disperse irretrievably upward. Tropospheric and stratospheric ducts can support different ranges and frequency content. Thermospheric returns can arrive later and differently filtered.

A station that receives multiple arrivals from one event may therefore be recording several atmospheric histories.

This is why long-range infrasound has a peculiar temporal richness. The source time may be sharp: an explosion, a meteor fragmentation, a volcanic blast. At a distant array, energy can arrive in packets separated by path. Apparent velocities differ. Frequencies differ. The wave train becomes a compressed description not only of the source but of the atmosphere it crossed.

Verification analysts need the atmosphere because they want the source. Atmospheric scientists can reverse the interest: if the source is independently known, the infrasound becomes a probe of the atmosphere.

That reciprocal use is one of the book’s central collisions.

A well-constrained explosion or meteor can be treated as an acoustic source of opportunity. If its location and origin time are known from other measurements, arrival times and bearings at distant stations test propagation models. If the energy is approximately constrained, amplitudes provide additional information, though with more assumptions. Repeat across seasons, latitudes and station paths and a verification network begins to accumulate a climatology of what the atmosphere lets through.

The challenge is that the atmosphere is not merely seasonal. It varies daily and sometimes faster. Stratospheric winds change. Waves in the middle atmosphere perturb propagation. Sudden stratospheric warming events can reorganize winds over large regions. Weather changes near-surface noise. Solar and upper-atmospheric conditions matter for the highest propagation paths. The transfer function is alive.

This makes nondetection intellectually dangerous. Imagine two stations at similar distance from a large event. One records a strong coherent signal. The other sees nothing convincing. If we assume spherical spreading in a static atmosphere, the second station becomes an anomaly. If the first path had favorable downwind stratospheric ducting and the second lay in an acoustic shadow, the pattern may be exactly what ordinary physics predicts.

A serious monitoring system therefore has to model the conditions under which absence should count.

This issue reaches beyond infrasound. Every remote-sensing technology has a detection model, whether acknowledged or not. Telescopes have limiting magnitudes and weather. Radars have clutter and propagation. Seismometers have regional geology and noise. Intelligence collectors have coverage gaps and adversary denial. What makes infrasound unusually pedagogical is that the atmosphere visibly breaks the naïve equation between distance and detectability.

The Hunga eruption turned this into planetary theater. On 15 January 2022, the atmosphere was struck hard enough that multiple wave types propagated around the globe. Researchers measured surface-guided Lamb waves, gravity waves and infrasound across enormous distances. The event became a natural experiment in the atmosphere’s impulse response. It was not merely loud. It illuminated modes.

That distinction matters because the word “sound” can flatten the event. A pressure sensor may record a Lamb wave with a period much longer than ordinary acoustic intuition. An infrasound array can record higher-frequency portions. Satellites can see gravity-wave structure in the atmosphere. GNSS receivers can detect ionospheric disturbances. Seismometers can register atmosphere-to-ground coupling. One physical event excites a hierarchy of Earth-system responses.

For treaty verification, this multiplicity is both useful and humbling. Useful because a large event gives analysts many ways to test models. Humbling because it demonstrates how incomplete any single channel is. The atmosphere can carry information around the globe and still refuse to reduce itself to one kind of wave.

There is also an asymmetry between modeling and observation. Global atmospheric models provide temperature and wind fields, but the middle and upper atmosphere are less densely observed than the lower atmosphere. Small errors in winds aloft can move predicted acoustic paths substantially over thousands of kilometres. Infrasound can therefore reveal mismatches in atmospheric models that were invisible from conventional meteorological observations.

Again the instrument reverses itself. The analysts thought they needed a model of the atmosphere to interpret the signal. Then the signal tells them something about the atmosphere the model failed to capture.

This recursive relationship is more important than the common phrase “dual use.” Dual use sounds like one instrument serving two independent purposes. In long-range infrasound the purposes are coupled. Better atmospheric science improves explosion monitoring. Well-characterized explosions, meteors and volcanoes improve atmospheric science. The treaty mission and the scientific mission feed the same uncertainty budget from opposite sides.

The atmosphere is an instrument because every signal is multiplied by it before we see the result. We do not get the source. We get source times propagation. To recover either factor, we need knowledge of the other.

That is why natural events become so valuable. A meteor with a known trajectory can reveal propagation. A volcano with a known vent can test direction. A large chemical explosion with a documented location can calibrate algorithms. These events are not equivalent to nuclear tests, and treating them as equivalent would be careless. They are constraints on different pieces of the same inverse problem.

The treaty listens to the atmosphere for evidence about explosions. The atmosphere answers with evidence about itself.
