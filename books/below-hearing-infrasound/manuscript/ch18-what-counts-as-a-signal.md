# What Counts as a Signal

A pressure wave crosses an infrasound array. The elements record it at slightly different times. Processing shows coherence. A direction emerges.

This is a signal.

It is not yet an event.

Other arrays record compatible arrivals. Timing and bearings intersect. A source region and origin time become plausible.

Now there is an event hypothesis.

Atmospheric modeling supports the paths. The waveform is consistent with an energetic atmospheric source. External observations show a meteor, a volcano, a rocket launch or an industrial explosion. Or the event coincides with seismic evidence from a suspicious underground source.

Now there is a source interpretation.

The progression sounds obvious. Most bad inference is the result of forgetting one of the steps.

The word “signal” has been stretched throughout modern culture until it means anything worth noticing: a stock-market movement, a social-media trend, a biomarker, a sensor return, a whistleblower allegation. The opposite word, “noise,” is used for everything that does not fit the current model. Infrasound gives us a more physical way to recover the distinction.

A signal is not whatever looks interesting. It is structure that survives comparison with the alternatives relevant to the claim being made.

For a microbarometer, a coherent wavefront is signal relative to local turbulent noise. For treaty verification, a volcano is noise relative to the question of a nuclear explosion. For volcanology, the same arrival is signal. For atmospheric science, the distortion accumulated along its path may be the signal. The categories move because the question moves.

This is why the International Monitoring System could become a planetary observatory without changing what its sensors were doing. The measurements were always broader than the mission.

The book began with a political problem: states want confidence that prohibited nuclear explosions will not remain invisible. The engineering solution was not a perfect bomb detector. It was a network that measures several physical consequences of energetic events and gives states common technical evidence from which to reason.

That architecture carries a lesson for every field that hunts rare anomalies.

First, separate detection from interpretation. A strange waveform is not a strange source until ordinary source classes have been tested.

Second, preserve the denominator. Sensors that did not detect an event matter when physics says they should have. Sensors that could not have detected it should not be counted as failed witnesses.

Third, model the observation process. The atmosphere is part of the infrasound detector. Selection effects are part of every detector.

Fourth, keep known alternatives rich. Meteors, volcanoes, chemical explosions, rockets and ocean noise are not annoyances to be removed from the archive. They define the space against which suspicious events are screened.

Fifth, use independent modalities. Seismic, acoustic, hydroacoustic, radionuclide, optical, satellite and other measurements fail differently. Agreement among them can support a claim more strongly than one spectacular sensor.

Sixth, allow uncertainty to remain a result. “Unclassified” is sometimes the correct end state of available evidence. It should not be promoted into a source identity merely because ambiguity is emotionally unsatisfying.

These principles are conservative only in the best sense. They conserve the possibility of genuine discovery.

A field that explains every anomaly immediately cannot discover novelty. A field that treats every anomaly as novelty cannot learn anything either. The narrow channel between them is built from calibration, background knowledge, explicit models and preserved uncertainty.

The infrasound network occupies that channel every day because its target is rare and the atmosphere is busy.

This is what drew me to the subject. The technology itself is elegant, but not magical. Microbarometers record pressure. Arrays estimate coherence and direction. Atmospheric models predict propagation. Processing associates arrivals. The extraordinary part is institutional: nations built a global instrument because they needed to disagree about nuclear testing without disagreeing about whether the planet had been measured at all.

Then the measurements escaped their assigned category.

Chelyabinsk arrived as an asteroid and became a network-performance study. Remote volcanoes became test sources and hazard observations. Hunga turned treaty stations into nodes in a planetary atmospheric experiment. Ocean microbaroms, the nuisance floor, became information about the ocean and middle atmosphere. Chemical explosions became discrimination cases. Rockets and aircraft became moving-source references.

None of these peaceful uses cancels the security purpose. They make it more intelligible.

The network teaches us that verification is not the search for a unique signature. It is the construction of a sufficiently constrained explanation.

That may sound weaker than certainty. In practice it is stronger, because it does not depend on the world behaving conveniently.

A unique signature is brittle. It fails when a different source imitates it. A constrained explanation can incorporate several channels, tolerate missing data, quantify propagation, account for noise and compare alternatives. It can survive an atmosphere that changes.

The same standard should govern more controversial domains.

If a materials-analysis claim suggests anomalous technology, chain of custody is part of the detector. If an alleged UAP event includes radar, infrared and visual observations, sensor geometry and classified context are part of the detector. If a psi experiment reports above-chance performance, randomization, cue control, scoring and the unpublished denominator are part of the detector. If an astrobiology instrument reports a biosignature, contamination and abiotic chemistry are part of the detector.

The object is never just the object. The evidence-production system comes with it.

This is why I resist the phrase “the data speak for themselves.” Data do not speak. Pressure changes do not know they came from a volcano. A seismic trace does not know it came from an explosion. A spectral line does not know whether it is biological. Evidence becomes informative through a model of how it was produced and what else could have produced it.

The model can be wrong. That is why calibration matters.

The network’s best teachers are often events outside its mission because they expose where the model breaks without carrying the same political stakes. A meteor can reveal directional assumptions. A volcano can reveal propagation failure. A giant eruption can reveal modes the normal event set barely excites. A persistent background can reveal seasonal sensitivity.

The planet is an adversarial test set that nobody curated.

There is a final political irony. The CTBT monitoring system exists because mistrust demanded verification. Yet the quality of verification depends on a scientific culture willing to publish model failures, missed detections and alternative explanations. The hardware was built for distrust; the knowledge requires habits of intellectual trustworthiness.

Those habits are portable.

State the observation before the interpretation. Keep failures. Track what could have been seen. Prefer multiple independent constraints. Make the forward model explicit. Distinguish a known unknown from an unknown cause. Do not turn classification gaps into ontology.

And when the world produces a signal nobody asked for, keep it.

You may discover later that the nuisance was the instrument telling you what it can actually know.
