# Secrecy, Openness, and Dual Use

Monitoring systems sit in an awkward political position. If they reveal too little, nobody outside the institution can evaluate their claims. If they reveal everything, they may expose operational weaknesses, sensitive methods or information states never intended for public release.

The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty’s verification regime occupies a particularly interesting middle ground because it was designed to support international confidence rather than one state’s secret intelligence. The International Monitoring System is a shared treaty architecture. Stations operate in host countries. Data flow through an international technical organization. States receive information and retain the responsibility to make compliance judgments. The network exists because unilateral assurance was not enough.

This does not make the system fully open.

There are operational manuals, authenticated data streams, national data centers, access rules and state-level analytical products. Some technical detail is public; some information is distributed within treaty structures. National intelligence agencies also maintain sensors and analyses outside the IMS. The public record is therefore a substantial but incomplete view of the total monitoring ecology.

That incompleteness is normal. It becomes dangerous when people infer too much from it.

One common inference is conspiratorial: if a global network exists and a claimed event has not been publicly discussed, authorities must be suppressing the detection. Another is dismissive: if no public network product exists, no detection occurred. Both assume that public availability maps cleanly onto physical observation.

It does not.

A more disciplined account separates four layers. First, the physical world: an event either generated signals or it did not. Second, sensor observability: operating instruments either recorded usable manifestations or they did not. Third, institutional processing: organizations may or may not have associated and analyzed those records. Fourth, public disclosure: those analyses may or may not be available to an outside investigator.

Confusing any pair creates false certainty.

This framework matters in UAP research, where claims often involve military sensors. A public absence cannot establish a sensor absence. A whistleblower’s assertion that “sensors detected something” cannot establish what the raw data showed. Classification can preserve legitimate secrets while simultaneously making external verification difficult. The epistemic consequence is not proof of the claim or proof of concealment. It is a wider uncertainty interval.

The CTBT case offers a partial antidote because its core monitoring system is internationally specified. Researchers know what kinds of treaty facilities exist and where the network is intended to operate. This does not make all data public, but it constrains speculation about the observing architecture more than an entirely classified program would.

There is a second secrecy problem: adversaries can learn from openness.

A state interested in evading detection would benefit from understanding sensor thresholds, station outages, atmospheric blind spots and screening algorithms. Verification organizations therefore have reasons not to publish every operational vulnerability in real time. Yet scientific progress benefits from precisely the studies that characterize detection thresholds and propagation failures. The same paper that improves modeling can reveal where modeling remains weak.

This is genuine dual use, not rhetorical dual use.

The answer cannot be a blanket rule. Atmospheric propagation science is widely published and cannot sensibly be classified as a whole. Station locations are treaty-defined. Many event studies are public. More specific operational performance details may be treated differently. The boundary has to be drawn claim by claim.

The important lesson is that secrecy changes the ecology of evidence even when no one is behaving badly.

If outside researchers cannot inspect the raw inputs behind a conclusion, replication becomes limited. If insiders cannot disclose contextual information, anomalous cases may remain unresolved publicly. If authorities summarize classified sensor data with qualitative language, small ambiguities can expand into decades of mythology because nobody can audit the original event.

Cold War UFO history contains many examples of this dynamic. Classified reconnaissance and weapons programs created observations that could not be explained openly at the time. Government denials sometimes protected programs rather than describing the true source. Later disclosure resolved some cases and poisoned trust around others. The lesson is not that secrecy manufactures every anomaly. It is that secrecy can preserve anomalies as cultural objects long after their technical cause is ordinary.

Treaty verification tries to solve a related trust problem by creating common instruments.

A state need not accept another state’s classified intelligence as the sole basis for an accusation if an international network produces shared technical observations. National systems still matter, but the IMS creates a common evidentiary floor.

That floor is scientifically important because it can be referenced openly enough for a transnational research community to exist around it.

There is also a civic benefit to publishing natural-event studies. A Chelyabinsk paper demonstrates network behavior without revealing a secret test case. A Hunga analysis can test global propagation openly. Volcano studies can stress association algorithms against known sources. These events allow operationally relevant science to be developed in public around non-adversarial data.

The natural world becomes a safe red team.

Again, the phrase needs qualification. A volcano does not try to evade. A meteor does not choose its trajectory based on station placement. A chemical accident does not optimize source depth. Natural and accidental events cannot replace adversarial testing. They can reveal physics and processing weaknesses that an adversary might otherwise exploit.

This distinction matters because secrecy can create a false aura of technical superiority. Classified systems are often assumed to be vastly more capable simply because outsiders cannot inspect them. Sometimes they are. Sometimes they are ordinary systems with better access, integration or placement. Sometimes secrecy protects methods whose limitations would look familiar to any engineer.

The IMS is useful as a corrective because so much of its basic architecture is visible. Sixty infrasound arrays do not become magical because they serve arms control. Their sensitivity still depends on wind noise, array geometry and atmospheric propagation. International authority does not suspend acoustics.

Openness makes those limitations easier to see—and therefore easier to improve.

The scientific record around the network is full of events that were not detected everywhere, propagation models that required refinement, algorithms that found only a fraction of a target catalog, and source inversions with wide uncertainty. This is what competent technology looks like when it is allowed to be studied rather than mythologized.

A mature security culture should want more of that honesty, not less.

There are secrets worth keeping. There are also uncertainties worth publishing. The hard institutional skill is knowing which is which.

Infrasound cannot solve that political problem. It can reveal its cost. Every time a natural event becomes scientifically valuable because the source is public and independently constrained, it shows what openness buys: better denominators, better models, better criticism, and fewer mysteries that survive merely because nobody is allowed to ask the instrument what it actually heard.
