# The Ocean in the Microphone

The most persistent infrasound source on Earth is not a bomb, a volcano or a meteor. It is the ocean talking to itself.

When ocean waves traveling in different directions interact, they can generate pressure fluctuations at twice the ocean-wave frequency. Some of that energy couples into the atmosphere as infrasound. The resulting signals are called microbaroms. They are present so often, over such large regions, that an infrasound analyst does not encounter them as curiosities. They are weather.

The name is deceptive. “Micro” makes them sound negligible. A persistent background can matter more to detection than a spectacular transient because it sets the threshold against which the transient must be found.

This is where the verification problem becomes ecological in the literal sense: the detector lives in an acoustic environment.

A station near the Southern Ocean, North Atlantic or other energetic wave climates may receive strong microbarom arrivals whose apparent directions shift with storm systems and atmospheric propagation. Seasonal changes in stratospheric winds can favor or suppress paths. The background itself therefore carries structure. It is not white noise. It has sources, geography and meteorology.

A naïve detector would suffer. Lower the threshold to catch weak explosions and false triggers multiply. Raise it to suppress the microbarom field and smaller events disappear. The obvious response is signal processing, but the deeper response is to understand the nuisance physically.

Microbaroms are a useful case because they force analysts to stop treating background as an absence of signal. The background is made of signals from sources that are irrelevant to the immediate question.

That distinction matters far beyond acoustics. In astronomy, the sky background is astrophysical. In particle physics, backgrounds come from other particles and detector processes. In epidemiology, baseline disease has causes. In intelligence, routine activity can imitate warning indicators. The word “noise” often means “causal structure we are not currently trying to infer.”

Once the cause is understood, nuisance can become data.

Ocean-generated infrasound can be used to study wave activity, atmospheric propagation and seasonal wind structure. Its persistence makes it a kind of repeating source, though not a controlled one. Analysts can watch how preferred arrivals shift as the atmosphere changes. A background that complicates explosion detection can help reveal the conditions through which an explosion would have to propagate.

This is an elegant bargain, but it should not be romanticized. The source region can be broad. Ocean-wave interactions are distributed rather than pointlike. Models of where microbaroms are generated depend on wave spectra and opposing components. The atmosphere then filters the result. An observed peak at a station is therefore the product of ocean state, source coupling, propagation and local noise.

The same inverse problem returns with different actors.

What makes this chapter important to the larger argument is not the ocean physics alone. It is the way a verification system’s unwanted background becomes a long-term calibration field. A station that has operated through many winters and summers has not merely accumulated years of suspicious-event coverage. It has accumulated years of how the atmosphere and background behave around that location.

That historical record changes what “normal” means.

A new sensor installed for a short campaign can be exquisitely sensitive and still know little about its own climatology. A treaty station that has operated continuously may know which directions become active in particular seasons, how noise changes through the day, and which recurring sources populate its detection space. That knowledge can be encoded statistically rather than as human familiarity, but the principle is the same: duration creates context.

Context is what anomaly claims usually lack.

Suppose an investigator finds a striking low-frequency arrival and asks whether it indicates an unknown aerospace event. The waveform may indeed be unusual. Without a background catalog, however, “unusual” can mean only unfamiliar to the investigator. Is the frequency band common during storms? Does the station regularly see signals from that azimuth? Could a microbarom source region produce a similar arrival? Were there aircraft, rockets, mining blasts or weather systems? Did another array see it? Was propagation from the proposed source region even favorable?

The purpose of these questions is not to explain anomalies away. It is to give an anomaly a denominator.

A mature monitoring network is partly a denominator machine. It watches when nothing politically interesting is happening. That sounds inefficient until the day something interesting happens. Then years of ordinary observation become the reference class.

The ocean is particularly good at supplying ordinary observation because it never stops moving. In winter storm belts, colliding wave systems can produce strong, repeatable microbarom bands. The detailed physics are technical, but the epistemic use is simple: the world provides a naturally recurring low-frequency transmitter whose variability can help expose the state of the propagation medium.

This turns the phrase “background noise” inside out. If you know enough about the background, it becomes foreground for a different question.

Verification organizations have practical reasons to care. Detection capability depends on station noise. A global performance model that assumes a fixed noise floor will be wrong in systematic ways. Day-night changes, seasonal ocean activity, wind at the station and atmospheric paths all alter what an event must overcome to be seen. Reliable thresholds are therefore local and temporal.

The ocean in the microphone teaches the network humility by repetition. Most of what a sensitive instrument hears is not the thing it was funded to find. That is normal. The mistake is to think this excess is waste.

Noise is waste only when nobody learns its grammar.
