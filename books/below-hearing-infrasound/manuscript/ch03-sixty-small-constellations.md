# Sixty Small Constellations

A global network begins as a map, and maps are dangerous because they make coverage look simpler than it is.

Place sixty dots around the world and it is easy to imagine sixty equal circles of hearing. That is not how infrasound works. Each station is an array, and each array lives inside local terrain, local wind, local weather and a global atmosphere whose propagation changes with direction and altitude. Coverage is not a set of neat disks. It is a moving field of probabilities.

The planned CTBT infrasound network consists of sixty stations in thirty-five countries. They are part of the larger International Monitoring System, whose treaty-designated facilities also include seismic, hydroacoustic and radionuclide infrastructure. The geographical distribution matters because no one station can make the global claim. The point is not to own the best microphone. It is to create enough independent geometry that events can be associated and located across political borders.

Inside each infrasound station, several sensors are separated by distances chosen to make coherent arrival estimation possible. The elements form a small constellation. A distant wavefront reaches one element, then another, then another. Those tiny time differences can be turned into a back azimuth—the direction from which the energy appears to have arrived—and an apparent horizontal speed. Multiple stations can then supply intersecting directional constraints.

This is basic array processing, but it carries a philosophical advantage that is easy to miss. A station does not need to know the meaning of a signal before it can measure its coherence and direction. The array separates an early question—did something propagate through the atmosphere?—from the later question—what was it?

That separation is why arrays are so useful in messy environments. Local wind noise may be strong at one sensor but less coherent across the array. A genuine distant wave can arrive coherently. The station can therefore turn spatial redundancy into skepticism.

The site itself is designed around that skepticism. Wind is an enemy because turbulence produces pressure fluctuations in the same low-frequency band the instrument wants. Infrasound stations use wind-noise-reduction systems, often networks of porous hoses or pipe arrangements connected to the microbarometer, to average pressure over an area and suppress local turbulent fluctuations. The exact implementations vary. The underlying idea is almost embarrassingly physical: if the nuisance is small-scale and the target is coherent across a larger area, average the nuisance before it reaches the sensor.

Then geography reappears.

A station near persistent surf faces a different background from one deep in a continental interior. Mountain waves, storms and human activity differ. Seasonal winds alter long-range propagation. A source to the east may couple efficiently into a stratospheric duct while the same source energy radiated westward does not reach a useful path. Even within one event, the source itself may radiate unevenly.

The Chelyabinsk meteor made this visible in a way an idealized explosion cannot. In 2013, the airburst was detected across the CTBT infrasound network at extraordinary range. Later analysis by Christoph Pilger and colleagues did something more valuable than celebrating the detections: it examined why some operating stations detected the event and others did not. Their results emphasized source directivity associated with the meteor’s trajectory, station noise and propagation. Detection favored some stations positioned broadly perpendicular to the trajectory even at very long distances.

A flat map would not have predicted that cleanly. A source moving through the atmosphere had made the network directional in an unfamiliar way.

This is the recurring lesson of global monitoring. More stations help, but station count by itself is not sensitivity. What matters is where they are, what their noise looks like, what the atmosphere is doing, what frequencies the source emits, and whether several paths happen to become usable at once.

The treaty architecture adds another constraint: stations are not merely scientific instruments placed wherever a principal investigator can obtain a grant and a permit. Their locations are embedded in an international agreement. Host states take responsibility for facilities and cooperate with the Technical Secretariat under agreed arrangements. Certification, authentication, maintenance and communications are therefore part of the observing system.

That institutional layer produces a kind of scientific asset that is expensive to replicate casually: long-lived global continuity. University field campaigns can be denser and more flexible. National networks can be excellent in their regions. A treaty system offers something different—a deliberately distributed international backbone designed to keep operating.

It is worth being careful here. The treaty did not invent global geophysics, and the IMS is not the only source of infrasound data. National and academic arrays predate and complement it. Researchers combine IMS observations with local stations, seismic networks, satellite data, weather models and many other sensors. The network’s distinctiveness lies in its global treaty geometry and operational persistence, not monopoly over the phenomenon.

That distinction also keeps the peaceful-use story from becoming propaganda. If a volcano observatory uses a local infrasound array ten kilometres from a vent, that may provide richer source information than a treaty station thousands of kilometres away. If a meteor is captured by dashboard cameras, satellites and seismic stations, those observations can constrain its trajectory and energy far better than distant pressure traces alone. The best science often comes from combination.

A global verification system is therefore an argument for interoperability, not sensor worship.

The small constellation at each station measures one direction and one apparent velocity. The constellation of stations measures a larger geometry. The larger IMS combines physical domains. External science adds still more constraints. At every scale the method is the same: do not ask one observation to carry a claim larger than it can support.

This is not how conspiracy stories about monitoring networks usually work. In those stories, one hidden sensor either sees everything or the absence of public data proves deliberate concealment. Real global monitoring is more awkward and more interesting. Some events are missed. Some arrive weakly. Some are recorded beautifully. Some produce multiple paths. Some are obvious only after atmospheric modeling. Some are easy to locate and hard to classify. Some become clear because another technology saw them too.

A station is a dot on a map. An observatory is the network of relationships among those dots, the atmosphere, and all the things loud enough to cross it.
