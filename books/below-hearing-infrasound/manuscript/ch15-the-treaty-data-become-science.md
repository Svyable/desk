# The Treaty Data Become Science

A scientific instrument is usually justified by the science it will do. The International Monitoring System reversed the sequence. Its global infrastructure was justified by a political agreement, and science arrived afterward through the data stream.

This matters because funding history shapes instruments.

A volcanologist designing a network would put more stations near volcanoes. A meteor scientist might optimize for atmospheric entry corridors statistically rather than politically. An atmospheric dynamicist might want dense meridional transects or relocatable arrays. A nuclear-test verification regime wants global coverage, survivable operations, authenticated transmission and an internationally agreed geometry. The resulting network is suboptimal for many scientific questions and uniquely useful for others.

The science grows in the mismatch.

When CTBTO describes “civil and scientific applications,” the phrase can sound like institutional public relations, a list of socially agreeable uses attached to a security system. Earthquake data can improve regional locations. Hydroacoustic stations can record whale calls. Radionuclide stations can track releases such as Fukushima. Infrasound can detect meteors and volcanoes. All true.

The more interesting fact is that the treaty architecture changes what kind of science becomes possible.

Long-term globally distributed stations create comparable time series. Authentication and operational maintenance produce confidence that records have not been casually altered. Remote sites sample parts of the planet that are expensive for individual research groups to instrument continuously. The network’s four technologies observe different physical domains at once. A global event can therefore be reconstructed across atmosphere, ocean and solid Earth with an infrastructure not built around that event.

Chelyabinsk shows the value. The meteor’s infrasound detections were not simply extra confirmation that a fireball occurred. They contributed to estimates of energy and to analysis of the global infrasound network’s performance. The scientific paper and the verification question used the same records differently.

Hunga magnified that pattern. A treaty network became one component in an unprecedented Earth-system observing campaign. Its infrasound stations joined barometers, satellites, GNSS receivers, seismic networks, hydroacoustic sensors and balloons. Researchers could watch atmospheric waves move around the planet repeatedly. The IMS was not the only network that mattered, but its global distribution made it difficult to ignore.

The peaceful-use narrative becomes stronger when it stops pretending the treaty mission disappears.

Scientific work on propagation feeds verification. Better middle-atmosphere models improve predictions of where explosion infrasound should travel. Better source models improve discrimination. Better understanding of microbaroms improves noise characterization. Better automated association developed for volcanoes may inform global event processing. Scientific access is not charity extracted from a security instrument. It can be part of the instrument’s technical ecology.

This relationship also creates tension. Open science favors broad access, reproducible methods and public archives. Verification regimes involve security, state authority and controlled technical processes. The CTBT has mechanisms for data distribution to states and for scientific engagement, but the network is not simply an open academic observatory. Researchers often work through national data centers, collaborations or approved access arrangements.

That friction deserves more attention than a celebratory account usually gives it.

When a network becomes scientifically valuable, pressure grows to open it. When the same network supports international security, pressure remains to preserve authentication, controlled operational procedures and state responsibilities. The question is not open versus closed in the abstract. It is which data, at what latency, with which metadata, under what terms, and for what purposes.

Scientific reproducibility can suffer when only derived products are public. Security can suffer when operational details expose vulnerabilities. Both concerns can be exaggerated by institutions protecting turf. Good governance has to distinguish genuine sensitivity from habit.

The network’s scientific conferences and workshops are one mechanism for managing that boundary. Researchers present methods, event studies and technological improvements. Natural events become shared objects around which the verification and academic communities can collaborate without needing a treaty violation to justify the work.

There is something quietly important in this. A system built because states do not fully trust one another creates a community that depends on international technical trust.

Stations are hosted around the world. Engineers maintain common standards. Data move across borders. Analysts compare events. Scientists from different countries study the same atmospheric waves. The political project begins in suspicion and requires cooperation to sustain its measuring apparatus.

That does not make arms control apolitical. It makes infrastructure a place where limited trust can be engineered without pretending broader trust exists.

Science benefits from that arrangement because continuity survives political cycles better than many ad hoc collaborations. A station in a remote region does not have to justify itself anew every year by producing a discovery. Its primary mission is persistent monitoring. The scientific archive accumulates almost as a by-product of readiness.

This is the opposite of the usual funding pressure in academia, where instruments are expected to answer a proposal’s questions on a grant timeline. A verification station can record a decade of mostly ordinary atmosphere until a Chelyabinsk or Hunga arrives. When the rare event comes, the old data provide context.

The value is latent.

There are costs. Treaty geometry is fixed when science might prefer relocation. Instrument bandwidth and site choices reflect verification requirements. Data policies can constrain access. Operational stability can make hardware innovation slower. A network optimized for persistence may not be optimized for experimentation.

The right comparison is therefore not “security network becomes better science network.” It is “security network creates a scientific instrument with a distinctive set of strengths and weaknesses.”

This distinction protects the argument from self-congratulation.

The IMS should not be treated as evidence that military or security spending automatically produces public scientific goods. Many classified systems generate no accessible benefit, and secrecy can block replication. The CTBT case is distinctive because international verification requires shared technical infrastructure and because the monitored physical phenomena overlap deeply with Earth science.

The overlap gives the data a second life.

A pressure trace recorded to guard against a nuclear explosion can become part of a meteor energy estimate. A recurring ocean signal that raises the detection floor can become a probe of atmospheric winds. A volcanic explosion that is irrelevant to treaty compliance can test global association. A giant eruption can reveal planetary wave modes.

This second life changes what the first life knows.

That is the loop I want to preserve. Treaty data become science. Science becomes better models. Better models become better verification. The network is not dual use in the static sense of one tool serving two masters. It is a coupled institution in which the supposedly secondary use modifies the primary one.

The planet keeps returning the favor because it will not stay quiet long enough for the network to specialize.
