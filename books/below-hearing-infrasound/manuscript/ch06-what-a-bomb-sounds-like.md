# What a Bomb Sounds Like

There is no single answer to what a bomb sounds like at five thousand kilometres.

That is the first thing to keep when moving from the physics of propagation back to the treaty mission. A large atmospheric explosion can produce an impulsive pressure wave rich in low-frequency energy. So can other energetic events. The task is not to memorize one shape and wait for it to appear. It is to accumulate enough features and independent evidence that one source class becomes more plausible than its alternatives.

The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty was built around that reality. Infrasound is one technology among four. The wider International Monitoring System combines seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound and radionuclide observations because nuclear explosions can occur in different environments and because no single physical observable is perfectly specific.

Consider an underground nuclear test. The primary remote signature is seismic: energy injected into the solid Earth. Analysts study location, magnitude, depth, waveform characteristics and how those compare with earthquakes or other explosions. Under some conditions an underground test may also generate infrasound through ground-to-air coupling, especially if shallow or associated with surface motion. Radionuclide evidence, if radioactive gases or particles escape, supplies a different kind of clue. None of these channels should be collapsed into “the bomb signal.” They are pieces of a physical event.

Atmospheric tests place infrasound in a more central role. A large explosion couples directly into the air. Long-range arrays can estimate arrival direction and timing, and multiple stations can contribute to location. Yet source identification still matters. A large chemical explosion also couples directly into the air. A meteoroid airburst may release comparable or greater energy. A volcanic eruption can produce a powerful acoustic pulse. If an analyst labels every large atmospheric impulse nuclear, the monitoring system fails at the most basic level.

The word “discrimination” sounds like a classification problem, and in part it is. Frequency content, signal duration, waveform shape, source motion, directionality, repeated activity and association with other detections can all help. But the strongest discriminant is often context constrained by independent physics.

A meteor is moving at hypersonic speed along a luminous trajectory. Optical and satellite observations may establish that trajectory. Its infrasonic radiation can behave like a line source rather than a stationary point explosion. A volcano sits at a known vent and may have seismic unrest, satellite thermal signatures or ash observations. A chemical explosion may occur at a documented industrial site. A rocket launch follows a trajectory and schedule. A nuclear explosion, if covert, may have less helpful external context, which is precisely why the treaty network exists.

The verification challenge is therefore asymmetrical. Known natural and industrial events are valuable because they teach the network the appearance of alternative sources. The prohibited event may be rarer and less cooperative.

This creates a calibration problem that would be familiar to medicine. If the disease is rare, a diagnostic test needs exceptional specificity or false positives overwhelm true cases. Nuclear tests are politically important but, thankfully, infrequent. The atmosphere, by contrast, is full of other energetic sources. A successful monitoring system must become expert in the negative class.

The Democratic People’s Republic of Korea’s declared nuclear tests provide real events against which the broader IMS has demonstrated performance. CTBTO states that the network detected all six declared tests between 2006 and 2017. The important lesson for this book is not that infrasound alone “heard North Korea.” Different technologies contributed differently, with seismic monitoring central to underground tests. The verification system works by combining them.

This is where popular descriptions of a “global ear” become risky. They suggest one sense organ. The actual system resembles a body with several senses and a nervous system that has learned their failure modes.

A seismic event under a test site may be suspicious. A matching infrasound arrival can support explosive coupling. Radionuclide detections may add evidence about nuclear character if transport and timing align. The absence of radionuclides does not by itself prove a conventional source, because containment matters. The absence of infrasound does not erase a seismic event, because atmospheric coupling and propagation matter. Each observation changes the likelihood landscape without becoming a verdict alone.

This probabilistic way of thinking is not a retreat from certainty. It is how technical confidence is built when the world does not provide unique signatures.

Nuclear explosions are physically distinctive in many ways, but remote sensors observe consequences rather than labels. A seismic wave does not contain the word “nuclear.” A pressure wave does not announce whether the explosive energy came from fission, ammonium nitrate or a meteoroid. Source identity is inferred from combinations of observables, models and exclusions.

That inference becomes politically consequential. States may have strategic incentives to characterize an ambiguous event in ways that support their interests. A shared monitoring system cannot remove political incentives. It can force argument into a more constrained arena. If everyone has access to authenticated observations, disagreement has to engage with timing, location, waveform, propagation and alternative sources rather than resting entirely on secret claims.

This is a subtle achievement. Verification technology does not make politics scientific. It makes some political claims answerable to physical evidence.

There is a corresponding danger. Technical products can acquire authority beyond what the data justify. A map of a candidate event looks definitive. A computed location can hide uncertainty. A yield estimate can be repeated without its atmospheric assumptions. A classification label can outlive the model that produced it. The more polished the output, the easier it is to forget that the underlying chain contains choices.

The discipline of the field is to keep that chain visible.

What did the stations record? Which arrays detected the event? Which did not? What was the noise at those stations? Which atmospheric model was used? How sensitive is the location to the assumed path? What alternative source classes fit? Did seismic or radionuclide channels provide independent evidence? Is the source known from external observation? Are the uncertainties symmetrical, or does one hypothesis require special pleading?

By the time those questions are answered, “what a bomb sounds like” has become the wrong question.

The right question is what set of observations would be difficult for a non-bomb explanation to reproduce.

That is the standard the natural world will test repeatedly in the chapters ahead.
