# Below Hearing

*Nuclear-Test Verification and the Planetary Infrasound Network*

Sven Hardy Benson

A microphone is a poor metaphor for an infrasound station. A microphone suggests a device waiting for sound. The treaty stations wait for pressure changes smaller and slower than ordinary hearing, but the more important difference is elsewhere: the air between source and sensor does not behave like empty space.

The atmosphere bends sound. It filters it. It opens ducts and closes them. It carries one frequency farther than another. It moves with the wind, changes with altitude, changes with season, and changes while the wave is in flight. A large explosion can vanish from one direction and appear thousands of kilometres away in another. A meteor can be obvious at a station on the far side of a continent and absent from a station that looks, on a flat map, better placed to hear it.

This is why the infrasound component of the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty’s International Monitoring System interests me. It was not built as a general-purpose observatory. It exists inside a verification regime aimed at a specific political problem: how states can have enough independent evidence to judge whether someone has conducted a nuclear explosion. The finished network is designed around sixty infrasound arrays distributed across thirty-five countries, one part of a larger system that also uses seismic, hydroacoustic and radionuclide technologies.

Then the planet kept showing up in the data.

Meteors. Volcanoes. Mining and chemical explosions. Aircraft. Rocket launches. Ocean-generated microbaroms. Auroral and atmospheric disturbances. In 2013, the Chelyabinsk airburst sent infrasonic waves across the world. In January 2022, the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai eruption produced atmospheric waves so large and persistent that they circled the planet repeatedly and became a natural experiment in global atmospheric propagation.

It is tempting to turn that into a cheerful story about scientific spillovers from arms control. That story is true but insufficient. The difficult thing about the network is not that it accidentally hears science. It is that everything else it hears is part of the verification problem.

A detector for forbidden explosions is useless if every meteor becomes a bomb, every volcanic eruption becomes a suspicious event, and every missing arrival becomes proof that somebody has learned to hide. Verification is an exercise in disciplined alternatives. The network has to detect, locate, characterize, compare, reject and combine evidence. It has to understand the ordinary world well enough to say something meaningful about the extraordinary claim.

The chapters that follow stay close to that problem. They do not claim that infrasound alone can identify every explosion. They do not treat a waveform as a verdict. They do not treat institutional confidence as a substitute for the details of propagation or source physics. They also do not treat natural events as mere distractions from the treaty mission. A meteor with a well-observed trajectory or a volcano with independent satellite data can become a calibration opportunity that no arms-control planner could schedule.

The result is an odd kind of observatory: one created by distrust between states, made scientifically richer by events nobody controls, and forced to learn the atmosphere because the atmosphere sits inside every measurement.
