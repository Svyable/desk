# An Accidental Observatory

There is a category of scientific instrument that nobody quite intended to build.

Weather satellites become fire detectors. Spy satellites become archaeological archives. Fiber-optic cables become seismic sensors. Cell-tower timing data becomes a map of human movement. The instrument performs its assigned job, but its existence changes the set of questions that can be asked.

The International Monitoring System belongs in this category with an important difference. Its accidental scientific uses are not separable from the verification mission. The network becomes better at arms control by understanding the supposedly unrelated signals that fill its sensors.

This is most obvious in infrasound because the atmosphere is promiscuous. The same arrays that listen for explosions record meteors, volcanoes, rockets, storms and ocean-generated background. Those sources overlap in frequency and propagation. Analysts cannot build a clean fence around the treaty signal and throw away everything else.

The observatory emerges from the rejected pile.

Consider what the network has accumulated. Years of global pressure observations. Repeated seasonal microbarom fields. Known mining and industrial sources. Volcanic eruptions from fixed locations. Meteors with optical trajectories. Rocket launches with schedules. Rare giant events such as Chelyabinsk and Hunga. Each event samples a different combination of source geometry, frequency, path and atmospheric state.

No single academic campaign would design that catalog. It is too heterogeneous.

Heterogeneity is normally a statistical nuisance. Here it is part of the value. If a propagation model works only for point explosions, meteors break it. If an association algorithm assumes stationary sources, rockets and bolides break it. If a noise model ignores ocean seasonality, microbaroms break it. If a dynamic-range assumption is too narrow, Hunga breaks it. The world performs adversarial testing without trying.

This is what an accidental observatory can do better than a purpose-built experiment: remain available for events nobody predicted.

The cost is lack of control. Scientists cannot choose the next Chelyabinsk. They cannot ask Hunga to erupt again with one parameter changed. They cannot randomize atmospheric winds. The archive is observational and opportunistic. Causal inference has to come from independent source constraints, repeated events, models and comparison across conditions.

That limitation is not unique to infrasound. Astronomy cannot rerun a supernova. Seismology cannot schedule a magnitude-eight earthquake. Planetary science often works with one-off encounters. The skill is to prepare instruments before the event and preserve enough context afterward.

Treaty monitoring is unusually good at preparation because readiness is the mission.

The station has to be operating on the boring day before the interesting day. Communications have to work. Timing has to be trusted. Calibration has to be maintained. The archive has to exist. This creates scientific option value. Nobody knows which rare event will justify the last decade of uneventful data.

There is a policy lesson hidden here. Infrastructure designed only around expected use can miss its highest-value future application. That does not mean every sensor network should be funded forever because something surprising might happen. It means continuity has a measurable scientific value when the phenomena of interest are rare and unpredictable.

Hunga could not have been studied retrospectively by a network installed afterward.

The same is true for anomalous events. If a government decides after a wave of UAP reports to deploy better sensors, it can improve future cases but cannot recover missing geometry from the famous old ones. If astrobiologists decide after a suspicious biosignature that they need a contaminant monitor, the original sample may already be gone. If earthquake scientists want pre-event baselines, instruments must be running before the earthquake.

Persistent monitoring is a form of epistemic insurance.

The treaty supplies that insurance for a particular set of physical observables. Its presence means that when an atmospheric event occurs, there may already be global low-frequency pressure records waiting to be examined.

The network is not universal. Its stations are sparse compared with weather networks. Its bandwidth and geometry reflect verification needs. Small local events may be invisible. Many scientific questions require denser regional arrays. But global continuity changes the scale at which rare energetic events can be studied.

This is why “accidental” should not be confused with “free.”

The scientific observatory exists because states pay to maintain a verification system. Researchers benefit from infrastructure whose costs are justified elsewhere. If the treaty mission disappeared, the scientific case for maintaining every station in its current configuration would have to be made independently. Some stations might survive; others might not.

The science is contingent on politics.

The politics, in turn, benefit from science. Better propagation models and event catalogs make the verification system more credible. This mutual dependence may be one reason the network has developed such a rich research culture. Scientific use provides additional constituencies for technical quality, while verification provides operational continuity that science alone might struggle to finance.

There is a cautionary version of this story. Secondary users can begin to imagine that the network was secretly built for their question. A UAP researcher may see global acoustic sensors and assume they constitute a hidden anomaly-detection architecture. A volcanologist may describe the IMS as a global volcano network. A planetary-defense researcher may emphasize bolide detection. Each interpretation captures a use and misses the institution.

The system’s design history matters because it explains its blind spots.

Treaty station placement is not optimized for asteroid entry detection. Array apertures and instrumentation are not optimized for every volcanic source. Data governance reflects international security. The accidental observatory is powerful precisely because it is slightly wrong for every secondary purpose in a different way.

Those wrongnesses are scientifically generative. They force collaboration with other sensors.

A meteor is better understood with optical and satellite data. A volcano with seismic and remote sensing. Hunga with barometers, GNSS, satellites, seismometers, ocean gauges and balloons. The IMS contributes one global physical channel to a larger reconstruction.

The observatory is therefore not the network alone. It is the network’s ability to join an event after the event becomes interesting.

This may be the most useful model for future planetary monitoring. Instead of imagining one perfect sensor built for unknown unknowns, build durable interoperable systems whose ordinary measurements can be recombined when something strange happens.

The trick is not to predict the anomaly.

The trick is to have been measuring the world before it arrived.
