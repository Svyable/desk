# Chapter 6 — Stargate delivery evidence

## Claim under test

Chapter 6 treats Stargate as a physical forecast: very large announced AI-infrastructure commitments are evidence that companies expect machine-intelligence demand to outrun the long lead times of power, buildings, networking, and chips. The manuscript is careful not to equate an announcement with completed capacity. That caution remains correct, but by mid-2026 the evidence had moved beyond announcements alone.

## Primary sources checked

- OpenAI, “Building the infrastructure for the Intelligence Age in Michigan,” June 1, 2026. OpenAI says it broke ground on The Barn, a 1 GW Stargate data-center campus in Saline, Michigan, with Oracle, Related Digital, and Walbridge. This is evidence of construction activity, not completed 1 GW operating capacity. https://openai.com/index/stargate-michigan-data-center/
- Oracle, “Data Centers,” current project-status page, accessed September 2, 2026. Oracle reports that its Abilene, Texas site had delivered 42% of total capacity, with the remainder expected in subsequent quarters. The same page says other Stargate-linked sites in Shackelford County, Doña Ana County, Port Washington, and Saline Township were contracted with customer delivery beginning in 2027. This is stronger evidence than a project announcement because it distinguishes delivered capacity from contracted future capacity. https://www.oracle.com/data-centers/
- Oracle, “Oracle, Related Digital, Blackstone, OpenAI, Walbridge and Governor Whitmer Celebrate Construction of Stargate Campus in Saline Township,” June 1, 2026. Oracle describes The Barn as under construction, with project financing including equity from Related Digital, funds affiliated with Blackstone, and long-term debt financing anchored by PIMCO-managed funds and accounts. The financing structure reinforces the chapter’s point that these projects convert demand forecasts into long-duration capital commitments. https://www.oracle.com/news/announcement/related-digital-oracle-openai-walbridge-and-governor-whitmer-celebrate-construction-of-stargate-campus-in-saline-township-2026-06-01/
- OpenAI and SoftBank Group, “OpenAI and SoftBank Group partner with SB Energy,” January 9, 2026. OpenAI says it signed a 1.2 GW data-center lease for the initial buildout at a previously announced Milam County site, while OpenAI and SoftBank each invested $500 million in SB Energy. This is a contractual and equity commitment, but not proof that the full 1.2 GW is operational. https://openai.com/index/stargate-sb-energy-partnership/

Sources rechecked September 2, 2026.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

The new evidence strengthens the chapter only if categories remain separate. “Broke ground,” “contracted,” “under development,” “delivered,” and “operational” are not synonyms. A 1 GW campus can be under construction while only a fraction of its eventual load is serving customers. A lease can bind future capacity without proving utilization. A financing package can be real while the project still faces execution, power, permitting, equipment, demand, and technology risks.

The strongest counterargument to the chapter’s capital-cycle thesis is therefore not that Stargate is merely vaporware. Parts of the program have plainly crossed into construction and partial delivery. The stronger challenge is whether the eventual capacity will be well utilized at returns sufficient to justify the capital, or whether rapid efficiency gains, demand disappointment, local power constraints, financing costs, or model changes strand portions of the buildout.

Oracle’s project-status page is especially useful because it shows both sides at once: Abilene has delivered part of its capacity, while several other sites remain contracted for future delivery. The evidence is neither “$500 billion has been spent” nor “nothing exists yet.” It is a pipeline moving through distinct states.

## Editorial implication

A later manuscript pass can sharpen the chapter by replacing the binary distinction between announcement and completion with a small physical progression:

**announced → financed/contracted → under construction → partially delivered → fully operating → utilized**

That progression is more faithful to how infrastructure actually arrives and gives the book a better test of its own thesis. The important question is not whether companies can announce enormous numbers. It is how much announced capacity survives each transition and what happens to price, product limits, and demand as real capacity reaches service.

This also improves the narrative logic. The chapter’s opening line — “A data center is a promise with a power contract” — becomes more than metaphor when the reader can see the promise hardening into leases, financing, construction, and delivered megawatts. The interesting uncertainty then moves forward: not “will anyone build it?” but “what will the economy do with what gets built?”

## Falsification test

The chapter’s strongest prediction would weaken if a large share of contracted Stargate capacity is canceled or indefinitely delayed; if delivered capacity remains persistently underutilized; if useful AI work per unit of infrastructure improves fast enough that aggregate capacity demand stops rising; or if financing and power costs force retail AI prices materially upward rather than allowing infrastructure abundance to lower the visible price of cognition.
