# Subsidized Intelligence

*How Twenty Dollars Bought the Frontier — and Who Paid the Rest*

There is a small recurring charge on millions of credit-card statements that would have been difficult to explain a few years ago.

Twenty dollars.

It does not buy a newspaper subscription, a gym membership, a music catalogue, or a streaming bundle. It buys access to a machine that can read a contract, explain a blood-test result without pretending to be a doctor, inspect a spreadsheet, write and debug software, search the web, draft a presentation, reason through a business decision, translate a conversation, generate an image, revise the image, compare documents, summarize a hundred pages, talk out loud, and spend minutes or hours researching a question before returning with a sourced report.

The list keeps changing because the product keeps changing.

That is part of the point.

In August 2026, OpenAI still prices ChatGPT Plus at $20 a month. The subscription is ordinary enough to be purchased with the same casual decision as a couple of streaming services. What sits behind it is not ordinary. The current generation of frontier models is trained and served on industrial systems whose important units are no longer servers but megawatts, gigawatts, campuses, accelerator fleets, fiber routes, power contracts, and financing commitments. OpenAI announced $110 billion of new investment in February 2026. Its Stargate effort grew out of a commitment framed in 2025 as $500 billion and ten gigawatts of United States AI infrastructure. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta are each spending or planning to spend sums on technical infrastructure that would once have looked more natural in a national budget than in an information-technology line item.

The consumer sees twenty dollars.

The supply chain sees concrete, transformers, substations, cooling systems, advanced packaging, high-bandwidth memory, networking switches, accelerators, power-purchase agreements, debt, equity, leases, and depreciation.

This book is about the distance between those two views.

I call it subsidized intelligence, but the word needs care. I do not know whether a particular ChatGPT Plus subscriber is profitable for OpenAI. Neither do you, unless you work close enough to the relevant internal accounts to see a number the company does not publish. The claim here is not that every $20 subscription loses money or that venture investors are literally paying a fixed portion of your monthly bill.

The subsidy is broader and more interesting than that.

A consumer price can be low because somebody else accepts a long payback period. It can be low because a cloud provider is building capacity ahead of demand. It can be low because one product is supported by a portfolio of enterprise contracts, API revenue, advertising, financing, or expected future businesses. It can be low because hardware gets more efficient each generation. It can be low because model architecture improves. It can be low because software learns to use fewer tokens for the same result. It can be low because competition makes suppliers pass efficiency gains through to customers instead of keeping them as margin. It can be low because capital markets believe that making intelligence abundant now will create enormous markets later.

Usually it is some mixture.

That mixture is the subsidy.

The important fact is that the person at the keyboard is not being asked to finance the machine in proportion to the machine's industrial cost. The customer is being offered a simple retail price while an ecosystem races to drive the cost of useful intelligence down underneath it.

This arrangement is not unique to artificial intelligence. Railways were built before most of the freight they eventually carried existed. Telephone networks required capital before the calls. Electrical systems laid wire and built generators in anticipation of a society that would invent appliances after power became available. Semiconductor fabs are financed years before their output reaches consumers. Mobile-phone plans conceal an astonishing stack of radios, fiber, spectrum rights, towers, switching equipment, software, and capital behind a monthly bill.

Infrastructure often arrives before behavior catches up.

What makes AI unusual is the thing being industrialized.

Cognition used to arrive attached to a person.

If you wanted a lawyer's analysis, you paid for a lawyer's time. If you wanted software, somebody had to write it. If you wanted a translation, a translation agency had to find someone who knew both languages. If you wanted a bespoke illustration, a designer or illustrator had to make it. If you wanted a literature review, someone had to read the literature. If you wanted a patient tutor at eleven at night, you needed an unusually patient tutor who happened to be awake.

Human intelligence has never been billed by the token, but it has always been expensive.

Now pieces of cognitive work can be produced by machines whose marginal costs are falling quickly enough that the industry talks openly about performance per dollar, cost per token, tokens per watt, and tokens per second. In 2023, the original GPT-4 API charged $30 per million input tokens and $60 per million output tokens for its standard context. Three years later, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna is priced at twenty cents per million input tokens and $1.20 per million output tokens for standard processing. The comparison is not apples to apples—models, capabilities, architectures, workloads, and quality have changed—but that is precisely why the curve matters. The cheaper model is not merely an old model sold at clearance. It belongs to a newer family with a context window measured at more than a million tokens.

A price decline is interesting.

A simultaneous increase in capability is more interesting.

The combination is how technologies escape their original category.

When storage became cheap, we stopped deciding carefully which photographs to keep. When bandwidth became cheap, we stopped treating video as a special event. When computation became cheap, it disappeared into watches, cars, thermostats, cameras, toys, factories, and greeting cards. The eventual sign of abundance is often not that people save money. It is that they stop conserving the thing.

That is where the phrase *too cheap to meter* becomes useful, provided we remember its history. The phrase is associated with Lewis Strauss's 1954 optimism about atomic power. Electricity did not become free. Meters did not disappear. Fuel cost turned out to be only one piece of the bill. Plants still required capital, operations, transmission, maintenance, regulation, and financing.

The phrase survived because it describes a recurring dream: a useful input becoming so abundant that counting each unit stops making sense.

AI may approach that condition in a peculiar way.

Tokens will still have costs. Data centers will still receive electricity bills. Chips will still depreciate. Networks will still have capacity limits. Premium reasoning may remain expensive. The meter will exist somewhere.

But it may move away from the user.

A person may pay twenty dollars a month and stop asking whether a particular paragraph required one thousand or ten thousand tokens. A company may buy an enterprise seat and stop calculating the marginal cost of asking a model to review one more contract. An autonomous agent may use millions of tokens in the course of a task because the relevant economic question is no longer how many tokens it consumed but whether the completed task was worth more than the compute.

That transition changes behavior.

Cheap intelligence does not simply replace expensive intelligence. It creates demand for thought that nobody previously purchased.

Every email can be checked. Every meeting can be summarized. Every contract can be compared against policy. Every customer can receive a personalized explanation. Every code change can be reviewed. Every student can ask another question. Every document can be translated. Every spreadsheet can be interrogated. Every small company can run analyses that once belonged to large companies. Every individual can arrive at a negotiation with a researcher, editor, analyst, programmer, and tutor waiting behind a single text box.

Whether all of that is wise is another question.

The economics arrives before the wisdom.

The central argument of this book is straightforward:

**We are watching intelligence move from a scarce professional input toward an abundant infrastructural one, and an extraordinary volume of capital is being spent to pull the cost curve down fast enough that people can consume far more cognition than they could ever have afforded from humans.**

Twenty dollars is the retail window into that transition.

Behind the window is one of the largest industrial buildouts of the era.

Ahead of it is the possibility that useful machine intelligence becomes cheap enough that the important question is no longer whether we can afford to ask the machine to think.

It is what we decide to ask it to think about once the answer is nearly always yes.
