# The Twenty-Dollar Window

Twenty dollars is a peculiar price for a revolution.

It is low enough to disappear among the recurring charges on a household statement. Less than many people spend on lunch for two. Roughly the price of a streaming subscription, a basic software utility, or a month of cloud storage with room left over. It does not announce itself as infrastructure. It does not feel like industrial policy. It does not suggest power plants, semiconductor fabs, cooling towers, fiber, substations, server racks, debt, venture capital, sovereign capital, or corporate balance sheets willing to absorb years of depreciation.

It looks like an app.

That is the first useful fact about modern artificial intelligence.

The user sees a chat box and a monthly number. Behind the chat box sits one of the largest capital races of the era.

As of August 2026, OpenAI lists ChatGPT Plus at $20 a month. The subscription includes broader model access than the free tier, higher limits, advanced reasoning, voice, image generation, file analysis, and access to research tools where available. The precise models and quotas change because the product is still moving. The price has proved more durable than many of the capabilities attached to it.

That asymmetry is worth staring at.

A person who subscribed to software twenty years ago generally knew what sort of thing was being rented. The program might edit photographs, manage accounts, or help write documents. Better versions appeared, but the category stayed legible. The price mostly bought access to a defined tool.

A subscription to a frontier AI product is stranger. It buys entry into a moving frontier. The thing purchased this month may be able to reason through a spreadsheet better than the thing purchased last month, search more sources, work across more files, write and execute code, generate images, hold a spoken conversation, or carry out a task over a longer sequence of steps. The customer is not merely subscribing to a fixed feature set. The customer is standing at the retail end of a research and infrastructure pipeline.

The pipeline keeps improving while the monthly number can remain unchanged.

That makes the twenty dollars easy to misunderstand.

One possible misunderstanding is to treat it as evidence that the underlying service must cost less than twenty dollars to provide. We do not know that. OpenAI does not publish the unit economics of the average Plus subscriber. The answer would be difficult to summarize even if it did. Some people barely use the service. Others use voice, images, deep research, code execution, long files, or reasoning-heavy models. Usage limits change. Model routing changes. A company can send different requests to different systems. Cached context may be cheaper than fresh context. Hardware utilization matters. Contracts with cloud providers matter. The marginal cost of an extra lightweight conversation is not the same as the economic cost of maintaining the frontier that makes the subscription attractive.

So this book will not pretend that a hidden income statement has been discovered by looking at the sticker price.

The more interesting observation does not require that claim.

Twenty dollars buys access to capabilities whose creation is being financed by sums so large that the numbers stop feeling like product development and start feeling like public works.

In February 2026, OpenAI announced $110 billion of new investment at a $730 billion pre-money valuation. The company described compute, distribution, and capital as the three things required to scale AI access. The financing included announced commitments from SoftBank, NVIDIA, and Amazon. A year earlier, OpenAI and its partners had announced Stargate with an intention to invest $500 billion over four years in U.S. AI infrastructure. Later updates described gigawatts of planned data-center capacity and hundreds of billions of dollars of intended investment.

Those are announced investments and commitments, not a single pile of cash already converted into working computers. The distinction matters. Data-center projects are staged. Contracts contain conditions. Capacity can be planned, financed, under construction, energized, or actually serving traffic, and those are different states.

But the direction of travel is not subtle.

Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon are simultaneously spending at scales that would have sounded implausible for computing infrastructure a few years ago. Their capital-expenditure figures are not clean measures of “AI spending.” They include data centers, cloud services, CPUs, networking, buildings, and businesses that predate the current AI boom. Amazon’s company-wide capex also supports activities far beyond AWS. It would be sloppy to add the headline numbers together and call the result the cost of artificial intelligence.

It would be equally strange to look away from them.

The companies supplying the computational substrate are telling investors, in unusually plain financial language, that they intend to devote enormous portions of their cash-generating businesses to expanding compute. Microsoft described tens of billions of quarterly capital expenditures, with a large share going to short-lived assets such as CPUs and GPUs. Alphabet guided to well over a hundred billion dollars of annual capital investment, primarily technical infrastructure. Meta’s 2026 guidance moved into a similar range. Amazon said it expected about $200 billion of capital expenditure across the company during 2026 and tied much of the strategic case to AI, chips, cloud capacity, and other major bets.

At the consumer end, the bill remains twenty dollars.

The mismatch is not necessarily irrational.

That point matters because “subsidy” easily turns into an accusation. A company can rationally invest enormous sums in infrastructure before the infrastructure reaches mature utilization. A cloud provider builds capacity that can generate revenue for years. A model company finances research that may lower future inference costs. Hardware bought today can support many customers rather than one subscription. A low consumer price can accelerate adoption, create habit, improve distribution, and produce future enterprise demand. The company may also impose usage limits that keep the economics of the subscription within acceptable bounds.

The customer does not need to be a money-losing customer for the system to be subsidized in the broader sense that concerns this book.

The industrial base is being built before the final market structure is known.

Capital is arriving ahead of certainty.

Competitors are pricing before the long-run cost curve has settled.

Cloud companies are financing infrastructure because they expect demand that does not yet exist at full scale.

Model companies are using today’s financing to create tomorrow’s cheaper capability.

Hardware companies are investing in the next generation of chips because customers have made commitments based on models that are themselves still changing.

In that environment, the retail price is not a transparent meter of the social resources being consumed. It is a strategic interface between a capital-intensive race and a user who mostly wants an answer.

This is not unprecedented.

Consumers often meet new infrastructure through a price that tells them very little about the cost of creating the network underneath it.

The first years of a telecommunications network are not priced as though each customer must personally finance a proportional share of every tower, trench, switch, spectrum license, and research program before placing a call. A new airline route does not charge the first passenger for the aircraft. Cloud computing turned buildings full of servers into a few lines on a monthly invoice. Search engines made enormous distributed systems feel free because advertising paid the bill. Social networks hid data centers behind a sign-up page.

Artificial intelligence inherits all of those tricks and adds a new one.

The product itself is partially made of ongoing computation.

A book, once printed, can be read again without the publisher running a new printing press for every paragraph. Conventional software can often serve another user at low marginal computational cost after the program has been written. A frontier model performs fresh work when it answers. The interaction may require model inference, memory, retrieval, safety systems, tool calls, storage, networking, and sometimes additional models specialized for images, audio, browsing, or code.

The user experiences this as thought on demand.

The provider experiences it as a workload.

That is why the twenty-dollar window is such a useful place to begin. It shows both the magic and the accounting problem in one frame.

Imagine trying to describe the subscription without using the word AI.

For twenty dollars a month, you receive access to a system that can edit a memo, explain a legal clause in ordinary language, inspect a spreadsheet, help debug software, translate a document, generate an illustration, summarize a long report, rehearse an interview, produce a study plan, search the web, and talk through a problem aloud.

None of those capabilities is a perfect replacement for the professional who specializes in the corresponding field. A generated explanation is not legal representation. A model looking at medical information is not a physician. Software help is not equivalent to a senior engineer responsible for production systems. An image generator is not an art director who understands a brand after years of work.

The comparisons become ridiculous when they are made too literally.

But the breadth matters.

Historically, broad access to competent assistance across this many cognitive domains required either a large organization, unusual wealth, an unusually capable network of friends and colleagues, or a great deal of personal time. Now a consumer subscription puts partial versions of those capabilities behind the same text field.

The important economic word is partial.

Artificial intelligence does not have to replace an expert to change the economics of expertise.

Suppose a tool can perform the first pass of a task that previously required an hour of someone’s time. Suppose it can turn a blank page into a draft good enough to criticize. Suppose it can read the fifty documents you would otherwise postpone reading. Suppose it can generate ten approaches to a problem before you choose which one deserves attention. Suppose it can make a small-business owner competent enough to ask a specialist better questions.

The value appears in the margin before it appears in replacement.

This is one reason consumer AI can feel economically larger than its subscription revenue. The customer is buying optionality across thousands of small tasks, many of which were previously too minor to justify hiring anyone at all.

You did not hire a researcher to compare six washing machines.

You did not retain a programmer to write a one-off script for renaming a folder of files.

You did not ask a translator to help understand three sentences in an old letter.

You did not employ an analyst to turn a CSV into a quick chart before a meeting.

You simply went without the capability, did a weaker version yourself, or spent more time than the result deserved.

Low-cost intelligence creates demand by making those previously uneconomic tasks worth attempting.

That is the first hint that the twenty-dollar price may be more important as a behavioral threshold than as a revenue number.

At a thousand dollars a month, a general AI assistant is a professional tool purchased when the expected return is obvious. At a hundred dollars, it can become a serious productivity expense. At twenty dollars, it enters the territory of curiosity. People subscribe without a capital-budget meeting. They use it on problems too small to model financially. They try it because a friend mentions it. They ask the embarrassing question they would not pay an expert to answer. They waste prompts.

Waste is often what abundance looks like at the beginning.

When long-distance calls were expensive, people planned them. When photographs cost film and processing, people conserved exposures. When storage was expensive, files were deleted. When bandwidth was scarce, video was compressed into misery.

As the price fell, behavior changed. People did not merely buy the same number of calls, photographs, files, and bits more cheaply. They used more of them.

Artificial intelligence is entering the wasteful stage.

People ask for five versions when one would have been enough. They generate software and discard it. They ask the model to summarize a document they could read because the summary costs almost nothing to request. They run the same analysis from different angles. They create pictures for jokes. They ask questions with no economic value whatsoever.

From one perspective, this is frivolous consumption of expensive infrastructure.

From another, it is the point.

An industry does not build a mass market by teaching people to ration the product forever. It works to push the cost low enough that rationing stops being the dominant behavior.

OpenAI’s own model pricing illustrates how aggressively the industry is pursuing that threshold. In July 2026 the company introduced lower-priced GPT-5.6 tiers and explicitly described the goal as advancing the price-performance frontier. GPT-5.6 Luna was listed at twenty cents per million input tokens and $1.20 per million output tokens in the API, far below the premium frontier tier.

Token prices should not be mistaken for intelligence prices. A weak model can be cheap per token and expensive per solved problem. A more capable model may produce a useful result in one pass that a smaller model misses repeatedly. Tool calls add cost. Long reasoning can add cost. Humans spend time checking the result.

Still, the direction matters.

The industry is not merely trying to make the best model.

It is trying to make a unit of useful cognition cheaper.

That creates an unusual race. Frontier capability and commodity pricing advance at the same time.

Normally a premium technology protects its price by staying scarce. A faster processor may cost more. A luxury material remains expensive because supply is constrained. An expert charges more as reputation rises.

AI companies are doing something more complicated. They are spending huge amounts to create systems that can perform more valuable work while simultaneously competing to lower the cost of calling them.

The frontier becomes a premium product for a moment, then pieces of it diffuse downward.

Yesterday’s astonishing capability becomes today’s default model and tomorrow’s small-model feature.

That diffusion is the mechanism behind the title of this book.

Subsidized intelligence is not one accounting trick. It is a phase in which capital, competition, and technical progress conspire to make capability appear at the edge of the network for less money than its industrial scale would suggest.

The subsidy can take several forms.

Investors can finance losses or research before revenue catches up.

Cloud providers can build ahead of utilization because they expect future contracts.

A model company can price a consumer tier strategically to establish distribution.

A profitable platform can bundle AI into an existing subscription and recover value elsewhere.

A hardware vendor can improve price-performance so rapidly that the same workload becomes cheaper even while total demand explodes.

Governments can accelerate power, manufacturing, or data-center infrastructure because they view AI capacity as strategic.

And sometimes there is no subsidy in the narrow sense at all. Sometimes a product is simply benefiting from a brutal cost curve.

Those mechanisms should not be blended into one vague story. The rest of this book will separate them.

For now, the important thing is to see the window.

On one side is a person deciding whether a twenty-dollar subscription is worth keeping for another month.

On the other side are corporations committing capital on the scale of national infrastructure programs because they believe machine intelligence will become one of the basic inputs of the economy.

Both sides can be rational at the same time.

The consumer can reasonably think, this is cheap.

The supplier can reasonably think, we need another hundred billion dollars of compute.

The contradiction disappears once price is understood as a point in a race rather than a settled statement of cost.

The twenty-dollar subscription is not proof that intelligence has become cheap.

It is evidence that some of the world’s richest institutions are determined to make it feel cheap enough that people stop asking whether they need it.

That may turn out to be the decisive transition.

The age of abundant intelligence does not begin when a model achieves a particular benchmark. It begins when ordinary people use capable systems on ordinary problems without first calculating whether intelligence is worth spending.

The window is already open.
