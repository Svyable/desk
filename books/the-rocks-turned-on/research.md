# Research ledger — The Rocks Turned On

## Working thesis

Earthquake electromagnetism is most useful as a study in the distance between a physical effect and a forecast. The field contains laboratory mechanisms, credible luminous observations, unquestionably real coseismic coupling, contested pre-seismic anomalies, and decades of failed attempts to convert them into a reliable alarm.

## Four evidence classes

### 1 — Verified / documented

- Earthquakes produce coseismic electromagnetic and ionospheric effects. USGS reviews distinguish these from unproven precursors.
- Dense GNSS observations detect coseismic ionospheric disturbances after large earthquakes; the 1 January 2024 Noto event produced acoustic-wave-linked ionospheric signatures beginning minutes after rupture.
- Laboratory studies report stress-activated electrical charge generation and propagation in igneous rocks.
- The 2014 *Seismological Research Letters* paper assembled 65 historical earthquake-light cases and reported a strong association with rift environments/subvertical faults within that selected catalog.
- USGS’s current geomagnetism guidance states that despite decades of work there is no convincing evidence of electromagnetic precursors suitable for earthquake prediction.

### 2 — Credible but incomplete

- Pisco 2007 optical observations have unusually useful timing and include flashes associated with seismic-wave passage; attribution mechanism remains open.
- L’Aquila, Quebec and southern Washington luminous reports deserve case-level provenance reconstruction rather than blanket acceptance or dismissal.
- Alum Rock ULF pulse increases can be reproduced under certain pulse definitions, but their interpretation is sensitive to thresholds and possible non-seismic sources.
- Geological concentration of reported earthquake lights near rifts may be meaningful but requires a denominator of eligible earthquakes without reported lights.

### 3 — Disputed / unconfirmed

- Claims that specific electromagnetic, thermal infrared, radon, ionospheric, animal-behavior, or luminous anomalies reliably predict earthquakes.
- Claims that satellite maps retrospectively centered near epicenters constitute forecast evidence without pre-registered thresholds and continuous false-positive accounting.
- Claims that the positive-hole mechanism has been demonstrated end-to-end from a natural pre-rupture source volume to a specific atmospheric or ionospheric precursor.

### 4 — Speculative synthesis

- Stress-activated charge carriers may contribute to a subset of luminous or electrical pre-/co-seismic observations under favorable geology.
- Rare pre-rupture electrical phenomena may be physically real yet operationally unusable because of heterogeneity, attenuation, low specificity, or variable lead time.
- The scientific history of earthquake precursors may be distorted by a structural asymmetry: after-effects have a known causal anchor, while before-effects acquire meaning retrospectively.

## Contradictions and incentive audit

- **Mechanism versus forecast:** laboratory plausibility can be rhetorically promoted into field efficacy. Keep every scale transition explicit.
- **Witness versus instrument:** eyewitness testimony may identify rare events missed by networks, but observers are nonuniformly distributed and retrospective reporting is contamination-prone.
- **Catalog versus denominator:** a catalog of 65 reported lights is not a sample of all eligible earthquakes. Do not report geological percentages without reconstructing selection.
- **Satellite abundance:** more sensors increase both discovery power and the number of anomalies available for retrospective matching.
- **Threshold flexibility:** precursor claims using pulse counts, spatial windows, smoothing, or anomaly thresholds must disclose whether choices were frozen before the earthquake.
- **Institutional incentives:** commercial prediction ventures benefit from positive signals; public hazard agencies bear false-alarm costs; neither incentive proves or disproves the phenomenon.
- **Publication bias:** positive precursor cases are intrinsically more publishable than years of null monitoring.

## Chronology spine

- 1960s–1990s: recurring research into tectonomagnetic, telluric, radon, luminous, animal and other proposed precursor phenomena.
- 1986: Derr and Persinger publish on luminous phenomena and small earthquakes in southern Washington.
- 2000: Freund publishes time-resolved charge generation/propagation work in igneous rocks.
- 2006: St-Laurent, Derr and Freund propose stress-activated positive holes as a mechanism for earthquake lights.
- 2007: Pisco M8.0 earthquake produces widely discussed optical observations; Alum Rock M5.4 later becomes a ULF precursor case study.
- 2009: L’Aquila earthquake renews attention to pre-/co-seismic light reports.
- 2014: Thériault et al. publish the 65-case earthquake-light/rift-environment analysis.
- 2021: USGS publication re-examines claimed Alum Rock ULF precursor pulses and emphasizes threshold arbitrariness and source attribution.
- 2024: Noto Peninsula M7.5 earthquake.
- 2025: peer-reviewed analysis resolves post-event ionospheric acoustic waves from Noto, reinforcing strong coseismic coupling while not establishing a precursor.
- 2026: USGS public guidance still states that convincing electromagnetic earthquake precursors have not been demonstrated.
