# The Rocks Turned On

## Earthquake Lights, Electric Stone, and the Prediction Signal That Refuses to Become a Forecast

### Sven Hardy Benson

## Author’s note

There is a photograph I keep wanting this subject to have.

A fault is loading. The rock is a few hours from failure. Somewhere above it, an instrument draws a clean line away from baseline. The line rises. Somebody in a control room sees it, understands it, calls the mayor, stops the trains, empties the brittle buildings, and then the earthquake arrives exactly where the signal said it would.

That photograph does not exist.

What exists is harder to write about and therefore more interesting. Rocks under stress can do electrical things. Earthquakes can disturb magnetic fields and the ionosphere. People have reported strange lights around earthquakes for centuries, and some modern cases have cameras or unusually good timing. Researchers have built mechanisms that are not obviously absurd. Other researchers have taken celebrated precursor signals apart and found arbitrary thresholds, cultural noise, geomagnetic contamination, missing denominators, and the oldest trap in anomaly science: deciding what counted after you already knew where the earthquake was.

I am not going to solve earthquake prediction in these pages. I am going to ask why a field can contain genuine physics and still fail to produce the instrument everyone wants.

# 1. The Night the Ground Lit Up

The embarrassing thing about earthquake lights is that people keep seeing them.

Not everyone. Not at every earthquake. Not in one tidy form. Reports include flashes, glows, luminous columns, floating balls, flames near the ground, and skies that seem to brighten without ordinary lightning. That variety is precisely why the subject spent so long in the scientific waiting room. A phenomenon that can look like six different phenomena has volunteered to be misidentified.

There are obvious impostors. Power lines arc. Transformers explode. Buildings short. Cars move through dust. Lightning happens during storms whether faults are moving or not. After a large earthquake, the night is full of damaged electrical infrastructure and frightened observers looking for meaning. A blue flash above a city after strong shaking is not a mystery until the grid has been acquitted.

The harder cases are the ones that occur where the grid explanation is weak, or before the main shock, or with timing good enough to compare against seismic records. The 2007 Pisco earthquake in Peru became important because security-camera records allowed researchers to compare reported flashes with seismic-wave arrival. Some flashes coincided with wave passage. That does not make them precursors. It does make them harder to wave away as folklore.

The first discipline of this book is therefore grammatical. Before, during, and after are not interchangeable words. A light caused by shaking may teach us about electromechanical coupling and nothing about prediction. A light two hours before rupture would pose a different problem. A light eleven days before rupture poses a harder one still: how do we establish that it belonged to the earthquake at all?

The subject becomes less paranormal as soon as the clocks get better.

# 2. A Prediction Is Not an Explanation

Earthquake science has a vocabulary problem because ordinary language treats prediction as a synonym for understanding.

It is not.

We understand a great deal about why earthquakes occur. Plates move. Faults accumulate strain. Frictional systems lock and slip. Seismic waves radiate from rupture. Statistical hazard can be estimated from fault histories and regional tectonics. Aftershock probabilities can be modeled. None of this gives a city a dependable alarm on Tuesday morning that says a magnitude 7 will begin beneath a particular valley before dinner.

A precursor has to earn more than physical plausibility. It needs lead time, spatial relevance, repeatability, a tolerable false-alarm rate, and performance against events that did not happen. The last requirement is where beautiful stories go to die.

Suppose an electromagnetic station records ten strange pulses in a month and an earthquake follows one cluster. The cluster looks prophetic when plotted with the earthquake marked in red. But what happened after the other pulses? How many quiet days had similar activity? How many earthquakes arrived without pulses? Were the thresholds selected before the event? Was the station near a road, fence, pipeline, railway, power system, thunderstorm corridor, or magnetic observatory that saw the same disturbance?

Prediction is a classification problem disguised as a narrative.

That is why this book will keep returning to denominators. An anomaly followed by an earthquake is not yet a precursor. It is a candidate pair. The scientific object is the full matrix: signal and quake, signal and no quake, no signal and quake, no signal and no quake.

The story wants the glowing night. The forecast needs the boring months.

# 3. The Catalog of Impossible Lights

In 2014, Robert Thériault, France St-Laurent, Friedemann Freund, and John Derr published a study in *Seismological Research Letters* examining 65 reported earthquake-light events extending back centuries. Their surprising result was geological rather than merely visual. A large fraction of the cases appeared associated with rift environments or subvertical faults capable, in their interpretation, of providing pathways for charge migration.

The paper is useful and dangerous in equal measure.

Useful because it refuses to treat every report as identical. Location matters. Fault geometry matters. Rock type matters. Timing matters. A mechanism should make the catalog less random.

Dangerous because a catalog assembled around known luminous reports can tell us about the cases we noticed without automatically telling us how often the proposed geological setting produces no lights. If most famous reports occur near rifts, we still need to know how many rift earthquakes pass in darkness. Selection can create impressive percentages out of sparse history.

This is not an accusation of bad faith. It is the central difficulty of rare-event science. Historical observers did not carry standardized photometers. Newspapers preferred spectacular skies. Populated earthquakes produced more witnesses than offshore ones. Night events had a better chance of generating light reports than noon events. Electrification changed the confounder field over time.

The catalog is therefore not proof. It is a map of where to put better instruments.

That distinction is easy to say and strangely hard to maintain. Once a pattern is visible, the mind begins treating it as a law. I want to keep the pattern provisional long enough to ask whether the geology really predicts the lights, rather than merely arranging them attractively after the fact.

# 4. Rift Valleys and Bad Denominators

Rifts are photogenic explanations.

The crust is stretched. Faults can be steep. Deep structures may provide pathways from stressed rock toward the surface. If electrical charge is activated at depth, a near-vertical route sounds exactly like what the mechanism ordered.

But geological fit has to survive base rates.

Imagine that 80 percent of well-documented earthquake-light cases occur in a certain structural environment. That sounds powerful until we learn how common that environment is among the earthquakes eligible to enter the catalog. If it is rare, the association becomes more interesting. If it is common, less so. If the historical catalog disproportionately samples populated continental rifts, the statistic changes again.

The right denominator is not all earthquakes on Earth. It is all earthquakes that could plausibly have produced an observable light under comparable conditions: magnitude, depth, time of day, cloud cover, population, electrical infrastructure, camera density, rock type, fault geometry, and distance to observers. Nobody has a perfect version of that dataset across four centuries.

This is where anomaly research often splits culturally. One camp says the data are too messy, therefore stop. Another says the pattern is too striking, therefore believe. Both reactions save time by refusing the same work.

I prefer the inconvenient middle. Treat the pattern as a hypothesis generator. Instrument the places the mechanism says should be favorable. Pre-register what counts. Record continuously. Publish the empty years.

A rare phenomenon does not become unscientific because it is rare. It becomes expensive.

# 5. A Battery Inside Granite

Friedemann Freund’s work asks us to take a piece of apparently dead rock and notice that its oxygen lattice may contain unfinished chemistry.

Nominally anhydrous minerals can incorporate hydroxyl-related defects during formation. Under the positive-hole model, pairs associated with peroxy defects can be broken by stress or heat, producing electronic charge carriers—defect electrons in the oxygen sublattice—that can propagate through otherwise unstressed rock.

The phrase “positive hole” sounds like a metaphor. It is solid-state physics vocabulary. The important claim is not that granite stores mystical energy. It is that stressed mineral structures can change their electronic behavior.

Laboratory experiments reported time-resolved charge generation and propagation in igneous rocks. Later work described positive-hole flow through stressed material. The broad fact that deformation, fracture, fluids, piezoelectric minerals, electrokinetic processes, and electronic defects can generate electrical phenomena in rock is not especially scandalous. The scandal arrives when one tries to scale a bench-top effect into a mountain and then into the atmosphere.

That scaling problem is the book.

A laboratory sample has known electrodes, dimensions, stress application, composition, and instrumentation. A crustal source volume is heterogeneous, hot, wet, fractured, conductive, pressurized, and mostly inaccessible. Charges may recombine. Fluids may dominate. Signals may attenuate. The surface may be kilometers away. The atmosphere adds another boundary. A satellite adds hundreds of kilometers more.

Mechanism papers often begin where critics want field evidence. Field papers often end where mechanism papers begin. The missing object is the chain between them.

# 6. Positive Holes

A useful theory should be allowed to become specific enough to fail.

In the positive-hole framework, stress activates mobile electronic charge carriers in certain rocks. These carriers can move out of the stressed volume. If enough reach the surface, they may alter surface potential, ionize air, contribute to corona discharge, or generate electromagnetic effects. In more ambitious versions of the chain, atmospheric ionization and coupling could influence observable infrared or ionospheric signatures.

Every arrow in that paragraph is a separate experiment.

Stress to charge activation. Activation to long-range transport. Transport through real crust. Accumulation at the surface. Surface field to air ionization. Ionization to visible luminosity or other remote signal. Signal amplitude above environmental noise. Signal timing relative to rupture. Specificity to earthquakes rather than other stress processes.

A theory can be plausible at arrow one and weak at arrow seven.

This is why I resist the phrase “earthquake lights explained.” The 2006 St-Laurent, Derr, and Freund paper proposed a mechanism involving stress-activated positive holes and surface electrical breakdown. Proposed is the operative verb. The paper gave the phenomenon a physically articulated candidate mechanism. It did not install a transparent window through the crust.

The difference matters because mechanisms gain cultural momentum. Once a named particle or defect enters the story, later reports begin to feel connected even when the field chain remains incomplete. A technical noun can become a permission slip.

Positive holes deserve better than that. They are interesting enough to be tested without being asked to certify every luminous anecdote ever collected.

# 7. Getting Charge Out of a Mountain

The Earth is not a wire with a fault attached.

Any electrical precursor theory has to survive conductivity. The crust contains minerals, fractures, fluids, temperature gradients, saline pathways, ore bodies, and structures at scales from grains to tectonic provinces. A charge carrier that moves impressively through a laboratory block may face a very different network underground.

This is where competing mechanisms matter. Electrokinetic effects can arise when fluids move through porous material. Piezoelectric effects can occur in appropriate minerals under stress. Fracturing can generate charge separation. Induction, telluric currents, and ordinary geomagnetic variation produce signals unrelated to impending rupture. Infrastructure adds its own electrical geography.

The temptation is to treat multiple possible mechanisms as cumulative support: if rocks can generate electricity five ways, surely one explains the anomaly. Scientifically, the opposite may happen. More mechanisms increase attribution difficulty.

A field signal without a discriminating signature becomes less useful as the list of possible sources grows.

This is also why distance matters. A precursor claimed hundreds of kilometers from a future epicenter carries a burden different from a signal measured directly across a stressed laboratory sample. What path transported the information? At what speed? With what attenuation? Why did it emerge there and not at a closer station?

A mountain is not just a bigger rock. Scale changes the experiment.

# 8. The Surface Problem

Eventually every deep-Earth precursor theory has to cross the ground.

The surface is where the story becomes visible and where it becomes vulnerable. If charge reaches a rock-air boundary, strong local electric fields could in principle produce corona discharge or ionization. That offers one route to luminous phenomena. It also offers measurable quantities: surface potential, air ion concentration, electric field, spectral emission.

Good. Measure them.

The trouble is that the surface is crowded with other causes. Weather changes electric fields. Aerosols matter. Humidity matters. Human electrical systems matter. Topography concentrates fields. Lightning can occur far from the point where a witness looks. Dust can charge. Rockfalls fracture material. Volcanic systems generate their own electrical phenomena.

An earthquake-light mechanism therefore needs more than a photograph of light. It needs a coupled observation: seismic timing, optical data, electrical measurements, meteorology, infrastructure state, geometry, and ideally independent stations.

The future experiment is obvious enough to describe and expensive enough not to exist at the necessary scale. Put calibrated low-light cameras, electric-field mills, magnetometers, air-ion counters, weather instruments, GNSS receivers, and seismic stations across a tectonically favorable region. Run them for years. Do not move the threshold after an earthquake. Keep the raw data. Invite hostile analysts.

The best anomaly experiment is designed by someone who wants the anomaly to survive criticism.

# 9. Cameras Arrive

Eyewitnesses are not obsolete because cameras exist. Cameras merely fail differently.

Security cameras can timestamp flashes. Dashcams can capture horizons. Smartphones can multiply coverage. But consumer sensors saturate, auto-expose, compress, roll their shutters, reflect light inside lenses, and record without calibrated spectral response. A bright frame is not automatically a bright sky.

Pisco matters because timing could be compared with seismic-wave passage. That moves the case from “people saw something” toward “a luminous event occurred at a measurable phase of the earthquake.” Yet that same timing weakens its value as prediction evidence. If the flashes track seismic waves, the mechanism may be coseismic.

This is a recurring irony. Better measurement can make an anomaly more real and less prophetic at the same time.

Science should welcome that outcome. The purpose of an instrument is not to protect the exciting interpretation. It is to reduce the number of interpretations that remain possible.

A camera network around future earthquakes could do more. Triangulation could estimate altitude. Multiple viewpoints could distinguish local electrical arcing from atmospheric luminosity. Spectra could separate line emissions from broadband sources. Power-grid telemetry could identify transformer events. Lightning networks could remove ordinary discharges.

The old literature asked, did the sky glow? The modern question can be: what emitted photons, where, when, at what spectrum, with what electrical and seismic context?

That is progress even if the answer is a transformer.

# 10. The Magnetometer Hears Everything

A magnetometer is an exquisitely sensitive device surrounded by civilization.

Railways, vehicles, pipelines, power systems, switching equipment, fences, storms, geomagnetic activity, and local electronics can all matter. The instrument does not know which source the researcher cares about. It records field variation.

This is why electromagnetic precursor claims are unusually exposed to contamination. A pulse appears. It is close in time to an earthquake. The graph looks dramatic. Then somebody asks whether a nearby station saw it, whether the pulse repeats on workdays, whether the amplitude scales with distance from a road, whether solar activity was elevated, whether the detector threshold was fixed beforehand.

The U.S. Geological Survey’s public position is unusually clear: electromagnetic variations after earthquakes are observed, but after decades of work there is no convincing evidence of electromagnetic precursors that enable reliable prediction.

That sentence should not be read as bureaucratic hostility to wonder. It is a challenge specification.

A convincing precursor has to defeat not only noise but the suspicion that analysts discovered the anomaly by looking backward from the known earthquake time. Continuous networks and blind testing are therefore more important than spectacular single events.

The magnetometer hears everything. The scientific task is to prove that one of those things was the fault preparing to break.

# 11. The Pulse Before Alum Rock

The 2007 magnitude 5.4 Alum Rock earthquake in California produced one of the cleaner examples of how a precursor claim should be attacked.

Researchers associated with QuakeFinder reported increases in ultra-low-frequency electromagnetic pulses before the earthquake. The claim was interesting because there was a nearby station and an event with a defined time and location. Years later, USGS-linked researchers re-examined the data and compared the QuakeFinder site with a Stanford-USGS station farther away.

They could reproduce an increase in pulse counts under definitions analogous to those used in the original work. That sounds like vindication until the next sentence: the number of pulses depended strongly on temporal and amplitude thresholds whose physical basis was unclear.

This is anomaly science in miniature.

The data are not imaginary. The pattern can be reproduced. The interpretation remains unstable because the detector itself contains choices. Change the threshold and the apparent precursor changes.

The reanalysis also investigated possible cultural and environmental sources. The lesson is not that every pulse came from a truck or a fence. It is that a precursor system cannot rely on an analyst choosing a favorable pulse definition after the event.

A useful warning device has to know what a pulse is before the earthquake does.

# 12. The Ionosphere After the Earthquake

Large earthquakes can reach space without any paranormal assistance.

The ground moves. The atmosphere above it is displaced. Acoustic and gravity waves propagate upward. In the ionosphere, changes in electron content can be detected by dense GNSS networks. Modern studies of events such as the January 1, 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake have resolved coseismic ionospheric disturbances minutes after rupture, including propagating acoustic signatures shaped by atmospheric winds and the geomagnetic field.

This is beautiful physics. It is also a trap for sloppy chronology.

If a satellite or GNSS receiver sees an ionospheric disturbance after a large earthquake, that observation demonstrates Earth-atmosphere-ionosphere coupling. It does not establish a precursor. The causal arrow is easy: rupture first, atmospheric wave second, ionospheric response third.

Pre-earthquake ionospheric claims are harder because the ionosphere is already alive. Solar flares, geomagnetic storms, tides, weather coupling, traveling ionospheric disturbances, day-night cycles, latitude, season, and instrument geometry all change electron density.

The existence of a strong post-earthquake pathway can psychologically subsidize a weaker pre-earthquake pathway. We know the Earth can disturb the ionosphere, so a disturbance before an earthquake feels like the same story running early.

It may be. It may not be.

Chronology is not a detail. It is the mechanism.

# 13. The Seduction of Before

After is physics. Before is prophecy.

That asymmetry changes how evidence is handled. A disturbance eight minutes after a magnitude 7.5 earthquake has an obvious anchor. A disturbance three days before it floats among thousands of other disturbances until the earthquake gives it a name.

Retrospective search is therefore the great seduction. Take a large earthquake. Open satellite, magnetic, thermal, radon, animal, atmospheric, and ionospheric datasets for the preceding month. Search many spatial windows, frequencies, smoothing methods, baselines, and thresholds. Something unusual will happen.

The more sensors civilization builds, the more guaranteed this becomes.

This does not mean precursors are impossible. It means discovery and validation must be separated. Use one earthquake to generate the hypothesis. Freeze the algorithm. Test it prospectively on the next hundred eligible periods. Count false alarms. Count misses. Publish failures.

The earthquake-prediction literature has often struggled because events are rare, instruments change, networks move, and researchers understandably want to learn from every large shock. But an algorithm that learns from the answer key while being graded is not a forecast system.

The future of this field depends less on finding stranger signals than on becoming crueler about time.

# 14. Satellites and Retrospective Miracles

Satellites make anomalies look authoritative because they see large areas and produce maps.

A colored patch appears over a future epicentral region. Total electron content is elevated. Infrared emission departs from a baseline. The shape looks centered. The date is close. The temptation is almost irresistible.

But orbital instruments introduce their own search space. Which pass? Which pixel radius? Which reference days? Which geomagnetic filter? Which local time? Which altitude model? Which statistical threshold? How many other patches appeared where no damaging earthquake followed?

A satellite precursor claim becomes persuasive only when its rules are specified before the target event and tested across enough non-events to estimate specificity.

This is especially important because space weather is global and structured. A geomagnetic disturbance can create regional patterns that happen to overlap tectonic zones. Human analysts are excellent at seeing geographic meaning in maps.

The proper comparison is not earthquake versus quiet-looking picture. It is forecast algorithm versus a baseline model over continuous time.

The satellite is not the problem. The retrospective miracle is.

# 15. The Prediction Problem

A reliable earthquake precursor would be one of the most valuable signals in geophysics. That fact creates both scientific motivation and social danger.

False alarms are not free. Evacuations move hospitals, schools, factories, transport systems, and frightened families. Repeated warnings that fail can destroy compliance. A system that catches half of damaging earthquakes but cries wolf every week may be worse than a hazard map and strict building code.

Misses are worse in a different way. A prediction system that appears scientific can create false reassurance when it remains quiet.

So performance has to be measured in decisions, not merely correlations. How much lead time? What geographic radius? What magnitude range? What probability increase? What false-alarm rate? What action should follow?

A weak but real physical effect may be scientifically profound and operationally useless. Remote sensing is full of such effects. Intelligence history is full of them too. Nature does not owe us a signal with the bandwidth and timing our institutions prefer.

The most mature conclusion may be that some pre-rupture electrical processes exist but are too heterogeneous, too local, too weak, or too nonspecific for deterministic warning.

That would not be failure. It would be an answer.

# 16. A Proper Precursor Experiment

Here is the experiment I want someone richer than me to run.

Choose several tectonic regions where the proposed mechanism predicts favorable geology. Install redundant seismic, magnetic, electric-field, atmospheric-ion, radon, meteorological, GNSS, low-light optical, infrared, and infrastructure-monitoring systems. Keep nearby control regions. Archive raw streams publicly after a fixed delay. Register candidate precursor algorithms before target earthquakes occur.

No hand-drawn anomalies. No moving windows after the fact.

Each algorithm emits a forecast object: time window, spatial region, magnitude threshold, confidence. Score it continuously for years. Let multiple teams submit competing algorithms against the same blind data. Include deliberately stupid baselines: historical seismicity alone, random alarms with matched frequency, seasonal models.

When an earthquake occurs, do not begin with the prettiest sensor. Begin with the frozen predictions.

This design is expensive because the null result requires patience. It is also the only result that can turn a fascinating mechanism into public safety.

The field does not need one more perfect retrospective plot. It needs an ugly prospective scorecard.

# 17. What the Skeptics Are Protecting

Skepticism has bad branding because it is usually shown as a man crossing his arms.

In earthquake prediction, skepticism protects something concrete: people from confident alarms that have not earned confidence.

The history of prediction contains enough failed methods, celebrated coincidences, animal stories, radon excursions, electromagnetic anomalies, and statistical mirages to justify a high bar. The USGS position that convincing electromagnetic precursors have not been demonstrated is not a declaration that rocks are electrically inert. It is a statement about evidence sufficient for prediction.

But skeptics can become lazy too. “No reliable prediction” can harden into “no interesting pre-rupture physics.” Those are different claims. Laboratory charge transport deserves replication. Well-documented luminous events deserve instrumentation. Coseismic electromagnetic and ionospheric effects are real research objects. Rare observations can be worth studying even when they never become alarms.

The productive skeptic does not protect orthodoxy. The productive skeptic protects the distinction between the thing observed and the thing inferred.

That distinction is the narrow bridge this entire subject has to cross.

# 18. The Rocks Turned On

The title of this book is deliberately stronger than its conclusion.

The rocks turned on. In laboratories, stressed rocks can generate and transport charge. During earthquakes, the solid Earth couples into electromagnetic and atmospheric systems in measurable ways. Some luminous reports near earthquakes are difficult enough to dismiss that they deserve better instruments rather than better jokes.

What has not turned on is the warning light.

That failure is more instructive than a clean debunking would be. If every earthquake-light report were a transformer and every electromagnetic anomaly a truck, the subject would be simple. If one sensor reliably rose before rupture, it would be simpler still. Instead we have the uncomfortable middle: mechanisms without a validated field chain, observations without clean denominators, correlations without operational specificity, and a planet whose background electrical behavior is richer than the signal we are trying to isolate.

There is a habit in frontier science of asking whether an anomaly is real. It is usually the wrong first question because reality has layers. A photon can be real while its attribution is wrong. A magnetic pulse can be real while its threshold is arbitrary. A laboratory carrier can be real while its tectonic importance is small. An ionospheric disturbance can be real while arriving eight minutes too late to predict anything.

The interesting work begins when we stop forcing all those realities into one verdict.

Maybe some earthquakes announce themselves electrically. If they do, the announcement has so far been written in a language we cannot reliably distinguish from the rest of the planet.

That is not mystical. It is an instrumentation problem, a statistics problem, a geology problem, and finally an epistemology problem.

Which is to say: it is a very good scientific problem.
