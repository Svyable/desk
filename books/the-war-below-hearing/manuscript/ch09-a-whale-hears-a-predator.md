# A Whale Hears a Predator

A beaked whale hunting in darkness makes itself audible on purpose.

That is the bargain of echolocation. The animal sends a click into black water because the returning echo reveals prey. The click also reveals the hunter to anything capable of listening. In an ocean containing killer whales, information is never one-way.

This tension helps explain why sonar research eventually moved from “how loud?” to “what does it mean?”

Beaked whales do not click continuously from surface to depth. Their foraging acoustics occupy a particular part of the dive. They descend quietly, begin echolocating in deeper water, hunt, then cease clicking before they return to the surface. Groups can show coordinated behavior. One proposed advantage is predator avoidance: acoustic silence and synchronized ascent can make it harder for killer whales to isolate a vulnerable target near the surface.

The evidence for every detail of this anti-predator story varies, but the broad evolutionary pressure is not fanciful. Killer whales prey on cetaceans. Deep-diving odontocetes have reason to manage when they advertise themselves acoustically.

Now insert a naval sonar pulse.

The pulse is not a killer-whale call. It is an engineered signal. Yet an animal's nervous system does not need to identify the source correctly for the source to trigger threat behavior. Evolution often favors broad rules when the cost of a false negative is death. An unfamiliar powerful sound that changes in level or direction may recruit an avoidance response even if it has no natural analogue.

This is where the phrase “sonar sounds like a predator” can mislead. The important similarity may be functional rather than acoustic.

A smoke detector does not sound like a fire. It sounds like something humans have learned to associate with fire. A gunshot and a shouted warning sound nothing alike, yet both can cause running. The nervous system classifies events by expected consequence, novelty and context, not by spectral resemblance alone.

Researchers have tested beaked whales with predator sounds partly for this reason. In the 2011 Tyack et al. study, tagged beaked whales were exposed to simulated military sonar, killer-whale calls and a control noise. Strong responses to both sonar and killer-whale stimuli suggested that the two could engage overlapping defensive behavior. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0017009.

The responses were striking because the whales often stopped the very behavior by which scientists could hear them.

A foraging beaked whale clicks. A disturbed beaked whale may go silent. On a passive acoustic range, silence can look like absence. The animal has not necessarily left immediately; it has become less observable.

This creates a measurement problem with an almost literary cruelty. The better the whale executes a silent avoidance strategy, the harder the acoustic observer must work to determine where it went.

Researchers solve this by combining channels. Seafloor hydrophones localize clicks while they exist. Tags record movement after clicking stops. Satellite tags carry the track beyond the short life of an archival tag. Visual teams provide species and individual identity. Controlled source telemetry supplies exposure. No single instrument tells the story.

The same is true inside the whale.

A predator response can include silence, increased travel, altered group spacing, a delayed return to feeding and modified dive timing. Those actions have energetic costs even if no decompression injury occurs. Deep foraging is expensive. Prey encounters are patchy. A whale that abandons a profitable prey layer after investing energy in descent cannot recover the lost opportunity instantly.

Repeated disturbance can therefore matter below the catastrophe threshold.

This is one reason population effects are hard to infer from strandings. A population can suffer years of small foraging costs without producing a beach full of carcasses. Conversely, a rare severe response can create a dramatic stranding without a detectable long-term population decline if the event is sufficiently infrequent.

The hazard has multiple scales.

Predator ecology also gives a clue to context effects. Animals do not respond identically to every hint of a predator. Distance matters. Direction matters. Whether the animal is feeding matters. Whether it has a calf matters. Prior experience matters. A killer whale heard far away may not trigger the same action as one approaching rapidly.

If sonar enters the same broad threat-decision machinery, a universal received-level threshold becomes biologically unlikely.

The DeRuiter study made this concrete. Two Cuvier's beaked whales strongly responded to controlled simulated sonar at received levels between 89 and 127 dB re 1 μPa, yet more distant operational sonar recorded at lower levels did not provoke the same response. One interpretation is simply level. Another is context: source distance, movement, predictability, waveform, location or the animal's state changed the meaning of the exposure.

Later studies increased sample size but did not turn the animal into a dosimeter.

The 2026 Southall study followed fifty-three Cuvier's beaked whales through controlled exposure trials and controls. The experimental scale allows statistical models of response across received level and range, but the paper remains fundamentally about behavioral probability, not one magic number. That is exactly what an anti-predator framework predicts.

A whale deciding whether to flee performs an implicit Bayesian problem without equations. How likely is danger? How costly is staying? How costly is leaving? What happened last time? Which direction offers safety? The output is behavior.

Humans perform versions of this constantly. We do not cross every street because an engine is audible. We do not ignore every engine because most cars do not hit us. Context transforms sound into risk.

The comparison should not anthropomorphize the whale. We do not know its subjective experience. But decision theory is useful because behavior evolved to trade false alarms against missed threats.

This creates an important prediction about habituation.

If a sonar signal is repeatedly encountered without a bad outcome, an animal may reduce its response. This is adaptive if the source is genuinely harmless in that context. It is dangerous to assume habituation means absence of effect, however. An animal may learn to tolerate disturbance while paying energetic costs, or a population may appear habituated because sensitive individuals left.

Researchers therefore distinguish within-individual habituation from population-level selection wherever possible.

The Southern California range is again valuable because identified whales can be followed across years. If known individuals with repeated exposure show changing response, habituation becomes more plausible. If the same tolerant individuals persist while others disappear, selection becomes possible. If responses remain strong despite exposure, neither simple story works.

Predator analogies also illuminate why certain geographic settings may be dangerous.

A whale escaping an actual killer whale may benefit from heading toward deep water, changing depth, grouping or going silent. A sonar source aboard a ship may create an acoustic field whose safest perceived direction leads toward shore or through a constricted channel. The animal's evolved response can be locally sensible and globally disastrous.

This is not unique to marine mammals. Moths evolved evasive maneuvers against bat ultrasound; artificial ultrasound can trigger them in contexts where the maneuver has no benefit. Nocturnal birds orient using cues that city lights can hijack. Sea turtle hatchlings move toward bright horizons that once reliably marked the sea and can now lead inland. Sensory ecology is full of environments in which old rules meet new signals.

The term for this family of problems is sometimes an ecological or evolutionary trap: a cue that historically correlated with fitness now drives maladaptive behavior in an altered environment.

It is tempting to label military sonar an acoustic evolutionary trap for beaked whales. That is an interesting synthesis, but the evidence is not yet strong enough to treat the label as fact. We do not know exactly what cue the whales are responding to or whether the response evolved specifically for killer-whale avoidance. The broader principle is safer: a novel anthropogenic signal can exploit a sensory-behavioral system whose output was shaped in a different acoustic world.

This framing has practical consequences for mitigation.

If risk is driven partly by novelty and perceived approach, ramp-up procedures might matter differently from abrupt onset. If animals can detect a gradually increasing source and leave before high exposure, ramp-up could reduce risk. But if low-level signals already trigger strong avoidance, ramp-up might begin disturbance earlier without guaranteeing a safer route. The effectiveness depends on behavior, not just acoustics.

Likewise, exclusion zones based on visual detection may fail if whales are submerged and silent. Passive acoustic monitoring may fail during the response phase. Geographic avoidance of high-density beaked-whale habitat can be more robust because it does not depend on detecting each animal in real time.

The Canary Islands moratorium is an example of that broader approach. Rather than attempting to spot every whale and manage exposure around each one, authorities removed relevant exercises from a known high-risk region. The apparent disappearance of recurrent mass strandings afterward is one reason place-based mitigation deserves attention.

Predator ecology also complicates experimental ethics.

A controlled exposure researcher wants a signal strong enough to elicit a measurable response but weak enough to avoid serious harm. If the mechanism of harm is behavioral, the very response being measured could theoretically create physiological risk. Experiments therefore use conservative escalation procedures, monitoring and shutdown criteria. This limits the ability to map the high-severity end of the response curve.

The result is a scientific asymmetry. We can measure where avoidance begins more safely than where avoidance becomes dangerous.

Historical strandings occupy that inaccessible tail.

This is why the connection between controlled studies and strandings must be reasoned rather than directly reproduced. Controlled exposures show that sonar can trigger the kinds of behavioral changes proposed in stranding mechanisms. Pathology shows that some stranded animals experienced gas/fat embolic injury. Natural experiments tie certain events to naval operations. No ethical study will deliberately connect all three in one living whale.

The predator frame is useful here because it gives a coherent reason for low-level responses. A direct injury mechanism expects greater physical dose to matter most. A threat response can begin when the signal is merely detectable and salient. The severity of the downstream consequence then depends on what the animal does.

This does not mean sound level stops mattering. A louder or closer signal may increase perceived threat, narrow choices and prolong response. It also increases the possibility of direct auditory effects. The two pathways can coexist.

The better model therefore has at least two curves: one for direct acoustic injury risk and one for behaviorally mediated risk. They overlap but need not share a threshold.

Regulation built around only the first curve will miss low-level strong responses. Regulation built around only the second could overpredict harm by treating every avoidance maneuver as dangerous. The scientific challenge is to map severity, duration and recovery.

A whale that stops clicking for ten minutes and resumes feeding is different from one that leaves a habitat for two days. A whale that extends a dive modestly is different from one whose altered sequence plausibly changes tissue gas dynamics. A group that shifts fifty meters is different from one driven toward a beach.

The signal begins in the ear, but the risk emerges in the choice architecture of the animal.

That is what the predator comparison reveals.

The sonar pulse is not dangerous merely because it is artificial. It is dangerous in some circumstances because evolution made sound actionable.

The ocean was already full of things worth listening to before warships arrived.
