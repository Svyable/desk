# The Whales That Stayed

A theory becomes more trustworthy when it survives the place where it ought to fail.

For the simple version of the sonar story, Southern California is that place.

Cuvier's beaked whales live on and around a U.S. Navy training range where mid-frequency active sonar is used repeatedly. The animals have been detected, photographed, tagged and followed across years. They forage there. Some individuals return. A long-term demographic study found a persistent local population rather than an empty acoustic desert.

If one begins with “naval sonar drives beaked whales out of habitat,” this is a problem.

It should be.

The most useful counterevidence does not make a thesis disappear. It forces the thesis to become more specific.

The Southern California Anti-submarine Warfare Range is instrumented with bottom hydrophones capable of detecting and localizing beaked-whale echolocation. Scientists have used that infrastructure to guide visual teams and build individual identification histories. Curtis and colleagues analyzed eleven years of photo-identification data, from 2007 through 2018, to estimate abundance, survival and trend for Cuvier's beaked whales using the range. Their 2021 *Marine Mammal Science* paper is at https://doi.org/10.1111/mms.12747.

The mean estimated annual number of animals was about 121, with broad uncertainty. Apparent annual survival was high. The estimated annual population trend centered near a slight decline, but the credibility interval was wide enough to include both a meaningful decline and increase. The authors emphasized that statistical power to detect abundance changes remained limited.

That is not a clean “no effect” result.

It is a clean warning against claiming population collapse where the data cannot show one.

The site also contains direct evidence of behavioral response. During actual sonar exercises, acoustic detections shifted toward the edges of the range, and tagged animals have moved away before returning. Controlled exposures have elicited strong avoidance and foraging disruption. The whales that stayed are not whales that failed to hear.

The interesting question is why persistence and response coexist.

One possibility is habituation. Animals repeatedly exposed to a stimulus without severe consequence can reduce their response over time. Habituation is common across taxa because constant false alarms are expensive. A whale living near a training range may learn, in whatever neurobiological sense applies, that familiar sonar patterns are not killer whales and usually do not require maximal escape.

The word “usually” matters.

Habituation can reduce acute response without making exposure costless. A whale might continue foraging under conditions that would make a naïve animal flee, yet still alter calling or fine-scale movement. The population could therefore persist while carrying small energetic costs.

A second possibility is selection.

Perhaps animals with strong responses spend less time on the range or leave permanently, while tolerant individuals remain. The observed population would then be biased toward animals that can live with sonar. This would look like habituation at the population level but has a different biological meaning.

Distinguishing the two requires individual histories.

If the same whale's response decreases across repeated exposures, habituation becomes more likely. If response remains a stable individual trait and the most reactive whales disappear from the study area, selection becomes plausible. Long-term photo-identification and tagging can begin to answer this, though sample sizes are demanding.

A third possibility is geography.

The range is offshore. Strong avoidance can carry a whale into other deep water rather than into a constricted island channel or beach. The same behavioral response that contributes to a stranding in the Bahamas or Canaries might merely displace an animal temporarily in Southern California.

This distinction is central to the book's thesis.

Behavioral response is not the same as catastrophic outcome. The environment transforms one into the other.

A fourth possibility is mitigation. Naval operations on modern ranges occur under environmental rules developed partly because of historical strandings. Lookouts, shutdown procedures, planning areas and species-specific restrictions can reduce the most severe exposures. The current range is not operationally equivalent to the circumstances of the 2000 Bahamas event.

A fifth possibility is that the severe mechanism itself is rarer than proponents sometimes imply. Most behavioral responses may never approach physiologically dangerous dive changes. Gas embolic injury could require an unusual sequence that occurs only in a small fraction of animals.

These explanations can all be partly true.

The Southern California population therefore acts as a control against overgeneralization. Any model that predicts routine mass mortality under ordinary range conditions is probably wrong. Any model that predicts no meaningful behavioral effect is also wrong.

The correct theory must fit both persistence and disruption.

This is exactly why long-term monitoring matters. Catastrophic strandings are emotionally and scientifically compelling, but they sample the tail of the distribution. A range population samples the center. To understand risk, we need both.

Imagine evaluating automobile safety only from fatal crashes. The resulting data would reveal important failure modes but tell us little about millions of uneventful journeys, near misses and adaptations drivers make. Conversely, evaluating only ordinary commutes would miss rare defects that kill people.

The beaked-whale literature spent its early years weighted toward crashes because beaches produced carcasses. Instrumented ranges added ordinary journeys.

That shift has moral consequences too.

If sonar disturbance is framed only in terms of strandings, then any mitigation that prevents beaching can be declared success. If repeated foraging disruption carries population costs, success requires a lower bar. The problem is that lower-level effects are harder to convert into policy because their consequences are uncertain.

A whale missing one meal is unlikely to matter. A lactating female repeatedly displaced from high-quality prey might. An individual leaving for two days may compensate. A population exposed to many small disturbances across years might change reproduction or habitat use in ways difficult to detect.

This is where population consequences of disturbance models enter. They attempt to link individual behavioral responses to changes in energy budget, health, survival and reproduction. The chain is long and assumption-heavy, but it is the only way to translate a tag trace into demographic risk without waiting decades.

The Southern California data can test those models.

If whales are predicted to incur large chronic energetic deficits, yet body condition, reproduction and survival remain stable, the model needs revision. If subtle demographic changes emerge despite apparent occupancy, simple persistence will no longer be reassuring.

The 2021 abundance study was appropriately cautious. Its inability to detect a strong trend is not proof of zero trend. The credibility interval itself is data about what remains unknown.

This is another place where public arguments mishandle uncertainty.

One side can cite the whales' persistence as proof that sonar is harmless. Another can cite wide uncertainty as if any decline is equally likely. Neither is justified. The posterior distribution constrains belief: dramatic population collapse is inconsistent with the observed continued presence and estimated abundance, while smaller long-term effects remain difficult to rule out.

The whales that stayed also offer a rare opportunity to study individual personality.

Behavioral ecology increasingly recognizes stable differences among individuals: boldness, exploration, sociality and response to disturbance. A few highly reactive whales could dominate controlled-exposure results if not modeled properly. Conversely, averaging across individuals can erase a vulnerable minority.

This has regulatory implications. A threshold protecting the median animal may fail the most sensitive. A threshold based on the most sensitive observed animal may impose enormous operational restrictions for little population benefit. The policy choice is partly normative: what fraction of animals, what severity of effect and what uncertainty should trigger action?

Science can quantify. It cannot decide the acceptable risk alone.

The Southern California range is also a reminder that institutions can create knowledge by being monitored over long periods. The Navy's operational presence made the question possible, but the fixed infrastructure allowed independent scientific interrogation that a transient commercial noise source might never receive.

There is a lesson here for environmental governance: instrument the controversial system.

If a technology cannot be eliminated, make its effects observable. Preserve source logs. Monitor sentinel species. Build long baselines. Fund individual identification. Require data standards that outlive one project. Design operations so natural experiments become analyzable rather than anecdotal.

The range did not solve the sonar problem.

It changed its resolution.

We can now say that Cuvier's beaked whales can persist in a frequently exposed habitat. We can also say that they alter behavior during sonar exposure. Some return after exercises. Some may be locally habituated. Population trend remains uncertain within measurable bounds.

Those facts prune the theory.

The hazard is conditional.

The whales that stayed made that impossible to avoid.
