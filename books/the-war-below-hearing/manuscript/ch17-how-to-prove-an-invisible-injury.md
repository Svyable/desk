# How to Prove an Invisible Injury

The ideal experiment does not exist.

Place a healthy beaked whale at a known depth. Measure blood gases, tissue nitrogen, heart rate, perfusion and bubble formation continuously. Expose the animal to a precisely characterized sonar field. Watch the behavioral response. Continue until a pathological threshold is crossed. Recover the animal immediately and perform a complete necropsy.

No ethical review board should approve that study.

The mechanistic question at the center of the sonar-stranding debate therefore has a structural gap. The transition from live behavior to lethal pathology cannot be reproduced deliberately in the species of interest.

This is not an excuse for permanent uncertainty.

It is a design constraint.

Many mature sciences work under similar constraints. Epidemiologists cannot randomly assign people to carcinogens. Aviation investigators cannot intentionally repeat fatal crashes with passengers. Climate scientists cannot run a second Earth. The solution is to combine evidence streams that constrain different parts of the causal chain.

The beaked-whale problem is unusually well suited to this approach because the streams are already visible.

Exposure can be measured through sonar telemetry and propagation modeling. Behavior can be measured with archival and satellite tags. Population presence can be monitored through passive acoustics. Pathology can be documented in opportunistic strandings. Physiology can be modeled from comparative anatomy and dive profiles. Geography can be reconstructed from bathymetry. Policy interventions can create natural experiments.

The challenge is to make those streams meet prospectively rather than only after a dramatic event.

A decisive modern research program would begin with preregistration.

Before exposure, researchers would specify competing hypotheses and the observations expected under each. A direct acoustic injury model predicts response related strongly to physical exposure metrics and perhaps auditory effects even without major behavior change. A behaviorally mediated decompression model predicts that the magnitude and structure of dive alteration should improve prediction beyond received level alone. A geography-mediated stranding model predicts interaction between avoidance direction and coastal/bathymetric configuration. A habituation model predicts lower response among repeatedly exposed individuals after controlling for other variables.

Then instrument the animal and environment as deeply as ethics allow.

High-resolution tags should record depth, acceleration, orientation and received sound. Satellite tags should follow post-exposure movement for days or weeks. Passive acoustic arrays should record both sonar and whale echolocation. Individual photo-identification should connect the trial to prior exposure history. Oceanographic profiles should characterize propagation. Bathymetry and source tracks should be preserved at enough resolution for independent modeling.

Add physiology where possible.

Direct blood sampling during a deep dive is unrealistic, but noninvasive proxies are improving. Cardiac rate can sometimes be inferred from sensors. Respiratory patterns after surfacing can be recorded. Drone photogrammetry can monitor body condition. Fecal, respiratory or skin samples may provide stress or health indicators over longer timescales. None measures nitrogen directly, but combined physiological data can falsify models that predict extreme stress where none is observed.

The gas model should be open.

Rather than one preferred compartment model, independent teams should run several published parameterizations on the same dive traces. Assumptions about lung collapse, perfusion and tissue kinetics should be explicit. The question is not whether one model can produce supersaturation. With enough parameters, many can. The question is whether realistic sonar-associated behavior consistently shifts predicted risk across plausible models.

Historical pathology should be reanalyzed with modern standards.

Where preserved tissues from important strandings exist, researchers could revisit them using current histological, imaging and molecular methods. Future strandings should receive rapid computed tomography before extensive manipulation whenever feasible, because imaging can help distinguish gas distribution before necropsy introduces artifacts. Gas composition should be sampled. Decomposition interval should be documented. Control carcasses from known non-sonar deaths should receive the same treatment.

The result would be a pathology baseline rather than a collection of exceptional cases.

Stranding response networks are critical here. A carcass found twelve hours earlier can be scientifically worth far more than one found three days later. Training, logistics and communication therefore become part of mechanism research.

The next piece is negative exposure data.

Scientists need well-characterized sonar exercises in beaked-whale habitat that do not produce severe effects. What distinguishes them from the historical strandings? Was received level lower? Were whales farther from shore? Was the population habituated? Was bathymetry different? Did animals respond behaviorally but recover?

Negative cases are not boring. They identify protective conditions.

The Canary Islands moratorium supplies an intervention negative: remove the exposure and the recurrent mass strandings stop. Southern California supplies operational negatives: repeated exposures occur without recurring mass strandings. The Marianas may supply an intermediate context. A unified database could compare them explicitly.

This requires military cooperation.

Exercise records should be preserved in a form that can eventually support environmental reconstruction even if tactical details remain classified initially. Time, location, source family and generalized waveform characteristics may be releasable sooner than exact system performance. Classification review should be built into the research pipeline so historical events do not remain opaque forever by inertia.

Independent oversight matters because the operator controls key data.

An ideal consortium would include the Navy, NOAA, academic researchers, veterinary pathologists, statisticians and external environmental scientists. Data access could use secure facilities for sensitive operational material while allowing independent analyses whose biological conclusions are publishable.

The experiment should also include behavioral controls unrelated to military sonar.

Killer-whale calls test predator-like response. Other anthropogenic sounds test whether the effect is specific to sonar or general novelty. Multibeam echosounders provide an instructive comparison because at least one study on the Southern California range did not find the same clear cessation of foraging associated with mid-frequency naval sonar; see https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0001385.

The point is discrimination.

If any novel sound produces the same dive response, the mechanism is broad disturbance. If military sonar uniquely produces it, waveform or learned association becomes more important. If killer-whale calls and sonar cluster together while controls do not, threat-response circuitry becomes more plausible.

Population effects require a different timescale.

Long-term photo-identification, passive acoustic occupancy, reproduction and survival should continue at heavily exposed and lightly exposed sites. The Southern California range can serve as one chronic-exposure population; quieter regions as comparison sites. Researchers should predefine the demographic effect sizes they could realistically detect.

This last point sounds technical and is often neglected.

A study that cannot detect a two-percent annual decline should not be cited as proof that no two-percent decline exists. Power belongs in the conclusion.

The 2021 Curtis et al. range study was exemplary in acknowledging limited power. Such candor prevents null results from acquiring stronger meaning in policy documents than they had in the paper.

Finally, define defeat conditions.

The behaviorally mediated injury hypothesis should lose credibility if well-characterized sonar exposures repeatedly produce the supposedly risky dive changes while sensitive physiological and long-term follow-up show no meaningful gas-related consequences, and if reanalysis of pathology demonstrates that observed gas is adequately explained by postmortem processes.

The habituation hypothesis should weaken if individual response does not decline with exposure history.

The geography hypothesis should weaken if severe strandings occur equally in open deep water without coastal constraint or if matched high-risk topographies show no association after correcting for search effort.

A strong direct acoustic-trauma model should weaken if auditory pathology does not track physical exposure and if severe events are better predicted by behavior than by acoustic dose.

These are real ways to be wrong.

That is what keeps the subject out of folklore.

The research program also needs a rule for testimony. Whale strandings attract witnesses: sailors, island residents, rescue workers, naval personnel. Their observations can identify times, sounds, ship movements and animal behavior. Testimony should be preserved and attributed but not upgraded automatically to instrumental evidence. A sailor saying sonar was active is useful; a ship log is stronger. A rescuer reporting bubbles is useful; CT and gas analysis are stronger.

The same hierarchy applies to institutional statements.

A Navy denial of causal certainty is not proof of innocence. An activist accusation is not proof of harm. A peer-reviewed model is not an observation. A regulatory authorization is not a scientific endorsement that every allowed exposure is safe.

Evidence classes keep the architecture clean.

The four used throughout this book can be applied directly to future cases.

Verified or documented: known sonar operations, measured exposures, tag traces, pathology, official policy, recorded strandings.

Credible but incomplete: physiological inferences from models, witness reports consistent with records, population effects within statistical uncertainty.

Disputed: specific causal attributions lacking full exposure or pathology, claims of direct acoustic bubble activation under field conditions.

Speculative synthesis: sonar exploiting an evolved anti-predator program in a way that creates an evolutionary trap, or individual acoustic experience producing population-level cultures of tolerance.

A research program built this way may never produce one photograph labeled “the mechanism.”

It can do something better.

It can make alternative explanations run out of room.

That is how invisible injuries become scientifically visible.
