# The Machine That Shouts Underwater

Active sonar begins with a problem humans created for themselves by becoming competent at hiding machines in water.

A submarine is difficult to find for reasons that are almost insultingly physical. Water absorbs electromagnetic radiation strongly enough that radar, the great revealer of twentieth-century skies, does not transfer neatly below the surface. Visible light disappears. Heat signatures blur into a large conductive medium. A submerged object can exploit depth, geography, ambient noise and sound-speed structure. The ocean is a better hiding place than empty air.

So navies learned to ask the ocean a question in sound.

Transmit an acoustic pulse. Wait. Listen for an echo whose delay and structure imply a target. Refine the waveform, source level, beam pattern and signal processing. Combine active sonar with passive listening, magnetic detection, intelligence, patrol aircraft, sonobuoys, towed arrays and undersea surveillance systems. Antisubmarine warfare becomes a contest between quieter machinery and better inference.

The whale enters this history not as an accidental metaphor but as another sophisticated user of sound. Toothed whales evolved active biological sonar long before navies. They transmit clicks and interpret returning energy. Beaked whales forage with echolocation at depth, producing signals with enough species-specific structure that passive acoustic researchers can often identify their presence even when no person sees the animals. That overlap—two active sensing systems in the same medium—makes the conflict unusually intimate.

It also makes public discussion of “sonar” frustratingly imprecise.

Sonar is not one sound. Military systems operate across frequency bands and tactical roles. A low-frequency system designed for long-range detection presents a different exposure than a mid-frequency hull-mounted antisubmarine sonar. A high-frequency mine-hunting system differs again. Pulse duration, repetition, bandwidth, source level, beam direction, vessel movement and environmental propagation all alter what reaches an animal. “Sonar was present” is not an exposure metric any more than “light was present” tells you whether someone encountered moonlight or a welding arc.

Mid-frequency active sonar became central to the beaked-whale controversy because several important strandings occurred near naval operations using systems in the kilohertz range. The Bahamas joint investigation examined tactical mid-range-frequency sonars. The 2002 Canary Islands event occurred during an international naval exercise involving mid-frequency sonar. Later controlled studies therefore used simulated military mid-frequency signals to ask how beaked whales responded under measured conditions.

The raw decibel numbers can be treacherous to non-specialists because underwater sound levels use reference quantities and measurement conventions that differ from familiar airborne acoustics. A level expressed in dB re 1 μPa underwater cannot be casually compared with a concert or jet engine quoted in dB re 20 μPa in air. Root-mean-square pressure, peak pressure and sound exposure level answer different questions. Source level is not received level. Range is not a fixed attenuator because propagation depends on the environment.

Those distinctions are often flattened exactly when politics becomes hottest.

One headline says sonar can exceed 200 decibels. Another notes behavioral responses around 100 or 140 dB. The numbers seem to imply absurd sensitivity until one remembers that they describe different places in the acoustic chain and different reference systems. A source may transmit at high level; the animal receives less after propagation. A biologically meaningful reaction can occur far below a level likely to cause immediate mechanical hearing injury. That gap is one of the reasons a behavioral mechanism became so important.

A sonar operator cares about propagation because a target echo can be lost or found depending on the water column. Temperature, salinity and pressure determine sound speed. Seasonal thermoclines bend rays. Deep sound channels can trap energy. Surface ducts can carry it. Bottom composition changes reverberation. Islands create shadows and complex reflections. Bathymetry can focus or constrain movement. In the Bahamas report, these were not generic caveats. They were part of the causal reconstruction.

The animal also cares about propagation, although not in the language of acoustic ray tracing.

A beaked whale hears a signal at a particular level, spectrum, direction and temporal pattern. It may hear reflections. The source may appear to move. The animal may be inside a steep-sided canyon or open basin. Its own echolocation gives it some representation of the terrain. The response is therefore to an event in a place, not to a decibel detached from geometry.

This seems obvious once stated, but regulation has to simplify.

Environmental impact analysis needs thresholds. How many animals are likely to experience behavioral disturbance? How many may incur temporary threshold shift or permanent auditory injury? How large an area is ensonified above a chosen criterion? How many days will training occur? These are administrative questions as much as biological ones, and they require models capable of producing countable outputs.

The current U.S. regulatory system makes that translation explicit. NOAA Fisheries issues Marine Mammal Protection Act authorizations for military readiness activities. For the Atlantic Fleet Training and Testing study area, the current seven-year regulations took effect 14 November 2025 and run through 13 November 2032. NOAA's page is at https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/action/incidental-take-authorization-military-readiness-activities-atlantic-fleet-training-and. The rule considers sonar, other acoustic sources, explosives, vessel strikes and multiple species, including several beaked whales. It distinguishes Level B harassment, which can include significant behavioral disruption or temporary hearing effects, from Level A harassment involving injury.

That bureaucratic vocabulary can sound bloodless until one realizes what it is trying to do: turn uncertain animal behavior into enforceable operating conditions before anyone knows exactly where every whale will be.

The problem becomes harder because detectability is poor. A mitigation rule may require observers to shut down or modify transmissions when marine mammals are seen within certain zones. But a beaked whale on a deep dive is not available to a person on a bridge. Visual detection probability depends on sea state, daylight, surfacing behavior and observer geometry. Passive acoustic monitoring helps when animals are clicking, but a disturbed beaked whale may stop echolocating—the very behavior later studies documented. In other words, the animal can become harder to detect when it begins responding.

This is one of several places where the military technology and biological problem loop into each other.

The most informative long-term beaked-whale studies have sometimes used Navy undersea ranges. A seafloor hydrophone array built to track acoustic events across a military test area can also detect foraging clicks from whales across hundreds or thousands of square kilometers. The Navy's instrumented ocean, created for warfare, becomes a scientific observatory.

The Southern California Anti-submarine Warfare Range has been especially valuable. Researchers can localize vocalizing Cuvier's beaked whales, track patterns during sonar exercises and guide visual or tagging teams toward animals that would otherwise be extremely difficult to find. A 2021 *Marine Mammal Science* paper by Curtis and colleagues estimated abundance, survival and trend for Cuvier's beaked whales using an eleven-year photo-identification program on a Navy sonar range. The paper, at https://doi.org/10.1111/mms.12747, estimated mean annual apparent survival around 0.95 and an annual population of roughly 121 animals on the range, while emphasizing wide uncertainty and limited power to detect abundance changes.

That result is not comfortable for either simple narrative.

If sonar inevitably makes a habitat unusable, a persistent local population on a frequently used range is awkward. If sonar is harmless because whales persist there, the strong individual behavioral responses, historical strandings and mitigation requirements are awkward. The interesting science lives in the awkwardness.

Animals can habituate to repeated nonlethal disturbance. They can alter space use. Sensitive individuals can leave while others remain. Population-level effects can be subtle enough to evade detection. A habitat can remain occupied while foraging costs change. Conversely, catastrophic strandings may require an unusual combination of sonar, bathymetry, novelty and animal behavior that a frequently exposed population does not usually encounter.

The source is therefore only one layer of the machine.

The operational environment matters. A multinational exercise near islands may generate a different acoustic geometry from recurring training on an instrumented offshore range. Multiple vessels may transmit. Animals may encounter approaching versus distant sources. Exercise tempo may change. The biological response may be driven partly by perceived source motion or by the impossibility of moving away along a preferred route.

The machine that “shouts underwater” is actually a distributed system: transmitter, waveform, platform, water column, seafloor, coastline, target problem, operator, doctrine. When environmental scientists ask what the whale heard, they are sampling one edge of that system.

This becomes politically inconvenient when sonar characteristics are classified or operationally sensitive. Scientific reproducibility prefers exact waveforms and source histories. Military security may limit them. Researchers can work with simulated signals whose relevant properties are known, but simulations raise their own question: does a whale react the same way to a controlled playback as to an operational warship conducting a realistic exercise?

The answer appears to be: not always.

DeRuiter et al.'s 2013 tagged-whale study found strong responses to controlled simulated sonar while more distant operational sonar did not elicit equivalent behavior in the same data set. The authors highlighted context as a possible moderator. Tyack et al. similarly compared controlled exposures and actual exercises. The 2026 Southall study expanded controlled-response measurements substantially. None of this means controlled experiments are artificial and therefore useless. It means that the biological system is interpreting more than pressure amplitude.

The point can be sharpened by comparison with other acoustic sources. In a 2020 *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* study, researchers examined the effect of 12 kHz multibeam echosounder mapping surveys on Cuvier's beaked-whale foraging at the Southern California range. They did not find the consistent cessation and departure pattern associated with mid-frequency active sonar studies. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0001385. Frequency alone does not explain that difference, and one should not overgeneralize from a specific survey, but the contrast reminds us that “anthropogenic sound” is as broad a category as “chemical.”

For the whales, the signal's meaning may depend on structure as much as energy.

For the Navy, signal structure carries tactical meaning too. Sonar waveforms are designed, selected and processed against target and environment. The same pulse can be powerful in one channel and disappointing in another. Operators train because the machine is not an automatic truth engine. It generates evidence that must be interpreted in context.

This parallel is worth holding onto. Both antisubmarine warfare and whale-response science are inference problems in the same noisy medium. A contact can be a submarine, biologic source, clutter or propagation artifact. A stranding can be caused by sonar, disease, navigational error, social cohesion, harmful algal toxins, weather, predation, or combinations. In both cases, isolated signatures are weak. Converging channels matter.

The strongest sonar-stranding cases are strong because the channels converge: timing, geography, known transmissions, characteristic species, pathological findings and later behavioral studies that make the proposed sequence plausible. Weak cases often possess only one or two of those lanes.

That distinction is essential because once an environmental hazard becomes culturally legible, it can become an all-purpose explanation. A whale strands near a naval region and sonar is blamed regardless of operational evidence. That is poor reasoning. The inverse mistake—demanding a perfect mechanistic autopsy before treating repeated associations as meaningful—is equally poor.

The machine deserves neither demonology nor immunity.

It deserves characterization.

What waveform? What source? What track? What propagation? What received exposure? What species? What dive state? What response? What lesion? What alternatives?

Those questions turn “sonar” back into an object science can work with.

They also expose the central asymmetry of this story. The machine was engineered for a known purpose and can be instrumented exhaustively. The whale was engineered by evolution for a different acoustic contest and spends most of its life outside ours.

When the two systems meet, the easier one to measure is not necessarily the one we understand better.
