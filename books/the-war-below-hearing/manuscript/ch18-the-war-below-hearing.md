# The War Below Hearing

The whales did not teach us that sonar is evil.

They taught us that a technology can be physically understood and biologically surprising at the same time.

Nothing about mid-frequency active sonar requires exotic physics. It is engineered sound propagating through seawater. The naval purpose is ordinary by military standards: find submerged objects that are difficult to detect by other means. The biological surprise came from the receiving system.

A beaked whale is not a hydrophone.

It hears, classifies, decides, moves, changes blood flow, spends oxygen, carries dissolved gas, hunts, avoids predators and navigates topography. The same acoustic pulse that a physicist describes with pressure and frequency enters an animal already halfway through a two-hour physiological event.

That is where the hazard lives.

The public story often begins with dead whales because death is visible. The scientific story became stronger when researchers learned to study survivors.

The 2000 Bahamas mass stranding produced an official joint Navy–NMFS conclusion that tactical mid-frequency sonar was the most plausible sound source involved, acting with environmental factors including propagation, bathymetry and constrained egress. The 2002 Canary Islands event added pathology: gas bubble-associated lesions and fat embolism in deep-diving whales stranded hours after naval sonar began. The Canary moratorium added an intervention: recurrent mass strandings stopped after relevant exercises were prohibited locally. Controlled exposures then added behavior: whales stopped clicking, altered movement, extended dives and abandoned foraging. Navy ranges added longitudinal context: some Cuvier's beaked whales persist under chronic exposure. The Marianas added a quieter, less frequently exposed comparison. The 2026 controlled-exposure work expanded direct observation to fifty-three animals.

This is not one piece of evidence repeated six times.

It is six kinds of evidence with different failure modes.

The joint report could misattribute a source but had access to operational data. Pathology can be confounded by stranding and decomposition but sees tissues no tag can see. A moratorium can coincide with other changes but tests the exposure at landscape scale. Controlled studies can be artificial but measure behavior directly. Range studies can miss small demographic effects but show persistence. Cross-site comparisons can be confounded but test context.

The convergence is the point.

What survives that convergence is narrower than some advocacy claims and stronger than some skeptical ones.

Military sonar can trigger strong behavioral responses in beaked whales at received levels below those associated with direct auditory injury. Some historically important mass strandings are closely associated with sonar operations. At least one major stranding series produced gas/fat embolic pathology compatible with disruption of diving physiology. Severe outcomes appear to depend on context, including geography and probably exposure history. Not every sonar exposure is dangerous in the same way. Not every beaked whale responds identically. The exact sound-to-bubble pathway remains incompletely demonstrated.

That is enough to change operations.

It is not enough to stop asking questions.

This middle position can feel unsatisfying because controversy rewards absolutes. “Sonar kills whales” fits on a sign. “There is no conclusive proof” fits in a legal brief. The actual science says that causation is distributed across an acoustic source, an animal's perception, its behavior, its physiology and the geography around it.

Distributed causation is harder to narrate.

It is also common.

A heat wave kills through temperature, housing, age, hydration, medication and social isolation. A wildfire disaster involves ignition, fuel, wind, building design, evacuation and infrastructure. A cyberattack succeeds through software vulnerability, credentials, network architecture and human response. No one serious insists that because several conditions are required, the initiating hazard is unreal.

The beaked-whale case belongs to this family.

Sonar may be the initiating disturbance without being the immediate physical cause of every lesion. The whale's own evolved behavior can become the transmission mechanism.

This is the book's central claim, and it has consequences beyond marine mammals.

Environmental regulation often separates “behavioral effects” from “injury” as if behavior were soft and injury hard. That distinction is useful administratively but false biologically when behavior controls exposure to physical risk. An animal displaced from food can starve. A bird attracted by light can collide. A turtle following a false horizon can crawl inland. A whale changing its dive can alter gas kinetics.

Sensory ecology belongs inside toxicology whenever information changes survival.

This is a frontier that military technology repeatedly exposes because military systems are designed to manipulate or exploit sensing. Sonar, radar, jamming, decoys and directed signals operate in perceptual environments. Their biological effects can therefore arrive through interpretation as well as energy deposition.

The right question is not only “how much reached the tissue?”

It is “what did the organism do next?”

The sonar story also offers a lesson about secrecy that is almost opposite the one people expect.

Yes, military classification complicates environmental science. Exact operational parameters can be withheld. Exercise records can be incomplete. Outside researchers depend on access. But the hardest causal gaps in this case are not simply hidden in classified files. They exist because a living whale cannot be instrumented internally at three kilometers depth without changing the problem, because fatal experiments are unethical, and because rare events resist statistics.

Secrecy matters.

Nature matters more.

This distinction is useful in every domain where classified technology meets anomaly. An unexplained effect near military activity does not become evidence of a secret exotic capability by default. First exhaust the known system, its known physics and the biology or environment receiving it. The beaked-whale case became extraordinary without requiring a secret weapon. Ordinary sonar met extraordinary physiology.

The story also complicates suspicion around state-funded science.

The Navy funded and enabled much of the research that documented the Navy's own environmental effects. Instrumented ranges built for antisubmarine warfare became whale observatories. This does not erase conflicts of interest. It shows that the correct response to institutional entanglement is transparency, independent methods and adversarial review—not automatic dismissal.

A mature system should make uncomfortable findings easier to produce.

The current research trajectory points that way. Larger controlled-exposure samples. Long-term individual identification. Passive acoustic monitoring. Better pathology protocols. More explicit response functions. Regulations that distinguish hearing injury from behavior. Place-based mitigation where historical risk is clear.

The remaining gap is integration.

We still need exposure histories tied to individual whales over years. We need better gas-kinetic models validated against real marine mammal physiology. We need rapid postmortem imaging and gas chemistry from fresh strandings. We need cross-site comparisons designed before events occur. We need to know whether habituation protects animals, conceals chronic cost or selects for tolerant residents. We need to quantify how often a dramatic avoidance response crosses from expensive to dangerous.

The decisive study may never be one study.

It may be a network whose components interlock tightly enough that no single missing measurement can carry all the uncertainty.

That would be appropriate for an animal whose life is itself a network of adaptations.

A beaked whale survives depth by coordinating lungs, blood, muscle, behavior and time. The military hazard becomes intelligible only when science coordinates acoustics, ecology, pathology, physiology and policy with similar care.

The analogy should end there. Whales are not systems diagrams.

They are animals living in an ocean humans have made strategically loud.

That ocean contains other changes too: shipping, seismic surveys, construction, mapping sonars, explosions and climate-driven shifts in prey. Military sonar occupies one slice of an expanding acoustic environment. Understanding its mechanism matters partly because it provides a well-documented control case for how anthropogenic sound can interact with behavior in ways simple noise metrics miss.

It also shows what successful mitigation can look like.

The Canary Islands did not wait for a perfect mechanism. Authorities changed operations in a place with a repeated severe outcome, and the characteristic mass strandings stopped. That does not prove every detail. It demonstrates that uncertainty and action are not opposites.

The better question is whether an intervention targets a causal link strong enough to justify its cost.

In the Canaries, it appears to have.

Elsewhere, the answer may be different. On a heavily instrumented offshore range, monitoring and operational controls may reduce risk sufficiently while training continues. In quiet island habitat with naïve animals, more conservative planning may be warranted. One policy does not need to fit every ocean.

This is what contextual science earns: contextual management.

The beaked whales forced us away from a fantasy in which environmental safety can be defined by one acoustic number. They made us watch behavior. They made pathologists look for bubbles. They made physiologists revisit decompression. They made naval ranges into biological observatories. They made regulators admit that an animal can be disturbed far below a level expected to injure its ear.

Most of all, they made causation move.

The injury was no longer a pulse traveling from ship to tissue in one straight line.

It became a sequence.

A sound in dark water.

A whale stops clicking.

The dive changes.

The body follows.

Sometimes the coastline is waiting.
