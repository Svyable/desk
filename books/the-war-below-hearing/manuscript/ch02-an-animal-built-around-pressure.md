# An Animal Built Around Pressure

Cuvier's beaked whale looks, from a distance, almost disappointingly ordinary. A gray-brown body, a small falcate dorsal fin set well back, a short beak that can be easy to miss, scars accumulating on adults. Nothing in the silhouette advertises the physiological problem hidden underneath it.

Then the animal dives.

The modern record includes dives beyond two thousand meters and durations beyond two hours. In 2014, Gregory Schorr and colleagues reported satellite-linked records from Cuvier's beaked whales off Southern California that included a dive to 2,992 meters and one lasting 137.5 minutes, then the deepest and longest directly measured dives for any mammal. Later observations pushed duration even farther. Numbers like these encourage a familiar style of wonder: deepest, longest, record-breaking. They are more useful if treated as boundary conditions.

At three thousand meters, hydrostatic pressure is roughly three hundred atmospheres. A lungful of gas taken at the surface does not remain a lungful in the intuitive sense as the whale descends. Gas volumes contract. Pressure gradients shift. Nitrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide move through compartments whose blood flow is being actively regulated. Alveoli eventually collapse, a feature long understood in diving mammals as potentially protective because collapse can limit further gas exchange at depth. The animal reduces peripheral circulation, slows some functions, preserves others, and turns stored oxygen in blood and muscle into a carefully managed account.

A beaked whale is not merely tolerant of pressure. It is a moving solution to pressure.

That distinction matters for sonar because a solution can be disrupted without any tissue being directly struck hard enough to look “injured” at the moment of exposure.

The older textbook image of marine mammal diving physiology was reassuring. Deep-diving cetaceans collapse the lungs, shunt blood, carry high concentrations of myoglobin in muscle, and therefore avoid the decompression problems familiar to human divers. Human scuba divers breathe compressed gas at depth; that practice loads tissues with nitrogen, and rapid ascent can allow dissolved gas to come out of solution as bubbles. Whales, by contrast, breathe at the surface and hold their breath. Once their lungs collapse sufficiently, gas exchange should diminish. Nature had solved the bends.

The broad logic remains real, but reality has been less categorical.

Evidence accumulated that marine mammals can carry gas burdens and even exhibit gas bubbles or lesions compatible with dysbaric processes under some circumstances. Work on stranded cetaceans found chronic lesions suggestive of gas embolic phenomena. Researchers began modeling nitrogen uptake during repetitive dives rather than assuming it disappeared as a problem. The important shift was not “whales get the bends like scuba divers.” They do not share the same exposure profile, anatomy or behavior. The shift was that diving physiology had margins, histories and failure modes.

A dive is not one descent followed by one ascent. It is part of a sequence.

Beaked whales forage repeatedly. A deep foraging dive can be followed by a series of shallower dives and surface intervals before the next deep excursion. These patterns affect gas exchange and recovery. Blood flow changes affect which tissues receive dissolved nitrogen. Exercise affects perfusion. The rate and shape of ascent affect pressure transitions. If an animal changes its behavior abruptly, it changes the physiological trajectory that would otherwise have unfolded.

This is the bridge sonar researchers had to cross after the Canary Islands pathology. Gas and fat emboli could not simply be assumed to prove decompression sickness, because gas can arise after death and because stranding itself causes damage. Nor could the presence of deep-diving adaptations be used to rule out gas-mediated injury by definition. The causal question became specific: could a sonar-evoked behavioral response alter dive patterns enough to push a beaked whale into a gas-management regime associated with injury?

Several mechanisms were proposed.

One was behavioral. A whale might interrupt a deep dive, alter ascent rate, spend a different amount of time at depth, change exertion, or perform an unusual series of dives. Any of those changes could alter tissue nitrogen dynamics. Another proposal involved direct acoustic effects on pre-existing gas nuclei, although the physical plausibility and required exposure conditions have been debated. A third possibility was interaction: tissue supersaturation produced by diving might make small gas nuclei more susceptible to acoustic or mechanical growth, while behavior simultaneously changes the pressure schedule.

The reason this became a serious scientific problem is that the lesions from the 2002 Canary Islands event were not abstract. Antonio Fernández and colleagues described severe congestion and hemorrhage, gas bubble-associated lesions and fat embolism in vital organs after fourteen beaked whales stranded during the Neo-Tapon naval exercise. Their 2005 paper, “Gas and Fat Embolic Syndrome Involving a Mass Stranding of Beaked Whales (Family Ziphiidae) Exposed to Anthropogenic Sonar Signals,” is at https://doi.org/10.1354/vp.42-4-446. They proposed that modified diving behavior could have produced nitrogen supersaturation beyond normally tolerated thresholds, that sound might affect bubble precursors in supersaturated tissue, or that both processes could operate together.

The wording is worth preserving. “Could.” “May.” “Alternatively.”

Later summaries sometimes turn the paper into a definitive autopsy of sonar-induced decompression sickness. It was not. It identified a pathological syndrome and proposed plausible pathways in the context of a remarkably suggestive exposure event. The exact mechanistic bridge remained open.

This is where biological adaptation creates an epistemic trap. An extreme diver can be both extraordinarily well adapted and unusually vulnerable to a certain kind of disruption. Those propositions sound contradictory only if adaptation is imagined as armor.

Consider a high-performance aircraft. Its flight-control system can hold an unstable configuration precisely because sensors, software and actuators continuously make corrections. The machine is capable because regulation is active. Interfere with the regulatory system and the same design that enabled exceptional performance can narrow the margin for error. The analogy should not be pushed too far—whales are not aircraft—but the useful point survives. A physiological feat can depend on behavior and control rather than brute structural immunity.

Deep diving requires a sequence of decisions the animal normally controls. When to begin descent. How quickly to travel. When to start echolocating. How long to forage. When to ascend. Whether to execute recovery dives. How to respond to predators and conspecifics. Evolution has tuned the physiology around distributions of those behaviors, not around every behavior mathematically possible.

An intense or biologically salient sound may matter because it changes the distribution.

The 2011 PLOS ONE paper by Peter Tyack and colleagues, “Beaked whales respond to simulated and actual navy sonar,” became important for exactly this reason. The team used tags and a naval testing range equipped with a large hydrophone array. In controlled playbacks, beaked whales stopped echolocating and showed unusual ascent and avoidance behavior at received levels below 142 dB re 1 μPa. During actual sonar exercises, beaked whales were detected more toward the range periphery and later returned toward the center after exercises ceased. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0017009.

Those observations do not show bubbles forming. They show the missing behavioral link becoming measurable.

Two years later, DeRuiter and colleagues reported direct high-resolution tag measurements from Cuvier's beaked whales exposed to simulated mid-frequency active sonar. The responses included stopping echolocation, rapid silent swimming away from the source, extended dives and prolonged periods without foraging. Again, behavior changed before anyone could claim direct tissue injury.

Then, in 2026, Brandon Southall and colleagues published controlled observations involving fifty-three Cuvier's beaked whales, combining long-duration satellite tags and high-resolution archival tags across experimental trials with simulated military sonar. The study, “Behavioral responses of goose-beaked whales (*Ziphius cavirostris*) to simulated military sonar,” appeared in *Ecosphere* and is available at https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.70501. Animals were exposed at modeled horizontal ranges from roughly two kilometers to more than two hundred, with received levels up to about 142 dB re 1 μPa RMS. The value of the study lies less in one magic threshold than in its scale and its ability to examine movement and diving responses under controlled conditions.

The larger the behavioral data set became, the harder it was to keep imagining sonar as a simple acoustic toxicant.

Different animals did different things. Context altered response. Distance did not translate to effect through one monotonic rule. Prior exposure and local soundscape could plausibly matter. Some populations lived on active naval ranges without producing constant strandings. An animal could respond to a relatively modest controlled signal and ignore a more distant operational one. The physical dose remained necessary to describe the exposure, but it was not sufficient to describe the biological meaning.

There is a temptation here to say that “fear” is the missing mechanism. Killer whales are predators of many cetaceans, and experiments using killer-whale calls can provoke strong anti-predator responses in beaked whales. Naval sonar may share acoustic features that cause it to be treated as a threat or may simply be novel and aversive. But “the whale thinks sonar is a killer whale” is cleaner than the evidence. The response could involve threat generalization, startle, disturbance, learned associations, spatial context or other perceptual mechanisms. The animal's internal category is not directly available to us.

What is available is action.

Stop clicking. Leave the area. Change ascent. Extend a dive. Delay foraging.

Those actions carry physiological costs independent of whether the whale is consciously afraid in any human sense.

The pressure problem also explains why beaked whales matter disproportionately. Other cetaceans hear sonar. Other cetaceans respond to vessels and noise. Yet repeated mass-stranding associations have been concentrated in certain deep-diving species. If simple ear damage were the whole story, susceptibility should map mainly onto hearing sensitivity and exposure. If behavior coupled to dive physiology matters, then deep, long-duration, repetitive divers become a special risk class.

That does not mean every beaked whale exposed to sonar approaches a decompression threshold. The range of normal diving behavior is enormous, and the physiological models carry assumptions about perfusion, lung collapse, tissue compartments and gas solubility that are difficult to validate in free-ranging whales. One cannot insert a tag-derived depth trace into a human diving table and diagnose an invisible animal.

The useful scientific move is to identify where the causal chain can be tested.

First, exposure must be reconstructed. What sound reached the animal, across frequency, time and direction? Second, behavior must be measured. How did the dive deviate from baseline? Third, physiology must be modeled and, where possible, measured with proxies or opportunistic samples. Did the altered dive materially change predicted gas burden? Fourth, pathology must be distinguished from postmortem artifact. Fifth, geography must be included because a behavior that is safe in open water may be dangerous near shore or in constricted channels. Sixth, prior exposure should be tracked because a familiar signal and a novel signal may not have equivalent biological meaning.

No one study can do all six easily.

That is why the historical strandings and modern experiments complement one another. Strandings offer pathology but weak behavioral reconstruction. Tags offer behavior but rarely pathology. Range hydrophones offer population-scale presence and vocal activity but imperfect individual identity. Regulations offer modeled exposure counts but not necessarily the causal detail of a rare catastrophic event. Controlled experiments improve inference but deliberately avoid the conditions likely to cause serious harm.

Ethics creates a ceiling on mechanistic proof.

If a researcher genuinely believed that a certain sonar exposure plus dive state could produce fatal gas embolism, the obvious randomized experiment would be unacceptable. We therefore learn from a patchwork: natural experiments, accidents, nonlethal controlled exposures, pathological cases, comparative physiology and models.

This is not unusual in environmental health. We do not demand randomized lethal exposure to prove every industrial hazard. But the absence of a single decisive experiment creates room for rhetoric. One side can insist that mechanism is not proven. Another can treat temporal association as complete proof. The responsible position sits between them and keeps asking which links have become strong enough to act on.

By now, one such link is strong: beaked whales can alter their diving and foraging behavior substantially in response to mid-frequency active sonar. Another is strong: some mass-stranding events have occurred in close temporal and geographic association with naval sonar. Another is strong: gas and fat embolic lesions have been documented in at least some relevant stranded animals. The exact mapping among exposure, behavioral response, gas dynamics, injury and beaching remains conditional.

The animal's exceptional diving ability does not make that conditionality surprising. It makes it expected.

Pressure is not one insult encountered at maximum depth. It is a moving constraint managed across time. The whale's body does not merely withstand the ocean. It schedules the ocean.

A sonar pulse enters that schedule as information.

And information can make physiology move.
