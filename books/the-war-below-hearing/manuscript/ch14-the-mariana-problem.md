# The Mariana Problem

The Mariana Archipelago presents nearly the inverse of Southern California.

The islands sit in deep Pacific water where beaked whales are present regularly, but naval sonar exposure is less routine in some monitored areas. Ambient noise can be low. Strandings are hard to detect because many shorelines are remote and response networks are sparse compared with heavily populated coasts.

This combination makes the region scientifically uncomfortable.

NOAA researchers examining stranding records and passive acoustic data concluded that some beaked-whale strandings in the Mariana Archipelago may be associated with sonar. The agency summarized the work in a feature published in 2020 and updated in 2024: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/beaked-whale-strandings-mariana-archipelago-may-be-associated-sonar.

The summary highlighted a hypothesis that deserves more attention than it receives: infrequent sonar use against a quiet acoustic background may produce stronger behavioral responses than the same kind of activity in populations exposed repeatedly.

This is the novelty problem made geographical.

Passive acoustic monitoring found beaked whales in a high fraction of monitored weeks at some Mariana sites. In other words, the animals were not occasional visitors accidentally caught near exercises. The habitat mattered to them. Yet because visual detection is difficult and beaches are remote, the baseline stranding rate is uncertain.

A single carcass carries more ambiguity when the denominator is poorly known.

The region therefore forces causal analysis to work without the abundance of evidence available in the Canary Islands. There may be no rapid multi-animal necropsy. Operational records can be incomplete publicly. A stranded whale may be found after decomposition. The exact time of death may be uncertain. This makes the case weaker at the event level.

At the same time, the comparative context is valuable.

If beaked whales on the Southern California range have extensive prior exposure and remain present, while whales in quieter regions react more severely to intermittent sonar, habituation becomes a testable explanation. The contrast does not prove it. It creates a prediction.

The prediction is straightforward: controlling for received level and other measurable variables, animals with greater prior exposure should on average show lower response probability or severity if habituation is operating.

Testing this requires individual exposure histories, which are difficult across ocean basins. Researchers can use location as a rough proxy, but that risks confounding habituation with genetics, prey, habitat, age structure and operational differences. Better studies would tag identified individuals and follow them through repeated exposures over time.

The Mariana problem also reveals a hidden assumption inside many environmental regulations: that the same species behaves similarly across its range.

Species-level thresholds are administratively convenient. Cuvier's beaked whales are grouped because they share anatomy and hearing characteristics. Yet culture and experience can vary among populations. Cetaceans are long-lived, social and capable of learning. A population that has grown up hearing sonar may not be behaviorally equivalent to one that has not.

This is not an argument for weaker protection in habituated populations.

Habituation can conceal cost. Animals may learn to tolerate a disturbance because leaving is worse. A population can remain in essential habitat despite repeated disruption. The absence of overt avoidance may mean the signal has become familiar, not harmless.

Nor is novelty automatically dangerous.

A naïve animal can hear a new signal and ignore it. The key question is how novelty interacts with perceived threat, source motion and behavioral state.

The Mariana Archipelago also makes detection bias impossible to ignore.

Imagine two regions with identical true stranding rates. One has populated beaches, trained responders, veterinary facilities and routine aerial survey. The other has remote islands, military access restrictions and little shoreline coverage. The documented rate can differ by an order of magnitude without any biological difference.

Historical databases need correction for this.

A rigorous sonar-stranding analysis should include coastline search effort, population density, carcass persistence, currents, scavenging and reporting infrastructure. Otherwise a map of strandings partly becomes a map of people.

The same is true underwater. Passive acoustic monitoring changes apparent whale abundance dramatically. NOAA's Mariana work found consistent beaked-whale presence because hydrophones listened when humans were absent. Before those instruments, the animals could be classified as rare simply because they were rarely seen.

This has consequences for environmental impact assessment.

If beaked whales are present in 80 or 90 percent of monitored weeks, an exercise planner cannot treat a lack of visual sightings as evidence of absence. Habitat-level probability becomes more informative than day-of-operation observation.

The Mariana case therefore supports a shift from reactive mitigation to anticipatory design.

Reactive mitigation says: transmit unless an animal is seen, then modify operations. Anticipatory design says: identify high-use habitat in advance, estimate acoustic exposure under realistic propagation, and alter location or timing before the ship arrives.

The second approach is less dependent on the whale revealing itself.

It is also more compatible with the behavioral mechanism. A whale that responds by going silent can defeat the monitoring system precisely when risk increases. Habitat protection does not care whether the animal is clicking at that moment.

The challenge is operational scale.

Navies require large training areas because antisubmarine warfare is spatial. Ships, aircraft, submarines and unmanned systems maneuver over hundreds or thousands of square kilometers. Simply drawing circles around every acoustic detection can make realistic training impossible. Environmental planning therefore has to identify which areas and seasons produce disproportionate risk rather than treating the entire ocean as equal.

This is where beaked-whale habitat models matter.

Species distributions can be associated with depth, slope, seamounts, canyons and prey conditions. The 2026 identification of ginkgo-toothed beaked whales off Baja California is a reminder of how incomplete even basic species knowledge remains. Researchers combined sightings, genetics, environmental DNA and acoustic signatures to definitively link a previously recorded click type with *Mesoplodon ginkgodens*. The open-access paper is at https://doi.org/10.1111/mms.70052.

In 2026, we are still discovering how to recognize some beaked whales at sea.

That fact should discipline confidence in older impact assessments.

A sonar operator in the 1980s could have transmitted through important beaked-whale habitat without anyone knowing which species was present. Historical absence of documented response is therefore weak evidence of biological absence.

The Mariana problem is, in part, an observability problem inherited from this history.

It also touches the politics of Pacific military expansion. Training ranges, missile defense, submarine operations and strategic competition give the region growing defense significance. Environmental review therefore occurs under pressure from both conservation law and geopolitical planning.

That pressure creates incentives to turn uncertainty into advocacy.

Environmental groups may emphasize worst-case strandings because rare species and military expansion make precaution attractive. Military documents may emphasize modeled negligible population effects because training requirements are substantial. Local communities may care about whales, fisheries, sovereignty and military employment in combinations that do not fit mainland narratives.

The only reliable escape is transparent evidence classification.

Documented: beaked whales use Mariana habitat regularly; sonar operates in the region; some strandings have temporal associations that researchers consider concerning. Credible but incomplete: infrequent exposure and low ambient noise may increase response severity. Disputed or unresolved: causal attribution of specific carcasses where exposure and pathology are incomplete. Speculative: population-specific acoustic cultures create materially different sonar vulnerability.

That four-part separation keeps the interesting hypothesis alive without laundering it into fact.

The Marianas also give the book a useful geographical progression.

Bahamas: a mass stranding with an official joint investigation and constrained channels.

Canary Islands: repeated events, strong pathology and a moratorium followed by no recurrence.

Southern California: a heavily instrumented range with behavioral responses and a persistent population.

Marianas: important habitat, less frequent exposure, sparse detection and concern about potentially stronger reactions.

No single site is representative.

Together they create a comparative science.

This is how the sonar question should mature: away from “does sonar affect whales?” and toward “which animals, under which acoustic and geographic conditions, respond in which ways, and which responses become consequential?”

The Mariana problem is valuable because the evidence is incomplete in exactly the dimensions that matter.

Quiet water.

Rare exposure.

Invisible animals.

Sparse beaches.

A strategically loud future.
