# The War Below Hearing

**Beaked Whales, Naval Sonar, and the Biology of Invisible Warfare**

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a particular kind of mistake that arrives wearing the costume of common sense. Sound hurts an animal, therefore the sound must have damaged its ear. A whale beaches after sonar, therefore the sonar must have struck it like an acoustic hammer. If the ear looks intact, perhaps the sound was innocent.

The beaked-whale story is harder than that, and much more interesting.

In March 2000, whales began stranding in the northern Bahamas while U.S. Navy ships were using tactical mid-frequency active sonar. The joint Navy–National Marine Fisheries Service investigation eventually called those sonars the most plausible sound source involved, while refusing to pretend that sound alone explained the event. Bathymetry, propagation, a constricted channel and an animal apparently unusually sensitive to those frequencies all mattered. Two years later, fourteen beaked whales stranded in the Canary Islands during an international naval exercise. Necropsies found hemorrhage, gas bubbles and fat emboli. The lesions looked less like a blown speaker and more like an animal whose exquisitely managed relationship with pressure had been knocked out of its normal operating regime.

That changed the question. The relevant unit was no longer merely sound pressure at an ear. It was a whole diving animal, carrying dissolved gas through repeated kilometer-scale excursions, deciding when to stop clicking, when to ascend, how fast to travel, where to surface, whether to forage, and whether to flee. Military sonar could matter by changing behavior. Behavior could change physiology. Physiology could change survival.

This book follows that chain without pretending the last link has been mechanically demonstrated in every stranding. It also follows the evidence that does not fit the easy story. Some beaked whales live on heavily used Navy ranges. Controlled exposures show strong responses at comparatively low received levels, but not every exposure produces the same reaction. Context matters. Familiarity may matter. geography matters. Different sonar sources are not interchangeable. Population-level effects remain difficult to estimate because the animals themselves are notoriously hard to see.

That uncertainty is not a defect to sand away. It is the subject.

Beaked whales live in an acoustic environment humans mostly encounter as an engineering abstraction. They spend long periods deep enough for darkness to be ordinary, hunt with narrow echolocation clicks, and can descend well beyond a kilometer. Cuvier's beaked whales have produced some of the deepest and longest mammalian dives ever directly measured. For a naval tactician, the same water column is a propagation medium, full of layers, ducts, convergence zones, reverberation and target uncertainty. The whale and the warship occupy one ocean but inhabit different maps of it.

The collision became visible because strandings leave bodies, and bodies leave pathology. It became measurable because navies also built extraordinary underwater listening ranges, some of which could be turned back on the environmental problem. The tools created to find submarines helped scientists find whales that had previously been nearly invisible. Tagged animals then supplied something the early stranding record could not: direct measurements of what a beaked whale does after it hears sonar.

The result is one of the more useful case studies in modern environmental epistemology. A classified or partly classified technology produces a biological effect. The effect is rare, context-dependent and difficult to reproduce ethically. The institution responsible for the exposure also funds much of the research required to understand it. Activists have reasons to emphasize the worst case. Military planners have reasons to emphasize mitigation and uncertainty. Scientists depend on access to ranges, animals, ships, tags, acoustic data and long time series. Regulators must convert incomplete biology into thresholds that can be written into permits. Courts ask questions that science does not naturally answer on a litigation schedule.

Everyone can be sincere and still pull the evidence in different directions.

The book keeps four evidence classes separate throughout. Documented facts include the Bahamas investigation, the timing of the 2002 Canary Islands event, the pathology, the controlled-exposure literature, Navy-range monitoring and current regulatory records. Credible but incomplete evidence includes mechanistic links between behavioral disruption and gas embolic injury, historical strandings with incomplete sonar records, and population consequences inferred from sparse observations. Disputed claims include precise causal reconstructions of individual strandings where records are incomplete or carcasses degraded. Speculative synthesis includes the broader idea that sensory disturbance should be treated as a systems-level hazard whenever an organism's survival depends on tightly managed behavior.

The distinction matters because the strongest version of this story does not need a conspiracy, an acoustic death ray, or a perfectly obedient dose-response curve. It needs only a technology powerful enough to change what an extreme diver does at the wrong moment.

The whale does the rest.
