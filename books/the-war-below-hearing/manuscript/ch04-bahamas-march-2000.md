# Bahamas, March 2000

The northern Bahamas are a good place to misunderstand acoustics.

The water is clear enough to invite visual intuitions. Islands sit close enough together that a map looks simple. Channels appear as blue corridors. But underwater sound does not read the chart the way a boat does. It bends through water whose temperature and pressure change with depth. It reflects from boundaries, refracts through layers, finds paths that can carry energy farther than a straight line suggests and leaves other places unexpectedly quiet. Add steep bathymetry and narrow passages, and a sonar exercise becomes an experiment in geometry whether anyone intends it to be or not.

On 15 and 16 March 2000, that geometry acquired bodies.

Multiple cetaceans stranded across the northern Bahamas while U.S. Navy vessels were conducting an antisubmarine warfare exercise. The later joint Navy–National Marine Fisheries Service investigation remains the most important primary document for reconstructing the event. The report can be read through NOAA's archive at https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/16198/ and downloaded as the original PDF from the same page.

The report did not merely note that naval activity occurred somewhere nearby. Investigators assembled ship tracks, sonar operations, stranding chronology, necropsy information and environmental conditions. They considered other candidate sound sources and potential causes. Their conclusion was unusually consequential for an official investigation of a military system: tactical mid-range-frequency sonars aboard U.S. Navy ships were the most plausible sound source involved in the strandings.

That conclusion has sometimes been compressed to “the Navy admitted sonar killed the whales.” Compression loses the interesting part.

The report did not describe a single-variable event. It emphasized that the sonar operated inside a set of environmental factors, including sound propagation conditions, bathymetry, a constricted channel with limited egress, and the presence of beaked whales that appeared sensitive to the frequencies involved. The investigators recommended focusing on the interplay between the source and those factors rather than treating the source alone as the whole explanation.

This was an early appearance of a concept that later experimental work would strengthen: context belongs inside the dose.

Imagine two whales receiving an acoustically similar signal. One is in open deep water with many escape headings. The other is in a steep-sided channel. One has experienced similar sonar repeatedly without injury. The other is naïve. One is ascending after a long foraging dive. The other is near the surface. If they respond differently, a regulation based only on received sound pressure will record an important variable and miss several others.

The Bahamas event made this problem visible before researchers could measure most of those variables directly.

Necropsies found hemorrhages, including around auditory structures in some animals. This initially made direct acoustic trauma an obvious hypothesis. A sufficiently intense sound can damage hearing tissue. That pathway is real in mammals and belongs in any complete risk model. Yet the observed lesions did not settle the mechanism. The animals had stranded, some had been alive on shore, and postmortem examination of cetacean ears is technically difficult. The report described evidence consistent with an intense acoustic event while acknowledging uncertainty over how exposure led to stranding.

At the time, one missing data stream was behavior.

No high-resolution tag had recorded the final hours of the stranded animals. There was no synchronized trace showing a whale at depth, a sonar transmission, cessation of clicking, a change in ascent, rapid travel toward land, altered blood flow, bubble formation and beaching. That complete chain still has not been captured in a fatal event, for obvious ethical reasons. The Bahamas reconstruction therefore had to move from known exposure and observed outcome through a mechanistic middle that could only be inferred.

This is where weak causal reasoning tends to split into camps.

One camp says temporal coincidence is not causation. True. Naval exercises and whale strandings can occur in the same region without one causing the other. Another says that if the timing, species, geography and pathology line up repeatedly, coincidence eventually becomes an increasingly expensive explanation. Also true. The scientific work is deciding which additional observations reduce the cost of one explanation relative to others.

The Bahamas case became stronger retrospectively because later events and experiments supplied missing pieces.

In 2002, beaked whales stranded in the Canary Islands within hours of the start of a naval exercise, and detailed pathology revealed gas bubble-associated lesions and fat embolism. In 2011, Tyack and colleagues directly measured beaked-whale responses to simulated and actual Navy sonar, including cessation of echolocation and unusual avoidance. In 2013, DeRuiter and colleagues recorded Cuvier's beaked whales swimming rapidly and silently away from simulated mid-frequency sonar and extending dives. In 2026, a much larger controlled-exposure study measured movement and diving responses across fifty-three Cuvier's beaked whales.

None of those later studies proves exactly what happened to an individual Bahamas animal. They change the prior plausibility of the intermediate steps.

This is how causal evidence often accumulates in systems where the decisive event is rare and cannot be reproduced lethally. A historical case offers outcome and exposure. A later mechanistic study shows a relevant behavior. Comparative physiology shows that the behavior could change gas management. Another natural experiment shows similar timing and pathology. A policy change removes a suspected exposure and the event type declines. No single piece carries the argument by itself.

The Bahamas report also marks the point where an environmental problem entered the bureaucratic machinery of military readiness.

Navies cannot simply stop using active sonar because there is biological uncertainty. Submarines are real strategic systems. Antisubmarine warfare training has operational value. At the same time, a military system does not become biologically harmless because its mission is important. The regulatory task is therefore not to decide whether national security or whales matter more in the abstract. It is to understand enough of the hazard to reduce unnecessary harm while preserving whatever training capability policymakers decide is necessary.

That framing produces uncomfortable research incentives.

If the Navy funds studies, critics may suspect capture. If naval ranges deny access, researchers lose some of the best instruments for studying beaked whales. If researchers emphasize catastrophic historical events, operators may argue that modern mitigation makes those events unrepresentative. If researchers study frequently exposed populations that remain present, advocates may worry that occupancy will be mistaken for absence of harm.

The right response is not to pretend incentives disappear. It is to make methods and provenance visible.

The Bahamas report is a good example because its institutional authorship is itself part of the evidence. The Navy had direct access to operational information. NMFS brought marine-mammal expertise and regulatory responsibility. The joint product could be criticized for what it did not know, but it cannot be dismissed as outsider speculation. Its conclusion against simple institutional self-interest—that Navy tactical sonar was the most plausible sound source involved—should carry weight precisely because of where it came from.

Institutional provenance does not make every sentence true. It changes what kind of claim is being made.

The report also disciplined later rhetoric by preserving alternatives. Sonar was implicated, but environmental conditions mattered. The event was not evidence that every sonar use creates strandings. Nor was the absence of strandings after most sonar exercises evidence that this event was unrelated. Rare hazards often require combinations.

Aviation offers a useful analogy. Severe turbulence does not crash every airplane. Icing does not bring down every aircraft entering cloud. A design can operate safely across thousands of flights and still possess a specific failure mode that appears only under a narrow conjunction of temperature, moisture, angle, speed and pilot response. Identifying that conjunction is not an attempt to claim airplanes are generally unsafe. It is how safety engineering works.

The beaked-whale case has a similar structure, except the animal is not engineered to a specification humans possess.

The possible conjunction includes species, dive state, sound characteristics, source movement, prior exposure, bathymetry and escape geometry. The Bahamas investigators could see several of those factors but not resolve their interactions.

One especially persistent question is why beaked whales.

The family appears disproportionately in sonar-associated mass strandings compared with many more commonly observed cetaceans. That taxonomic concentration argues against a purely random beaching process. It also argues against an effect that depends only on gross acoustic intensity. If many species share the water, yet a particular class of extreme divers appears repeatedly, some combination of auditory sensitivity, anti-predator behavior, diving physiology or habitat use becomes a plausible discriminator.

The exact weighting remains unsettled.

Beaked whales may be behaviorally sensitive to sonar-like signals. Their deep repetitive dives may create unusual nitrogen dynamics. Their preferred habitats often include steep slopes, canyons and islands where propagation and escape geometry become complicated. Their low surface availability can make them difficult to detect during mitigation. Their anti-predator strategy may involve silence and movement patterns that become costly when triggered at depth.

A serious explanation probably does not need to choose only one.

The Bahamas report's focus on interaction therefore aged well. Environmental causation is often a network rather than a bullet. The sound changes the animal; the animal changes its dive; the dive interacts with physiology; geography shapes movement; pathology makes navigation or survival harder; beaching adds secondary trauma.

Every arrow can be challenged. That is a strength if the model is explicit. It creates places to look.

The opposite style of explanation—“the sonar did it”—is politically potent but scientifically sterile. It has no obvious defeat condition other than absence of sonar. The equally broad rebuttal—“there is no proven mechanism”—can be sterile too, because mechanisms are rarely delivered complete before policy must act.

The useful middle is comparative prediction.

If behavioral disruption matters, tagged whales should alter dive and movement patterns after relevant exposures. They do. If extreme diving physiology contributes, beaked whales should be a risk class distinct from shallow-diving species. The stranding record is consistent with that, although not uniquely diagnostic. If geography matters, certain topographies should be overrepresented. Several notorious events occurred around islands and steep bathymetry. If repeated exposure produces habituation or selection, populations on frequently used ranges might respond differently from naïve populations. There is evidence compatible with such context effects, but it remains incomplete. If mitigation works, changes in naval operations should reduce strandings in places where they were recurrent. The Canary Islands would later provide a provocative example.

The Bahamas case sits at the head of these predictions because it forced the questions into a public document.

It also helped shift marine bioacoustics from measuring whether animals could hear a sound toward asking what they did with it. Hearing is a sensory event. Disturbance is a behavioral event. Injury may be a physiological event. Stranding is a geographic and behavioral endpoint. Mortality can be secondary to all of them.

Collapsing those levels creates bad thresholds.

A sonar pulse can be below a level expected to cause direct auditory injury and still be biologically important if it triggers a strong avoidance response. Conversely, an animal can hear a signal without meaningful consequence. Exposure is not effect. Effect is not injury. Injury is not death. But a causal chain can connect them.

The Bahamas whales forced regulators and scientists to stop treating those distinctions as academic.

On a map, the strandings look like points. On a timeline, they look like a cluster. In a pathology report, they become tissues. In a sonar log, pulses. In an oceanographic model, rays and channels. In a whale's life, something happened between a deep acoustic world and a beach.

The job of this book is not to fill that interval with confidence we do not possess.

It is to narrow it.
