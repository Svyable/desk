# The Range That Could See Them

The Navy built underwater ranges to see things that were otherwise difficult to see.

That is almost the whole story in miniature.

A submarine operating below the surface is hard to observe directly, so military test and training ranges instrument the ocean. Hydrophones listen. Tracking systems locate. Acoustic sources are recorded. Exercises that would be opaque from a passing research vessel become measurable inside a fixed field of sensors.

Beaked whales turned out to benefit from the same infrastructure, scientifically if not biologically.

They are notoriously difficult visual targets. A Cuvier's beaked whale can spend most of an hour—or much longer—beneath the surface, appear briefly, breathe, and disappear again. Sea state can erase the small visual cue of a surfacing animal. Group sizes can be small. Some species are difficult to distinguish at a distance. Conventional line-transect surveys therefore face low detection probability precisely for animals of greatest interest in the sonar problem.

Underwater, the whales are less discreet.

During deep foraging, beaked whales produce characteristic echolocation clicks. Their signals can be detected by bottom-mounted hydrophones and, with enough sensors, localized. An array designed to track acoustic events across a naval range can therefore reveal a hidden biological population repeatedly using the same water.

The Southern California Anti-submarine Warfare Range became one of the most important places in the world for this work.

Researchers were able to combine passive acoustic detection, visual observation, photo-identification, satellite tagging and controlled exposure experiments. The instrumented range solved a basic field problem: how do you find an animal that is underwater most of the time? Listen for the clicks, localize them, move the research team toward the animal, and use the range itself as context for exposure.

The 2011 Tyack et al. study is a landmark partly because of this infrastructure. Hydrophones over a large area showed that during actual sonar exercises, beaked whales were detected more toward the periphery of the range. After the exercise, detections gradually returned toward the center. A satellite-tagged whale moved outside the range during an exercise and came back over the following two to three days. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0017009.

A beach was no longer required to reveal an effect.

This is a profound change in the evidence base. Strandings select the most dramatic endpoints. Range monitoring can detect sublethal displacement and foraging disruption in animals that survive and never approach land. It turns the sonar problem from a forensic science into a behavioral ecology.

The range also complicates the narrative because whales keep living there.

Long-term work has identified a persistent Cuvier's beaked-whale population using a frequently active military area. Curtis and colleagues analyzed eleven years of photo-identification data from 2007 through 2018 and reported estimates of apparent annual survival, abundance and trend in *Marine Mammal Science*. Their mean estimate of annual abundance was about 121 individuals, with wide uncertainty, and the estimated annual rate of change was centered near a small decline but with a credibility interval spanning both decline and increase. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1111/mms.12747.

This is precisely the sort of result a polarized debate finds hard to metabolize.

If sonar is described as a universally intolerable habitat poison, the population's persistence looks exculpatory. If sonar is described as biologically negligible, the direct evidence of avoidance and foraging disruption looks incriminating. The data refuse both slogans.

A population can persist in a disturbed habitat for many reasons.

The habitat may be unusually valuable. Animals may habituate. Some individuals may tolerate disturbance better than others. The actual level of harmful exposure may be episodic. Mitigation can reduce the most dangerous circumstances. Offshore geography may make strong avoidance less likely to end in stranding. The population may incur costs too small to detect with available statistical power. Immigration can mask local demographic effects. Or sonar effects may truly be modest for many individuals at that site.

The point is not to preserve every hypothesis forever. It is to ask what the available data can discriminate.

Curtis et al. emphasized limited power to detect population trends. Rare, long-lived marine mammals are hard demographic subjects. A small annual change compounds over decades but can sit inside wide uncertainty for years. Apparent survival confounds mortality with permanent emigration. Abundance estimates depend on sighting probability. Even a well-instrumented range does not turn a whale population into a laboratory colony.

This is one reason behavior has become the most measurable scale.

An individual whale can carry a tag. Researchers can compare its movement before, during and after exposure. They can measure when echolocation stops. They can estimate received sound. These are within-animal changes on timescales of minutes to days, much easier to detect than a two-percent demographic effect spread across a decade.

But behavior is not population consequence.

The bridge requires an energetic or survival model. How much prey does the animal lose when it terminates a foraging dive? How often does disturbance occur? Can it compensate later? Does avoidance move it into worse habitat? Are calves affected differently? Does repeated displacement alter reproduction? A response that looks dramatic on a tag may be inconsequential if rare and fully compensated. A subtle response may matter if it occurs every day.

The range can help answer frequency.

Because acoustic monitoring runs over long periods, researchers can quantify how often whales use different parts of the range and how detections change around exercises. This is superior to extrapolating from a handful of controlled playbacks. It also introduces operational complexity: real exercises vary. Multiple sources may operate. Exact tactical details can be sensitive. The researchers do not always receive a perfectly standardized experimental treatment.

That imperfection is scientifically valuable in another way. It tests whether controlled-exposure findings transfer to realistic operations.

A whale can react strongly to a clean simulated sonar signal from a research source and differently to a distant operational source. The range lets both contexts be observed. The mismatch is not a failed replication; it is evidence that the causal variable is richer than one acoustic level.

This is where the concept of “context” risks becoming a garbage bin. If every unexpected result can be blamed on context, the theory becomes unfalsifiable.

The remedy is to decompose context into measurable candidates.

Source-animal distance. Source speed. Approach versus recession. Number of transmitting platforms. Received level. Signal bandwidth. Duty cycle. Prior sonar exposure. Dive phase. Group size. habitat. bathymetry. Ambient noise. Predation cues. Individual identity.

A large enough data set can estimate some of these effects. The 2026 Southall study moves in that direction by combining multiple controlled trials, controls and two tag types across fifty-three Cuvier's beaked whales. It gives researchers enough observations to model response rather than tell anecdotes.

The range also exposes a paradox in mitigation.

Frequent sonar use may create a population that is less behaviorally reactive because animals have habituated, while a rarely exposed population may respond more strongly to the same signal. If regulators derive thresholds from the habituated population, they could underestimate risk elsewhere. If they derive them from the most reactive population, they could overpredict disturbance on familiar ranges.

A robust policy therefore needs transfer functions, not universal constants.

The Mariana Archipelago offers a useful contrast. NOAA researchers have noted that beaked whales there occupy important habitat and that relatively infrequent sonar combined with quiet ambient conditions may increase response severity. The agency's summary is at https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/beaked-whale-strandings-mariana-archipelago-may-be-associated-sonar. Whether rarity of exposure is the key cause remains a hypothesis, but it is the kind of hypothesis the Southern California range can help test.

The instrumentation itself has another effect: it changes what counts as absence.

Before passive acoustic monitoring, a region with few beaked-whale sightings might be described as low density. Hydrophones can reveal regular presence in almost every week. In the Mariana work cited by NOAA, beaked whales were acoustically detected in a very high fraction of monitored weeks at some locations. The animals were not rare in the habitat; they were rare to human eyes.

This matters for historical strandings.

If old surveys underestimated beaked-whale abundance, apparent increases in strandings after the arrival of naval sonar might partly reflect changes in detection effort and knowledge. Good causal analysis has to include that possibility. At the same time, modern acoustic monitoring can identify habitat where risk was previously invisible.

The range therefore improves both numerator and denominator.

It counts behavioral events that never become strandings and measures animal presence that visual surveys miss. This lets researchers ask a more meaningful question than “how many whales washed up?”: how many animals were exposed, how many responded, how many altered foraging, how many left, and how many returned?

Still, some outcomes remain nearly inaccessible.

No hydrophone can hear a gas embolus. A tag cannot necropsy its host. A surviving whale cannot tell researchers whether it experienced subclinical bubble formation. The range narrows the causal chain without closing it.

This is why the sonar problem rewards methodological pluralism. Military telemetry gives exposure. Passive acoustics gives presence and vocal behavior. Tags give individual movement. Photography gives identity and survival histories. Pathology gives lesions. Models connect behavior to physiology. Regulations create operational data. Each method sees a different whale.

The instrumented range is where many of those whales overlap.

There is an irony here that should not be prettified. Antisubmarine warfare created both the disturbance and some of the best tools for measuring its biological consequences. Navy funding supported research that documented strong beaked-whale responses to Navy sonar. The institution generating the hazard also generated knowledge capable of constraining it.

That is not evidence that the system works perfectly. It is evidence that institutional incentives are more complicated than a simple cover-up story.

The Navy has reasons to protect operational capability, limit legal exposure and defend training. It also has reasons to understand a hazard that can halt exercises, trigger litigation, damage public legitimacy and kill protected species. Scientists have reasons to preserve access and funding. They also have professional reasons to publish results that survive peer review. Environmental groups have reasons to emphasize risk. They can also improve science by forcing disclosure and mitigation.

The range sits where those incentives collide in data.

A whale clicks. A hydrophone hears it. A ship transmits. The whale goes silent. A satellite tag shows movement. Days later the animal returns.

That sequence does not tell us everything.

It tells us far more than a beach ever could.
