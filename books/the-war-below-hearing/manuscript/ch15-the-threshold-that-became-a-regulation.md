# The Threshold That Became a Regulation

A threshold looks like a scientific discovery after it has been printed in a table long enough.

Before that, it is usually a negotiation between data and administration.

Marine-mammal acoustic regulation needs numbers. Ships cannot operate under an instruction that says “avoid disturbing whales in biologically meaningful ways.” Environmental planners need to estimate how many animals may be exposed. Permits need definitions. Enforcement needs criteria. Models need cutoffs.

So the messy behavioral literature becomes thresholds.

This translation is necessary. It is also where uncertainty hardens.

The Marine Mammal Protection Act distinguishes forms of “take,” including harassment. Current Navy authorizations model Level B harassment from behavioral disturbance and temporary hearing effects and Level A harassment from injury. NOAA's current Atlantic Fleet Training and Testing regulations took effect on 14 November 2025 and run through 13 November 2032. The agency's action page is at https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/action/incidental-take-authorization-military-readiness-activities-atlantic-fleet-training-and.

The rule runs hundreds of pages when published in the Federal Register because one number cannot carry the system.

Different marine mammals hear different frequency ranges. Sonar systems differ. Exposure can be impulsive or continuous. Auditory injury depends on accumulated energy as well as peak conditions. Behavioral disturbance is not captured by a single universal response. Density models estimate how many animals occupy ensonified areas. Mitigation changes actual exposure. The output is an authorization that looks legal because it has to be legal and scientific because it has to be defensible.

Beaked whales are where this machinery shows its seams.

Early generic behavioral thresholds were often higher than levels at which controlled beaked-whale studies later documented strong reactions. Tyack and colleagues noted responses around levels below 142 dB re 1 μPa. DeRuiter and colleagues reported Cuvier's beaked-whale responses beginning within an 89–127 dB range during controlled playbacks. These observations made it difficult to say that behavioral consequences begin only at a single high level.

Regulators adapted by using species- and context-informed response functions rather than pretending the old number was a biological wall.

That evolution is how science is supposed to enter policy.

The difficulty is communicating what the updated numbers mean.

Suppose a model assigns a probability of behavioral harassment at a given received level. That does not mean a whale below the level is safe and one above it is harmed. It means available data support an estimated probability distribution. The curve may incorporate uncertainty, and the legal definition of harassment may capture responses far less severe than injury.

Policy needs a yes/no count from a continuous biological world.

The same issue appears in air-quality standards, radiation limits and occupational noise rules. A regulatory limit is not the point where physics switches state. It is a management line drawn using evidence, acceptable risk, feasibility and law.

This becomes politically combustible when opponents quote different thresholds as if they are competing measurements of truth.

An advocate cites a controlled response at 100 dB. A Navy analysis cites an injury threshold much higher. Both numbers can be correct because they describe different endpoints. The debate becomes false when “response” and “injury” are treated as synonyms.

The sonar case requires at least four levels of effect.

Detection: the animal can hear the signal.

Behavioral response: the animal changes activity.

Biologically significant disturbance: the response is large or persistent enough to affect feeding, movement, reproduction or other important function.

Injury: tissue or physiological damage occurs.

Stranding and death sit beyond injury as outcomes that may involve additional geography and behavior.

A useful regulation should not collapse them.

The 2025–2032 Atlantic rule explicitly authorizes large numbers of Level B takes because “take” in this context does not mean death. Most modeled takes are behavioral disturbances or temporary hearing effects. The same rule separately analyzes the much rarer potential for serious injury or mortality from explosives and vessel strikes. That distinction is essential for reading the numbers honestly.

It also reveals a legal oddity: one whale can be “taken” many times statistically without being captured or killed.

Public reaction to enormous take numbers is therefore predictable. The word sounds like removal. In regulatory practice it often counts disturbance events. Agencies could communicate this better, but critics should not exploit the ambiguity.

The deeper problem is how to value disturbance.

A behavioral response can range from turning slightly to abandoning a productive feeding area. A model that counts both under one legal category may be conservative for one purpose and biologically crude for another. Researchers therefore build severity scales and population-consequence models.

For beaked whales, response duration matters enormously.

DeRuiter et al. observed delayed return to foraging after controlled exposure. Tyack et al. observed movement away from a range with return over days. A lost deep-foraging opportunity can be more costly than a brief heading change. The same legal “take” can therefore contain very different energetic consequences.

The future of regulation should move toward dose-context-response surfaces rather than hard universal lines.

That sounds computational because it is.

One axis is received acoustic exposure. Another is source-animal distance or approach geometry. Another is dive state. Another is prior exposure history. Another is habitat. Individual identity can be a random effect. Outcome can be ordinal: none, mild, moderate, severe. A model estimates probabilities.

Operational planners can then ask a better question: under this exercise design, how many animals are expected to experience each severity class?

This does not eliminate normative choices. Someone still decides how much severe disturbance is acceptable.

The law makes those choices through phrases such as “least practicable adverse impact,” negligible impact and small numbers, each with its own administrative history. Military readiness receives special statutory treatment in some contexts because Congress has explicitly weighed defense needs.

Science can inform those decisions but cannot hide them.

This is worth saying because environmental disputes often outsource values to models. A technical document produces a contour map and the contour becomes policy. The assumption looks objective because it is quantitative.

Every contour contains a judgment about endpoint, uncertainty and acceptable error.

Beaked whales have repeatedly forced the system to revise those judgments.

Historical mass strandings made catastrophic risk legible. Controlled exposures showed strong responses below generic behavioral thresholds. Long-term range studies showed that persistence is possible under repeated exposure. The Canary Islands moratorium showed that place-based restrictions can remove a recurring severe outcome. Modern population work shows how difficult it is to infer long-term demographic consequences.

A sensible regulatory architecture would therefore be layered.

First, protect known high-risk places where history and habitat make catastrophic outcomes more plausible. Second, use real-time mitigation where detection is feasible. Third, model auditory injury separately from behavioral disturbance. Fourth, use species-specific behavioral response functions when available. Fifth, monitor long-term population and habitat use so sublethal costs are not invisible. Sixth, update thresholds when controlled evidence changes.

This is not radical. It is what mature risk regulation looks like.

The hardest part may be institutional memory.

Thresholds persist because software, training, permits and expectations grow around them. Updating a number can alter predicted takes dramatically, which in turn affects operational planning and litigation. There is therefore bureaucratic friction against rapid revision even when science moves.

The beaked-whale field benefited from unusually direct experimental evidence. Many protected species do not have controlled-exposure studies. Regulators must extrapolate from related animals. That makes the temptation to overread beaked-whale thresholds especially dangerous.

A Cuvier's beaked whale is not a generic marine mammal.

Its hearing, diving, ecology and anti-predator behavior are specific. The reason it is scientifically useful is precisely that specificity.

The regulation should preserve as much of it as operationally possible.

The best threshold is not the number that ends argument.

It is the number whose assumptions are visible enough to be replaced.
