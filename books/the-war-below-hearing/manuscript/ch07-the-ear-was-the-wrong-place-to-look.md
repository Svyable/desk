# The Ear Was the Wrong Place to Look

The ear is where a reasonable investigator starts when an animal has been exposed to intense sound.

Noise damages ears. Blast waves damage ears. Sonar is sound. The syllogism is so intuitive that it shaped much of the early public understanding of whale strandings. Images of naval sonar were paired with phrases like “acoustic trauma,” which in turn encouraged a picture of mechanical injury traveling directly from transducer to cochlea.

There are real reasons to investigate that pathway. Mammalian hearing organs are vulnerable to excessive sound. Temporary threshold shift and permanent threshold shift are established phenomena. Marine-mammal regulators explicitly model auditory effects from acoustic exposure. The middle and inner ear can show hemorrhage after trauma. Nothing in the behavioral hypothesis erases direct auditory risk.

The mistake is assuming that the ear is the only place where sound can become biology.

An auditory system evolved because sound changes behavior. If a signal is intense enough to damage tissue, one mechanism is obvious. If it is merely salient enough to change what an animal does, the consequences can occur elsewhere entirely.

This sounds almost trivial in human life. A car horn need not injure your ear to make you step backward into danger. A smoke alarm does not burn you; it changes behavior under pressure. A false missile warning can alter the actions of an entire command system without delivering any physical energy to the targets it describes. Sensory inputs matter because organisms and institutions act on them.

Beaked whales make that general principle physiologically extreme.

Their routine behavior is coupled to pressure, gas exchange, oxygen stores, echolocation and foraging. Alter behavior at depth and the downstream system changes. The same received sound level can therefore have consequences that an auditory injury threshold will never predict.

The direct-response studies made this impossible to ignore. In the 2011 PLOS ONE study by Tyack and colleagues, tagged beaked whales exposed to simulated military sonar stopped echolocating and altered their movement. One satellite-tagged whale left the Navy range during an exercise and returned over subsequent days. The authors concluded that beaked whales showed avoidance and foraging disruption at levels below those commonly used at the time to define behavioral disturbance. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0017009.

In 2013, DeRuiter and colleagues reported perhaps the clearest early high-resolution example. Two Cuvier's beaked whales exposed to controlled mid-frequency sonar playbacks stopped normal fluking and echolocation, swam rapidly and silently away, extended their dives and delayed subsequent foraging. The received levels during the playbacks ranged from 89 to 127 dB re 1 μPa. The same animals did not show equivalent responses to more distant naval sonar in the observed intervals, one reason the paper emphasized context. See https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2013.0223.

None of those behaviors requires an injured ear.

Indeed, an animal must hear well enough to respond.

That simple fact changes hazard analysis. A classic toxicological curve often assumes more dose means more direct biological damage. A sensory-mediated hazard can be nonmonotonic or context-dependent. A lower-level but novel, approaching or behaviorally meaningful sound could provoke a stronger response than a louder, familiar or distant sound. The animal is not a passive dosimeter.

This is one reason regulatory acoustics has had to distinguish auditory injury from behavioral harassment. NOAA's current rules for Navy training explicitly model both. The categories can look bureaucratic, but they map onto different biological ideas. A sound may be loud enough to cause hearing effects. Or it may be lower and still disrupt feeding, movement, communication or other natural behavior.

Beaked whales push the second category toward injury.

The usual regulatory concept of behavioral disturbance imagines costs such as missed feeding, displacement or energetic stress. Those matter. But an extreme diver raises a more acute possibility: behavior itself may alter physiological safety margins. The distinction between “behavioral” and “physical” effects then becomes less clean than the paperwork suggests.

A whale that extends a dive is behaving. Its blood gases are physical. A whale that swims harder is behaving. Perfusion changes are physical. A whale that changes ascent pattern is behaving. Tissue supersaturation is physical. The causal arrow crosses administrative categories without asking permission.

The ear-centric model also had trouble explaining taxonomic selectivity.

If the dominant mechanism were straightforward direct acoustic trauma at sufficiently high received levels, vulnerability should map strongly onto exposure and hearing sensitivity. Yet sonar-associated mass strandings have repeatedly featured beaked whales, especially Cuvier's beaked whales, despite the presence of many other cetaceans in naval operating areas. That concentration does not by itself prove a behavioral-decompression route, but it suggests that something about beaked-whale ecology or physiology matters beyond generic hearing damage.

Their anti-predator behavior became one candidate.

Beaked whales are preyed upon by killer whales. A deep-diving animal that forages acoustically has a problem: clicking advertises its presence. Researchers have found evidence that beaked whales use distinctive acoustic and movement strategies that may reduce predation risk, including limiting when they echolocate and coordinating surfacing behavior. A threatening sound could therefore trigger a strongly selected escape program.

Controlled studies using killer-whale calls are informative because they supply a naturalistic comparison. Tyack and colleagues exposed tagged beaked whales not only to simulated sonar but also to killer-whale vocalizations and other sounds. Strong responses to both sonar and predator cues supported the idea that sonar can engage anti-predator-like behavior without proving that whales literally misidentify a naval signal as an orca.

This distinction is worth guarding.

The phrase “sonar sounds like killer whales” travels easily. Acoustic similarity can be discussed, but behavioral equivalence does not require perceptual confusion. Humans flee both gunshots and fire alarms without thinking they are the same object. An animal can classify multiple unfamiliar or intense signals under a broad threat response.

The response may also be learned.

Animals repeatedly exposed to Navy sonar in one location may come to associate certain patterns with nonlethal disturbance. Others in rarely exposed regions may treat similar sounds as more threatening. That possibility is consistent with research suggesting differences between populations and locations, including the observation that some whales remain on heavily used ranges.

Again, the ear can be perfectly functional throughout.

The behavioral emphasis also clarifies why received-level thresholds have behaved badly as universal predictors. In some experiments, beaked whales responded strongly at surprisingly low levels. In other circumstances, animals tolerated higher or more distant exposures. Source proximity, movement and context all appear capable of changing response probability.

From an engineering perspective, this is annoying. A simple threshold is operationally useful. If risk begins at X dB, planners can model contours, draw buffers and count expected exposures. If risk depends on novelty, dive phase, source trajectory and local bathymetry, the problem becomes probabilistic and data hungry.

But the animal did not evolve for administrative convenience.

The 2026 *Ecosphere* study by Southall and colleagues helps because it moves beyond tiny sample size. Fifty-three Cuvier's beaked whales were monitored in relation to simulated military sonar, using a combination of long-duration satellite tags and shorter high-resolution archival tags. There were nine sonar trials and four no-sonar controls, producing seventy-two exposure events. Received levels reached roughly 142 dB re 1 μPa RMS, with modeled horizontal ranges from around two to more than two hundred kilometers. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.70501.

The study does not reduce response to one trigger number. That is its value.

Large behavioral data sets allow researchers to estimate response probability as a function of received level, range and context rather than chasing one universal threshold. They also reveal individual variation. Some whales alter movement more than others. Some dive changes differ from horizontal avoidance. Repeated exposures can be compared. Controls help distinguish normal variability from sonar-associated changes.

This is the shape the science should have if the behavioral model is correct.

The ear-centric model remains important for a different reason: it supplies a potential confound in strandings. If sonar can both alter behavior and cause auditory injury under some conditions, an impaired whale might navigate poorly after exposure. Hemorrhage around auditory tissues in stranded animals could reflect direct trauma, secondary effects or both. The causal chain need not choose one mechanism universally.

A systems account should therefore resist the temptation to replace one monocause with another.

Direct auditory effects exist. Behavioral avoidance exists. Gas/fat embolic lesions have been documented. Social following may contribute to group strandings. Geography matters. Individual health matters. Predator-avoidance behavior may matter. The scientific task is to identify which combination operated in a given event.

The strongest evidence for behavior comes from direct measurements, not from interpretation of carcasses.

Tags record depth, acceleration, orientation and sometimes sound at the animal. Hydrophone arrays detect echolocation and sonar. Satellite tags extend movement tracks across days or weeks. Together they can show that an animal was foraging normally, encountered an exposure, stopped clicking, changed speed or direction, altered the dive and resumed normal behavior later.

These data are the behavioral equivalent of a flight-data recorder.

They also have limitations. Tags can alter behavior slightly. Sample sizes remain modest relative to population variability. Controlled exposures are deliberately cautious. Simulated sonar may not reproduce every feature of operational exercises. Tagged animals may come from populations with particular exposure histories. Researchers often cannot recover physiological samples from the same individual.

Still, the progression from inferred disturbance to directly measured response is one of the clearest achievements in the field.

It also changes what a skeptic must explain.

After the controlled-exposure literature, it is no longer credible to argue that beaked whales simply ignore mid-frequency sonar until it becomes loud enough to injure hearing. They can respond strongly well below direct-injury conditions. The live question is how often, how strongly, under what circumstances and with what downstream cost.

Those costs span scales.

At the mild end, an animal misses a feeding opportunity. Deep foraging dives are expensive and prey patches are not guaranteed. Repeated disturbance can therefore carry energetic costs. At the next level, a whale abandons a preferred habitat temporarily. If many animals do this, local density changes during exercises. At a more severe level, the altered dive could affect gas kinetics or lead an animal toward shore. Finally, a rare response under the wrong geography could contribute to stranding and death.

One acoustic event may sit on this entire continuum.

That is why counting only carcasses will underestimate impact and counting every behavioral response as injury will overestimate it.

The relevant scientific object is the response function: probability and severity across exposure, context and individual state.

This is more difficult than measuring damaged hair cells. It is also closer to the real animal.

The ear was not the wrong place to look because ears do not matter. It was the wrong place to stop looking.

A sensory organ is the beginning of a causal chain. In the beaked-whale story, the most consequential tissue may be kilometers of ocean away from the ear: muscle perfused during escape, blood carrying dissolved nitrogen, lungs collapsing and reopening, vessels receiving bubbles, a brain deciding whether to click, a coastline appearing in the wrong direction.

Sound enters through hearing.

The hazard can leave through behavior.
