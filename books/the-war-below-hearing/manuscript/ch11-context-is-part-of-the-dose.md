# Context Is Part of the Dose

Toxicology trained us to like dose.

A substance enters a body. More exposure usually means more opportunity for effect. The curve may not be simple, but the organizing variable is satisfying: quantify the agent, quantify the response, estimate risk.

Sound tempts the same treatment. Measure received level. Plot response. Establish a threshold.

Beaked whales have been uncooperative.

A whale can respond strongly to a relatively low-level simulated sonar exposure and show little obvious response to another signal at a comparable or lower level. Animals on a frequently used Navy range can remain present despite repeated exercises. Mass strandings cluster in some places and not others. The same family of acoustic technology seems capable of producing everything from no detectable reaction to temporary foraging cessation to rare catastrophic events.

This variability is not evidence that there is no effect. It is evidence that level is not the whole dose.

The 2013 DeRuiter study is a clean example. Two tagged Cuvier's beaked whales responded strongly to controlled mid-frequency active sonar playbacks. The researchers recorded cessation of echolocation, rapid silent swimming away from the source, extended dives and longer periods before the animals resumed foraging. The responses began at received levels between 89 and 127 dB re 1 μPa. Yet distant operational sonar recorded during the deployments did not elicit the same behavioral pattern. The paper is at https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2013.0223.

It would be easy to say context mattered and move on.

That phrase needs to earn its keep.

One candidate is distance. A nearby source at modest received level may be perceived as more threatening than a distant source producing a similar level through favorable propagation. Directional information, reverberation and rate of change can provide cues to range and approach. A predator that is near and coming closer is not equivalent to one far away.

Another candidate is source motion. A ship moving toward an animal changes not only level but the apparent trajectory of threat. A controlled research source following a designed path may create a different movement cue from a distant naval exercise.

Another is waveform. Military sonars are not generic tones. Bandwidth, modulation, pulse length and repetition vary. A whale may respond differently to signals with similar integrated energy if their temporal or spectral structure differs.

Another is dive state. A whale actively feeding at depth has made an energetic investment and may face different pressure-management constraints from one near the surface. The cost of abandoning a dive is not constant.

Another is social state. A whale with a calf or in a coordinated group may make different decisions. Group responses can propagate behavior socially even when individuals receive different acoustic exposures.

Another is prior experience. A population repeatedly exposed to sonar may habituate. An individual may recognize a familiar nonlethal pattern. Or repeated exposure may sensitize rather than habituate under some circumstances.

Another is habitat. Deep offshore water offers escape options different from an island channel. The Bahamas investigation explicitly included bathymetry and constricted egress among the factors relevant to the 2000 event.

Another is ambient sound. In a quiet region, a sonar pulse may have higher salience even at the same absolute received level. In a noisy operating area, the animal's baseline may be different.

These variables are not excuses. They are hypotheses.

The standard for keeping one is that it predicts response before the next event.

If source approach matters, an approaching source should elicit stronger or earlier avoidance than a receding one after controlling for received level. If prior exposure matters, individually identified whales with repeated history should show systematic changes in response. If habitat constriction matters, similar behavioral reactions should be more likely to lead to strandings near certain bathymetry. If dive phase matters, responses during deep foraging should produce different downstream dive changes from exposures near the surface.

The 2026 Southall et al. *Ecosphere* study is important because fifty-three Cuvier's beaked whales and seventy-two exposure events give researchers enough observations to begin asking multivariable questions. The signals simulated powerful Navy mid-frequency active sonar and were received over a broad range of distances and levels, up to roughly 142 dB re 1 μPa RMS. The study is at https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.70501.

Large samples do not make context disappear. They make context estimable.

This is a general maturation pattern in behavioral ecology. Early studies establish that an effect exists. Later studies ask why individuals vary. At first variability looks like noise. Eventually some of that noise becomes biology.

Regulation has to live through the transition.

A government cannot wait until every causal variable is understood before setting operating rules. It therefore uses conservative thresholds, species-specific criteria, exposure models and mitigation zones. These are necessary simplifications. Problems arise when the administrative threshold is mistaken for a natural law.

A threshold can mean several different things: above this level a certain fraction of animals is predicted to show disturbance; above this level temporary hearing effects become plausible; above this level auditory injury risk is modeled. The number may be derived from pooled data across species or adjusted by hearing group. It can carry uncertainty buffers. None of those meanings says that animals below the number are biologically unaffected.

Beaked whales helped force that distinction because controlled studies documented substantial responses below some earlier generic disturbance thresholds.

The opposite mistake is to treat any response below a threshold as evidence that the regulation is useless.

An animal turning away briefly is not the same as an animal abandoning foraging for hours, and neither is automatically equivalent to injury. Regulation necessarily chooses a level of effect to manage. The scientific contribution is to estimate response probability and severity honestly enough that policymakers know what their line leaves out.

This is especially important when the hazard pathway is behavioral.

A lower received level can matter more in a context that provokes a severe response. A higher level can matter less if the animal recognizes the source and continues normal behavior. The relationship between physical dose and biological consequence is mediated by interpretation.

Human medicine already contains analogous cases. A small amount of an allergen can produce a severe reaction in a sensitized person while a larger amount is harmless to another. A modest visual cue can trigger a seizure in a susceptible individual. A low-volume alarm can provoke evacuation if believed credible; a louder ignored alarm may produce no action. These analogies do not prove whale mechanisms. They remind us that the chain from stimulus to outcome can contain state-dependent amplification.

The sonar case adds geography as an amplifier.

The Bahamas report's constricted channel, the Canary Islands' steep oceanic terrain and the recurring association of strandings with island settings raise the possibility that avoidance becomes dangerous when the environment limits routes. A whale fleeing horizontally may enter shallower water. Social cohesion may draw additional animals. Once an extreme deep diver is in a coastal basin, its normal behavioral repertoire may be poorly suited to escape.

The animal's own response can therefore transform acoustic exposure into geographic risk.

This helps explain why a mass-stranding mechanism can be real even if most exposures never produce strandings. The endpoint requires a sequence: hear, respond, move, enter risky geometry, perhaps alter dive physiology, perhaps become impaired, beach. Remove any link and the event may stop at temporary disturbance.

Risk analysis often calls this a fault tree.

Engineers do not reject a failure mode because it requires several conditions. They identify the conditions and estimate joint probability. A nuclear plant accident, aviation loss or spacecraft failure can depend on multiple individually ordinary events aligning. Rare environmental catastrophes deserve the same reasoning.

The beaked-whale fault tree might include species susceptibility, particular sonar characteristics, high salience or novelty, deep-dive state, strong avoidance, unfavorable bathymetry and physiological consequences. We do not yet know the exact branches or probabilities. But this structure is more informative than asking whether sonar is “safe” or “unsafe.”

It also creates a route to targeted mitigation.

If certain habitats amplify consequences, avoid sonar there. If season affects beaked-whale density, schedule around it. If source approach produces stronger responses, alter exercise geometry. If gradual ramp-up helps animals leave safely, use it—but test whether it actually reduces severe responses. If prior exposure lowers risk, do not generalize that result to naïve populations.

Place-based mitigation can be especially powerful because it does not require detecting the whale at the moment of exposure.

The Canary Islands moratorium illustrates this. After repeated mass strandings, Spain prohibited relevant naval exercises in waters around the islands. Fernández and colleagues reported in 2013 that the recurrent mass-stranding pattern ceased afterward. The intervention did not need to know each whale's hearing threshold. It removed a high-risk context.

The Mariana Archipelago raises the inverse concern. NOAA scientists have noted regular beaked-whale presence, infrequent sonar use and quiet ambient conditions, suggesting a context in which novelty or salience may be greater. The agency's discussion is at https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/beaked-whale-strandings-mariana-archipelago-may-be-associated-sonar.

The contrast with Southern California is analytically useful.

Frequently exposed population, instrumented offshore range, persistence of whales. Less frequently exposed island population, quiet background, concern over strandings. These are not enough cases for a definitive natural experiment, but they motivate a comparative program.

The field could define a context vector for every well-documented exposure: received level, signal features, source range and trajectory, bathymetry, ambient noise, individual exposure history, dive state, group composition and outcome. Historical strandings would have missing data, but modern controlled experiments and operational monitoring could populate much of the matrix.

Then “context” would stop being a word and become a model.

This is the direction the science is moving, and it mirrors a broader lesson in biology. Organisms are not linear sensors. They infer, remember and choose. Even reflexes are modulated by state. A stimulus has meaning because of the system receiving it.

For the beaked whale, the dose is therefore partly outside the waveform.

It is in the animal's history.

It is in the depth beneath its body.

It is in the slope ahead.

It is in whether the sound is familiar.

It is in what the whale was doing when the ocean changed.
