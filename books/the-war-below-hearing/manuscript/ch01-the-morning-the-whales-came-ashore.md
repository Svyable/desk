# The Morning the Whales Came Ashore

On 15 March 2000 the northern Bahamas became the sort of place where causation stops being philosophical. Whales were on the beach.

The animals were not one species washed up after one obvious storm. They included beaked whales, the deep-diving cetaceans that spend much of their lives beyond ordinary human observation, and the strandings unfolded while U.S. Navy ships were operating in the region. That conjunction would eventually become the center of a joint investigation by the Navy and the National Marine Fisheries Service. The resulting report is useful partly because it does not read like a clean courtroom verdict. It reads like competent people trying to reconstruct an event from bodies, ship records, acoustic modeling, oceanography and an incomplete understanding of an animal that was then almost comically difficult to study.

The report, published in 2001 as the *Joint Interim Report: Bahamas Marine Mammal Stranding Event of 15–16 March 2000*, is still available through NOAA's institutional repository: https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/16198/. Its conclusion is stronger than some later retellings and weaker than others. The investigation team wrote that, given the temporal and geographic coincidence of the strandings with sonar use and the nature of the physiological effects, tactical mid-range-frequency sonars aboard U.S. Navy ships were the most plausible sound source involved. It also said that the source acted inside a specific environmental arrangement: propagation conditions, bathymetry, a constricted channel with limited avenues of escape, and beaked whales that appeared unusually sensitive to the projected frequencies.

That second sentence matters more than it looks.

If the event were simply a case of acoustic energy exceeding a tissue threshold, the geography would be scenery. It was not. If the whales were merely passive receivers, the geometry of escape would not belong in the causal model. It did. The report was already pointing toward the central problem that would occupy the next quarter century of research: the hazard might not be reducible to how loud the sonar was at the animal. It might depend on what the animal did after hearing it.

The bodies supplied another clue. Necropsies described hemorrhage associated with the ears and other tissues. In 2000 there was understandable interest in the possibility of direct acoustic trauma. A military sonar sounds, in ordinary language, like a thing that could simply hurt a hearing organ by being too loud. Humans know about explosions, impulse noise, ruptured eardrums, temporary threshold shifts and permanent hearing loss. That conceptual transfer was almost automatic.

But beaked whales were about to make that transfer unreliable.

They are strange even by whale standards. The family Ziphiidae includes animals that can disappear beneath the surface for an hour or more, feeding at depths where ambient pressure is hundreds of times what it is at sea level. Cuvier's beaked whales have since produced directly measured dives below 2,000 meters and beyond two hours, records that would have sounded extravagant in the early sonar-stranding literature. They hunt in darkness by echolocation. They spend relatively short periods at the surface. They can be nearly invisible to visual surveys even in places where acoustic monitoring later shows them to be regular residents.

This makes the first epistemic trap easy to see in hindsight. Before good tags and seafloor hydrophone arrays, a beaked whale was often most available to science when something had gone wrong badly enough for the animal to die or strand. The data set was therefore selected by catastrophe.

A stranded whale is information-rich and information-poor at the same time. It can be measured, scanned, dissected and sampled. Histology can reveal hemorrhage, parasites, infection, inflammation, emboli and tissue damage that no shipboard observer could detect in a live animal. But the animal has also crossed an enormous causal boundary. Beaching itself causes physiological chaos. Decomposition begins. Gas can form after death. Rescue attempts, predation and the mechanical stresses of stranding complicate the picture. The sequence of behavior that preceded landfall is largely gone.

The Bahamas case forced investigators to infer an invisible interval between sound and shore.

One can read the report now and feel the field trying to invent itself. What source levels were transmitted? How did the water column bend the sound? Which vessels were where? What frequencies reached the animals? Were explosions involved? Could the lesions be explained by pre-existing disease? Why did these species strand and others not? Why here? Why then? If sonar mattered, what mechanism connected it to the observed damage?

Those questions acquired political weight quickly because the technology was not merely industrial noise. It belonged to antisubmarine warfare. Active sonar is designed to make an ocean reveal objects that would prefer to remain hidden. A transmitting vessel sends acoustic energy into the water and listens for returns. The military value of such systems rests precisely on their ability to project sound over meaningful distances through a medium in which electromagnetic sensing is severely constrained. A whale that evolved to read an acoustic ocean was living inside the same physics that navies had learned to weaponize.

The collision had been developing for decades.

Historical reviews of beaked-whale strandings began to notice that certain unusual mass-stranding patterns appeared after the introduction and expansion of powerful naval sonars. The historical record is imperfect. A stranded whale on a sparsely populated island in 1950 is less likely to be documented with the same precision as one found after modern marine-mammal response networks existed. Naval operations themselves can be incompletely public. Species identification in older records can be poor. It is therefore dangerous to draw a bright line through one technology-introduction date and declare everything after it caused.

Still, a pattern became difficult to dismiss. Greece in 1996. The Bahamas in 2000. The Canary Islands repeatedly, including the event that would become crucial in 2002. These were not all identical circumstances, and that is important. The point is not that a single sonar setting reliably beaches a whale. The point is that a rare taxonomic group kept appearing in a rare type of event around naval acoustic activity often enough that the problem survived increasingly serious scrutiny.

This is where the language around environmental harm begins to matter. The phrase “sonar killed whales” is simultaneously useful and crude. It communicates a real concern but erases nearly every scientific distinction needed to understand it. Which sonar? At what frequency? At what source level? With what duty cycle? At what range? In what bathymetry? What received level reached which animal? Was the animal foraging, traveling, socializing or resting? Had it encountered sonar before? Did it perceive the sound as a novel threat, a predator-like cue, an annoying background or something else entirely? Did it ascend rapidly? Stop clicking? Change heading? Extend the dive? Alter vascular regulation? Accumulate a different nitrogen burden? Strand because of physiological impairment or because escape behavior took it into dangerous water?

Those are not lawyerly evasions. They are candidate causal variables.

One of the most consequential findings to emerge later was that beaked whales can show strong behavioral reactions to simulated mid-frequency sonar at received levels far below the simple numbers that dominate public arguments about loudness. In the 2013 *Biology Letters* study by Stacy DeRuiter and colleagues, two tagged Cuvier's beaked whales responded to controlled playbacks at received levels between 89 and 127 dB re 1 µPa by stopping normal fluking and echolocation, swimming rapidly and silently away, and extending the dive and subsequent non-foraging interval. The paper is available at https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2013.0223. Distant naval activity at lower received levels did not produce the same response in those observations. The message was not simply “lower threshold.” It was that context changed the meaning of the same general class of sound.

That possibility is visible, in primitive form, in the Bahamas report itself. The investigators did not identify a free-floating acoustic number that explained the strandings. They identified a source interacting with a landscape of water and an animal.

A military range is built around the fact that underwater sound is contextual. Sound speed changes with temperature, salinity and pressure. Rays bend. Layers channel energy. Seafloor and surface matter. Islands and canyons matter. Operators do not treat the ocean as a uniform bucket of water because the ocean refuses to behave like one.

The biological side eventually demanded the same sophistication.

The relevant dose could include novelty. It could include the animal's current dive state. It could include prior exposure. It could include the perceived direction and movement of the source. It could include the geometry of a channel. It could include the cost of abandoning a deep foraging dive. It could include what happens when an animal adapted to exquisite pressure management performs an emergency maneuver.

This was a difficult proposition for regulation because regulations prefer variables that can be counted before an event. Received sound level can be modeled. Distance can be measured. Source level can be specified. “What the whale thinks is happening” cannot be placed neatly into a permit, especially when the whale spends most of its life beyond view.

The Bahamas event also established another recurring tension: the Navy was both operator and indispensable data holder. This is not unique to military environmental science. Industries often possess the platforms, access, sensors and operational records needed to study their own effects. But the national-security layer makes the relationship sharper. Sonar characteristics can be sensitive. Exercise details can be operationally important. Range instrumentation may be military infrastructure. Scientists need access to data generated by systems whose first purpose is not ecology.

The resulting research ecosystem can be misread in two opposite ways. One can assume that Navy funding invalidates any reassuring result, turning every scientist into an apologist by association. Or one can assume that a joint government investigation automatically exhausts the question because official institutions have more data. Both are lazy. Funding, access and institutional incentives belong in the evidence ledger, but they do not replace methodological evaluation.

The Bahamas report deserves to be taken seriously precisely because it did not fully absolve the institution that helped produce it. Its language is careful but consequential. Tactical mid-frequency sonar was the most plausible sound source involved. Environmental factors mattered. The event remained mechanistically incomplete.

That is a useful starting posture for the whole subject: enough evidence to abandon innocence, not enough to abandon discrimination.

A few years later, the Canary Islands would supply the bodies that changed the physiological argument. Tagged whales would later supply behavior. Long-term acoustic ranges would supply movement and occupancy. Regulatory processes would supply large numerical models of exposure. A 2026 controlled-exposure study would report direct measurements from fifty-three Cuvier's beaked whales exposed to simulated military sonar, a sample size that would have seemed impossible when the Bahamas animals were being examined.

But on the morning after the first strandings, almost none of that existed.

There were beaches, whales, warships and a medium that carried sound farther than intuition does on land.

Sometimes a field begins because an observation refuses to fit the available boxes. The Bahamas whales did not fit “noise pollution” very well. They did not fit “hearing damage” very well. They did not fit a clean environmental toxicology model in which a dose enters a body and a lesion follows.

They suggested something more uncomfortable: a sensory signal could become an injury by persuading a healthy animal to use its own body differently.

That is the proposition this book will keep testing.
