# The Dive as a Controlled Emergency

A deep dive looks calm from the surface because the violence is hidden in arithmetic.

At one atmosphere, a whale breathes, oxygenates blood, fills the lungs and begins with a body arranged for surface life. Then it descends through a pressure gradient no terrestrial mammal experiences routinely. Every ten meters adds roughly another atmosphere. Gas spaces compress. Circulation changes. The animal stops breathing but continues metabolizing. Muscle becomes a major oxygen reservoir. Heart rate and peripheral blood flow shift. At some depth the lungs and alveoli compress enough that pulmonary gas exchange is greatly reduced. The whale continues downward into darkness, hunts actively, then reverses the entire pressure trajectory on the way back.

It does this again and again.

Calling such a dive “normal” is true only in the evolutionary sense. Physiologically, it is a controlled emergency repeated as a lifestyle.

This matters because safety margins in extreme systems can depend on sequence. A deep diver is not protected by one feature called “adaptation.” It survives through coordination among anatomy, circulation, behavior and timing. The failure modes therefore belong to the coordination.

The classic diving response is often taught as a set of components: bradycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction, redistribution of blood to oxygen-sensitive organs, high myoglobin concentration, collapsible lungs. Each is real. But a living whale does not activate them as a checklist. The response changes dynamically with exercise, depth, anticipation and activity. A foraging whale swimming hard after prey does not have the same circulation as a quietly gliding animal at the same depth.

The sonar problem enters at exactly this point.

A sensory disturbance can cause the animal to stop the behavior around which its physiology was being regulated and begin another one. It may accelerate. It may cease echolocation and terminate feeding. It may alter ascent. It may extend the dive. It may swim horizontally away from a source. It may change the pattern of recovery dives after surfacing. Each alteration changes the time course of oxygen use and gas exchange.

The word “panic” is too coarse for this.

Panic suggests disorganized behavior. The tag records often show organized avoidance. A whale can respond strongly and still execute a structured escape. From a physiological perspective, organized does not mean free of cost. An anti-predator response evolved for one threat geometry may become maladaptive when the “predator” is a moving acoustic source operating over long distances.

Killer whales offer a useful evolutionary comparison. Beaked whales are vulnerable to predation, and their acoustic behavior appears shaped partly by the need to avoid detection. Deep foraging clicks are produced in a restricted phase of the dive. Animals may ascend silently. Groups can coordinate surfacing. Such strategies reduce acoustic exposure to eavesdropping predators.

A naval sonar signal could engage parts of that threat-management system.

The 2011 Tyack study included killer-whale playbacks and simulated sonar. Both could elicit pronounced responses. This did not show that beaked whales mistake sonar for killer whales. It showed that naval sound can reach a behavioral circuit capable of producing predator-scale avoidance.

That is important because a predator encounter is normally temporary and spatially local. A military exercise can involve multiple platforms and repeated transmissions over a broad area. The animal's evolved escape response may not have been shaped for a threat whose apparent acoustic footprint behaves like this.

The dive then becomes a controlled emergency interrupted by another emergency.

What happens to nitrogen under those conditions is difficult to calculate because the relevant internal variables cannot be measured easily in a free-ranging whale. Researchers therefore build compartment models. Different tissues are assigned perfusion rates and gas-exchange characteristics. Lung collapse is modeled at some depth. Dive profiles are fed through the model. The output estimates tissue nitrogen tension and supersaturation through time.

Every assumption matters.

Move the lung-collapse depth and nitrogen uptake changes. Change blood-flow allocation and different tissues load differently. Change exercise and perfusion changes. Change surface interval and off-gassing changes. Models can therefore produce apparently precise curves whose uncertainty is biological, not mathematical.

This does not make modeling futile. It makes model comparison essential.

A good model should first reproduce what is known from normal diving. If it predicts catastrophic supersaturation during routine dives that healthy animals perform daily, something is missing. If it predicts no meaningful gas burden under any realistic condition, it must explain pathological observations suggesting that bubbles can form in cetaceans. The useful model occupies the uncomfortable middle: normal behavior remains generally safe, while some unusual sequences move certain tissues closer to risk.

Sonar-response dive traces are natural stress tests for such a model.

Take a baseline foraging dive and compare it with a controlled-exposure dive from the same or a comparable animal. The sonar-associated animal may stop clicking, increase horizontal movement, modify ascent and remain away from foraging longer. Feed both profiles into a range of gas-kinetic models. If none predicts a meaningful difference under any plausible physiology, the behavioral-decompression hypothesis weakens. If several independently parameterized models identify the same vulnerable phase or tissue, it strengthens.

Then compare those predictions with pathology.

Do the tissues expected to experience the highest supersaturation correspond with lesions? Are gas emboli found where the model would place them? Are there species differences consistent with different dive profiles? Can chronic lesions in animals not involved in strandings calibrate the normal background?

This program is slower than declaring the bends. It is also capable of being wrong in productive ways.

One reason the problem resists closure is that the animal's surface behavior can hide the internal cost of a dive. A whale may return to the surface and appear to breathe normally while tissues are still equilibrating. A subsequent shallow dive can change off-gassing. A series of dives matters more than one maximum depth.

This is familiar to human divers in a different form. Repetitive dive tables exist because residual nitrogen carries history forward. A beaked whale's physiology is not governed by a recreational dive table, but the principle that gas kinetics remembers previous pressure exposure still applies.

The sonar encounter also carries history.

An animal that has heard similar sonar repeatedly may respond less. One that has recently escaped a predator may respond more. An animal in poor energetic condition may pay a greater price for missed feeding. A female with a calf faces different movement constraints. A whale in steep terrain may have fewer safe routes away.

Risk therefore becomes conditional on both physiological and experiential state.

This helps explain why most sonar exposures do not produce strandings even if sonar is causal in some mass strandings. Catastrophic outcomes often live in the tails of interacting distributions. The wrong animal, at the wrong dive phase, in the wrong geography, encountering the wrong source trajectory may produce an event that thousands of other exposures do not.

Public reasoning struggles with this because rarity is used both to dismiss and dramatize.

“If sonar were dangerous, whales would strand every time” is poor reasoning if the hazard is combinatorial. “A stranding occurred during sonar, therefore sonar caused it” is equally poor if the background event rate and alternatives are not considered. The scientific question is whether the conditional probability of stranding changes under certain sonar contexts and whether observed mechanisms support that increase.

The Canary Islands provide one useful conditional case. Recurrent strandings occurred around exercises, then apparently ceased after a moratorium on relevant naval sonar exercises. The Bahamas provide another. The Mariana Archipelago has produced concern about sonar-associated beaked-whale strandings in a region where NOAA researchers noted relatively infrequent sonar use and quiet ambient conditions as possible factors increasing response severity. NOAA's summary is at https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/beaked-whale-strandings-mariana-archipelago-may-be-associated-sonar.

The Southern California range provides a contrasting case: frequent sonar, persistent Cuvier's beaked whales, measurable behavioral responses, but not a constant stream of mass strandings.

These locations are not contradictions to be averaged away. They are comparative experiments.

One hypothesis is habituation. Animals with repeated nonlethal exposure learn that certain sonar patterns do not predict immediate predation and respond less strongly. Another is selection: the most sensitive animals leave the range, leaving a tolerant subset. Another is geography: open offshore basins permit avoidance without trapping animals near shore. Another is operational: source patterns, duty cycles and exercise configurations differ. Another is demographic: local animals differ in age or group structure.

Each hypothesis predicts measurable differences.

Habituation predicts changes within individuals across repeated exposures. Selection predicts stable individual response differences and altered population composition. Geography predicts stronger stranding risk in constrained topographies even at comparable behavioral response. Operational differences predict different response curves after controlling for received level. Demographic explanations predict association with age, sex or social context.

A mature science would fit these together rather than searching for one master variable.

The dive itself supplies a useful conceptual frame because it is already a coupled system. Oxygen, nitrogen, circulation, locomotion, sensory behavior and prey pursuit cannot be separated completely in life. Our disciplines separate them because laboratories and journals require manageable questions.

The whale recombines them.

This is why the most convincing future study will probably not be a louder controlled exposure. It will be a more integrated one: high-resolution tag, source telemetry, passive acoustic array, environmental profile, individual exposure history, physiological modeling and long post-exposure follow-up. If noninvasive physiological sensors improve—heart-rate proxies, blood-oxygen estimates, perhaps novel biochemical sampling—the missing middle could narrow further.

Even then, fatal mechanism may remain partly inferential because deliberately driving an animal into injury would be unethical.

Safety science often lives with this constraint. We do not require a bridge to collapse again under controlled conditions after identifying a structural failure mode. We build models, examine wreckage, test components, reproduce subcritical behavior and change operating envelopes.

The whale case deserves the same engineering humility.

An animal capable of a three-kilometer dive is not fragile in the ordinary sense. It is extraordinarily robust inside a physiological operating envelope we only partly understand. The concern is that sonar can make the animal redraw that envelope in real time.

A body adapted to pressure is still a body governed by pressure.

The dive is safe because it is managed.

Change the manager, and the same ocean becomes a different experiment.
