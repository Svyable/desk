# Who Pays for the Evidence

The sonar literature has an institutional feature that makes everyone nervous: much of the best research exists because the Navy paid for it or supplied access to the places where it could be done.

This is not a footnote.

It is also not a verdict.

The U.S. Navy operates the sonar systems, controls important test ranges, holds detailed exercise records and funds marine-mammal research through offices such as the Office of Naval Research and environmental readiness programs. Without that infrastructure, scientists would have had far fewer opportunities to locate, tag and follow beaked whales around realistic sonar activity. Some of the papers most damaging to a simplistic “no effect” position were produced with substantial Navy support.

That fact breaks the easy corruption story.

It does not eliminate conflicts of interest.

Institutions fund research for reasons. The Navy wants to understand and mitigate environmental effects partly because strandings can harm protected species, trigger litigation, interrupt training and threaten public legitimacy. A study that identifies a workable mitigation procedure has operational value. A study that finds negligible population effects also has value. A study that discovers severe risk can impose costs.

Researchers know all of this.

They also need ships, tags, aircraft, hydrophones, permits, field teams and years of observation. Beaked-whale research is not cheap bench science. The animal can disappear for hours in an ocean measured in millions of square kilometers. A fixed Navy range with hundreds of hydrophones is an extraordinary scientific resource.

The correct epistemic response is to track dependence explicitly.

Which parts of the data were collected by military systems? Who controlled raw access? Were analysis plans independent? Did funders review manuscripts? Are negative findings publishable? Are exact sonar parameters available to peer reviewers? Can outside teams reproduce the analysis with public data? Which scientists have long-term institutional relationships with operators?

These questions are better than “was the study Navy funded?” because they identify actual pathways of bias.

Funding can influence research before a statistic is calculated.

It shapes which questions are asked. A program may prioritize mitigation-relevant thresholds over broader ecological effects. It may study species in operational areas rather than equally important habitats elsewhere. It may fund work that helps satisfy regulatory requirements. None of those choices falsifies the resulting science, but they structure the knowledge landscape.

Environmental advocacy has its own selection pressures.

Organizations litigating against sonar have reason to foreground mass strandings and severe responses. Dramatic carcass photographs communicate harm better than Bayesian abundance intervals. A study finding a low-level response can be described as proof of danger even when the measured behavior was reversible. Legal campaigns reward claims that fit statutory hooks.

Again, motive is not falsification.

Advocacy groups have also played a legitimate role in forcing environmental review, preserving records and challenging weak assumptions. Litigation can expose data that would otherwise remain obscure. Public controversy creates funding for questions institutions might prefer not to ask.

The scientific ecosystem is healthiest when these incentives oppose one another in public.

This is one reason primary documents matter so much in the sonar case.

The 2001 Bahamas report is not a press release about the report. It is the joint investigative record. Read it. The 2005 Fernández pathology paper is not an activist summary. Read it. The controlled-exposure papers contain sample sizes, received levels and response definitions. Read them. The Federal Register rules contain the agency's actual thresholds and modeled takes. Read them.

Provenance is an antidote to incentive speculation.

It does not make the sources neutral. It lets readers see what each source is positioned to know.

The Bahamas joint report is unusually important because the Navy had access to operational records outsiders lacked and nevertheless concluded that tactical mid-frequency sonar was the most plausible sound source involved. That is a claim against the simplest institutional interest. It therefore carries evidentiary weight, though it does not magically prove every mechanistic detail.

The Canary Islands pathology came from academic and veterinary teams with different institutional incentives. The Spanish moratorium created an independent policy intervention. The cessation of recurrent mass strandings afterward was reported by researchers who had examined the earlier animals.

Controlled-exposure studies often include authors affiliated with Navy-funded programs, universities and independent marine research organizations. Their results have repeatedly documented strong responses.

The overall record therefore lacks the signature of a field successfully suppressed by its funder.

It does show something subtler: access-dependent science.

Access dependency can create caution. A researcher who loses permission to work on a range may lose years of data. This does not require explicit censorship. Professional relationships can encourage measured language, avoidance of sensational claims and focus on questions acceptable to operators.

Measured language is not necessarily bias. In a controversial field, it can be a virtue.

The danger appears when access becomes nonportable. If only one research group can analyze a unique military data stream, independent verification becomes difficult. The solution is not to abandon the data. It is to maximize public release of derived data, metadata, methods and nonsensitive operational parameters.

National security imposes real limits here.

Exact sonar capabilities, tactical waveforms and submarine-detection performance can be classified for legitimate reasons. A paper cannot always disclose everything a reviewer would want. The field must then distinguish what inference can survive redaction.

If a biological conclusion depends critically on a classified source characteristic, outsiders should know that limitation. If the conclusion is robust across a public range of plausible parameters, the classification matters less.

This is a general rule for science around secret systems: classify the instrument if necessary, not the uncertainty.

The sonar case also shows how institutional incentives change over time.

Before the Bahamas event, beaked-whale sonar effects were a niche concern. After government investigators implicated Navy systems, the institution acquired a strong reason to fund research. As litigation and regulation expanded, detailed environmental analysis became a condition of continued operations. The Navy's incentive moved from not knowing to needing defensible knowledge.

This transformation happens in other hazardous technologies.

Aviation manufacturers fund crash research because unresolved hazards threaten the fleet. Pharmaceutical companies run safety studies because regulators require them. Nuclear operators monitor radiation because ignorance is operationally unacceptable. The fact that a stakeholder pays for measurement is a reason for governance, not automatic rejection.

Governance can include preregistration, independent statistical review, public protocols, data repositories and adversarial collaboration.

The 2026 Southall study is a useful model in one respect: a larger controlled-exposure sample and explicit no-sonar controls reduce dependence on anecdotal interpretation. The details of funding still matter, but better experimental design narrows the space in which incentives can operate.

Adversarial collaboration could go further.

Imagine a consortium with Navy scientists, academic bioacousticians, veterinary pathologists, environmental NGOs and statisticians agreeing in advance on response metrics and analysis thresholds. The Navy supplies access. Independent teams hold copies of nonsensitive data. Competing models are specified before exposure. The result is published regardless of direction.

Such a design would not eliminate disagreement.

It would make disagreement expensive in the right way.

Critics would have to point to data and assumptions rather than motives.

The funding question matters most when evidence is weak. If a tiny uncontrolled study aligns perfectly with the sponsor's interest, provenance should lower confidence. If a large, transparent, independently reviewed study reports inconvenient effects despite sponsor interest, provenance may actually strengthen confidence by demonstrating that the institutional system did not suppress the result.

The sonar record contains both kinds of evidence across its history.

That is why the source log in this book marks funding, authorship and document type where relevant. A government report, peer-reviewed paper, NGO statement and Navy environmental impact statement do different epistemic jobs.

None should be laundered into another.

This principle is especially important because military topics attract conspiracy thinking. Real classification creates empty spaces. Funding relationships create suspicion. Strandings are emotionally vivid. It becomes easy to imagine that a decisive hidden document exists proving either total innocence or a secret weapon effect.

The public record does not need that fantasy.

The documented story is already strange enough: a Navy sonar program contributed to a mass-stranding hazard; a joint Navy report said so; Navy-funded science later measured strong whale responses; Navy ranges became indispensable biological observatories; regulation now incorporates those findings while authorizing continued training.

That is not a cover-up narrative.

It is a contested institution learning around a technology it cannot simply abandon.

Whether it has learned enough is a separate question.

The answer should come from whales, measurements and outcomes—not from the logo on the grant.
