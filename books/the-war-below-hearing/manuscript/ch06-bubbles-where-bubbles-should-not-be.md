# Bubbles Where Bubbles Should Not Be

Gas is treacherous evidence in a dead whale.

That sentence is the reason the decompression story has never been as simple as its popular summaries. A necropsy can reveal bubbles in vessels and tissues, but death itself changes gas chemistry. Decomposition creates gas. Recovery changes pressure. Cutting open a carcass exposes spaces to air. An animal that has stranded alive may suffer circulatory collapse, overheating, crushing under its own weight and secondary vascular injury before it dies. If the scientific question is whether bubbles formed inside the animal while it was still swimming, every hour between exposure and examination matters.

This is why the Canary Islands pathology work mattered. The investigators were not merely reporting that someone saw bubbles in stranded whales. They described a pattern of acute lesions across multiple relatively fresh animals, including intravascular gas and fat emboli, hemorrhage and congestion. The distribution suggested a systemic event rather than a handful of postmortem pockets. Fernández and colleagues published the syndrome in 2005 at https://doi.org/10.1354/vp.42-4-446.

The phrase they used—“gas and fat embolic syndrome”—was deliberately more descriptive than “the bends.”

Human decompression sickness is itself a family of processes, not one lesion. Gas bubbles can obstruct blood flow, activate inflammatory pathways, damage endothelium and create neurological or musculoskeletal symptoms. In a scuba diver, the exposure history is usually reconstructable: depth, breathing gas and ascent profile can often be estimated. A whale presents a stranger problem. It breathes at atmospheric pressure before diving, collapses parts of the respiratory system at depth, redistributes blood and repeats these dives through a lifetime.

For much of twentieth-century marine-mammal physiology, that seemed to solve nitrogen risk elegantly. Lung collapse at depth should isolate air from pulmonary circulation and halt further nitrogen uptake. The animal, unlike a scuba diver, is not continuously breathing compressed gas. If gas exchange stops early enough during descent, deep pressure alone should not load tissues indefinitely.

The simplification was useful and incomplete.

Before complete alveolar collapse, gas exchange can continue. The depth at which it effectively shuts down depends on anatomy and lung volume. Different tissue compartments take up and release nitrogen at different rates. Blood flow changes radically during a dive. Repetitive diving creates histories rather than isolated exposures. Exercise alters perfusion. Some gas can remain in circulation and tissues. Models therefore began to predict conditions under which marine mammals could become supersaturated even while possessing powerful adaptations against decompression injury.

The question became empirical: do they ever show evidence of it?

Pathologists reported lesions in stranded cetaceans that looked compatible with chronic gas-bubble disease. Sperm whale bones have shown changes discussed in relation to dysbaric osteonecrosis. Gas bubbles have been detected in some stranded marine mammals under conditions where simple decomposition was not an adequate explanation. These observations did not establish sonar as a cause. They did something more foundational: they weakened the premise that diving mammals are categorically outside decompression biology.

Once that premise weakened, sonar had a plausible physiological route.

The key word is plausible.

A mechanism becomes scientifically useful when it makes discriminating predictions. If behavioral alteration produces harmful supersaturation, then risk should depend on dive history and on the kind of response. An animal near the surface should not be physiologically equivalent to one midway through a prolonged deep foraging dive. A rapid ascent should differ from a prolonged horizontal escape. Repeated avoidance dives should differ from one isolated disturbance. Species with different dive patterns and lung anatomy should have different vulnerability.

If acoustic energy directly stimulates bubble growth, the predictions change. Risk should depend more specifically on frequency, pressure amplitude, pulse structure and pre-existing gas nuclei. The physical thresholds for significant bubble activation become central. A direct mechanism would also need to explain why ordinary acoustic experiences, including the whale's own intense echolocation clicks, do not routinely produce the same injury.

If both mechanisms interact, the experiment becomes harder but not impossible. One can model supersaturation first, then ask whether realistic sonar fields change bubble dynamics at those states.

The field has spent years trying to distinguish these possibilities without the experiment everyone would reject: deliberately expose deep-diving whales until they develop gas emboli.

This ethical constraint makes pathology disproportionately important.

A stranded animal is an unplanned endpoint. It is tragic, but it offers tissue access that controlled exposure research cannot. The correct response is to extract maximum information without making the carcass say more than it can. That requires standardized necropsies, rapid response, careful gas sampling, imaging where feasible, histology and detailed exposure reconstruction.

It also requires negative cases.

What do gas distributions look like in whales that strand for reasons unrelated to sonar? How does decomposition change those distributions over time? What lesions occur in animals that die from entanglement, ship strike, infectious disease or live stranding? Without controls, every bubble risks becoming confirmatory.

The same issue appears in human forensic pathology. A lesion can be real and nonspecific. Causation depends on pattern, timing and alternatives.

The Canary Islands work gained force because the lesions appeared across several animals from one tightly timed event and because the species involved were extreme divers with a known temporal association to sonar. It also gained force later when similar mechanistic concerns appeared in other contexts. But the evidentiary class should remain clear. The pathology supports a gas/fat embolic syndrome. It does not uniquely identify which acoustic or behavioral process created it.

One of the most attractive popular sequences is “sonar causes whales to panic, they surface too fast, nitrogen forms bubbles, they beach.” It feels satisfying because each link resembles a familiar human experience. A diver comes up too quickly and gets decompression sickness. But beaked-whale responses recorded by tags are more complicated. Animals can stop clicking, accelerate, swim away silently and extend dives. Some responses involve unusually long ascent phases rather than a straight sprint to the surface.

That does not kill the decompression hypothesis. It makes the nitrogen model more interesting.

A longer dive can increase some tissue gas burdens even if ascent is slow. Elevated exercise can change blood flow. A prolonged avoidance response can alter the pattern of shallow recovery dives. A whale may change the depth at which it ventilates different tissues. The risk may come from a sequence rather than one ascent rate.

This is a recurring problem in biology: a mechanistic cartoon can survive after the actual mechanism has moved on.

The cleanest way forward is to treat the animal as a set of coupled compartments whose behavior determines the pressure-time input. Researchers can use tagged dive profiles to drive gas-kinetic models, vary assumptions about lung collapse and perfusion, and compare predicted supersaturation under baseline versus sonar-response trajectories. The output is not a diagnosis. It is a way to ask whether the observed behavioral changes are large enough to matter under plausible physiology.

Those models should then be challenged by data from animals not involved in sonar exposures. If routine deep foraging dives already create similar predicted supersaturation with no apparent injury, the model may be overestimating risk or missing protective mechanisms. If disturbed dive sequences push specific compartments into unusual states, the hypothesis gains discriminating power.

The field also needs to account for individual variation.

Age, body condition, pregnancy, health, previous dives and perhaps anatomical differences could alter gas kinetics. The whales that strand may not be a random sample of those exposed. A population might contain highly responsive individuals that leave a range, tolerant individuals that remain, and vulnerable animals whose physiology or location makes a rare exposure dangerous. This selection problem helps explain why mass strandings cannot be converted directly into a population-wide threshold.

The bubbles also complicate how we talk about “injury.”

A whale can experience subclinical gas formation without obvious impairment. Human divers can carry venous gas bubbles without symptomatic decompression sickness. Marine mammals may tolerate some bubble formation routinely. The dangerous transition may involve bubble size, location, inflammatory response, embolization or interaction with fat. A binary model—bubbles equal disease, no bubbles equal safety—will fail.

That is especially relevant to strandings because impairment does not have to be fatal at sea to become fatal near land.

A mildly disoriented or physiologically compromised deep-ocean animal can enter shallow water where its navigation and diving strategies stop working. Social species may follow one another. Beaching then adds massive secondary stress. By the time pathologists examine the carcass, primary and secondary injuries are mixed.

The geography therefore remains part of pathology.

This is one reason the Bahamas report emphasized constricted channels and bathymetry. A biological disturbance may be survivable in open ocean and catastrophic near a coast. The same internal insult can have different external outcomes depending on where it occurs.

It is also why absence of strandings on a naval range does not prove absence of biologically significant responses. An offshore population can stop feeding, move tens of kilometers or alter dives and still never encounter a beach. Strandings are a conspicuous endpoint, not a complete measure of harm.

This distinction becomes crucial in the Southern California data. Cuvier's beaked whales live on and around an active Navy range. Researchers have observed strong sonar-related movement and foraging responses, yet the site does not produce a Bahamas-like mass stranding every time sonar is used. The apparent contradiction dissolves if catastrophic stranding requires a particular intersection of exposure, behavioral response, physiological state and geography.

The bubbles, then, are not a smoking gun in the cinematic sense.

They are a clue to what kind of gun one might be looking for.

They redirect attention from the ear to the whole diver. They make the animal's pressure history relevant. They suggest that a sensory disturbance can become a circulatory problem. They explain why the same acoustic source might have different consequences depending on when and where it is heard.

They also impose a burden on skeptics and proponents alike.

A skeptic cannot simply say that breath-hold divers do not get decompression injury. The comparative and pathological literature has made that categorical claim untenable. A proponent cannot simply point to gas bubbles and declare sonar proven. Gas has alternative origins, and the exact route from sound to lesion remains incompletely measured.

This is the evidence posture the subject deserves: narrower claims, stronger links.

The verified layer is that acute gas/fat embolic lesions were documented in beaked whales from a sonar-associated mass stranding. The credible but incomplete layer is that altered diving behavior and/or acoustic interaction with supersaturated tissues contributed to those lesions. The disputed layer includes claims that one specific bubble mechanism has been demonstrated in free-ranging whales. The speculative layer asks whether similar systems-level injury can occur in other extreme divers or under other intense anthropogenic acoustic exposures.

Keeping those layers separate does not weaken the story.

It gives it edges.

A field with edges can design the next experiment. It can ask which tagged response produces the greatest modeled change in nitrogen load. It can examine whether chronically exposed populations show different diving patterns. It can collect postmortem computed tomography before decomposition advances. It can compare gas composition across cause-of-death groups. It can use ex vivo tissues and physical models to test whether realistic sonar pressures materially alter bubble nuclei. It can combine these data without requiring one study to do everything.

The strongest causal models are often assembled like this: not from one perfect observation, but from components that fail in different ways.

The sonar log can be wrong about the whale. The tag can be wrong about pathology. The carcass can be wrong about behavior. The model can be wrong about physiology. When several independent streams agree despite those different weaknesses, confidence grows.

A bubble is a tiny thing to carry this much epistemic weight.

But inside an animal built to dive kilometers beneath the surface, a bubble appears where pressure management has stopped being invisible.
