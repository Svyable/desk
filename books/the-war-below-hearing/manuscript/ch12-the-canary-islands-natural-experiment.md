# The Canary Islands Natural Experiment

A natural experiment is what scientists call an intervention they did not design but can still learn from.

The Canary Islands supplied one in 2004.

For years the archipelago had been associated with unusual beaked-whale mass strandings occurring around naval exercises. The 2002 Neo-Tapon event was the best documented: fourteen beaked whales stranded, starting about four hours after mid-frequency sonar activity began, and necropsies revealed the gas and fat embolic syndrome that changed the field. Another event followed an exercise in 2004.

Then Spain imposed a moratorium on naval exercises involving the relevant sonar in waters around the islands.

In 2013, Antonio Fernández, Manuel Arbelo and Vidal Martín wrote in *Nature* that there had been no further mass strandings of the characteristic type after the moratorium. Their correspondence is short enough that its implication can be missed. It is available at https://doi.org/10.1038/497317d.

A suspected exposure was removed, and a recurrent outcome stopped.

This is one of the stronger patterns available in environmental causation when randomized experimentation is impossible. It is also easy to overstate.

The Canary Islands did not run two identical oceans in parallel, one with sonar and one without. The number of pre-moratorium mass strandings was small. Detection effort changed over time. Naval activity outside the restricted area continued. Whale abundance and distribution can vary. Other environmental factors can change. A decade without a rare event does not produce infinite statistical certainty.

But the intervention was targeted at the suspected trigger, and the temporal break was striking enough to matter.

The evidentiary logic resembles public health before modern randomized trials. If a neighborhood repeatedly suffers cholera and cases collapse after the water source changes, the intervention is not made irrelevant because every bacterium was not traced. If aircraft accidents associated with a procedure cease after that procedure is banned, the safety signal counts even if the exact mechanical sequence differed among crashes.

Natural experiments are strongest when the intervention is specific and the predicted consequence occurs.

The Canary moratorium passes that test reasonably well.

It also interacts with the pathology. If the pre-2004 strandings had been random events unrelated to sonar, removing sonar should not obviously make them stop. If sonar were the sole cause everywhere, removing it from one region would predict local change but say little about other regions. The observed result therefore supports a context-dependent causal model particularly well: sonar plus this place plus these animals created a recurrent risk that could be reduced by changing operations.

The word “plus” is doing real work.

The Canary Islands are volcanic oceanic islands with steep bathymetry and deep-water habitat close to shore. Beaked whales use the region regularly. An animal can be at foraging depth while geographically not far from land. Sound propagation around islands can be complex. These features make the area unlike a flat open-ocean test tank.

A strong avoidance response there has different possible consequences than the same response in an offshore basin.

This is why habitat-level mitigation can outperform animal-level mitigation for rare extreme events. A bridge observer cannot reliably spot a whale that is a kilometer underwater. A passive acoustic system can miss an animal after it stops clicking. A shutdown rule based on real-time detection therefore has unavoidable blind spots. Avoiding high-risk geography removes the exposure even when the animal is invisible.

The moratorium also exposes a tension between mechanism and prevention.

Once the dangerous operation stops, the site no longer produces the events needed to study the mechanism. There are no new sonar-associated mass strandings to necropsy. No one can compare another fresh pathological series under improved imaging. A successful intervention starves the science of catastrophe.

This can create a perverse standard of proof. Skeptics may say the mechanism remains incomplete because no one demonstrated it experimentally. But the obvious experiment—resume high-risk sonar exercises until whales strand—would be ethically indefensible.

Safety science must therefore learn from subcritical experiments and historical failures.

Controlled-exposure studies are the subcritical component. They show how live whales alter behavior under carefully limited sonar exposure. Pathology from historical strandings supplies the severe endpoint. Physiology models the bridge. The moratorium supplies an intervention test. None is complete alone.

Together they make a coherent causal structure.

This structure also shows why “no strandings” is a poor universal safety metric.

The Canary intervention appears to have prevented catastrophic mass strandings locally. It does not tell us whether lower-level disturbance would have occurred under continued exercises, how many foraging dives were interrupted before 2004, or whether animals were displaced temporarily. The endpoint it removed was dramatic enough to observe from shore. Sublethal effects can continue elsewhere without producing carcasses.

Regulators therefore need a hierarchy of outcomes.

At one end is detection: the whale hears the sound. Then behavioral response: altered clicking, swimming or dive profile. Then functional cost: lost feeding or displacement. Then physiological stress or injury. Then severe injury, stranding and death. Each step is rarer and harder to measure, but more consequential.

The Canary natural experiment primarily informs the far end of that hierarchy.

This is important because critics sometimes use frequent non-catastrophic exposures to dismiss rare catastrophic ones. A Navy range can host whales year after year; therefore sonar cannot cause strandings. That inference ignores fault-tree logic. A system can operate safely under most conditions and still possess a rare failure mode.

The inverse inference is also wrong. A catastrophic failure mode does not make every routine operation catastrophic.

The Canary data fit a model in which certain combinations of species, sonar, geography and behavior create elevated stranding risk. That is more useful than a universal claim because it tells us where prevention should begin.

The next step is comparative geography.

Which sonar-associated mass strandings occurred near steep island slopes, canyons or constricted passages? Which did not? What were the source tracks relative to shore? Were whales likely driven from deep to shallow water? Were multiple transmitting platforms involved? Did environmental propagation create unusually broad or intense ensonification? Did the animals have prior exposure history?

A rigorous analysis would include negative controls: similar naval exercises in comparable habitats where no mass strandings occurred, and similar beaked-whale strandings where naval sonar was absent.

Without those controls, geography can become a just-so story.

The 2000 Bahamas investigation already pointed toward this comparative approach by emphasizing a constricted channel, propagation and limited egress. The Canaries add steep island habitat. Greece adds another geographically complex case. The Marianas add modern concern in an island setting. Southern California adds an offshore instrumented range where whales are repeatedly exposed yet persist.

The map starts to look like a hypothesis.

If geography amplifies behavioral risk, mitigation can be more intelligent than a single global decibel threshold. Navies could identify habitats where deep-diving cetacean density, slope geometry and acoustic propagation combine unfavorably. Exercises could be moved, seasonalized or modified there while continuing elsewhere under monitoring.

This is the practical virtue of causal specificity. It creates options between “ban sonar” and “do nothing.”

The Canary Islands also provide a rare case in which a government treated environmental uncertainty as sufficient for decisive operational change. That is politically interesting because military sonar sits behind a national-security rationale. Restricting exercises is not costless from the operator's perspective. Training areas are chosen for logistical, tactical and diplomatic reasons. A moratorium therefore reveals that the evidence had crossed some institutional threshold even before every mechanistic question was closed.

The decision itself is part of the historical evidence.

Governments act for many reasons, including public pressure. A policy change does not prove the scientific claim motivating it. But when authorities repeatedly confronted with strandings alter operations and the strandings cease, that sequence deserves to be in the causal ledger.

The Canary case also warns against treating secrecy as the dominant epistemic obstacle.

Military details can be classified or incomplete, but much of the crucial evidence here is public: dates of exercises, strandings, necropsies, published pathology and the moratorium. The hardest unknown is not hidden in a safe. It is inside the whale.

What physiological state existed minutes before beaching? How did the animal interpret the sound? Which route did it swim? Did bubbles form before disorientation or after? Did social behavior pull healthy companions toward shore? Those questions survive even with perfect access to the sonar log.

This is a useful corrective for anomalous subjects more broadly. Secrecy can obscure evidence, but not every gap is evidence of secrecy. Sometimes nature is simply difficult to measure.

The beaked-whale story benefits from this discipline because military classification attracts speculation easily. One can imagine secret sonar modes, classified intensities or exotic effects whenever a public mechanism remains incomplete. That move is unnecessary. The documented systems and observed responses are already sufficient to create a serious scientific problem.

No hidden weapon is required.

Nor does the public record justify saying every relevant operational parameter is known. Exercise details can be incomplete, especially historically. The correct posture is narrower: work with documented exposures, mark missing parameters, and do not use the missing values as permission to invent capabilities.

The natural experiment remains powerful under that restraint.

Repeated mass strandings occurred in a known beaked-whale habitat associated with naval exercises. Detailed pathology from 2002 identified an acute gas/fat embolic syndrome. Spain restricted the relevant exercises in 2004. The characteristic mass strandings stopped.

One can contest the strength of each arrow.

It is difficult to make all of them disappear at once.

That is what convergence looks like in field science.

The most interesting consequence may be that a place became safer before the mechanism became complete. This reverses the usual demand for certainty. Prevention did not wait for a sensor inside every whale.

The whales stopped coming ashore.

Sometimes that is not the end of the scientific argument.

It is the reason there can still be one.
