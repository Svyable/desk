# 10 — Satellites Enter the Experiment

Telstar 1 launched on July 10, 1962.

One day after Starfish Prime.

The timing is so neat it sounds invented. The engineering record is better.

Telstar was not merely another satellite. Built by Bell Labs for AT&T and launched by NASA, it was a machine for turning orbit into communications infrastructure. It relayed television and telephone signals across the Atlantic and became a symbol of exactly the civilian future space was about to acquire.

It also carried radiation instruments. That accident of design matters because Telstar did not simply become a victim of the artificial belt. It measured the environment that was damaging it.

The failure began as a command problem, not a cinematic death in orbit. Bell Labs engineers recorded degraded command performance before the command function was lost on November 4, 1962. The spacecraft could still transmit. The trouble lay in redundant command decoders whose transistors had been exposed to the enhanced radiation environment.

That distinction gave the engineers something to work with.

They tried ordinary commands, continuous commands, commands from Johannesburg, and specially modified command patterns that avoided the more vulnerable parts of the decoder logic. They also exploited periods when power could be removed from the command circuitry. On January 3, 1963, both decoders had recovered enough for normal operation to resume.

The recovery is as important as the failure.

The contemporaneous Bell System analysis did not claim a single magical fix. Laboratory tests showed that damaged transistors could improve when operating conditions changed, while the orbital radiation intensity was also falling. By January 1, Telstar's average radiation flux had dropped below one-fifth of its mid-November value and below one-tenth of what it had measured in July. The spacecraft was moving through a changing dose field while engineers were changing the electrical conditions inside the failing circuit.

Then the command malfunction returned on February 21.

This is what “Starfish damaged Telstar” looks like when the slogan is unfolded: semiconductor surface damage, a vulnerable circuit, a radiation field that varied with both belt decay and orbit, partial loss of command authority, improvised workarounds, apparent recovery, and relapse.

The phrase “Starfish killed Telstar” is close enough to truth to be dangerous because it erases the mechanism.

Radiation damage is cumulative and component specific. Energetic particles penetrate shielding according to their energy and the material in front of them. They can produce ionization, displacement damage, charging and secondary bremsstrahlung. Solar cells degrade. Semiconductor thresholds shift. Dielectrics accumulate charge. Different component technologies fail in different ways.

Telstar's own radiation experiment made that visible. Bell Labs investigators used the measured particle exposure to estimate ionization at different depths in the spacecraft and to connect the environment with semiconductor degradation. In the main power plant, they found proton and electron contributions to the measured damage were of comparable importance. The satellite was becoming, unwillingly, a calibrated radiation-effects experiment.

Ariel 1 provides a different failure mode. It was already in orbit when Starfish occurred. A later NASA account records that on July 12—three days after the detonation—the satellite began alternating between good transmissions, periods of silence and periods when only an unmodulated carrier came through. Investigators traced the behavior to the undervoltage cutout system and a reduction in solar-cell output. The same account reports reasonable agreement between observed power degradation and the roughly 20 percent decline predicted from the trapped Starfish electrons and the shielding over Ariel's solar cells.

Its tape recorder failed permanently around August 1.

Two satellites, two architectures, two kinds of trouble. Telstar's command electronics became unreliable and temporarily recoverable. Ariel's power system lost margin as its solar array degraded. Neither story fits comfortably inside the verb “destroyed.”

That matters for every casualty list that followed.

TRAAC and Transit 4B are frequently included among Starfish casualties or heavily affected spacecraft. Soviet Kosmos spacecraft appear in some retrospective lists. Later summaries sometimes say that seven satellites failed within months, many through solar-cell damage, or compress the episode into the claim that Starfish disabled a large fraction of the satellites then in orbit.

Those statements are useful only if the denominator survives inspection.

A spacecraft can be degraded, partially disabled, intermittently recoverable, mission-limited or rendered useless while continuing to transmit. It can fail from radiation, from an unrelated defect, or from several insults acting together. A modern satellite-victim table therefore needs more than names. It needs orbit, subsystem, onset date, measured or modeled dose, failure signature, contemporaneous diagnosis, recovery history and competing causes.

The variation is not noise around the story. It is the story.

Why did one spacecraft fail quickly and another persist? Orbit. Shielding. Electronics. Solar-array design. Component margins. Dose history. Prior damage. Operational recovery. The artificial belt supplied the environment; spacecraft engineering determined how that environment became failure.

Telstar is especially revealing because the causal chain can be watched almost in real time. A nuclear test on July 9 changes the trapped-particle population. A communications satellite launched July 10 measures that altered environment. Months later its command electronics degrade. Engineers modify how the circuit is operated. The radiation field weakens. The command system recovers. Then the malfunction returns.

That is a far better historical experiment than a list of dead spacecraft.

It also makes modern extrapolation harder. Electronics in 1962 were primitive by current standards but often physically larger and sometimes tolerant of effects that trouble dense modern devices less, while vulnerable in other ways modern spacecraft are specifically designed to avoid. Today's radiation-hard components, shielding models and environmental standards are vastly better. At the same time, spacecraft now carry more integrated electronics, depend on more tightly coupled services and occupy a much denser orbital economy.

Low-Earth constellations may replace failed units quickly. Geostationary assets cannot. Different orbits cross different magnetic shells. A belt created at one injection geometry will not dose every spacecraft equally. A component that survives total ionizing dose may still be vulnerable to charging, single-event effects or degraded power margin.

There is no honest scalar called “percentage of satellites destroyed.”

A serious modern assessment must couple a time-evolving radiation-belt model to the actual orbital population and then to component-level dose-response and failure models. It must also follow what happens after the first spacecraft fails: loss of positioning and timing, communications bottlenecks, collision tracking, weather data, financial synchronization and military command links.

Most of those dependencies barely existed in 1962.

Telstar makes the historical point without requiring a hypothetical modern catastrophe. The first commercial communications satellite carried instruments capable of measuring the radiation environment that was slowly breaking its own command system. Civilization had begun to build infrastructure in orbit one day after demonstrating that the orbital environment itself could be made hostile.

No novelist would be allowed the timing. The transistor curves are what make it real.
