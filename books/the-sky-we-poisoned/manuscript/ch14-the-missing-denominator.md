# 14 — The Missing Denominator

How many satellites did Starfish Prime kill?

It is the wrong question in exactly the way irresistible questions often are.

A widely repeated modern summary says that of roughly twenty-four satellites in orbit, Starfish damaged at least one-third. That statistic is powerful because the denominator is tiny enough to picture. Eight machines out of twenty-four. Space itself appears to have become lethal.

But the statistic bundles different categories: immediate anomaly, accelerated degradation, complete failure, probable radiation contribution, and historical attribution. It also treats “satellite” as if a scientific detector, navigation test article and communications spacecraft had the same orbit and electronics.

They did not.

A proper denominator begins with every spacecraft operating during the relevant interval. For each one we need orbital elements, shielding, component technology, pre-Starfish health, radiation measurements along the orbit, subsequent anomaly chronology, solar events, commanded operations and failure mode.

Then we need a counterfactual: what would likely have happened without Starfish?

That is harder.

Telstar supplies unusually strong evidence because its command-system degradation was investigated in detail and the artificial radiation environment was measured. Ariel 1 project personnel suspected Starfish-related damage soon after the event. Other cases are less tidy.

The danger is anthology science.

Collect enough damaged spacecraft, mention that they all flew after July 9, and the human mind builds causality for free. A rigorous history needs the survivors just as badly. What shielding or orbit kept them alive? Which spacecraft crossed the belt and did not fail? Which experienced degradation but completed mission objectives? Which failed for clearly unrelated reasons?

The survivors estimate susceptibility.

This logic generalizes to every anomaly field. Famous successes without trial denominators inflate apparent effect size. Remote-viewing stories need every task, not only striking transcripts. UAP sensor incidents need total flight hours and false-alarm rates. Medical side-effect stories need exposed populations. Satellite casualty claims need the fleet.

Starfish is unusually favorable because the fleet was small enough that a complete reconstruction is conceivable.

That reconstruction should be done.

The book's publication-grade edition needs a table with one row per spacecraft in orbit during the relevant months. Columns: launch date, mission, orbit, shielding estimate, major electronics, solar-array type, Starfish belt intersection, measured or modeled dose, anomaly onset, failure date, engineering attribution and confidence class.

The result may make the headline weaker.

Good.

If the event damaged fewer spacecraft than folklore says, we learn which vulnerabilities mattered. If the one-third figure survives, we have something stronger than repetition.

The same denominator problem infects ground effects. How many Hawaiian streetlight circuits existed? Which ones failed? What lengths and orientations were involved? What protective devices? How many communications systems experienced no anomaly?

Without non-events, coupling models can fit too easily.

The missing denominator is partly archival. Weapons-effects reports emphasize detections. Spacecraft histories emphasize mission anomalies. Survivors do not produce dramatic memos. Data preservation follows institutional interest.

Classification adds another asymmetry. Some U.S. measurements remained nonpublic for decades. Russian/Soviet records are uneven. Contemporary scientists therefore inherit a sample shaped by secrecy as well as physics.

The correct conclusion is not that hidden data overturn the public story. Hidden data cannot be used that way. The conclusion is that uncertainty bars should include archival selection.

That is a rare habit in historical physics.

We are comfortable propagating detector noise through an equation. We are less comfortable propagating the probability that an entire class of reports was never declassified, lost, or never written because nothing happened.

The denominator is a form of instrumentation.

Build it, and the history becomes an experiment again.
