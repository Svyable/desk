# 11 — The Soviet Sky Tests

The United States was not alone in discovering that the upper atmosphere could be weaponized as apparatus.

The Soviet Union conducted its own series of high-altitude nuclear tests, commonly discussed under the Project K label. The open record includes five tests across 1961 and 1962, with three larger 1962 shots occurring over Kazakhstan. Later English-language technical accounts connect the K series to missile-defense development and report severe electromagnetic effects on long communications and power lines.

Here the archival footing changes.

For Starfish, a researcher can move through NASA reports, U.S. government histories, contemporaneous Western journals, spacecraft records and modern Livermore simulations. The Soviet record available in English is patchier. Some details come from Russian memoirs, post-Soviet technical papers and U.S.–Russian exchanges decades later.

The correct response is not to ignore them. It is to label the provenance.

One of the 1962 Soviet high-altitude tests, often called K-3, is widely reported as a roughly 300-kiloton detonation near 290 kilometres altitude on October 22. That date should stop the reader: the Cuban Missile Crisis was unfolding.

Nuclear weapons were being detonated in near-Earth space while superpower leaders were simultaneously managing the most dangerous nuclear confrontation of the Cold War.

The K tests reportedly instrumented long telephone and power lines to measure electromagnetic coupling. Later technical accounts describe large currents, blown protective devices and damage to electrical infrastructure. These observations are valuable because they differ from the Hawaiian case in geometry and network design.

They also invite overstatement.

A later report that hundreds of kilometres of line experienced protection failures can mutate into a claim that a nuclear burst “destroyed the Soviet grid.” It did not. The infrastructure was deliberately instrumented in a test region. Some equipment failed dramatically. The proper object of study is the measured response of long conductors.

This distinction matters because Soviet K data are often used in modern EMP argument as if they were a controlled contemporary grid experiment. They were not. Soviet electrical networks, line construction, grounding and protection in 1962 differ from modern systems. The test remains uniquely valuable, but uniqueness should increase caution rather than reduce it.

The Soviet tests also produced artificial radiation belts. NASA's 1972 review notes narrow and broad injected electron populations from U.S. and Soviet high-altitude detonations. In other words, the magnetosphere did not care about ideology.

Two adversaries independently discovered the same externality.

This convergence is more interesting than conspiracy. When separate weapons programs operate under the same physical laws, they often find similar effects. Convergent state interest can therefore be evidence that a phenomenon is strategically salient without proving that every claimed application worked.

The same logic applies to psi programs, hypersonics, directed energy, submarine detection and exotic propulsion. States investigate things because the cost of missing a real adversary capability can exceed the cost of funding uncertain research. A classified program proves interest first. Efficacy requires another evidentiary step.

For the K tests, efficacy in one narrow sense is established: high-altitude nuclear explosions can produce large electromagnetic and magnetospheric effects. The open question is the strategic usefulness planners attached to them.

The timing with the Cuban Missile Crisis also reveals an arms-race pathology. Both sides conducted high-altitude testing during periods of extreme mistrust. A distant observer detecting a nuclear burst or unusual electromagnetic effect could not assume it was merely scientific. Test and attack shared signatures.

This is where physics meets signaling.

An experiment that modifies the ionosphere can interfere with the very communications systems governments rely on to interpret each other. A weapons-effects test can therefore change both the physical environment and the epistemic environment of a crisis.

That is not speculation about October 1962 causing a specific misinterpretation. There is no need to invent one. It is a structural fact: nuclear testing, radar, communications and strategic warning occupied the same technical ecosystem.

Project K also complicates the comforting idea that Starfish alone taught the world to stop. The superpowers were not each waiting to be shocked by one American experiment. They were conducting their own high-altitude programs inside a broader atmospheric-testing race.

The Limited Test Ban Treaty closed the domain because multiple pressures converged: fallout, verification politics, public fear, arms-race fatigue, the Cuban crisis and recognition that atmospheric and space testing carried transboundary consequences.

Starfish and K made the transboundary physics impossible to deny.

The magnetic field had become an arms-control participant.
