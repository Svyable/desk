# 12 — The Fishbowl

Starfish Prime was not the only U.S. attempt to detonate nuclear devices at high altitude in 1962. Operation Fishbowl was a sequence of planned shots, failures, partial failures and successful detonations that makes the phrase “the Starfish test” dangerously tidy.

Bluegill. Kingfish. Checkmate. Names designed to sound harmless enough for a test calendar.

The sequence matters because it provides variation.

Different yields and altitudes produced different signatures. Some launch attempts failed catastrophically. Successful shots later in 1962 generated magnetotelluric and VLF effects recorded at distant stations. A *Nature* paper by Santirocco and Parker described signatures from several of the smaller U.S. high-altitude explosions as observed near Rochester, New York.

This is not a footnote to Starfish. It is the beginning of a parameter study.

If Starfish is one giant perturbation, the smaller Fishbowl events let researchers ask how response scales with yield, altitude and geometry. In a normal laboratory one would design a systematic sweep. In a weapons program the sweep is constrained by launch vehicles, device designs and military objectives. Still, nature sees the inputs and returns data.

One experiment became particularly audacious in method. A 1962 high-altitude nuclear explosion was used as a neutron source for a time-of-flight measurement of uranium-233 fission cross sections. Sounding rockets carrying fission counters were positioned so that neutrons from the distant explosion traversed a flight path on the order of 1,280 kilometres before measurement. Richard Albert published the results in *Physical Review* in 1966.

Read that twice.

A nuclear weapon detonated in space became the source in a neutron physics experiment whose baseline was a significant fraction of a continent.

This is the sort of detail that makes Cold War science hard to caricature. The same program capable of environmental recklessness could produce genuinely ingenious measurement. Scientists turned an event of absurd scale into apparatus.

The neutron experiment also sharpens the distinction between source knowledge and environmental knowledge. For time-of-flight work, a short, intense neutron pulse and known geometry are assets. For magnetospheric response, the same detonation is an uncontrolled perturbation with many coupled channels.

Scale can simplify one measurement and complicate another.

Fishbowl failures further demonstrate that every planned high-altitude event had an accident envelope. Bluegill attempts experienced launch and pad mishaps before a later successful shot. Johnston Island itself suffered contamination and infrastructure damage from failed nuclear-armed launches.

These accidents are often excluded from the grand physics story because they happened before the interesting altitude. That is a systems-engineering mistake.

The environmental risk of a high-altitude nuclear test equals the risk conditional on successful detonation plus the risk of everything required to put the device there. A rocket carrying a nuclear package is part of the experiment.

This becomes relevant to any future proposal involving nuclear power or propulsion in space. The benefit may occur in orbit. The failure can occur on the pad.

Fishbowl also helps disentangle prompt electromagnetic effects from persistent radiation belts. Smaller or lower-altitude shots produced strong local and distant electromagnetic signatures without recreating Starfish's long-lived belt at the same scale. That tells us the two phenomena are related through the source but not interchangeable.

One can have spectacular EMP without the same persistent trapped population. One can alter radio propagation without producing a year-long hazard at every orbit. Yield and altitude steer energy into different channels.

This sounds elementary. Public discussion routinely forgets it.

The 1962 series therefore deserves to be read as a multidimensional response surface: altitude on one axis, yield on another, geomagnetic position on another, atmospheric density on another, instrumentation geometry on another.

No single event maps the whole surface.

The treaty arrived before the experimenters could finish.

That statement should not be read with regret. Closing the atmospheric and outer-space nuclear laboratory was a civilizational achievement. It also means the scientific response surface remains sparsely sampled.

Modern simulation tries to interpolate between forbidden data points.

The better the simulation becomes, the more valuable those old points are.
