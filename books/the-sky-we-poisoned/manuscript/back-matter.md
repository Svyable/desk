# Back Matter

## Chronology

**1958 — Discovery and intervention.** Explorer-era measurements establish Earth's radiation belts. Nicholas Christofilos advances the artificial-belt idea. Operation Hardtack includes Teak (Aug. 1) and Orange (Aug. 12), large high-altitude nuclear tests near Johnston Island. Operation Argus detonates three low-yield devices over the South Atlantic on Aug. 27, Aug. 30 and Sept. 6, creating measurable artificial electron belts.

**1961 — Testing resumes.** The Soviet Union resumes nuclear testing after the moratorium and conducts early Project K high-altitude work.

**June 20, 1962 — First Starfish attempt fails.** The Thor launch vehicle malfunctions and the nuclear package is destroyed, contaminating the Johnston Island area.

**July 9, 1962, 09:00:05 UTC — Starfish Prime.** Approximately 1.4 Mt detonates at roughly 400 km altitude near Johnston Island. Optical, ionospheric, magnetic, electromagnetic and trapped-particle effects are observed.

**July 10, 1962 — Telstar 1 launches.** The early communications satellite enters an environment already altered by Starfish.

**July–November 1962 — Artificial belt and satellite effects.** Injun and other instruments measure enhanced radiation. Multiple spacecraft experience degradation/failure later associated to varying degrees with the artificial belt.

**October–November 1962 — Soviet high-altitude tests.** Large Project K detonations over Kazakhstan generate strong electromagnetic effects and artificial trapped-particle populations.

**Late 1962 — Fishbowl continuation.** Additional U.S. high-altitude shots and failed attempts provide further electromagnetic and geophysical measurements.

**August 5, 1963 — Limited Test Ban Treaty signed.** United States, Soviet Union and United Kingdom sign in Moscow.

**October 10, 1963 — Treaty enters into force.** Atmospheric, underwater and outer-space nuclear explosions are prohibited for parties.

**1960s–1970s — Radiation-belt physics matures.** Artificial-belt measurements become benchmarks for trapping and loss theory; satellite radiation engineering grows out of hard experience.

**2006 onward — Declassified data reenter science.** Historical Starfish records support new analysis and modeling.

**2025 — First-principles reconstruction.** Belyaev, Larson & Cohen publish particle-in-cell simulations of the Starfish diamagnetic cavity and electromagnetic response in *JGR: Space Physics*.

**August 10, 2026 — Old belt, new physics.** Olifer & Mann publish a digitized reconstruction of historical Starfish electron measurements, finding evidence consistent with Kennel–Petschek-like self-limiting behavior below about 2 MeV.

## What would change the book's thesis

The book's narrow physical thesis would be weakened if primary reanalysis showed that the supposed artificial belts were largely instrumental artifacts, that long-lived Starfish enhancement was grossly overestimated, or that major satellite degradations attributed to the belt were demonstrably unrelated. Existing contemporaneous and modern evidence makes those outcomes unlikely.

The modern-risk synthesis would be weakened if coupled belt/orbit/electronics modeling showed that realistic contemporary spacecraft architectures are broadly insensitive to even severe artificial-belt scenarios, or that natural self-limiting mechanisms collapse relevant fluxes before meaningful integrated dose accumulates. That remains an empirical/modeling question and should not be prejudged.

## Highest-value research still needed

1. Recover a complete spacecraft-by-spacecraft 1962 casualty/survivor denominator with engineering failure records.
2. Obtain and digitize all public Injun, STARAD and sounding-rocket particle measurements with calibration metadata.
3. Seek release of older DTRA-held STARAD records described in the 2026 literature where classification no longer serves a compelling purpose.
4. Build a synchronized Starfish event archive joining magnetometers, ionosondes, VLF, optical photometry, particle detectors, weapon timing, geomagnetic state and solar conditions.
5. Reconstruct Soviet Project K from Russian-language primary engineering and scientific records rather than Western summaries.
6. Couple validated source/belt models to modern orbital populations and component dose-response distributions without publishing operational targeting recipes.
7. Compare artificial-belt loss against extreme natural radiation-belt events to identify resilience measures useful under both causes.

## Note on secrecy

This manuscript uses only lawfully public information. It does not claim access to classified weapons-effects data, current operational plans or nonpublic vulnerability assessments. Missing classified records are treated as missing data, never as evidence for a preferred conclusion.
