# The Sky We Poisoned

## Starfish Prime, Artificial Radiation Belts, and the Forgotten Physics of Nuclear Space

### Sven Hardy Benson

There is a species of experiment we no longer know how to repeat because we became civilized enough, frightened enough, or dependent enough on the apparatus around us that repeating it would be insane.

Starfish Prime belongs to that species.

At 09:00:05 universal time on July 9, 1962, a thermonuclear device detonated roughly four hundred kilometres above the Pacific near Johnston Island. Four hundred kilometres is an awkward number. It is too high for the word atmosphere to feel comfortable and too low for outer space to feel empty. The International Space Station lives in that neighborhood. So do many satellites. In 1962 there were barely enough satellites for anyone to think of the region as infrastructure.

The device yielded about 1.4 megatons. The first-order effects were theatrical: a flash, artificial aurora, a luminous sky. The second-order effects were more interesting. The explosion ionized a volume of near-Earth space, pushed against the geomagnetic field, launched electromagnetic disturbances around the planet, and loaded trapped electron populations into magnetic shells that had only recently been discovered. The Earth had natural radiation belts. Now it also had one made by people.

The belt lasted.

That is the sentence that changes the book.

A blast is an event. A radiation belt is an environment. An environment can outlive the decision that created it and become someone else's operating condition. Telstar 1 launched the day after Starfish Prime into a near-Earth environment that had been altered by a weapon test. It later failed after radiation damage. Other spacecraft suffered. The exact accounting is messier than the folklore, which is why the accounting matters.

The oddity did not begin with Starfish. Nicholas Christofilos had already proposed that beta electrons from high-altitude nuclear explosions could be trapped by Earth's magnetic field. Operation Argus tested the idea with three small devices over the South Atlantic in 1958. It worked. The military-scientific machine had learned that a bomb could be used not simply against a point on a map but as an injector into a planetary field.

That phrase—planetary field—sounds grandiose until one remembers what a magnetosphere is. It belongs to everyone underneath it. No border contains it. No owner maintains it. A perturbation injected into it does not ask which satellite is military, commercial, scientific, Soviet, American, British, or merely unlucky.

The book is not an argument that high-altitude nuclear explosions would end civilization. Nor is it a nostalgia piece about secret Cold War science. It is an attempt to recover an experiment whose results became more important after the laboratory was closed.

The Limited Test Ban Treaty entered into force in October 1963 and prohibited nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, underwater, and in outer space for its parties. The treaty was driven by more than Starfish. Fallout, arms-race politics, verification, and the Cuban Missile Crisis all mattered. But one consequence is that no comparable high-altitude test has been conducted by the original signatories since. The best empirical dataset for some classes of nuclear-magnetospheric coupling therefore comes from instrumentation designed when memory was measured in magnetic tape and satellite fleets could be counted without a database.

That data is not dead. In 2025 Lawrence Livermore researchers published particle-in-cell simulations reproducing important pieces of Starfish Prime's diamagnetic-cavity evolution and electromagnetic response from first principles. In August 2026, L. Olifer and I. R. Mann published a reconstruction of the artificial electron belt from digitized Injun and STARAD measurements, arguing that the flux may have approached a Kennel–Petschek-like self-limiting level below about 2 MeV. Their paper contains a sentence I cannot get out of my head: much of the public data had to be recovered by digitizing figures from old papers. A planetary experiment was performed once, and sixty-four years later physicists are reading its trace with a virtual ruler.

This is how the book will proceed. When a fact is documented, I will call it documented. When a mechanism is reconstructed, I will say what the reconstruction assumes. When a satellite failure is attributed to Starfish, I will distinguish strong engineering evidence from historical repetition. When modern vulnerability is discussed, I will not smuggle classified capability into the text by pretending a public estimate is intelligence.

The central question is more durable than the weapon: what happens when a technological civilization discovers that it can modify a shared planetary environment faster than it can understand the consequences?

We have asked that question about climate, oceans, ecosystems, radio spectrum, orbital debris, and now artificial intelligence. We asked it first, in a more literal way than most people know, with bombs in the sky.
