# 17 — Reconstructing a Forbidden Experiment

The Starfish Prime tapes became more valuable after nobody was allowed to make new ones.

Some important magnetic data from the 1962 event remained classified for decades. When records were eventually released, modern researchers inherited a peculiar calibration target: measurements from a forcing event too dangerous and legally constrained to reproduce.

This is a dream problem for computational physics and a nightmare for validation.

A model can be made arbitrarily sophisticated. Without data from the relevant regime, sophistication is decoration. Starfish supplies data from an extreme regime.

The Topanga work published in 2025 demonstrates the opportunity. Lawrence Livermore researchers used an ion-kinetic particle-in-cell framework to simulate the expanding debris plasma, diamagnetic cavity and late electromagnetic response. The computational domain spans thousands of kilometres vertically and huge angular extents of Earth. The model follows the cavity for tens of seconds—an eternity for kinetic plasma computation.

It reproduces important observed behavior.

That does not make it “the answer.” Validation should be plural. Magnetic-field traces, timing, cavity evolution, ionospheric response and particle injection constrain different pieces of the simulation. A model that fits one waveform can still get the particle distribution wrong.

This is where the old experiment can be improved without repeating it.

The data should be reconstructed as one synchronized event archive.

Every surviving magnetometer trace. Every ionosonde. Optical photometry. VLF measurements. Satellite particle counts. Rocket telemetry. Weapon timing. Geomagnetic indices. Solar conditions. Spacecraft ephemerides. Ground electrical reports. Calibration records. Instrument saturation notes.

The event needs a data lake sixty-four years late.

There is precedent. Olifer and Mann effectively built part of this archive manually by digitizing historical particle-flux figures. Their paper also notes that more complete STARAD data are reportedly curated by the Defense Threat Reduction Agency but were not available to them. That is an important, bounded archival fact. It does not justify speculating about what the unavailable measurements show.

It does justify asking for release.

The scientific case for declassifying obsolete measurements can be strong when the physics remains relevant and the weapon-design sensitivity is low or can be redacted. Old geospace data can validate models used for satellite resilience without revealing modern capabilities.

This is one of the rare areas where historical declassification could directly improve contemporary physical science.

The reconstruction should also include negative data. Instruments that saw nothing constrain propagation. Satellites that did not degrade constrain dose models. Ground networks unaffected by the pulse constrain coupling.

A model should be forced to predict the absences.

The final step is uncertainty quantification. Historical measurements have digitization errors, unknown calibration drifts, sparse spatial coverage and timing uncertainty. Weapon parameters themselves may be known imperfectly in public. Rather than choose one best-fit simulation, researchers should infer ensembles of source conditions consistent with the full archive.

Then ask what modern observables those ensembles predict.

This converts a weapons-effects anecdote into a benchmark problem.

The benchmark could be open.

Imagine a public Starfish challenge dataset with masked validation channels. Plasma codes submit predictions of magnetic traces, electron spectra and ionospheric response. Models are scored on held-out measurements. No one gets to adjust after seeing the answer.

The experiment cannot be repeated.

The analysis can still be preregistered.

That would be a fitting end to the scientific arc: a secret Cold War explosion reborn as an open adversarial model test.
