# 7 — Hawaii, 1,300 Kilometres Away

The Hawaiian streetlights are one of those historical facts that became less useful each time somebody repeated them.

Starfish Prime detonated roughly 1,300 kilometres from Hawaii. Electrical effects were observed there. Streetlights failed. Burglar alarms went off. A microwave communications link was disrupted. The event helped make electromagnetic pulse a public idea.

Then the sentence inflated.

In retelling, a collection of local electrical failures becomes “Hawaii went dark.” The transformation is narratively efficient and technically destructive.

A high-altitude nuclear electromagnetic pulse is not a magic field called EMP that turns electricity off. It is a time-dependent electromagnetic disturbance with components arising from different physical processes and timescales. How it couples to hardware depends on geometry, conductor length, orientation, frequency response, grounding, protection, network topology and device susceptibility.

If three hundred streetlights on a 1962 island circuit fail, the engineering question is not whether electricity is fragile. It is what voltage and current transients appeared where, through what coupling path, with what protective devices, and why neighboring systems behaved differently.

The low infrastructure density of 1962 cuts both ways. There was less to damage, but the systems that existed were not designed against modern HEMP standards. Hawaiian streetlight strings used long conductors that offered efficient coupling paths. Today's grid has far more electronics, long lines and control systems, but it also has surge protection, grounding standards, sectionalization and a half-century of electromagnetic compatibility engineering. The correct comparison is not “more electronics equals more damage.” It is a network calculation.

Starfish became foundational partly because the event was large enough to reveal physical channels people had underestimated. The burst-generated plasma cavity and ionization altered the geomagnetic environment. Prompt gamma interactions generated fast electron populations that contributed to early-time pulse components. Slower magnetohydrodynamic and geomagnetic evolution contributed to late-time fields. Modern literature separates these into E1, E2 and E3 families, though the historical event did not arrive labeled for the engineers.

The 2025 Topanga work is especially interesting on E3 because it uses a kinetic plasma model to reconstruct the global response rather than treating the late-time field as a fitted waveform. Belyaev and colleagues simulate the expanding diamagnetic cavity, the induced current systems and how the electromagnetic disturbance reaches the ground through and around a heavily ionized region.

This is old weapons physics becoming modern plasma physics.

It is also where a common analogy needs repair. Solar geomagnetic storms can drive geoelectric fields in long conductors and threaten power systems. High-altitude nuclear explosions can also generate late-time geomagnetic disturbances. That does not make the sources equivalent. A coronal mass ejection drives the magnetosphere from outside for hours. Starfish inserted an explosive plasma source inside near-Earth space. Their spectra, spatial patterns and temporal evolution differ.

Engineers may use related models. Nature does not owe us the same waveform.

The strongest claim we can make from Hawaii is therefore narrower than the folklore and more consequential. A nuclear explosion hundreds of kilometres above Earth produced measurable electrical disturbances more than a thousand kilometres away, and some ordinary infrastructure malfunctioned. The event demonstrated that a line-of-sight blast radius is the wrong mental model for electromagnetic coupling in near-Earth space.

That is enough.

The weaker historical record concerns exact damage counts and causes. Popular sources repeat approximately three hundred streetlights. Some mention alarms and telephone links. Those details should be preserved where traceable, but not promoted into a quantitative modern grid-loss model. The 1962 distribution system was its own organism.

The Soviet K tests later provided a second, differently instrumented family of long-line observations. Soviet engineers reportedly measured severe induced currents on long telephone and power lines during high-altitude shots over Kazakhstan. Those records are valuable because they attack the same coupling problem with different geography and infrastructure. They are also harder for an English-language researcher to reconstruct to primary-document standards.

This asymmetry matters. The United States has declassified large archives and published extensive retrospectives. Soviet technical records emerged unevenly through later Russian publications, joint exchanges and secondary analyses. When a tidy table places Starfish beside K-3, the visual symmetry can exceed the archival symmetry.

The book will not pretend otherwise.

Hawaii also teaches a subtler lesson about public risk communication. Dramatic understatement and dramatic overstatement produce the same failure from opposite directions. Say “only a few streetlights” and you erase the proof of long-range coupling. Say “Hawaii blacked out” and you substitute myth for system response.

The truth is harder to sloganize: a burst in near-Earth space drove electromagnetic effects through a planetary-scale plasma environment into specific electrical networks whose topology determined the damage.

That sentence is ugly.

It is also what engineers need.
