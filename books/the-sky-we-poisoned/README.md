# The Sky We Poisoned

## Starfish Prime, Artificial Radiation Belts, and the Forgotten Physics of Nuclear Space

**Sven Hardy Benson**

A Desk book in progress.

### Thesis

In 1958 and 1962 the United States and Soviet Union did something that now reads like a piece of speculative fiction: they deliberately detonated nuclear weapons at high altitude and in near-Earth space, not merely to test bombs but to modify the magnetosphere itself. Small Argus devices made narrow artificial radiation belts. Starfish Prime, a 1.4-megaton burst at roughly 400 km on July 9, 1962, injected beta-decay electrons into the geomagnetic trap, produced artificial aurora, drove a global electromagnetic disturbance, and left an enhanced radiation environment that persisted for well over a year. Satellites failed in the aftermath. Some failures are securely linked to enhanced radiation; some historical attributions are looser than the retellings admit.

The underwritten physics is not the spectacle of a nuclear flash over the Pacific. It is that the first generation of space physicists accidentally performed a planetary-scale plasma experiment before humanity had even learned how dependent it would become on the plasma environment they were perturbing. Their experiment remains essentially unrepeatable. Much of the decisive data survives only in old plots, declassified tapes, technical reports, and spacecraft failure histories. In August 2026 researchers were still extracting new physics from digitized Starfish-era measurements, finding evidence that the artificial belt may have approached a natural Kennel–Petschek-like self-limiting flux below about 2 MeV. Sixty-four years after the detonation, the experiment is still running intellectually.

This book asks what those events actually established about magnetospheric trapping, electromagnetic pulse, radiation-belt loss, satellite vulnerability, and the limits of deliberate planetary engineering. It keeps separate four questions that are often mashed together: what the explosions unquestionably did; what later investigators infer from incomplete measurements; what Cold War accounts claim without a recoverable denominator; and what modern strategists speculate a high-altitude nuclear burst would do to today's vastly denser orbital infrastructure.

### Evidence posture

The manuscript uses four explicit evidence classes.

1. **Verified / declassified / documented.** Operation Argus; Teak and Orange; Starfish Prime; U.S. and Soviet high-altitude tests; creation of artificial electron belts; measured geomagnetic and ionospheric disturbances; artificial aurora; the 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty; satellite radiation damage where supported by engineering records; modern particle-in-cell reconstruction; and the 2026 reanalysis of digitized historical electron data.
2. **Credible but incomplete.** Exact partition of individual satellite failures among Starfish radiation, pre-existing defects, solar weather and ordinary aging; reconstructed electron injection inventories; detailed Soviet test phenomenology in the English-language record; and some mechanisms controlling belt decay when only sparse historical measurements exist.
3. **Disputed or weakly confirmed.** Popular claims that Starfish “knocked out a third of all satellites” when each spacecraft is not traced to a primary failure analysis; simplified claims that Honolulu was “blacked out” rather than experiencing localized streetlight and communications effects; precise modern casualty or infrastructure estimates extrapolated from a 1962 event; and claims about classified contemporary high-altitude nuclear capabilities not supported by public documentation.
4. **Speculative synthesis.** That artificial radiation belts are best understood as an early form of inadvertent planetary engineering; that orbital civilization now has a new kind of collective vulnerability because one burst could change the shared radiation environment rather than merely destroy a target; and that modern simulation plus surviving 1962 data can become a test bed for designing space systems resilient to both nuclear and extreme natural magnetospheric shocks.
