# The Bank That Looked Safe

Banks sell confidence in units of time.

A depositor hands over money today because she expects to retrieve it tomorrow. A borrower receives money today and promises to repay it over years. Between those clocks sits the bank, transforming short, nervous liabilities into long, uncertain assets.

This arrangement is useful precisely because it is fragile.

If every depositor demanded cash at once, even a solvent bank could struggle. The bank's assets may be valuable but illiquid. Mortgages cannot be turned into currency instantly without loss. Loans are promises stretched across years. The institution survives because withdrawals are normally staggered and because depositors believe others will also remain calm.

Banking therefore contains a built-in reliability tax: liquidity buffers, capital requirements, deposit insurance, access to central-bank facilities, stress testing and supervision. Each one looks expensive in normal times.

Capital is especially offensive to return-on-equity arithmetic. A bank funded with more shareholder equity has a thicker cushion against losses, but leverage is lower. In a calm period, the less-capitalized bank can look more efficient. Liquidity holdings create a similar complaint. Cash and highly liquid government securities generally earn less than riskier loans. A large liquidity buffer is money deliberately prevented from working as hard as it could.

This is not a flaw in the concept. That lower return is the price of optionality.

The trouble begins when private incentives and system incentives diverge.

A bank executive can increase profit by taking risks whose downside will be borne partly by deposit insurers, creditors, taxpayers or the wider economy. Regulators respond by requiring cushions. Banks then argue, often with reason, that excessive cushions can reduce lending and economic growth. The debate becomes a contest over the right size of the reliability tax.

The 2008 crisis revealed how badly the system could mismeasure that tax.

Institutions had sophisticated risk models, diversified portfolios and elaborate funding structures. Yet much of the apparent diversity depended on the same underlying assumptions: housing prices would not fall sharply across the country, securitized assets could be sold, counterparties would remain solvent, short-term funding markets would stay open.

The architecture had many instruments but too few independent assumptions.

This is the financial version of two generators in one flooded basement.

After the crisis, regulators imposed stricter capital and liquidity rules and created more demanding stress tests. The logic was straightforward. If banks create large social costs when they fail, society has an interest in making them carry more self-insurance before failure.

But reliability rules decay politically because their benefits are invisible during prosperity.

As years pass without crisis, safeguards are recast as burdens inherited from a different era. Financial innovation seems to route around old risks. New entrants argue that legacy rules are unfair. Shareholders compare returns across jurisdictions. Politicians hear complaints about credit availability. The memory of the last collapse becomes an abstraction.

Then a new form of run appears.

The speed of modern banking has changed the reliability problem. In earlier eras, a bank run required physical movement. Depositors queued. News spread through newspapers, radio, television and conversation. Today large balances can leave through phones and corporate treasury systems in hours. Social media can coordinate fear across an industry. Digital banking compresses the interval between suspicion and withdrawal.

This means a liquidity buffer calibrated to an older withdrawal speed can be mathematically adequate and operationally obsolete.

Reliability is always relative to tempo.

A fire door designed for a building population of two hundred is not necessarily adequate after occupancy doubles. A backup battery sized for a five-minute outage is not reliable after the network recovery process grows to thirty minutes. A bank liquidity regime built around slower runs may not survive instant coordination.

The same issue appears in markets. High-frequency trading and automated risk controls can move capital rapidly in response to common signals. Individually rational systems may sell simultaneously. Liquidity that seemed abundant in quiet conditions disappears when everyone needs it.

The paradox is that speed improves normal efficiency while increasing the value of buffers.

This has consequences beyond finance. Any system that accelerates decision-making without accelerating recovery acquires a new mismatch. AI agents can place orders, write code, move inventory, rebalance portfolios and alter network configurations faster than humans can review the effects. The reliability tax must then include not only spare capacity but speed limits, circuit breakers, staged deployment and the ability to halt automated action.

Finance offers one of the oldest technologies for this: the margin requirement.

Margin is inconvenient by design. It forces a trader to carry resources against adverse movement. Clearinghouses demand collateral. Exchanges halt trading under extraordinary conditions. Banks undergo stress tests against scenarios they hope never occur. These mechanisms slow or constrain profitable activity because failure is not purely private.

The deeper lesson is that reliability sometimes requires making the locally optimal action impossible.

That claim makes market-oriented people understandably nervous. Rules can be clumsy. Regulators can defend incumbents, misread innovation or hard-code obsolete assumptions. A buffer required by law can become a universal target and create new common behavior. There is no reason to assume that public institutions can calculate resilience perfectly.

But the alternative is not a neutral market.

When failure spills outward, the absence of reliability rules is itself a subsidy. A firm can earn returns by consuming resilience that somebody else is expected to provide during the crisis.

Deposit insurance is an obvious example. It stabilizes banks by reassuring depositors, but it also weakens depositor incentives to monitor bank risk. Regulation must then compensate for the discipline that insurance removed. The safety mechanism changes behavior and creates the need for another safety mechanism.

Reliability systems are recursive.

Every protection changes incentives. Every backup invites dependence. Every guarantee can encourage risk. Every reserve can be undermaintained because people trust the reserve. The task is not to build a single perfect safeguard but to understand how layers interact.

A bank is therefore less like a vault than an ecosystem of promises about who will stand behind whom when ordinary confidence breaks.

The reliability tax in finance pays for that chain of standing behind.

The dangerous moment comes when everyone assumes someone else has paid it.
