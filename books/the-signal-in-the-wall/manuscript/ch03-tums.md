# 3. TUMS

Bureaucracies domesticate mystery by giving it an acronym.

The Technical Unidentified Moscow Signal became TUMS. Later, with more than one signal in play, records used MUTS: Moscow Unidentified Technical Signals. The names are useful because they preserve the institution's actual epistemic state. Unidentified did not mean extraterrestrial, miraculous, or unknowable. It meant a measurable technical phenomenon whose function had not been established.

That is a better word than anomalous.

Anomaly invites ontology. Unidentified technical signal invites a spectrum analyzer.

The Moscow files show agencies doing the ordinary things states do when confronted by an unexplained adversary capability. They measured. They argued about levels. They compared standards. They tested shielding. They asked what Soviet equipment might be nearby. They considered whether the signal was connected to U.S. intelligence operations on the embassy roof. They briefed senior officials. They considered medical implications because people were being exposed.

Then measurement error entered the story.

According to the [National Security Archive's reconstruction of Projects PANDORA and BIZARRE](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence-russia-programs/2022-09-13/moscow-signals-declassified-microwave), early experiments used inaccurate CIA readings that overstated the beam's power. The distinction was not small. When later measurements put the Moscow exposure lower, the interpretation of animal experiments changed. At corrected levels, reported primate performance degradation did not hold.

This is the sort of detail that deserves more attention than the project code name.

A code name survives because it is memorable. A calibration correction dies because it is tedious. Yet the calibration correction carries more epistemic weight. If an experiment models the wrong exposure regime, even an internally valid result may fail to answer the operational question.

Modern directed-energy speculation repeatedly encounters the same problem. People say 'microwaves can cause effects' as if frequency, peak power, average power, pulse width, repetition rate, geometry, absorption, body orientation, distance, and duration were ornamental details. They are the mechanism.

A field is not a field is not a field.

The TUMS story also demonstrates a familiar intelligence asymmetry. The target sees emissions and effects but not purpose. The emitter, if it is a state service, knows purpose but may deny the emission. Secrecy therefore widens the space available to hypothesis. The more politically consequential the hypothesis, the more tempting it becomes to use absence of disclosure as supporting evidence.

That is backwards.

Classification can explain why evidence is unavailable. It cannot substitute for evidence.

The best use of TUMS is not to prove a modern weapon. It is to create a benchmark for what a real, persistent foreign electromagnetic operation looks like from the target side: repeated technical observations, a spatially coherent field, long-term monitoring, diplomatic démarches, engineering countermeasures, internal disagreement, and eventually documentary seams.

If a future AHI case produces comparable environmental evidence, the argument changes immediately.

If it does not, the absence is not proof of absence—but it remains evidence about the quality of the case.
