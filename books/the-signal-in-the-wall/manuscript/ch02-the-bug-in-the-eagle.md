# 2. The Bug in the Eagle

Before trying to injure a diplomat with physics, consider the older and more practical intelligence problem: hearing what the diplomat says.

The Soviet Union had already demonstrated exquisite patience with embassy surveillance. The famous Great Seal bug—an apparently passive listening device concealed inside a carved wooden Great Seal presented to the U.S. ambassador—worked because an external radio-frequency signal could illuminate the device and cause it to retransmit room audio. The lesson is not that the Moscow microwave signal definitely powered a hidden bug. The lesson is that radio energy, covert rooms, and passive surveillance were already members of the same operational family.

This makes one mundane explanation for some Moscow emissions more interesting than the lurid one.

The National Security Archive documents U.S. concern that microwave transmissions might be connected to devices planted in embassy walls. American security teams discovered numerous microphones and compromised spaces. Defense officials considered whether at least some signals could activate or interrogate audio devices. The archival language is cautious because the evidence was incomplete. That caution is exactly what later retellings tend to delete.

A spy service does not need to invent psychotronics when it can steal conversations.

There is an important methodological point here. Intelligence operations are optimized for purpose, not narrative elegance. A signal aimed at a building can be simultaneously alarming to occupants, interesting to biomedical researchers, useful to counterintelligence officers, and entirely unrelated to a deliberate health attack. The same physical artifact can enter several bureaucratic stories at once.

That multiplicity matters for anomalous health incidents because modern debate often asks the wrong binary question: weapon or no weapon? Real state systems contain jammers, radars, communications links, surveillance illuminators, tracking systems, test emitters, power-transfer experiments, security sensors, and accidental electromagnetic environments. A person can be exposed to an engineered field without the engineering purpose being to injure the person.

Purpose is an inference layered on top of exposure.

In Moscow, the United States had direct evidence of exposure and incomplete evidence of purpose. In Havana-era cases, the order is reversed in public discourse: a possible hostile purpose is often narrated before a field has been captured. This is one reason the older case is so useful. It shows how much easier attribution becomes when engineers have the signal itself.

Frequency. Power density. Pulse structure. Angle of arrival. Time of day. Spatial footprint. Change after shielding. Correlation with equipment. Correlation with human presence. These measurements are not glamorous, but they are the difference between an intelligence problem and a ghost story.

The Great Seal bug also supplies a warning against technological anachronism. Once a strange device is revealed, later observers imagine it as evidence that any more advanced rumor was feasible at the same time. But an ingenious passive resonator is not a neurological weapon. Technical lineage has to be demonstrated component by component.

A real bug proves a real bug.

A real beam proves a real beam.

The interesting question is what happened when American agencies, unable to settle the beam's purpose, decided to ask the human body.
