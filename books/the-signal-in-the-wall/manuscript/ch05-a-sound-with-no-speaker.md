# 5. A Sound With No Speaker

The strangest part of this story is also the cleanest.

People can hear pulsed microwave energy.

The phenomenon has been studied for decades and is often associated with Allan H. Frey's early work. Later experimental and theoretical literature converged on a thermoelastic explanation: a brief RF pulse is absorbed, produces an extremely small and rapid temperature rise, launches an acoustic pressure wave, and stimulates the ordinary auditory apparatus through bone conduction. James C. Lin's review describes microwave hearing as a recognized bioeffect with a physical mechanism; animal experiments recorded cochlear responses following pulsed irradiation.

No radio station whispers into consciousness. No thought is decoded. The skull becomes, briefly and inefficiently, part of an acoustic transducer.

This matters because witness descriptions from some anomalous health incidents include clicks, buzzing, pressure, or a sensation localized to a region of space. A mechanism capable of producing sound perception without a conventional acoustic source therefore deserves consideration.

But deserving consideration is where the sentence should stop.

A mechanism is not an event report. It is not a device. It is not a deployment history. It is not an adversary.

The mistake is easy because the fit feels satisfying. A person reports a sound with no obvious speaker; microwave hearing produces a sound without a speaker; therefore microwaves caused the event. That syllogism ignores base rates and alternatives. Acoustic sources can be hard to localize indoors. Ultrasound can mix down through nonlinearities. Electronics can emit odd transients. Insects can create startling directional sounds. Stress and vestibular disorders can alter perception. A real external stimulus can be misidentified. A real physiological event can have no hostile source.

The microwave auditory effect earns a place in this book because it converts one feature from 'impossible' to 'physically possible.' That is substantial. It is not the same as likely.

This is a recurring problem in frontier subjects. Establishing that nature permits something feels like half the argument. In practice it may be five percent. Aircraft are physically possible; that does not identify a light in the sky as an aircraft. Rare diseases exist; that does not diagnose a patient. Meteorites fall; that does not make every crater meteoritic.

Mechanism plausibility changes the prior. Evidence from the actual event must do the rest.

There is another subtlety. Microwave hearing is generally associated with pulse conditions that produce a thermoelastic wave without gross heating. That means a person could perceive an effect without a burn and without an obvious external noise source. It also means post-event medical imaging might not reveal the kind of structural injury people intuitively expect from an 'energy attack.' This is scientifically interesting and rhetorically dangerous.

Interesting, because it suggests a transient physical effect can be real while leaving limited persistent signature.

Dangerous, because the absence of a signature can then become unfalsifiable support: if scans are negative, advocates can say the mechanism predicts negative scans; if scans are unusual, advocates can say the mechanism caused injury.

A theory that explains every possible outcome explains nothing.

The proper use of microwave hearing is to derive predictions: frequency regime, pulse structure, threshold, spatial dependence, shielding response, simultaneous environmental capture, and repeatability. It turns a story into an instrument plan.

That is enough.
