# 1. The Beam That Was Actually There

The first mistake is to begin in Havana.

Begin in Moscow, in a chancery building that the United States occupied in 1953, where technicians eventually established that microwave energy was being directed at the embassy. Not a metaphorical signal. Not a witness remembering a sensation thirty years later. A measurable radio-frequency phenomenon that U.S. officials mapped, monitored, complained about, and tried to understand.

The [National Security Archive's declassified Moscow Signal collection](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence-russia-programs/2022-09-13/moscow-signals-declassified-microwave) is a useful antidote to two opposite forms of stupidity. The first says governments do not do bizarre things with electromagnetic energy, therefore any later allegation must be fantasy. The second says governments did one bizarre thing with electromagnetic energy, therefore a later allegation is confirmed. History is less cooperative. It gives us a real beam and then refuses to tell us, cleanly, what the beam was for.

American officials considered several purposes. Jamming or interfering with U.S. electronic intelligence was one. Activating or interrogating listening devices inside the building was another. A biological or behavioral purpose was investigated and never demonstrated at the measured levels. The archive is not evidence of a Soviet mind-control weapon. It is evidence of an adversary repeatedly irradiating a diplomatic facility for reasons the target government could not confidently determine.

That distinction matters because uncertainty is often treated as if it were a blank page. It is not. The Moscow record has shape. Signal strength changed. Directionality was measured. Diplomatic protests were made. Technical teams were involved. Shielding was discussed. Exposure assumptions were revised. Programs acquired names. Senior officials put the matter into memoranda and telephone calls. The thing left administrative residue.

This is what reality looks like when classified bureaucracy touches a weird event. It does not look like a perfectly hidden story. It looks like fragments created by different offices for different reasons.

In June 1967, Secretary of State Dean Rusk raised the electromagnetic signal with Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko. Later administrations kept pressing. In the mid-1970s, stronger or additional signals sharpened concern. In December 1975 Henry Kissinger called Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin and referred directly to the beam. The exchange, preserved in the archive, is almost comic because statecraft often is: the men joke while discussing an exposure they do not understand and cannot publicly explain.

The comedy ends when you remember who lived in the building.

Embassy personnel and family members did not initially receive the full story. Officials worried about leaks, diplomatic consequences, and uncertain medical meaning. By early 1976 the secrecy itself was becoming untenable. Ambassador Walter Stoessel pressed for staff briefings. The archive shows an institution trapped between two fears: telling people about a poorly understood hazard, and not telling them about a poorly understood hazard.

The Moscow Signal therefore gives this book its first rule: **an extraordinary mechanism does not become ordinary merely because one historical case is documented, but neither may we erase the documented case because later claims are inconvenient.**

There really was a beam.

There is no responsible shortcut from that sentence to Havana.
