# 6. The Skull as a Transducer

The human head is a bad antenna and a surprisingly serviceable piece of matter.

Radio-frequency energy entering tissue is partly reflected, partly transmitted, partly absorbed. Absorption depends on frequency, geometry, dielectric properties, orientation, and body structure. When a short pulse deposits energy quickly enough, the resulting thermal expansion can launch a pressure transient. The pressure wave moves. The cochlea detects motion. The brain reports sound.

The mechanism is ordinary physics arranged in an unfamiliar sequence.

That ordinariness is important. People hear 'microwave auditory effect' and imagine a fringe phenomenon. The effect is unusual in daily life because daily RF environments are not normally configured to produce it at perceptible thresholds. But unfamiliarity is not the same as theoretical fragility.

The mechanistic literature does not establish that microwave auditory exposure causes the broad chronic syndrome reported by all AHI claimants. In fact, that leap is precisely where evidence thins. A perceptual click is one endpoint. Persistent dizziness, cognitive complaints, sleep disturbance, headache, imbalance, tinnitus, mood effects, and disability are a heterogeneous clinical field.

One stimulus can have sequelae. Different stimuli can produce overlapping symptoms. Stress can amplify recovery problems without being the original insult. Functional neurological disorders can be disabling without implying fabrication. Diagnostic labels can cluster people who do not share a cause.

This is why the phrase 'Havana Syndrome' is scientifically expensive. A syndrome name can help clinicians communicate before etiology is known, but it also creates a bucket. Once the bucket exists, every new case is compared with previous cases and the phenotype can widen. The widening phenotype then makes it harder to infer a single exposure mechanism from the group.

Mechanism should therefore be reconstructed from the narrowest, earliest, most time-locked features: what was perceived at onset, where the person was standing, whether moving altered the effect, whether anyone else perceived it, whether electronics recorded anything, whether walls or windows mattered, whether the event recurred, and what objective findings appeared immediately afterward.

The 2020 National Academies committee emphasized distinctive acute reports more than chronic nonspecific symptoms when it judged directed pulsed RF the most plausible mechanism among those it considered. That caveat often vanishes in public argument. The committee did not say every chronic symptom was diagnostic of RF exposure. It explicitly entertained multiple contributing factors.

A skull can be a transducer.

A diagnosis cannot be one merely because the sentence is memorable.
