# 13. The National Academies Choose a Mechanism

In 2020 the National Academies did something unusually consequential: it ranked mechanisms.

The committee's report, [*An Assessment of Illness in U.S. Government Employees and Their Families at Overseas Embassies*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25889), concluded that directed, pulsed radio-frequency energy appeared the most plausible mechanism among those it considered for many of the distinctive acute symptoms, especially the early directional phenomena. It also discussed persistent postural-perceptual dizziness and psychological conditions as possible reinforcing or additive factors and allowed that different cases might have different causes.

The report became a talisman.

For advocates of a directed-energy hypothesis, a National Academies committee had said the words. For skeptics, the committee lacked exposure measurements and had to reason retrospectively from symptom descriptions. Both are true.

The report's strongest contribution was not attribution. It was the insistence that a transient RF hypothesis is testable prospectively. The committee noted that modern electronics can measure incident RF power, frequency, pulse width, repetition rate, and angle of arrival in real time. That recommendation is more important than the mechanism ranking because it points toward evidence that could escape retrospective ambiguity.

The committee did not identify a weapon. It did not identify an actor. It did not demonstrate that a measured field coincided with any Havana event.

It said a mechanism was physically germane to a subset of reported features.

That is a serious finding. It deserves neither inflation nor dismissal.

The missing artifact is still the same one: a captured event.
