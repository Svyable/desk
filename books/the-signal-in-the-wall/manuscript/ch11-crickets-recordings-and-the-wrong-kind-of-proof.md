# 11. Crickets, Recordings, and the Wrong Kind of Proof

At some point every modern mystery acquires an audio file.

Recordings associated in public discussion with Havana were analyzed by scientists who found strong similarity between at least one prominent recording and the call of a Caribbean cricket. The finding became a weapon in both directions. Skeptics used it to imply the entire syndrome was an insect. Advocates treated that as absurd and therefore dismissed mundane acoustic analysis altogether.

Both reactions are too ambitious.

If a recording is a cricket, the recording is a cricket.

That can disconfirm a claim that this particular audio sample captures an exotic weapon's emitted sound. It does not prove that the person who made the recording had no symptoms. It does not prove every sensory event had the same source. It does not address a mechanism, such as microwave hearing, in which the primary perceived sound need not be an external acoustic wave available to a conventional microphone.

This is an example of **evidence alignment**. Every measurement has a target proposition.

A microphone tests for airborne pressure waves at the microphone.

An RF detector tests for electromagnetic fields in its band and dynamic range.

An MRI tests for certain persistent structural or functional differences at a later time.

A witness report records experience and context.

An intelligence intercept may bear on actor capability or intent.

The tragedy of anomaly research is how often one instrument's negative result is promoted into a universal negative.

The cricket work is valuable because it demonstrates that memorable sensory evidence can have an ordinary source. It should raise the prior probability of ordinary explanations for at least some events. It should also motivate synchronized measurement. If a future subject hears a click while a microphone records nothing, a broadband RF system records a pulse, and the effect changes predictably with shielding or movement, the evidentiary situation changes dramatically.

The right lesson from the cricket is not ridicule.

It is: bring more than one sensor.
