# 7. Safety Standards Are Not Ontology

Nothing makes an argument about invisible energy worse faster than a safety limit.

One side points to a level below a regulatory threshold and says: harmless. The other side points to a country with a stricter threshold and says: dangerous. Both may be using an administrative boundary as if it were a law of nature.

Exposure standards are built for purposes. They incorporate assumptions about known effects, averaging times, occupational versus public settings, measurement practicality, uncertainty, and policy choices. A standard can be conservative. It can lag science. It can be aimed at preventing established thermal injury while saying little about a narrow pulsed sensory effect. It can differ across jurisdictions without one side possessing secret biological truth.

The Moscow Signal history became entangled in exactly this problem. U.S. officials compared measured levels with American and Soviet standards while simultaneously admitting uncertainty about long-duration exposure. Early power estimates were themselves contested. Political language then attached to technical numbers.

This chapter's argument is simple: **a field being below a safety standard does not prove the field is nonexistent, and a field being above another standard does not prove it caused a symptom.**

The right question is effect-specific.

If the hypothesis is bulk heating, calculate absorbed power and temperature rise. If the hypothesis is microwave hearing, calculate pulse conditions and thermoelastic pressure. If the hypothesis is interference with electronics, measure coupling into the device. If the hypothesis is chronic biological injury, identify a plausible endpoint and dose-response relationship rather than borrowing the existence of another effect.

The word 'nonthermal' causes particular mischief. It is often used as a magic category for anything occurring below obvious heating. But the microwave auditory effect itself is commonly described through thermoelastic expansion: heating exists, just at a tiny and rapid scale that creates pressure rather than a sustained sensation of warmth. 'No noticeable heating' and 'no thermal physics' are not synonyms.

Language matters because these disputes are fought in compressed public formats. The phrase 'below safety limits' feels dispositive. So does 'nonthermal biological effect.' Neither should be allowed to carry more than its technical meaning.

The best defense against rhetorical smuggling is a link budget and an endpoint.

Source power. Antenna gain. Distance. Atmospheric loss if relevant. Wall attenuation. Incident field. Absorption. Pulse duration. Repetition. Peak and average exposure. Expected pressure transient. Expected audible threshold. Expected collateral electromagnetic signature.

Numbers make bad myths because they have units.
