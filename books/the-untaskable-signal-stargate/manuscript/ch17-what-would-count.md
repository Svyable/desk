# What Would Count

A contested field eventually needs a list of ways to lose.

Remote viewing accumulated arguments for decades partly because its defeat conditions remained negotiable. A bad session could be noise. A failed replication could use the wrong protocol. A successful experiment could be criticized for leakage. A clean experiment could be criticized for dependence on the originating research community. An operational miss could coexist with laboratory significance. A striking operational hit could be dismissed as selection.

Every move has some legitimacy.

Together they can make the subject immortal.

The solution is not to pick a side. It is to specify in advance what future evidence would force each side to change.

Start with the strongest proponent claim that can still be tested cleanly: humans can obtain target-relevant information under conditions that block known sensory channels, at rates exceeding chance, and the effect is not explained by methodological artifacts.

What would support it now?

A preregistered, multi-laboratory protocol designed jointly by proponents and skeptical methodologists. Automated random target selection. Cryptographically committed target lists. Full blinding of viewers, interviewers, judges, and analysts where appropriate. Complete session retention. A locked primary endpoint. Large enough samples to estimate effect size rather than merely detect significance. Independent replication at laboratories with no personnel overlap. Public data sufficient for adversarial reanalysis. Prospective identification of any “elite” viewers before the test set begins.

Most important: an effect large enough to survive disagreement about reasonable analytic choices.

A discovery that exists only under one bespoke scoring method remains fragile.

What would count against the claim?

Repeated, well-powered adversarial replications in which the pre-specified effect collapses toward chance, especially when the protocol preserves every feature proponents previously said was necessary. Evidence that apparent effects track procedural leakage, target-pool structure, judge expectations, optional stopping, or subject selection would further reduce the anomalous interpretation.

No single failure should be decisive if the claimed effect is probabilistic. But a sequence of clean failures should move belief.

The operational claim requires a different test.

Build an intelligence-like benchmark in which the target space is broad and the evaluator does not know the answer. Ask viewers to produce prospectively scorable propositions, not just descriptions that can be interpreted later. Give each proposition a confidence level. Compare performance against structured human guessing, domain-expert baselines, random-description controls, and conventional analytic methods. Measure not only accuracy but information gain, false-lead burden, timeliness, and decision value.

If remote viewing is useful, it should beat something.

That sentence is almost offensively simple. The historical program too often compared itself with chance rather than with the alternative tools a customer would actually buy.

Chance is not the competitor.

Satellite imagery is a competitor.

Human intelligence is a competitor.

Open-source analysis is a competitor.

Structured red teaming is a competitor.

Doing nothing is sometimes a competitor.

An operational service must justify its marginal contribution.

The most interesting modern test would combine the scientific and operational standards. Viewers would face targets drawn from a large, precommitted space. Their outputs would be transformed into probabilities or ranked hypotheses before ground truth. Analysts would be blinded to whether reports came from actual-target sessions or decoy sessions. Decision tasks would measure whether the reports improve performance.

This would test a possibility the historical program never cleanly separated: perhaps remote-viewing text is useful because it stimulates analysts, not because it contains target information.

If analysts improve equally when given plausible decoy transcripts, the creativity explanation wins.

If actual-target transcripts produce significantly better decisions, the information claim strengthens.

Mechanism experiments would come later or in parallel. If an anomaly survives, researchers could vary distance, time relation, shielding, target entropy, neurological state, geomagnetic conditions, feedback, and other variables. A real phenomenon should eventually reveal structure. Even quantum mechanics, invoked loosely in many paranormal discussions, is not magic. Quantum effects obey precise constraints.

A hypothesis that “consciousness is nonlocal” without a quantitative prediction is not yet an explanation.

The same standard applies to skeptical explanations.

“People are suggestible” is not enough.

“Experimenter effect” is not enough.

“Cold reading” is not enough if the protocol excludes interpersonal feedback.

A skeptic should identify mechanisms capable of producing the observed effect size under the actual protocol. Vague conventionalism is intellectually symmetrical with vague paranormalism.

This is where Ray Hyman’s methodological concern remains powerful but incomplete. Shared protocol lineage can sustain shared artifacts. To close the case, one wants either the artifact itself or adversarial replications that remove the effect without requiring a new rescue explanation each time.

The field never received a universally accepted decisive sequence of that kind.

That is not evidence for psi.

It is a description of why the argument persisted.

Historical programs also need defeat conditions. What evidence would overturn this book’s thesis that the key failure was translation from anomaly to intelligence?

One possibility would be a declassified or otherwise authenticated operational evaluation showing sustained prospective accuracy, customer-specific information gain, and decisions materially improved by remote-viewing reports across a large consecutive case series. Such a record would force the book to narrow sharply. It would show that the capability crossed the operational gap even if the 1995 reviewers failed to recognize it.

Another would be documentation proving that the public termination was knowingly deceptive and that a successor program maintained validated operational performance under a different compartment. Mere allegations of continuation would not be enough. One would want budgets, tasking records, evaluation standards, authenticated reports, or credible official evidence subject to independent scrutiny.

Conversely, what would strengthen the skeptical synthesis?

A complete archival reconstruction showing that celebrated operational hits dissolve when original tasking, full transcripts, misses, and feedback timing are restored. If every famous case can be explained through selection, cueing, prior knowledge, or retrospective interpretation, the residual anomaly would shrink toward the laboratory dispute alone.

This book does not assume that reconstruction will end in one direction.

It should be possible for some cases to survive and others to collapse.

That is what evidence usually looks like when mythology is removed.

The four evidence classes remain useful here.

Verified and declassified facts can establish program structure, sessions, contracts, and official judgments.

Credible but incomplete evidence can preserve participant testimony and case-specific claims whose documentation is partial.

Disputed allegations can be recorded without being upgraded.

Speculative synthesis can connect patterns while carrying an explicit label.

These categories are not ranks of emotional respect. They are instructions for what a sentence may claim.

The deeper value of defeat conditions is cultural. They make curiosity compatible with closure.

A person can investigate remote viewing seriously without promising to believe forever. A skeptic can permit the possibility of being surprised without surrendering standards. An agency can explore a low-probability capability without turning exploration into institutional commitment.

That last skill is rarer than it should be.

Governments need places where strange questions can be asked.

They also need exits.
