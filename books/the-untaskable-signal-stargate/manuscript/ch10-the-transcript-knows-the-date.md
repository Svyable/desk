# The Transcript Knows the Date

A paranormal claim can fail because a date was left on a page.

This is one of the reasons experimental science is humbling. The grand interpretation may concern unknown human faculties, nonlocal information, or physics beyond current models. The decisive detail may be clerical.

David Marks and Richard Kammann’s critique of early remote-viewing work became famous because they found that judging materials contained cues capable of revealing the proper matching sequence. Later discussion focused particularly on extraneous information in transcripts—dates and references that could help a judge identify which description belonged with which target. Marks subsequently argued that when such cues were removed, the impressive matching performance disappeared.

The historical specifics have been fought over for decades, and they should not be simplified beyond what the documents support. Proponents disputed aspects of the critique and emphasized later protocols designed to avoid the problem. Critics argued that the original work’s evidentiary force had been substantially undermined. The clean conclusion is narrower: at least some influential early remote-viewing experiments were vulnerable to sensory-cue criticism serious enough that one cannot cite their published probabilities as if the paranormal channel were the only available explanation.

That should have been an ordinary scientific event.

A vulnerability is found. The protocol changes. New experiments are run. Confidence shifts according to what survives.

Instead, the cue controversy became cultural property. Skeptics used it as proof that remote viewing had been “exposed.” Proponents used later methodological improvements as proof that skeptics were stuck in the past. Each side was partly right about the other side’s worst habit.

The useful question is what the field learned.

The first lesson was that blind judging has to be blind to more than target identity. The entire information environment around the judging process must be sanitized. Target material should be randomized independently. Transcripts should contain no order cues. Judges should not infer sequence from physical differences. Experimenters interacting with judges should lack target knowledge. Scoring rules should be fixed.

The second lesson was that the target pool itself can leak structure.

If targets are too distinctive from one another, matching becomes easier for reasons unrelated to anomalous perception. That is not necessarily a flaw if the experiment’s null distribution properly reflects the pool, but it changes what the result means. A viewer may need only coarse category information to rank a waterfall above an office building. Operational targets are often much more similar to one another.

The third lesson was social: adversarial replication matters.

A group that invents a protocol becomes skilled at it in ways that are hard to formalize. It knows how to recruit subjects, prepare targets, conduct sessions, and judge results. When another group fails to replicate, the original group may reasonably argue that tacit knowledge was lost. But if success always requires the originating community’s tacit knowledge, the claim remains fragile.

This is not unique to parapsychology. Experimental psychology, biomedicine, and even parts of physics have struggled with lab-specific effects. The difference is that remote viewing’s proposed mechanism was so extraordinary that common-mode methodological error carried unusual explanatory power.

Ray Hyman would later make a version of this point in the 1995 evaluation. The apparent consistency of positive results did not satisfy him that paranormal functioning had been established because the research program lacked the kind of independent methodological replication that would rule out shared flaws. Jessica Utts judged the evidence differently. She argued that the accumulated effect was sufficiently stable and well-controlled to support the existence of anomalous cognition.

The disagreement is often described as statistics versus skepticism. That is too crude.

Both were arguing about what kind of explanation a statistical pattern licenses.

A low p-value answers a conditional question: if the null model and analysis are correct, how surprising is a result at least this extreme? It does not list every way the null model or analysis might be wrong. Hyman’s position, in essence, was that methodological dependence remained a live alternative. Utts’s position was that repeated positive results under increasingly controlled conditions made unspecified artifact an increasingly unsatisfying explanation.

This is a real inference problem.

At what point does “there must be another flaw” become less parsimonious than “there is an effect we do not understand”?

Science has no universal numerical answer. It uses replication, mechanism, predictive success, independent laboratories, effect size, dose-response structure, convergence with other measurements, and the cost of alternative explanations. Remote viewing had some of these and lacked others.

It had repeated experiments.

It lacked an accepted mechanism.

It had some methodological evolution.

It lacked broad hostile replication.

It had statistical claims.

It lacked a robust operational transformation.

The cue problem stayed relevant not because it explained everything forever, but because it demonstrated how easily an information experiment can fool itself without anyone cheating.

That point becomes more powerful, not less, in the age of machine learning.

Modern researchers know that a model can appear to classify medical images while actually reading hospital-specific markers, scanner artifacts, or text embedded in the image. A benchmark can be “solved” by leakage between training and test sets. A language model can exploit formatting patterns correlated with labels. Performance is real in the narrow sense—the system predicts correctly—but the claimed capability is wrong.

Early remote viewing had the same epistemic shape. A judge might correctly match transcripts to targets. The claimed explanation was anomalous information transfer. Marks and Kammann proposed that the real predictive feature could be mundane transcript metadata.

This is why the details matter more than rhetoric.

If a critic says “cueing,” ask which cue, in which experiment, and whether removing it eliminates performance.

If a proponent says “later protocols fixed that,” ask which later protocol, whether the target selection and judging were independently controlled, and whether the result replicated outside the originating network.

The archive should be read at that resolution.

The famous Nature paper remains historically important even if some early evidentiary claims weakened. It made a claim testable enough to attract a specific countermechanism. That is better than a phenomenon that survives only by escaping measurement.

The field’s response also matters. Later remote-viewing experiments increasingly used automated target selection, standardized target pools, blind judging, and formal statistical analysis. Those improvements are evidence of scientific pressure working. A skeptic should acknowledge them because otherwise skepticism becomes unfalsifiable: every future experiment is condemned by an old flaw whether or not the flaw remains.

But improved control raises the standard for what counts as success.

As protocols close obvious leakage, small effects become the center of the dispute. The stronger cinematic claims—people reading coordinates, seeing secret facilities at will—do not emerge automatically from a modest statistical excess. The cleaner the experiment, the more important effect size becomes.

A tiny robust anomaly could be scientifically revolutionary and operationally trivial.

That sentence should be pinned above the entire STARGATE archive.

It allows us to take Utts seriously without turning her statistical assessment into evidence that psychic spies worked reliably. It allows us to take Hyman seriously without pretending he proved every observed deviation was caused by a known cue. It allows us to treat Marks and Kammann’s work as a major correction without using it as a universal solvent.

The transcript that knows the date is not the whole story.

It is the warning label on the story.

When information is the claimed phenomenon, every piece of information in the protocol is suspect.
