# The Enemy Might Know Something

The Cold War bought many experiments for a reason that is easy to confuse with belief.

It bought insurance against surprise.

A military or intelligence bureaucracy does not face scientific questions in the same way a university department does. A university can decide that an extraordinary claim lacks sufficient evidence and spend its next grant on something more promising. An intelligence service must also ask what happens if the claim is unlikely but true, especially if an adversary may be working on it. The relevant variable is not only probability. It is probability multiplied by consequence.

This is how a field can receive official attention without receiving official endorsement.

During the 1970s, U.S. intelligence collected and circulated reporting on Soviet and East European parapsychology, “paraphysics,” psychokinesis, biological information transfer, and other unconventional subjects. Declassified references show that DIA and CIA produced assessments with titles such as “Soviet and Czechoslovakian Parapsychology Research” and “Soviet and East European Parapsychology Research.” Another body of work considered alleged “psychoenergetic” devices and unusual human-performance effects.

These papers are now routinely offered as proof that the Soviet Union possessed capabilities the American public was not told about.

They prove something narrower and more interesting: American officials considered the possibility important enough to collect intelligence on it.

An intelligence report about a foreign research program has at least three evidentiary layers. First, the report may establish that named institutes, researchers, publications, or government entities existed. Second, it may summarize claims made by those researchers. Third, it may estimate what those claims mean for national security. The first layer can be firm while the third is highly uncertain.

This should be obvious, but secrecy encourages category errors. A classified document feels like validated hidden knowledge. In reality, classified systems contain rumor, preliminary analysis, foreign propaganda, worst-case assessments, rejected ideas, and questions whose uncertainty is itself sensitive.

The Soviet material mattered because it could produce a strategic mirror.

Suppose American analysts see reports that Soviet laboratories are studying telepathy or psychokinesis. They do not know whether the work is good. They do know that if even a small fraction is real, the implications could be severe. So the United States funds an investigation. Soviet intelligence sees signs of American interest and may conclude the Americans have results. Both sides can increase activity because of the other side’s activity. No paranormal effect is necessary to generate the arms-race behavior.

This is the first serious competing explanation for the longevity of remote-viewing research: it may have been sustained less by cumulative proof than by adversarial uncertainty.

The explanation is powerful, but not sufficient.

The American program did not merely read Soviet journals. It conducted experiments. Government personnel and contractors reported sessions they believed contained anomalous information. Some sponsors became convinced enough to continue. The program developed training methods, target procedures, and operational relationships. A pure mirror model cannot explain every internal decision unless one assumes the people involved were indifferent to their own results, which the record does not support.

The more realistic picture is a feedback loop between foreign-threat perception and domestic anomaly.

Reports of Soviet interest made unconventional human-performance work easier to justify. Apparent American successes then made the foreign reports seem less absurd. The existence of both programs could be treated as mutual corroboration. This is an epistemic trap common to intelligence competition: when two adversaries investigate the same uncertain possibility, convergence of attention can be mistaken for convergence of evidence.

Nuclear weapons are the counterexample that makes the trap hard to ignore. During the Manhattan Project and Soviet atomic effort, mutual concern tracked a physical capability that was spectacularly real. Radar, missiles, satellites, cryptography, and stealth also rewarded states that took technical surprises seriously. The Cold War trained institutions to fear dismissing the weird thing that would later become obvious.

Remote viewing entered through that door.

The budget scale mattered. A modest contract at SRI or a small Army unit was cheap relative to major weapons programs. The option value was attractive: if the work failed, the government lost a limited sum and some dignity. If it succeeded at the strongest claimed level, the payoff could be revolutionary. A collection method unconstrained by distance, line of sight, borders, or conventional shielding would alter the economics of secrecy.

Low cost and high hypothetical payoff can keep a weakly evidenced program alive longer than outsiders expect.

That does not mean “any weird thing should be funded.” Institutions face opportunity costs and reputational costs. More importantly, a low-cost exploratory program should have stopping rules. What evidence after five years would count as failure? After ten? What operational metric must improve? How many protocol revisions are acceptable before the original claim becomes unfalsifiable?

The remote-viewing history is partly a history of stopping rules that remained soft.

One reason was mission ambiguity. Research and operations could rescue each other. When operational results disappointed, advocates could argue that the science still showed an effect and better training was needed. When laboratory work faced methodological criticism, operational anecdotes could restore confidence. A program with two success criteria can survive by moving between them.

Another reason was classification. Secrecy can protect a fragile research program from premature ridicule, allowing exploration of ideas that public institutions would not touch. That can be valuable. It can also protect the program from the ordinary abrasion of scientific criticism. Outside researchers cannot examine protocols they do not know exist. Insider critics may face organizational pressures different from journal referees. Failures can remain locally known while successes travel farther.

The Soviet threat added a final shield against termination: uncertainty itself could be framed as risk.

If we stop and the Soviets continue, what if they discover something?

This question is rational at the beginning. It becomes less rational if decades of research fail to produce a dependable capability. The burden should migrate over time. Early exploration asks skeptics to tolerate uncertainty. Mature programs must ask proponents to demonstrate utility.

That migration is central to this book.

The strongest case for funding remote viewing in 1972 is not the strongest case for funding it in 1992. At the start, officials confronted a novel claim, some intriguing demonstrations, and foreign reporting. Twenty years later, they possessed a large internal record. The program should no longer have been judged by whether the hypothesis was worth a look. It should have been judged by what the look had bought.

This is where the 1995 evaluation becomes more than a scientific argument. It was an institutional reckoning with accumulated option value. Had the cheap bet matured into an intelligence asset?

CIA’s later public account says no. Its summary of the independent review acknowledges that some accurate remote-viewing experiences appeared to defy randomness, while emphasizing that the effect was too unreliable, inconsistent, and sporadic for intelligence use. That language is remarkable because it refuses the clean ending both camps prefer. The agency did not say, in its public retelling, that every anomalous result was explained away. It said the program failed the utility threshold.

A skeptic can object that this leaves too much rhetorical oxygen for psi. A believer can object that an effect need not be operationally mature to be scientifically real. Both objections are fair. Neither changes the decision problem.

An intelligence agency is not a metaphysics foundation.

If a source cannot tell the customer when it is right, the customer must verify it through other means. If verification is already available, the source may add little. If verification is unavailable, the customer cannot distinguish signal from error. This is the operational vise that closed around remote viewing.

The Cold War could fund the question because surprise was expensive.

It could not indefinitely excuse an answer that remained untaskable.
