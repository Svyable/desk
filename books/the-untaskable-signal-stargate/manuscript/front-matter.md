# The Untaskable Signal

*STARGATE and the Gap Between Anomaly and Intelligence*

Sven Hardy Benson

This book is about a documented U.S. government research and intelligence history, not a claim that paranormal perception has been established. The public record contains genuine declassified programs, experiments, operational tasking, favorable assessments, methodological criticism, failures, and an external 1995 review that separated disputed laboratory evidence from poor operational utility.

The book uses four evidence classes throughout. **Verified/declassified/documented** means a claim is directly supported by a public primary or authoritative record. **Credible but incomplete** means testimony, reporting, or analysis has meaningful support but lacks enough independent documentation to close the question. **Disputed allegation** means a claim exists but lacks adequate independent confirmation. **Speculative synthesis** means the author is connecting documented pieces into an interpretation that should be tested rather than inherited as fact.

No classified access is claimed or implied. All intelligence-related material in this manuscript comes from lawfully public sources, especially CIA’s declassified STARGATE collection, published scientific literature, the National Research Council record, and the 1995 American Institutes for Research evaluation as publicly available.

The central question is deliberately narrower than “Are psychic powers real?” It is: **what happens when a statistically contested human anomaly is asked to become a dependable intelligence service?**

The strongest competing answer is that there was never an anomaly worth translating. Early experiments were vulnerable to cueing and selection, later positive results remained methodologically dependent, and operational anecdotes expanded through hindsight. This book does not dismiss that explanation. It puts it under the same pressure as the proponent account.

The promise to the reader is not revelation. It is bookkeeping: who knew what, when; what a transcript contained before feedback; how a target was selected; which result was statistically unusual; which result was operationally useful; and where testimony, classification, or later memory tries to carry more weight than the evidence can hold.
