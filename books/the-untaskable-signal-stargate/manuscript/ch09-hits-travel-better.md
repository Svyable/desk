# Hits Travel Better

Every intelligence system creates stories about itself.

The best stories are not always the most representative ones.

A remote-viewing program was especially vulnerable to this because its successes were narratively portable. A viewer describes an unexpected feature. Later the feature is confirmed. The sequence can be told in three sentences and survive for decades. A failed session is harder to remember. “The viewer produced a mixture of generic impressions and incorrect specifics that did not materially reduce uncertainty” is not a story people repeat over dinner.

This creates selection pressure before any conspiracy enters the picture.

Hits travel.

Misses file.

The effect is familiar in medicine, investing, sports, and military history. We remember the patient who survived after an unconventional treatment, the investor who called a crash, the scout who found a star, the intelligence warning that proved prescient. The larger the universe of attempts, the more exceptional events it will contain. Without the denominator, exceptional events become evidence for exceptional mechanisms.

Remote viewing adds two amplifiers.

First, the product is flexible language. A transcript may contain dozens of sensory fragments, shapes, nouns, sketches, and tentative interpretations. After the target is known, the subset that matches can be extracted. Second, the program was classified. Most outsiders could not compare the famous success to the full run of mundane attempts. Secrecy did not create confirmation bias, but it gave confirmation bias better cover.

The declassified archive partially repairs this by preserving ugly sessions.

That is one of the most valuable features of the record. Reports exist that say there was little target correlation. Training sessions wander. Operational reports include contradictions. The paperwork shows a program that could record failure, not merely a later publicity machine manufacturing triumphs.

But declassification creates a new selection process.

Search interfaces reward memorable keywords. Journalists and enthusiasts find pages mentioning submarines, hostages, rockets, coordinates, and famous viewers. Few readers sample session reports randomly. The archive is too large and uneven. A second mythology can therefore grow from genuine primary documents selected non-randomly.

Primary does not mean representative.

This distinction should sit on the desk whenever a declassified document goes viral.

A stamped page saying that certain impressions were later “confirmed” is meaningful. It is stronger than a thirdhand anecdote. Yet we still need to know how many impressions were made, who performed the confirmation, what counted as a match, and how many comparable sessions were not confirmed. A primary source can preserve a biased judgment perfectly.

The problem is not solved by assuming officials were gullible. Nor is it solved by assuming classification means the confirmation was rigorous. We have to reconstruct the evaluation itself.

One way to see the issue is to imagine a viewer generates twenty descriptors per session across one hundred sessions. That is two thousand descriptors. Even if the descriptions are independent noise—which in reality they would not be—some will match low-probability target features surprisingly well. If human memory preserves those twenty and forgets the other 1,980, the program can feel miraculous.

Now suppose there is a weak genuine effect layered on top. The same selection process still exaggerates it.

This is important because the argument is not binary. Selection bias can coexist with anomalous information. Correcting selection does not necessarily reduce performance to chance, but it changes the magnitude one should infer from anecdotes.

The 1995 statistical debate can be understood as an attempt to escape anecdotal selection by evaluating series. Jessica Utts emphasized formal results across controlled experiments rather than famous operational stories. That was the right level of analysis for the scientific question. Ray Hyman’s response pushed one level deeper: a series can still share design assumptions and methodological ancestry. Aggregation does not cure a common-mode error.

Intelligence customers had the inverse problem. They did not consume aggregate z-scores. They consumed individual reports. Even if a series showed a small effect, any particular operational session could remain difficult to distinguish from noise.

This creates the strange possibility that both the believer’s favorite statistic and the skeptic’s favorite operational criticism are true.

That possibility is the book’s main refusal.

We will not force the evidence into one dramatic verdict when it may support different verdicts at different scales.

There is also a social reason hits travel better. A program under bureaucratic threat naturally collects success stories. This is not fraud; it is organizational behavior. Managers defending budget lines present accomplishments. Champions remember breakthroughs. Customers who were impressed become references. Negative customers stop calling.

The resulting sample of testimonials becomes endogenously positive.

Any service organization has this problem. A consulting firm’s case studies are not a random sample of engagements. A hospital’s miracle stories are not its outcome table. A startup’s customer quotes are not retention statistics. Remote viewing was a service inside institutions, and its oral history should be read with the same caution.

The program’s critics also had selection incentives. A skeptic writing a retrospective may foreground notorious methodological failures and ignore later protocol improvements. A debunker can build a reverse case-study deck.

The answer is not symmetrical cynicism. It is sampling.

How would one sample the archive fairly?

Ideally, start with a defined time period and recover every session within it, not every famous session. Preserve original tasking and timestamps. Code the output before viewing target feedback. Have multiple independent judges score specificity, accuracy, novelty, and operational relevance. Record negative as well as positive claims. Compare viewers with baselines. Then separate training from operations.

This would not settle mechanism, but it would tell us much more about performance.

The public archive may permit parts of such a study, though missing files and redactions complicate completeness. The fact that this reconstruction is still laborious decades later is itself informative. A program that wanted to demonstrate operational value should have built such an evaluation pipeline contemporaneously.

Perhaps some internal systems existed and are absent from the public record. That possibility belongs in evidence class two: credible but incomplete. It cannot do work until documents appear.

The most difficult anecdotes involve details that appear extraordinarily specific.

If a viewer described a feature that was highly unusual, unknown to the tasker, recorded before feedback, and later confirmed by an independent source, the case deserves attention even if the overall program was weak. A rare event can challenge a null model. The proper response is not to average it away automatically.

But rare events demand the strictest chain of custody.

Was the transcript altered?

Was the detail actually present before confirmation?

How many candidate interpretations existed?

How rare was the feature among plausible targets?

Did the verifying source know about the remote-viewing claim?

Was the target selected after the fact because the session looked good?

Could ordinary inference from known target class explain it?

Anecdotes become evidence as those questions are answered.

Remote-viewing culture sometimes treats demand for this reconstruction as moving the goalposts. It is the opposite. The goalpost is being located.

Extraordinary specificity is precisely what could overcome the operational weakness of vague language. If the program repeatedly generated such details under clean conditions, that would be its strongest case. The fact that we have famous examples but no universally accepted corpus of prospectively scored extraordinary specifics is therefore important.

The best-known hits became folklore because they were exceptional.

An intelligence service needs the exception to become routine enough that it stops being a story.
