# The Program With Too Many Names

Conspiracy stories love code names because code names imply continuity.

The remote-viewing program had many of them, and that fact is often used as evidence that something vast hid behind the public record. SCANATE. GONDOLA WISH. GRILL FLAME. CENTER LANE. SUN STREAK. STAR GATE. The names form a satisfying procession, as if one secret machine simply changed labels whenever outsiders got too close.

The documentary record is messier.

Sponsors changed. Organizational homes changed. Contracts changed. Personnel changed. Research and operations moved in and out of the same umbrella. The final retrospective label “STARGATE” is useful for talking about the broad history, but it can make different programs look like chapters in a single master plan. They were related. They were not identical.

This matters because institutional continuity is itself evidence. If an organization repeatedly renames and transfers a project, there are at least two broad explanations. One is covert persistence: the capability is important enough to protect and move. The other is bureaucratic instability: nobody quite knows where the work belongs, champions keep it alive, skeptics keep pushing it away, and each new sponsor inherits a partially solved problem.

Remote viewing contains evidence for the second pattern in abundance.

The early CIA-supported SRI research belonged to a period when the agency was exploring whether claimed paranormal phenomena had intelligence relevance. By the late 1970s, Army and DIA involvement became more prominent. The Army’s Intelligence and Security Command formed a team. Government documents discuss GRILL FLAME and related efforts. Later, the program moved through additional organizational structures and contractors, including SRI and SAIC, before the mid-1990s transfer back to CIA for evaluation.

A simple line from “CIA psychic spies” to “STARGATE” erases the politics inside those transfers.

Programs do not migrate for one reason. Budget ownership matters. Legal authorities matter. The preferences of senior officers matter. Congressional interest matters. A sponsor may believe in a capability but lack funds; another may inherit it reluctantly. A research contractor can maintain technical continuity even as the government customer changes. A small program can survive because no single decision point is large enough to force a definitive reckoning.

That last possibility deserves more attention.

Large weapons programs create obvious gates. Billions of dollars require milestones, tests, appropriations, hearings, schedules, and hardware. A small anomalous-research program can persist in a bureaucratic ecological niche. Its cost is low enough to escape major scrutiny. Its potential payoff is high enough to defend against termination. Its outputs are ambiguous enough that both supporters and critics can find evidence. Its secrecy reduces outside pressure. Such a program can become institutionally immortal without becoming operationally mature.

This is not unique to government. Companies maintain speculative research projects for similar reasons. A cheap project with a charismatic champion and potentially enormous upside can live through reorganizations long after its original business case becomes blurry. What is unusual here is that the product was information about information.

The program’s names also tracked shifts in emphasis. Some phases were framed as research into psychoenergetics or anomalous cognition. Others emphasized operational remote viewing. Training systems emerged. Tasking became more structured. The Army’s program developed procedures intended to turn a claimed spontaneous faculty into a teachable discipline.

That is an extraordinary institutional act even if the faculty was not paranormal.

A bureaucracy was trying to industrialize intuition.

The attempt reveals a problem common to all weak-signal human capabilities. Suppose a few individuals appear unusually good at a task whose mechanism is unknown. The organization has several choices. It can keep using those rare individuals. It can study what they do and try to train others. It can build a selection test. Or it can abandon the capability because it cannot be scaled.

Remote viewing explored all three of the first options at various times.

This created another source of ambiguity. If performance varies sharply by individual, aggregate results can look weak while a small subset looks exceptional. Advocates can then argue that averaging destroys the signal. Skeptics can answer that identifying exceptional performers after observing their data creates selection bias. Both points can be true.

Operational organizations often live with individual differences. Fighter pilots, interrogators, analysts, surgeons, snipers, and translators vary. The problem is not variation itself. The problem is whether high performers can be identified prospectively and whether their superiority persists on new cases.

A viewer who is “good” because of three famous past hits is not yet a calibrated asset. A viewer who can be selected through blinded trials, then outperform controls on future targets, is more interesting.

Program continuity should have made that test easier. Years of tasking create longitudinal records. A mature organization could estimate each viewer’s hit rate, descriptor distributions, false-positive patterns, target-class strengths, and degradation under stress. It could compare trained and untrained personnel. It could examine whether particular interviewers improved results. It could quantify whether operational customers gained value.

The declassified record contains pieces of this, but not a public performance system comparable to what one would expect from a mature sensor program.

That absence is not proof that no classified evaluation existed. It is an evidentiary boundary. We have to distinguish “not in the public archive” from “never happened.” But claims of hidden superior evaluation carry the burden of evidence. An absent secret report cannot be used to rescue a visible weak record.

The code-name sequence also feeds a more dramatic allegation: that public termination in 1995 merely moved the work into deeper classification. This possibility cannot be logically disproved. Many programs migrate. Intelligence agencies do not publish comprehensive maps of current sensitive research. But possibility is not evidence.

The public record supports a simpler conclusion. CIA accepted the program back from DIA under conditions that included an external evaluation. The American Institutes for Research review examined research and applications. CIA’s public account says it did not restore the program because the phenomenon was too inconsistent and unreliable for intelligence use. Congress then pushed declassification of the historical records. A vast documentary collection became public.

To claim that an operational psychic-espionage program secretly continued after that point requires additional evidence, not merely the fact that secret programs exist.

This is where the code names can mislead. Once the reader learns that GRILL FLAME became CENTER LANE and later SUN STREAK, it feels natural to imagine an unknown name after STAR GATE. The pattern invites extrapolation. History is not obligated to continue a pattern because the pattern is narratively pleasing.

There is a second reason to resist code-name hypnosis. Names encourage us to ask whether “the program” worked, when the useful unit may be smaller.

Did a particular laboratory protocol produce above-chance rankings?

Did a particular viewer generate useful descriptions?

Did a particular operational task add information?

Did a training method improve prospective performance?

Did a customer act differently because of the report?

A program-level verdict can hide heterogeneous answers. STARGATE might contain a statistically interesting research subset and an operationally disappointing tasking subset. Or early results might have been artifact while later methods improved. Or a few viewers might have outperformed while group averages failed. Treating the whole archive as one binary experiment prevents these distinctions.

The government itself sometimes blurred them. Research success could justify operational exploration. Operational anecdotes could justify research. Foreign-threat assessments could justify both. Each strand reinforced the others while using a different evidentiary standard.

This is a classic way organizations become hard to falsify.

If a laboratory series fails, perhaps the viewers were not selected properly. If an operation fails, perhaps the target was unsuitable. If a viewer succeeds, perhaps the training works. If a critic identifies leakage, redesign the protocol. Redesign is exactly what science should do, but a program needs a point at which repeated rescue hypotheses reduce confidence rather than merely generate another phase.

The many names tell us that the question survived.

They do not tell us why.

To answer that, we have to look at what the program actually handed its customers. Not the code word on the folder. The report inside.
