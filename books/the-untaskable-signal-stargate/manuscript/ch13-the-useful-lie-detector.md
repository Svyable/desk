# The Useful Lie Detector

The intelligence question can be phrased without mentioning psychic phenomena at all.

How good does a source have to be before it is useful?

The answer is not “better than chance.”

Chance is a scientific baseline, not an operational threshold.

A source can perform above chance and still be useless if the improvement is too small, too expensive, too slow, or impossible to distinguish on individual cases. Conversely, a source can be useful while wrong often, if its errors are calibrated and the cost of checking is low.

This is why ordinary intelligence disciplines cannot be ranked by accuracy alone. A human source who is correct sixty percent of the time may be invaluable if she has access no one else has and her reporting sharply changes priors. A satellite with occasional cloud cover may be indispensable because clear images are highly specific. A cryptanalytic lead may fail ninety-nine times and justify itself on the hundredth if the payoff is enormous and the false leads are cheap to discard.

Remote viewing needed an equivalent utility model.

It never acquired one persuasive enough to survive external review.

The operational evaluator’s problem was not merely that some sessions were wrong. Every source is wrong. The deeper problem was discriminability: customers could not tell which remote-viewing information was accurate before independent confirmation. Correct details arrived mixed with incorrect details. Reports were often broad. The source lacked a confidence measure that reliably separated signal from noise.

This is what makes the comparison to a lie detector useful.

Polygraphs, whatever one thinks of their scientific validity, produce structured measurements interpreted according to procedures. Their history is also full of disputes about false positives, countermeasures, examiner effects, and overclaiming. A tool can survive institutionally because customers believe it adds information even while scientists argue about mechanism and error rates.

Remote viewing had the harder task because it did not produce a stable scalar output. It produced descriptions.

Descriptions need interpretation. Interpretation multiplies degrees of freedom.

An operationally useful descriptive source would need something like a confidence hierarchy. Certain elements might be highly reliable: geometry, perhaps. Others less so: names, colors, numbers. Viewers themselves sometimes reported confidence or distinctions between raw impression and analytical overlay. A mature system could test whether those self-ratings predicted accuracy.

If they did, the customer would gain a way to weight the report.

If they did not, confidence would be theater.

The public history does not show such a calibration becoming the foundation of doctrine.

This matters because the strongest defense of weak sources is usually fusion. “No one source is perfect; combine it with others.” True. But fusion helps only if analysts know how to weight each source. Adding an uncalibrated stream can make the combined estimate worse.

Suppose imagery suggests a facility contains missile components. Human reporting is uncertain. A remote viewer describes “medical” features. Should the analyst reduce confidence in the missile hypothesis? Ignore the report? Search for a dual-use explanation? Without a known likelihood ratio for remote-viewing descriptors, the fusion step becomes subjective.

Subjective fusion is where confirmation bias thrives.

A believer may use remote viewing to reinforce a favored interpretation. A skeptic may discard a true anomaly. The institution needs a rule that works regardless of belief.

One could imagine a disciplined Bayesian scheme. Historical trials estimate how often a particular viewer correctly reports water, underground spaces, moving machinery, people counts, cardinal directions, or geometric classes. Each new descriptor updates hypotheses according to measured performance. The viewer’s identity, target category, and protocol conditions enter the model. Over time the system learns.

The fact that such an architecture sounds obvious now does not mean it was easy then. The data were heterogeneous. Ground truth was incomplete. Sample sizes per viewer and descriptor were small. Natural language coding would itself be subjective. Still, the conceptual requirement existed.

A source without a calibration layer asks customers to believe the source as a whole.

The operational record suggests customers did not.

Some requested repeated sessions. Some found particular outputs useful or intriguing. But the 1995 evaluation reportedly found that remote-viewing information had not demonstrated consistent value. That is not the same as saying no customer ever benefited. Large organizations can contain local successes inside global failure.

The real standard is counterfactual.

What decisions changed because of the source, and were those changes better than decisions made without it?

This is a harder question than “was any statement correct?”

If conventional intelligence already suggested a target, remote viewing that points to the same target may raise confidence. That can be useful. But to demonstrate marginal value, one has to compare outcomes with and without the remote-viewing report. Intelligence organizations rarely run randomized trials on live crises.

The absence of clean counterfactuals helps weak capabilities survive.

A tasker remembers that remote viewing supported the eventual answer. Would the tasker have reached the answer anyway? Hard to know. A failed mission can be blamed on other factors. A success has many parents.

This is why intelligence evaluation often relies on customer surveys, case reviews, and judgments of unique contribution rather than laboratory-style causal inference. The AIR operational review inherited those limitations.

Its negative conclusion should therefore be neither inflated nor dismissed.

It is not a randomized controlled trial proving zero operational value.

It is an external assessment that, after examining available cases and customer use, did not find sufficient reliable utility to justify continuation.

That is enough for a budget decision.

The phrase “useful lie detector” in the chapter title is deliberately provocative because it captures a category error. Institutions often keep contested tools not because the tools reveal truth directly, but because they create behavior or structure that someone finds useful. Polygraph programs can elicit admissions independent of physiological validity. Brainstorming methods can generate leads independent of the theory behind them. A remote-viewing session might occasionally prompt analysts to consider hypotheses they would otherwise miss.

If that happened, remote viewing could have ancillary value even if psi were false.

But ancillary value must be weighed against contamination. A random hypothesis generator can expand search, but it can also waste attention. An analyst could obtain the same creativity from red teams, scenario generation, or structured analytic techniques without invoking anomalous cognition.

This creates a devastating alternative explanation for some operational praise: perhaps the process occasionally helped because it forced unusual associations, not because information crossed space paranormally.

Again, the explanation should not be assumed. It should be tested.

Give analysts remote-viewing transcripts generated against unrelated targets and see whether they find comparable “useful” leads. Compare them with structured random prompts. Measure whether actual target transcripts improve outcomes beyond generic ambiguity. This kind of placebo-controlled operational evaluation could separate informational content from creativity effects.

The program ended before such a modern evaluation culture took hold.

Its archive therefore leaves us with an asymmetry.

We can find sessions that look impressive.

We can find sessions that look terrible.

We can find statistical arguments for an aggregate effect.

We can find institutional judgments that the effect was not operationally reliable.

What we do not find is a simple, portable performance specification: under condition X, viewer Y provides information of quality Z with error rate Q.

That missing specification is the difference between a phenomenon and a tool.

The intelligence community spent years trying to cross it.
