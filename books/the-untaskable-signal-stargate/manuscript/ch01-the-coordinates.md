# The Coordinates

The document does not begin with a claim about telepathy. It begins like paperwork.

A date. A session number. A classification marking. A sentence explaining that a remote-viewing session was conducted in response to a request from somewhere else in the government. Then the warning, repeated across years of Army and Defense Intelligence Agency records, that the viewer’s impressions were raw intelligence data. No intermediate analysis. No evaluation. No collation. Interpretation belonged to the requester.

That sentence is more interesting than the paranormal claim.

It is the institutional seam in the whole story. On one side sits an experimenter or interviewer, trying to create a condition in which a person can describe a hidden place, object, or event without normal sensory access. On the other sits an intelligence customer who does not need a statistically interesting deviation from chance. The customer needs a ship, a building, a hostage, a launch site, a date, a location, a piece of equipment, preferably before somebody else knows it. Between them sits language: rounded, metallic, cold, vertical, movement, water, a feeling of enclosure, several structures, perhaps a road.

The strange history usually called STARGATE becomes much less silly, and much less comforting, when read at that seam.

The United States did spend government money on remote viewing. That fact is no longer an allegation, and it has not been one for decades. The declassified archive is enormous. CIA, Army, DIA, Stanford Research Institute, later Science Applications International Corporation, and a changing cast of program names appear in it: SCANATE, GONDOLA WISH, GRILL FLAME, CENTER LANE, SUN STREAK, and eventually STAR GATE. The names can make the program feel more coherent than it was. In reality, funding, sponsorship, personnel, mission, and enthusiasm shifted repeatedly. Some parts were laboratory research. Some were training. Some were operational tasking. Some were evaluations of foreign work. Some were bureaucratic survival.

That is evidence class one: documented fact. Programs existed. Contracts existed. sessions existed. Intelligence organizations asked for them. The archive includes failures as well as successes. One 1978 training report states that the target images described by the viewer showed “absolutely no target correlation.” A 1982 training report says post-session analysis found “very little correlation.” Other reports claim correspondences that sponsors regarded as striking. Still others simply hand the raw transcript to the customer and decline to settle the question.

Evidence class two is harder: credible but incomplete testimony and reporting about what particular sessions accomplished. Former participants have described memorable hits. Officials sometimes recorded favorable judgments. Some declassified summaries say impressions were later confirmed by human sources or national technical means. Such statements matter. They are not nothing. But they do not automatically establish the underlying mechanism, because intelligence validation is itself a process with possible leakage, flexible matching, incomplete records, and hindsight. A report that says a session was “confirmed” establishes that an official assessment existed. To decide what the confirmation means, one wants the original tasking, the transcript before feedback, the contemporaneous scoring rule, the target information available to everyone in the chain, and the independent source that later settled the fact.

Evidence class three is the layer that grows fastest in popular memory. This is where exceptional operational stories acquire hard edges they often did not possess in the primary record. A viewer located a missing aircraft exactly. A psychic found a hostage. A session described a Soviet submarine before satellites did. A hidden installation was revealed from thousands of miles away. Some of these stories have documentary cores. The mistake is to treat every later retelling as if it were a clean prospective trial with a frozen target and a single unambiguous answer. The archive is full of reasons not to do that.

Evidence class four is where the book will occasionally take a risk, but label the risk. Perhaps remote viewing is a weak anomalous information channel. Perhaps the laboratory effect, if real, does not survive the constraints that make intelligence useful. Perhaps the apparent laboratory effect is produced by methodological artifacts that became increasingly subtle as protocols improved. Perhaps some of the government’s persistence came from adversarial uncertainty: if a Soviet program might be real, even a small probability could justify a small American program. Perhaps secrecy itself changed the epistemology by preventing ordinary scientific correction while simultaneously giving anecdotes the glamour of classified knowledge. Those are hypotheses. They are not facts concealed behind a wink.

The usual debate asks the wrong first question: Was remote viewing real?

Real can mean too many things. A statistically detectable anomaly in repeated laboratory trials is one kind of reality. A stable human capability is another. A mechanism is another. A trained operational skill is another. An intelligence discipline capable of reducing uncertainty for a decision maker is another. If those categories are collapsed, everyone wins his preferred argument. The believer points to a low probability value. The skeptic points to an operational failure. The program manager points to a striking session. The intelligence officer points to unreliability. They may be answering different questions without noticing.

The sharper question is what happens when an anomaly is asked to become a service.

Intelligence is cruel to effects that cannot be tasked. It is not enough to say something unusual happened. The unusual thing must happen on demand, against the target the customer actually cares about, within the available time, under conditions that prevent ordinary acquisition, in language specific enough to change an action, and often without rapid feedback. It must also survive the base rate problem. If an analyst receives ten pages of impressions and one phrase fits the target beautifully, the value of that phrase depends on how many other phrases could have fit something else. If a viewer describes “water,” “metal,” and “a large structure” while the target is a naval installation, the match may feel meaningful. If those words occur frequently across sessions, less so. Intelligence does not get to score only the memorable nouns.

Science imposes a different cruelty. A laboratory can simplify the target and formalize the judge’s task. It can randomize, blind, repeat, and compute. It can compare a transcript against a small set of candidate targets. Those are virtues if the question is whether performance departs from chance under the protocol. They can also create a distance from operational use. A forced-choice ranking among five possible photographs is not the same task as telling a commander which warehouse contains a hostage. A protocol can get scientifically cleaner while becoming less like intelligence.

That tension appears early. In 1974 Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff published “Information transmission under conditions of sensory shielding” in Nature. They reported experiments at Stanford Research Institute that they believed suggested a perceptual channel not accounted for by known senses. The paper became a hinge in the history because it put a spectacular claim inside recognizable experimental machinery: targets, shielding, judges, statistics. Nature’s decision to publish did not certify the phenomenon. The journal’s own framing made that plain. Publication created a public object that other researchers could attack.

They did.

David Marks and Richard Kammann later argued that judging material in remote-viewing experiments contained sensory cues, including ordering information that could let judges match transcripts to targets without paranormal perception. Their critique is not a generic appeal to fraud or stupidity. It is exactly the kind of mundane mechanism good experimental work must fear: information traveling through the protocol by a route nobody intended. The dispute became a preview of the entire field. What looked like a signal could be real, but if a conventional channel remained open, the paranormal explanation had not earned exclusivity.

The government program did not simply stop there. That persistence is one reason the subject deserves a book instead of a punch line.

Cold War institutions routinely funded things because the cost of being surprised mattered. Reports of Soviet and East European parapsychology circulated through U.S. intelligence. Whether those reports accurately represented Soviet capability is a separate question. An adversary’s interest can itself become a cause of your interest. If Moscow spends money on psychotronics, Washington must decide whether Moscow knows something, is fooling itself, is fooling you, or is exploiting your fear that it knows something. Every branch of that tree can generate paperwork.

The asymmetry is obvious in hindsight. A modest remote-viewing program was cheap next to satellites, submarines, bombers, or signals-intelligence systems. If the claimed capability had even a small chance of being genuine and militarily useful, exploration could look rational. This does not mean continuation was always rational, or that every sponsor made a good decision. It means “the government funded it” is weak evidence for the phenomenon and potentially strong evidence about the government’s uncertainty.

This distinction matters because classified research easily acquires an aura of secret validation. The public sees a stamped page and assumes the stamp protects a truth. More often it protects a question, a source, a collection method, a vulnerability, a bureaucracy, or the fact that somebody in authority considered a possibility worth examining. Intelligence agencies investigate false leads professionally. Their archives contain failed ideas because failure is one of the things archives are for.

Remote viewing adds another complication. Its outputs are language, sketches, impressions, and sometimes numbers. These are unusually elastic objects. A radar return can be reprocessed, but it still comes from a physical sensor with defined geometry and noise. A sentence such as “I get something tall, isolated, with a sense of cold” can be compared with many targets after the fact. The evaluator’s mind becomes part of the instrument. That does not invalidate the enterprise. It means the scoring system is not administrative trivia. It is the detector.

The Army reports often recognized this by emphasizing rawness. The viewer supplies impressions. Somebody else interprets. That division protects the viewer from analytic contamination and acknowledges a practical problem, but it also creates a second channel for flexibility. If the customer is allowed to decide which fragments matter, then the operational score can drift toward subjective validation unless the evaluation rule is frozen before the answer is known.

There is a useful analogy in medicine. A screening test can have a measurable sensitivity and specificity in a controlled population and still be a poor diagnostic tool when prevalence changes. The test did not stop working; the decision environment changed. Remote viewing, if granted its strongest laboratory case for the sake of argument, faces an even harsher transfer problem. The target space of real intelligence is enormous. The cost of false specificity can be high. Feedback is noisy. Ground truth can arrive late or never. Analysts already possess fragmentary conventional information that can unconsciously shape interpretation. A weak channel that looks impressive when choosing among five photographs may become nearly useless when the possible world is effectively unbounded.

This is not an argument that the laboratory findings are therefore genuine. It is a way to keep the questions separate long enough to test them.

In 1995, when CIA commissioned the American Institutes for Research to evaluate the accumulated program, that separation became explicit. Statistician Jessica Utts argued that the experimental database showed evidence of anomalous cognition. Psychologist Ray Hyman agreed that some statistical patterns required explanation but disputed the inference that psychic functioning had been established, emphasizing methodological lineage, independence, and possible flaws. The operational review was more damaging to the program’s future. CIA’s later public summary says the phenomenon was judged too unreliable, inconsistent, and sporadic to be useful for intelligence, and the agency did not restore the program.

That ending has often been flattened into one of two myths. In the skeptical myth, the government spent twenty years discovering nothing and finally admitted it. In the believer’s myth, the government proved psychic spying worked and then publicly killed the program for reasons that are themselves suspicious. Both are easier to tell than what the documents suggest.

The more interesting possibility is that the program accumulated different answers at different levels.

At the level of individual sessions, there were failures, ambiguous passages, and apparent hits.

At the level of laboratory statistics, there were results that some qualified evaluators considered anomalous and others considered methodologically unresolved.

At the level of mechanism, there was no accepted physical or biological account that turned the effect into ordinary science.

At the level of operational intelligence, customers did not receive a capability reliable and specific enough to justify continuation.

None of those sentences logically erases the others.

This book will live in the gaps between them.

The archive permits a discipline that the culture around psychic phenomena often resists. We can say that a session happened without saying it worked. We can say an evaluator found a low probability without saying clairvoyance caused it. We can say an intelligence office funded research without saying the office endorsed the theory. We can say a skeptic identified a vulnerability without assuming every later protocol shared it. We can say a viewer produced a striking description without pretending that striking descriptions were consistently actionable.

The word “psi” will appear here because historical participants used it and because a compact label is convenient. It will not do explanatory work. Naming the anomaly is not explaining it.

The same rule applies to “remote viewing.” The term sounds like a faculty, almost an organ: remote vision. Operationally, the practice often looked less like seeing than reporting fragments under a structured protocol. Shapes, textures, temperatures, movement, spatial relations, occasional nouns, sketches. The protocol itself sometimes encouraged viewers to avoid premature analytical labeling, on the theory that naming an object too quickly could distort raw impressions. That creates a peculiar intelligence product: deliberately pre-analytic material delivered to analysts who need conclusions.

A capability can die in that translation even if something anomalous survives upstream.

That is the thesis worth testing.

To test it fairly, we have to resist two pleasures. The first is ridicule. A declassified psychic-spy program is intrinsically funny, and some episodes invite comedy. But ridicule is a poor instrument for explaining why competent institutions revisit strange claims. The second is enchantment. Classification markings, famous viewers, Cold War targets, and stories of impossible knowledge create their own narrative voltage. A stamped SECRET page can make weak evidence feel heavier than it is.

The documents deserve neither reflex.

They deserve bookkeeping.

Who knew the target? When did they know it? What information reached the viewer? What information reached the interviewer? Was the transcript fixed before feedback? Who judged the match? Against how many alternatives? Was the scoring rule chosen in advance? Did the session answer the customer’s actual question? Did it add anything not already suspected? Did the information arrive in time? Was the apparent hit recorded contemporaneously or reconstructed later? How many misses surround it? Could a conventional leak, cue, or analytic prior explain the result? If not, what exactly remains unexplained?

Those questions are unromantic. They are also how the story gets stranger.

Because after the obvious mistakes are removed, after the anecdotes are demoted to anecdotes, after the classified stamp is stripped of mystical authority, a residue remains that serious people disagreed about. The 1995 evaluators did not simply divide into believer and fool. They disagreed over what a persistent statistical deviation licensed one to infer. That is a real scientific dispute, even if one thinks the eventual paranormal interpretation fails.

And the operational record supplies something the laboratory cannot: a stress test against reality’s target set.

A commander does not care that the viewer’s rank-order score is above chance across a series if tonight’s description cannot distinguish a warehouse from a water-treatment plant. A hostage team cannot act on “stone, damp, underground” unless the phrase narrows a search. An analyst cannot reward a session for mentioning cranes after learning that cranes were present if cranes were one of twenty equally vague impressions. The customer lives in the denominator.

So does the scientist.

The first coordinates in this book are therefore not latitude and longitude. They are four coordinates of evidence: what was documented, what was credibly reported but incompletely established, what was alleged, and what we are tempted to synthesize from the gaps.

The program’s viewers were asked to describe places they could not see.

Our harder task is to describe the program without seeing into it what we already believe.
