# Raw Intelligence

The phrase appears again and again in the declassified session reports: raw intelligence data.

It sounds modest. It is actually a theory of responsibility.

The viewer reports impressions. The program does not guarantee their meaning. The requester interprets and uses them. In conventional intelligence this division is familiar. A signals intercept is not an intelligence estimate. A human source report is not a policy conclusion. Raw collection passes through analysts who compare, weigh, corroborate, and contextualize.

Remote viewing borrowed that architecture, but the fit was imperfect.

A conventional source usually has an intelligible access path. A wiretap records a channel because hardware is connected to it. A satellite image depicts a place because optics and orbital geometry put that place on a sensor. A human source reports what she heard or saw through social access. Analysts may doubt reliability, but they can model the route by which information should have entered the report.

Remote viewing proposed a source without an accepted route.

That made rawness more dangerous.

If a viewer says “water, tall verticals, gray metal, vibration, circular opening,” the analyst faces two uncertainties at once. First: what does the language refer to? Second: which parts, if any, originate in the target rather than imagination, expectation, prior knowledge, or noise? A conventional analyst can sometimes separate source reliability from report content. Here the source’s reliability may vary from descriptor to descriptor within the same paragraph.

The program’s solution was often to emphasize low-level sensory impressions and discourage premature naming. In some remote-viewing systems, “analytical overlay” became a term of art for the mind’s attempt to convert fragments into an object too early. The viewer might be trained to report coolness, texture, scale, movement, geometry, sound, or spatial relation before saying “ship” or “tower.”

As a discipline of observation, this is psychologically plausible. Anyone trying to identify a faint object can mislead himself by naming it too soon. Once the brain settles on “boat,” every ambiguous feature becomes boat-shaped.

But operationally the technique creates a paradox.

The viewer is trained not to conclude. The customer needs conclusions.

So the burden shifts downstream, to an analyst who already knows something about the mission. That analyst may be exactly the person most capable of converting fragments into useful intelligence and exactly the person most vulnerable to fitting fragments to an expectation.

This is not a remote-viewing-specific defect. Intelligence analysis is inference under partial information. Analysts routinely assemble fragments. The special problem is that the fragments here may not have a stable likelihood function. How much more likely is “cold, metallic, enclosed” if the target is a submarine than if the target is an industrial building? Without that calibration, the analyst has no principled weight.

A source report can still be useful without equations. Experienced analysts use qualitative reliability all the time. But experience requires feedback. The source has to build a track record against ground truth.

The declassified archive gives glimpses of such judgments. Some sessions were evaluated as poor. Some were described as having good correlation. Some records note that later intelligence confirmed specific impressions. Yet the public record does not present a single mature scoring system through which every operational session can be compared.

That matters because the raw-intelligence label can become a one-way valve.

When a session contains a striking correspondence, advocates can credit the viewer. When it contains errors, defenders can say the customer misinterpreted raw data. The ambiguity that makes analysis necessary also makes accountability slippery.

A mature capability would need to predefine where responsibility lies.

Suppose a viewer reports ten descriptors. Before feedback, an independent analyst translates them into three testable propositions. Those propositions are scored when ground truth arrives. The original descriptors remain preserved. Over many cases, one can estimate whether translation adds value or merely improves retrospective fit. Different analysts can be compared. A viewer can be assessed on raw descriptors and operational propositions separately.

This architecture would answer a question the program often left blurry: is the alleged signal in the viewer, the viewer-interviewer dyad, or the analyst’s pattern recognition?

That question matters because a system can perform above chance without its components doing what designers think they do.

Imagine that viewers generate broad but weakly target-related descriptors. Skilled analysts, given contextual knowledge, may turn those fragments into useful hypotheses. The combined system could outperform chance even if no single descriptor is impressive. Conversely, analysts may create the appearance of performance by mapping generic phrases onto what they already expect.

Only prospective scoring separates those possibilities.

The intelligence phrase “raw data” also creates an epistemic prestige problem. Data sounds more objective than impression. A viewer’s sensation of “angular, reddish, open” becomes an entry in a classified report. The format can harden uncertainty. Bureaucracies are good at turning tentative observations into durable artifacts.

This does not mean officials were fooled by their own paperwork. Many reports explicitly warn that interpretation is the requester’s responsibility. The caveat is evidence that people understood the limits. But caveats and institutional behavior can diverge. Once a report enters a file, it can be cited, summarized, and remembered without its qualifiers.

The same thing happens in science. A paper says “suggests.” A review says “shows.” A textbook says “is.” Remote-viewing history adds classification and operational lore to the compression chain.

One of the book’s tasks is to reverse that compression.

The raw report is not a verdict.

The analyst’s match is not a mechanism.

The confirmation memo is not automatically an independent replication.

A later anecdote is not the contemporaneous record.

At the same time, raw reports are precious because they let us see what was actually said before mythology completed the sentence. When a report contains no target correlation, that failure survives. When a transcript contains a remarkable specific detail, that survives too. The archive can be read against both believers and debunkers if one resists extracting only the convenient lines.

This is why operational sessions are in some ways more illuminating than the laboratory statistics.

The laboratory can tell us that a scoring procedure detects a departure from chance. The operational transcript forces us to confront what the departure looks like in language. It may be a sketch that resembles a target structure. It may be a cluster of accurate sensory descriptors. It may be one vivid hit surrounded by mistakes. It may be nothing.

The customer cannot act on a p-value.

The customer acts on the nouns.

That sounds unfair to a subtle phenomenon. Intelligence is unfair. It has to be. A source that only becomes impressive after ground truth is not collection in the ordinary sense. It is a puzzle about cognition.

Perhaps that puzzle is scientifically important. Perhaps, under controlled protocols, humans do access information in ways current models do not explain. But to cross into intelligence, the puzzle must acquire a calibration curve.

How often does “water” mean water?

How often does a drawing of a cylinder precede a cylindrical target rather than follow one in memory?

How much should an analyst update on “underground”?

Which viewers are better at geometry than numbers?

What descriptors are common baseline language?

Does performance change when the target class changes?

Do analysts perform better when blind to the mission?

A sensor program would ask these questions automatically.

The remote-viewing program sometimes behaved like a sensor-development program and sometimes like an artisanal intelligence service. The tension between those identities helps explain its longevity. Artisanal systems can survive on reputation and memorable cases. Sensor systems eventually demand calibration.

By 1995, calibration won.
