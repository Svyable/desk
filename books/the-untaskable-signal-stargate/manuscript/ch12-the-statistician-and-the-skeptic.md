# The Statistician and the Skeptic

In 1995 the remote-viewing argument reached a form almost too neat for its own good.

Jessica Utts was a statistician with a long-standing interest in parapsychological data. Ray Hyman was a psychologist and prominent skeptic of paranormal claims. Both were asked, within the American Institutes for Research evaluation commissioned for CIA, to assess a research record accumulated through years of government-supported work.

Later retellings often stage them as opposing lawyers.

Utts: proven.

Hyman: debunked.

The actual disagreement is more useful.

Utts argued that the laboratory evidence for anomalous cognition was statistically persuasive. In her assessment, effects had appeared repeatedly, experimental quality had improved, and the probability that the results were merely chance fluctuations was extremely small. She treated the empirical anomaly as established strongly enough that future work should focus less on proving existence and more on understanding and application.

Hyman did not answer by saying the numbers were invented. He challenged the inference from statistical regularity to paranormal functioning. He argued that the experiments were not sufficiently independent in their methodological lineage, that possible flaws could recur across a family of studies, and that the evidence did not justify the strong causal conclusion.

This is the argument the field deserved.

It moves beyond the cheap question of whether someone believes in ESP and asks what repeated statistical deviation means when the underlying experiments share a research tradition.

Suppose ten laboratories copy the same assay, and all detect a weak effect. If the assay contains an unnoticed contamination pathway, ten replications reproduce the contamination. Geographic independence is not methodological independence.

Now suppose the assay is repeatedly criticized and modified, yet the effect persists. At some point the contamination explanation begins to require a sequence of different hidden flaws that all push in the same direction. The anomaly explanation becomes more attractive.

Where that point lies is not written into probability theory.

This is why arguments about “the p-value” rarely settle controversial science. The p-value depends on a model. The real dispute often concerns the model’s completeness.

Remote viewing magnified this because the claimed causal category was so broad. “Anomalous cognition” did not identify a mechanism. It collected whatever remained after ordinary sensory channels were supposedly controlled. A residual category can be useful during discovery, but it is vulnerable to being filled by every unrecognized conventional process.

Hyman’s burden was therefore easier in one sense and harder in another.

He did not need to identify one universal artifact if he could show that the research architecture left credible room for ordinary explanations. But as controls improved, “there might be a flaw” risked becoming unfalsifiable skepticism. If no conceivable protocol could persuade the critic because the conclusion itself was deemed impossible, the scientific game would be rigged.

Utts’s burden had the mirror problem.

She could show that the null of pure chance under the specified analyses looked implausible. But she still had to establish that the alternative was paranormal information transfer rather than some correlated procedural feature. Statistical rejection does not name the cause.

Both burdens matter.

One of the book’s guiding rules is that unexplained is a legitimate state. Science is allowed to hold a result between explanation and dismissal.

That state is psychologically uncomfortable. People prefer “psychic powers are real” or “the whole thing was nonsense.” Institutions prefer decisions. Journalism prefers headlines. An unresolved small effect is not narratively cooperative.

The 1995 report separated the institutional decision from the scientific uncertainty.

The operational component of the review examined whether remote viewing had actually served intelligence customers. Here the verdict was much less favorable. Reports were often vague, inconsistent, or inaccurate; useful information could not reliably be distinguished in advance from erroneous material; and customers did not have a dependable basis for action. Whatever statistical anomaly might exist in controlled research had not become a useful intelligence discipline.

This is the book’s hinge.

The laboratory dispute can remain open without keeping the program open.

A government customer can say: perhaps there is an effect; perhaps there is not; in either case, we cannot use this product reliably enough.

That position is stronger than it first appears because operational utility is not a lower scientific standard. In some respects it is higher. The world supplies uncontrolled target classes, ambiguous ground truth, time pressure, adversarial deception, and costly false positives. A method that survives the laboratory can fail honestly in the field.

The distinction also protects science from military exaggeration. A classified customer’s disappointment does not determine whether a weak human-performance effect exists. Intelligence agencies are not the final arbiters of cognition.

Likewise, a statistical anomaly does not obligate an intelligence agency to become a patron of basic science indefinitely.

The two reviewers became symbols because they seemed to embody opposing epistemologies: quantify the effect versus distrust the inference. The better reading is that mature inquiry needs both roles inside the same room.

The statistician asks whether the pattern is too structured to dismiss as chance.

The skeptic asks whether the pattern has been attributed to the right cause.

The operator asks whether the pattern changes a decision.

STARGATE failed to make all three answers line up.

This three-way split helps with other contested sciences. Consider a biomarker correlated with disease. The statistician may establish robust association. The mechanistic scientist may doubt causality. The clinician may find that the marker adds nothing beyond existing tests. All three claims can coexist. Calling one “the truth” and the others objections flattens the system.

Remote viewing should be granted the same conceptual sophistication we grant less embarrassing subjects.

The AIR review also raises a question about the politics of expertise. When agencies commission outside evaluations of controversial internal programs, reviewer selection matters. A proponent and a skeptic can produce balance, but balance is not the same as adjudication. The reader must still evaluate the arguments. The existence of two expert opinions does not imply a fifty-fifty probability.

What matters is where they agree.

They agreed there was a body of data worth analyzing.

They agreed that statistical results could not be dismissed with a hand wave.

They disagreed over whether the evidence established anomalous functioning.

The operational evaluation found no compelling case for continued intelligence use.

That common ground is narrower than psychic enthusiasts prefer and stranger than casual skeptics expect.

CIA’s later public description of the review captures the institutional version: enough apparently accurate experiences existed to defy randomness, but the phenomenon was unreliable, inconsistent, and sporadic, and therefore not useful for intelligence purposes.

One should be cautious even with that summary. It is a later agency account compressing a complicated review. The original AIR report deserves priority for precise claims. Still, the public wording is revealing because it does not choose the rhetorically easiest closure. CIA could have said “it was fake.” Instead it emphasized utility.

Utility is where governments can stop without solving metaphysics.

The program’s defenders sometimes treat this as bureaucratic cowardice. If an anomalous effect exists, why abandon it? The answer is opportunity cost. Intelligence budgets buy capabilities. A source that produces ambiguous reports requiring verification by conventional means may be dominated by those conventional means. Basic research could continue elsewhere, but the operational unit has to justify itself as an operational unit.

Skeptics sometimes make the inverse mistake. If the program was operationally useless, why take the laboratory statistics seriously? Because useless effects can still be real. Many scientifically important phenomena have no immediate application. Operational failure narrows one claim, not every claim.

The 1995 disagreement is therefore not a frustrating failure to settle the issue.

It is the cleanest map we have of the issue.

A possible anomaly.

An unresolved cause.

An unusable service.

Three sentences. None cancels the others.
