# Secrecy Is an Experimental Condition

Classification is usually treated as background in the STARGATE story.

It should be treated as a variable.

Secrecy changes what can be checked, who can criticize, how failures circulate, how successes are remembered, and what later historians mistake for evidence. It does not merely hide the experiment from the public. It changes the social environment in which the experiment develops.

This can help frontier research.

A classified sponsor can protect an unpopular question from ridicule and career risk. Researchers can investigate a low-probability possibility without having to persuade an entire academic field first. If the possibility touches national security, this insulation may be rational. Intelligence agencies are supposed to examine things before they become obvious.

But insulation has costs.

Science is not only a method performed inside a room. It is an adversarial social process. Competitors try to reproduce results. Referees attack assumptions. Specialists notice technical mistakes. Graduate students discover that a celebrated effect vanishes under a trivial control. A classified program narrows this corrective community.

Remote viewing’s sensitivity made the narrowing unusually consequential because the alleged phenomenon was information transfer. Every unnoticed procedural feature could matter. The more eyes on the protocol, the more likely someone would see a conventional channel.

Secrecy also affects incentives inside the sponsor.

When a controversial program is known only to a small circle, reputational selection changes. People who find the subject interesting are more likely to remain involved. Skeptics may transfer out or disengage. Customers who receive poor results may stop tasking the unit, leaving a user population enriched for people who have had positive experiences. Over time, the surviving network can become more enthusiastic without anyone deliberately suppressing criticism.

This is selection pressure.

A classified niche can evolve its own standards of credibility.

The reverse can happen too. A hostile manager can kill a useful unconventional program because it is embarrassing. Secrecy concentrates authority, so local personalities matter more. The same insulation that protects fragile inquiry from public ridicule can protect it from public support.

The historical record contains hints of both dynamics but does not let us quantify them cleanly.

This is why claims about suppressed success must be handled carefully. Participants sometimes suggest that bureaucratic politics, skepticism, or fear of ridicule shaped the program’s fate. That is plausible. Government programs are political. It is not evidence that the underlying capability worked.

A failed technology can have enemies.

A successful technology can have enemies.

The existence of enemies does not decide which technology you have.

Declassification adds another layer. Once the STARGATE files entered the public domain, classification markings themselves became rhetorical objects. A document stamped SECRET or NOFORN feels like a message from behind the curtain. The reader may infer that whatever appears on the page must have been believed or operationally important.

That inference is unsafe.

Classification protects information according to potential damage from disclosure, not according to truth value. A false report can reveal a sensitive collection target. A speculative memo can reveal what the government does not know. A failed experiment can reveal capabilities, vulnerabilities, personnel, or priorities. The stamp authenticates handling status, not ontology.

This lesson extends far beyond parapsychology.

UAP documents, foreign-materiel reports, directed-energy studies, biological-effects research, exotic propulsion proposals, and intelligence assessments all attract the same category error. People read secrecy as validation. Sometimes secret information is indeed better than public information. Sometimes it is worse, because it has not been exposed to broad correction.

The interesting question is how secrecy shapes evidentiary ecology.

Imagine two identical anomalous effects. One is studied openly by twenty laboratories. The other is studied for twenty years by a small classified network. The open field may suffer publication bias, fashion, and academic incentives. The classified field may suffer narrow replication, undocumented tacit methods, limited peer criticism, and selective customer feedback. When the second archive is released, its longevity can be mistaken for cumulative confirmation.

Persistence is not the same as progress.

A program can persist because it keeps producing better evidence. It can also persist because no single result is decisive enough to kill it and the cost of continuation is low.

STARGATE appears to have contained both progress and persistence. Protocols improved. Statistical methods became more formal. Yet the operational capability remained uncertain. Secrecy helped preserve the experiment long enough to generate a substantial record. It may also have delayed the kind of independent adversarial replication that could have forced a clearer scientific outcome.

This is speculative synthesis, not a documented official conclusion.

We can test parts of it by comparing open and classified strands of the history. The 1974 Nature publication exposed early methods to criticism. Marks and Kammann’s cue analysis followed. Later government-funded work, some classified or contract-restricted during its life, developed within a smaller community. The 1995 AIR review reopened the accumulated program to external evaluation at the moment of institutional decision.

The pattern is suggestive: openness produced method criticism; closure produced longevity; external review produced termination of the intelligence application.

That sequence does not prove secrecy caused methodological weakness. It identifies a mechanism worth taking seriously.

There is another consequence of secrecy that is rarely discussed: it changes the denominator available to participants themselves.

Compartmentalization can prevent one group from seeing all failures and successes elsewhere. A viewer may know his own memorable sessions. A tasker may know a handful of cases. A senior manager may receive summaries filtered through subordinates. In a compartmented system, nobody necessarily possesses the complete performance distribution.

This is normal intelligence practice for security reasons.

It is terrible experimental practice if the objective is to estimate an effect size.

A scientific program needs aggregation. A security program limits aggregation.

STARGATE lived at that contradiction.

The government wanted to protect targets, methods, and customers. Yet to evaluate remote viewing properly, analysts needed broad access to complete series, failures included. If operational records could not be freely pooled with research data, the program’s learning loop would weaken.

Again, the public archive does not let us say exactly how severe this problem was. The important point is structural. A classified human-performance program can be scientifically distorted by the same compartmentation that protects its mission.

This suggests a broader principle for studying alleged secret technologies.

Do not ask only, “What might secrecy be hiding?”

Ask, “What might secrecy prevent the insiders themselves from knowing?”

The romantic image of a black program is a room where a small group knows the truth.

The bureaucratic reality may be a hallway where each room knows a different fragment.

For remote viewing, that possibility changes how we interpret both confidence and doubt. A participant who says the program worked may be accurately reporting his local sample. A manager who says it did not may be accurately reporting aggregate customer value. A statistician may see an anomaly in controlled trials. A skeptic may see unresolved methodological dependence. None needs to be lying.

Secrecy can preserve contradictory truths long enough for each to become a culture.

When the doors finally open, the public asks which room was right.

The better question is what the building did to the evidence.
