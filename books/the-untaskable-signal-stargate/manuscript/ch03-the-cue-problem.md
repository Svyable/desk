# The Cue Problem

A good paranormal experiment is mostly a war against boring explanations.

That is not an insult. It is the job.

If a subject describes a hidden target, the impressive interpretation is that information crossed a boundary ordinary perception cannot cross. Before accepting that interpretation, however, the experiment has to defeat every cheaper route: prior knowledge, accidental cueing, target regularities, experimenter behavior, judging artifacts, transcript order, sensory leakage, selective reporting, flexible scoring, and plain chance. The stranger the proposed channel, the less glamorous the controls become.

Remote viewing learned this publicly.

David Marks and Richard Kammann’s criticism of early SRI work turned on a devastatingly mundane question: what else was in the transcript package besides the viewer’s purported psychic impressions? If a judge received material containing dates, references to previous sessions, target-order clues, or other extraneous information, matching could become possible without remote perception. The judge might think he was comparing psychic descriptions to locations while actually reconstructing the experiment’s sequence.

The point was not that viewers had necessarily cheated. The point was that the test did not isolate what it thought it isolated.

This distinction is easy to lose because “sensory leakage” sounds like a hole in a wall. In experiments about hidden information, leakage is any conventional structure that correlates with the answer. A trial number can leak. A handwriting pattern can leak. A file sequence can leak. The experimenter’s choice of adjective can leak. The physical room may be perfect while the paperwork is transparent.

Marks and Kammann’s critique therefore belongs to the same intellectual family as cryptanalysis. You do not ask whether the ciphertext looks mysterious. You ask whether any feature of the system carries information about the plaintext. If one does, the stronger explanation has to wait.

The controversy also created a temptation for skeptics: once a serious cue had been identified, it became easy to treat the entire subject as solved forever. That move is no better than a proponent treating one successful session as permanent proof. Experimental generations differ. Later protocols can remove known cues. A field can learn.

The right skeptical question is not “was a flaw ever found?” It is “does the flaw explain the data being cited now?”

That sounds elementary. It is difficult in remote viewing because the archive spans decades, institutions, and changing methods. Early SRI demonstrations, Army operational sessions, later contractor laboratory trials, and retrospective government evaluations are often blended into one thing called STARGATE. A criticism that applies beautifully to one layer may not touch another. A positive result in a later automated protocol may say nothing about the reliability of an operational session conducted years earlier.

The book therefore needs a rule more severe than either camp usually prefers: no inherited innocence and no inherited guilt.

Every experimental claim has to carry its own controls.

This matters especially for the famous idea of the blind judge. Blinding is powerful, but “blind” is not binary. A judge can be blind to the target identity while seeing metadata correlated with target identity. An interviewer can be blind to the exact photograph but know the general target class. A viewer can be blind to the operational target but know that the tasking office is interested in Soviet military sites. Each piece of partial knowledge shrinks the hypothesis space.

That shrinking can be useful operationally. It is dangerous experimentally.

Imagine being told nothing except that the target is associated with naval intelligence. You write “water,” “metal,” “large curved object,” “confined interior,” and “machinery.” The output may be sincere and may even feel spontaneous. But your prior has done work. Against the universe of possible targets, the description is remarkable. Against a distribution dominated by ships, ports, submarines, and naval facilities, less so.

A valid protocol has to decide which universe it is testing against.

This is one reason target selection became increasingly formal in later parapsychology. Random target pools, decoy sets, automated selection, and blind ranking are not bureaucratic ornaments. They define the null hypothesis. If the null is fuzzy, a probability value can look more precise than the experiment.

The same issue appears in operational intelligence in an uglier form because the null cannot usually be manufactured. A customer may ask, “Describe the current location of person X.” There is no natural set of four decoy locations. The world supplies an effectively unlimited target space. Even if the viewer produces target-related fragments, evaluating how much information those fragments contain becomes difficult.

This is where retrospective matching becomes seductive.

After person X is found in a low concrete building near a river, phrases such as “flat roof,” “gray,” “water nearby,” and “industrial feeling” light up. Phrases such as “mountain,” “red,” “open field,” or “wooden” fade into the transcript’s background. Human memory compresses the session toward the match. The transcript, if preserved intact, can resist that compression. But only if someone scores all of it.

The archive is valuable because it contains exactly these raw materials. Many Army session reports include transcripts and drawings rather than just triumphant summaries. Some tell the reader directly that correlation was poor. Those failures prevent the program’s history from becoming pure legend.

A 1978 report, for example, states that target images “appeared to show absolutely no target correlation.” The sentence is almost refreshing. It shows a bureaucracy capable of recording a miss. A 1982 report notes “very little correlation.” These are not disproofs of every other session. They are denominator.

Denominators are what anecdotes fear.

Anecdotal evidence becomes strong when the event was predicted in advance, recorded before feedback, highly specific, independently verified, and drawn from a known number of attempts. It becomes weak when an exceptional case is selected from an unknown population. Remote viewing’s public mythology often preserves the outliers and discards the sequence.

Classification intensifies that selection problem. During the life of a classified program, outside researchers cannot see the full denominator. After declassification, records arrive unevenly, with redactions, missing context, changing release dates, and searchability that favors memorable terms. The public reconstructs a statistical history from documents optimized for administration, not later science.

The cue problem therefore exists at two levels.

Inside the experiment, cues may let information travel conventionally.

Outside the experiment, archival selection may cue the historian toward the dramatic cases.

A responsible reconstruction has to control both.

This is one reason the 1995 review matters. By then, the debate was no longer only about whether early transcripts contained clues. The later research program had implemented more sophisticated experimental procedures, and Jessica Utts argued that the accumulated statistical evidence was too consistent to dismiss as chance. Ray Hyman did not simply repeat “there were cues in the 1970s.” His disagreement was broader: the claimed replications were not as independent as they appeared, the body of work still shared methodological ancestry, and evidence of statistical departure did not automatically establish paranormal functioning.

That argument is more intellectually interesting than the cartoon version.

Suppose a family of laboratories uses descendants of the same protocol and repeatedly finds a small effect. One interpretation is replication. Another is a shared artifact reproduced with the method. Scientific independence is not just a change of address. A replication is strongest when investigators who do not share the original assumptions can reproduce the phenomenon with methods that close the original escape routes.

Remote viewing rarely achieved that clean social separation.

The field was small. Researchers, subjects, methods, sponsors, and intellectual lineages overlapped. This does not make the results invalid. It changes the weight one should place on “independent replication.” A phenomenon becomes harder to explain away when it survives hostile competence.

There is also a deeper problem. Every time a flaw is found and repaired, the protocol becomes more elaborate. If an effect shrinks, proponents may say the phenomenon is fragile. If it remains, skeptics search for another artifact. At some point the dispute can become structurally endless unless the effect grows large and robust enough to overwhelm procedural details.

Physics often escapes this trap with signal-to-noise. A new particle does not need philosophical agreement if multiple instruments see an unmistakable signature at predictable energies. Remote viewing never produced that kind of surplus. Its alleged effect lived close enough to ambiguity that scoring choices and protocol lineage remained central decades later.

That weakness does not mean “nothing happened.” It means the field lacked an experimental result that forced convergence.

The cue problem also exposes a philosophical mistake in how people talk about skepticism. Skepticism is sometimes treated as a personality: the disposition to say no. In experimental science it is better understood as channel accounting. Where could the information have come from? What transformations occurred? Which observer knew what? What was frozen when? What alternatives were available?

Those questions are equally useful whether one hopes remote viewing is real or false.

Indeed, a proponent should be more interested in them. A genuine anomalous channel buried inside a contaminated protocol is scientifically tragic. Ordinary leakage steals credit from the extraordinary effect. The only way to rescue it is to make the ordinary routes painfully impossible.

That is why the best part of the remote-viewing literature is not its most spectacular claim. It is the long argument over how to build a target that cannot whisper its identity through the experiment.

This argument migrated from academic work into operational practice imperfectly. Army reports sometimes described raw viewer impressions and left interpretation to requesters. That separation may have reduced one form of contamination by keeping the viewer away from analysis. But it did not solve the scoring problem. The customer often knew the mission, had other intelligence, and could recognize fragments that fit.

In an intelligence office, recognition is useful. In an experiment, it can be bias.

A disciplined program would need two products from every operational task: the intelligence report and the evaluation record. The first could allow analysts to synthesize with everything they know. The second would have to preserve an uncontaminated copy of what the viewer produced, the information available before tasking, the evaluation criteria, and the eventual ground truth. Only then could the organization learn whether the source added value prospectively.

The declassified record suggests this learning architecture was uneven.

That is hardly unique to psychic spying. Intelligence disciplines routinely suffer from feedback problems. Human sources disappear. Satellite interpretations are revised. Threat assessments are judged against political events that may have multiple causes. The difference is that conventional sources have mechanisms. We know roughly how a satellite acquires an image and how a human source obtains access. Remote viewing’s alleged mechanism was unknown, so empirical calibration had to carry nearly the entire burden.

Weak calibration plus unknown mechanism is a bad combination.

It also creates room for personalities. When formal reliability is unclear, reputations matter more. Certain viewers become known for hits. Certain taskers become believers or skeptics. Informal judgments substitute for quantified performance. That can be rational locally—people always learn which colleagues are good—but it makes the program difficult to evaluate from outside and easy to mythologize later.

A famous viewer’s exceptional session can become the unit of history even if the program’s average output was mediocre.

The cue problem teaches us to refuse that unit.

The unit is the sequence.

What was the protocol before the remarkable trial? What happened on the trials before and after it? How many viewers attempted comparable targets? What was the scoring rule? Did the result survive blind judging? Did anyone publish the miss distribution? Was the transcript intact? Did feedback alter later memory? Could conventional knowledge explain the broad target class? Did the session provide information unavailable elsewhere?

This is slower than telling the story of a psychic who saw across continents.

It is also more generous to the possibility that something anomalous happened.

Because once conventional cues are removed, retrospective editing is controlled, and the denominator is restored, any surviving signal becomes more interesting. Skepticism earns its keep by leaving a smaller residue, not by declaring the residue impossible in advance.

The remote-viewing program spent years chasing that residue.

The intelligence community’s mistake, if we want to call it one, was not necessarily that it asked the question. The more consequential mistake may have been failing to force the answer into a form that could terminate the program cleanly either way.

A good experiment should make belief less necessary.

A good intelligence service should demand the same.
