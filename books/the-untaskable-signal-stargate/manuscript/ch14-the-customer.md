# The Customer

An intelligence capability does not fail when a scientist dislikes it.

It fails when the customer stops calling.

That is too simple as a history of STARGATE, but it points toward the part of the archive that receives less attention than the experiments. Programs survive through users. A collection discipline needs taskers who know what questions to ask, analysts who know how to interpret the output, and decision makers who believe the answer is worth the cost of obtaining it.

Remote viewing had customers, but customer satisfaction was never the same thing as scientific validation.

This distinction matters because intelligence customers judge reports pragmatically. A customer may value an unverified source because it produces leads. Another may reject a scientifically interesting source because the format is inconvenient. Operational usefulness is shaped by workflow as much as raw accuracy.

The remote-viewing product had several workflow problems.

It was verbose relative to its reliable content. It often required interpretation. It could contain mutually inconsistent elements. Its timing varied. It did not come with a universally accepted confidence scale. And because the mechanism was unknown, customers could not readily infer when conditions should make a session better or worse.

Compare that with imagery. An analyst receiving a cloudy satellite image knows why the image is bad. A source report from a human agent may come with access and reliability ratings. Signals intelligence may have collection metadata. Remote viewing often failed opaquely.

Opaque failure is expensive.

If a viewer provides poor information, the customer does not know whether the phenomenon failed, the viewer failed, the protocol failed, the target was unsuitable, or the analyst misunderstood. Each explanation suggests a different remedy. Without diagnosis, the program can only try again.

This is one reason repeated tasking can become seductive. A second session might clarify the first. Multiple viewers might converge. Follow-up questions might sharpen details. But every additional pass increases the volume of material and the chance of accidental correspondence. Unless the combination rule is specified in advance, replication inside the operation can become a form of search through output.

Customers also create feedback that changes the source.

If taskers praise certain sessions, viewers learn which kinds of descriptions are valued. If operational targets cluster around military facilities, target expectations creep into the community. If a particular analyst likes sketches, the program may produce more sketches. Over years, the service co-evolves with its customers.

This can improve performance without proving the underlying theory. A consultant becomes better at a client’s problems because she learns the domain. A remote-viewing unit could likewise accumulate ordinary contextual expertise while remaining formally blind to specific targets.

That expertise is a confound and an asset.

Operationally, no one should care if useful knowledge comes from tacit domain learning rather than psi, provided the product works. Scientifically, the distinction is decisive.

A mature evaluation would therefore test viewers on targets outside their familiar mission distributions as well as inside them. If performance collapses outside the known domain, ordinary priors become a stronger explanation. If it persists across arbitrary novel targets under strict blindness, the anomalous case strengthens.

The customer relationship complicates this because customers want relevant targets, not arbitrary ones.

Once again, the needs of science and operations pull apart.

The AIR evaluation’s operational work reportedly included case review and interviews with users. The resulting judgment was not that every customer hated the program. Some found particular material interesting. But the review did not find a consistent pattern of intelligence value sufficient to justify continued use.

That phrase—sufficient to justify—matters.

Organizations do not require zero value to terminate a program. They require alternative uses of money and attention to be better.

A handful of useful leads across years may be genuine and still fail the portfolio test.

This is difficult for proponents because an extraordinary capability seems as though it should be nurtured indefinitely. But if the extraordinary capability remains weak, the opportunity cost is not merely budget. It is institutional credibility. Analysts asked to integrate unreliable material may begin ignoring the channel. Sponsors may become reluctant to attach their names. A program can become operationally dead before its formal termination.

Skeptics can make the reverse mistake by treating customer rejection as epistemic proof. Customers reject technologies for bad reasons all the time. Early innovations are awkward. Organizations resist tools that disrupt workflow. A scientifically real phenomenon might be abandoned before engineers learn to exploit it.

So customer behavior is evidence about usefulness, not reality.

This distinction is especially important when discussing intelligence because secrecy hides the market signal. In a commercial product, falling sales are visible. In a classified program, outsiders cannot easily tell whether customers are enthusiastic, indifferent, or merely complying with a sponsor. Declassified evaluations therefore carry unusual weight.

But they should be read critically too.

An agency deciding whether to inherit a controversial program has incentives. Bureaucratic ownership can shape evaluation. The transfer from DIA to CIA in the mid-1990s occurred in a political context where the program’s future was already in question. An external review commissioned at that moment was not a neutral event floating outside institutions.

This does not invalidate the review. It tells us to separate the quality of its evidence from the politics of the decision.

The key operational finding remains plausible because it aligns with the structure of the product: ambiguous reports, inconsistent accuracy, and inability to distinguish correct from incorrect material before verification are exactly the failure modes one would expect to frustrate customers.

The public CIA account later summarized the same problem in blunt terms: too unreliable, inconsistent, and sporadic for intelligence use.

There is a hidden compliment in that sentence.

“Sporadic” implies that something sometimes looked good.

The issue was that intelligence cannot schedule sporadic.

A customer needs to know not only that the source can be right, but how to treat the next report. This is why reliability is more important than spectacle. A mundane source with understood limitations can outperform an extraordinary source with rare brilliance.

The history of technology is full of machines that lost to worse machines because the worse machines were dependable.

Remote viewing’s advocates sometimes emphasized exceptional operators. Perhaps the program should have been treated like an elite craft rather than a mass sensor. The analogy might be a cryptanalyst with unusual intuition, a tracker, or a legendary interrogator whose skill resists formalization.

That possibility deserves respect. Institutions do rely on exceptional people.

But exceptional people still need prospective evaluation. If the elite viewer’s reputation comes from selected past hits, the craft model becomes circular. A real elite should keep being elite on new cases.

The customer is where all these abstractions become expensive.

The scientist can tolerate unresolved mechanism.

The historian can tolerate ambiguous evidence.

The customer has to decide what to do by Tuesday.
