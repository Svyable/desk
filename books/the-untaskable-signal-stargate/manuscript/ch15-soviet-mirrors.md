# Soviet Mirrors

The Soviet Union appears in the remote-viewing story twice.

First as a target.

Then as an explanation for why the United States was looking at all.

Those two roles are often merged into a stronger claim: both superpowers pursued psychic warfare because both had discovered that it worked. The public record does not justify that conclusion.

It does justify a stranger one. Both superpowers had institutions willing to investigate ideas at the boundary between physiology, psychology, physics, propaganda, and military utility, and each side’s awareness of the other could magnify the perceived stakes.

American intelligence reporting from the 1970s discussed Soviet and Czechoslovak parapsychology, “paraphysics,” psychokinesis, Kirlian photography, biological information transfer, and related topics. Some reporting came from open publications. Some came through intelligence channels. Some assessments treated the Soviet work as potentially relevant to national security.

These documents tell us a great deal about American threat perception.

They tell us less than popular retellings assume about Soviet results.

A foreign-intelligence report is not a replication. If a Soviet researcher claims psychokinetic influence on an object, an American analyst can accurately report that claim without independently establishing the effect. The report may still be valuable because it maps research priorities, institutions, personalities, and possible lines of development.

The distinction becomes crucial when the Soviet program itself is opaque.

Closed systems generate rumors efficiently. Laboratories can have military sponsors without producing military capability. Researchers may exaggerate to secure resources. Officials may sponsor marginal work because ideological prestige, biomedical curiosity, or strategic uncertainty makes it cheap. Western observers can interpret secrecy as evidence of success when secrecy is simply normal governance.

There is also deliberate deception.

Intelligence history provides many cases in which states encourage adversaries to waste resources, misunderstand capabilities, or fear nonexistent systems. The remote-viewing literature sometimes invokes disinformation as a universal explanation, usually in whichever direction protects the speaker’s preferred conclusion. That is too easy.

We should ask instead what deception would predict.

If Soviet authorities wanted the United States to believe in a mature psychotronic capability, one might expect curated leaks, dramatic demonstrations, or publications designed to amplify threat. Some Soviet and Russian media and research claims certainly attracted Western attention. But proving a coordinated deception campaign requires documentary evidence of intent. Absent that, “disinformation” belongs in the hypothesis column.

The same applies to the claim that Soviet success frightened Washington into STARGATE.

The U.S. record shows concern about foreign work. It does not show that American officials possessed decisive proof of operational Soviet psychic espionage.

Why, then, did Soviet interest matter so much?

Because intelligence competition is asymmetric around unknown unknowns.

If your adversary invests in an apparently absurd technology, you can ignore it. If the technology is truly absurd, you save money. If it is revolutionary, you may discover that after the adversary has an advantage. A small exploratory program can be rational insurance against that tail risk.

This logic explains many government studies that later look embarrassing. It also explains studies that later look visionary. The decision cannot be judged solely by outcome because decision makers did not know the outcome.

The correct retrospective question is whether the probability and consequence estimates were reasonable given the evidence available at the time, and whether the program updated appropriately as evidence accumulated.

Remote viewing may have passed the first test better than the second.

Early exploration was defensible. Reports of foreign interest, surprising domestic demonstrations, and low program cost created a rational case for controlled investigation. But as years passed, American researchers generated their own data. The Soviet mirror should have become less important. The program no longer had to ask only “what if Moscow knows something?” It could ask “what have we learned?”

If domestic evidence remained ambiguous and operational performance remained weak, continued foreign interest should not reset the clock indefinitely.

This is a general lesson about threat-driven science.

Adversary behavior is evidence, but it is noisy evidence.

Countries copy each other’s mistakes. They fund prestige projects. They respond to internal politics. They pursue capabilities because doctrine demands them, not because experiments succeed. They also hide genuine breakthroughs. The observer must distinguish convergence caused by nature from convergence caused by strategic mirroring.

The history of anti-ballistic missile systems, directed-energy weapons, chemical agents, submarine detection, and missile defense contains versions of this problem. Remote viewing is simply the purest case because the underlying phenomenon itself was contested.

The Soviet mirror also matters culturally. In American paranormal lore, “the Russians were doing it too” functions as an authority signal. It transforms an eccentric laboratory into a geopolitical race. The same move appears in stories about UFOs, psychotronics, exotic propulsion, and mind-control devices.

Sometimes geopolitical convergence is genuinely important. If independent technical cultures measure the same anomaly with different instruments, that can be strong evidence. But independent interest is not independent confirmation.

Two governments reading the same ambiguous literature can converge on the same question without converging on the same answer.

The distinction should shape how we treat claims about Chinese, Israeli, Russian, or other state research today. A government program proves that someone authorized a program. A patent proves that someone filed a patent. A classified reference proves that someone considered information sensitive. None of these alone proves efficacy.

At the same time, dismissing state interest as meaningless throws away useful evidence about perceived stakes, institutional pathways, and what kinds of anomalies persist across cultures.

The right question is what crossed borders: results, rumors, personnel, methods, threat estimates, or merely fascination?

For remote viewing, the public record supports substantial cross-border awareness and concern. It supports American attempts to assess and replicate claims. It supports a long-lived domestic program whose sponsors sometimes invoked foreign work.

It does not support a clean story of two superpowers secretly wielding proven psychic weapons.

The mirror reflects interest.

It does not tell us what stood in front of it.
