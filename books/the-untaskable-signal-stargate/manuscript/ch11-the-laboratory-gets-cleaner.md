# The Laboratory Gets Cleaner

Remote viewing did not remain frozen in 1974.

That fact is inconvenient to simple skeptical histories and essential to a fair one.

Once early experiments drew criticism, later researchers tightened procedures. Target selection became more automated. Judging became more formally blinded. Free-response descriptions were converted into ranks or scores. Different target pools were tested. Statistical methods became central. By the later SRI and SAIC period, the strongest proponent case no longer depended entirely on the spectacular demonstrations associated with Uri Geller or Pat Price.

The argument shifted.

Instead of “look at this astonishing session,” it became “look at the distribution.”

This is a healthier scientific form because distributions are harder to mythologize. A single transcript can be cherry-picked. A pre-specified series can be analyzed as a whole. If viewers consistently rank correct targets above decoys more often than chance permits, the effect can be quantified even when no one trial looks miraculous.

This is also where the operational problem becomes sharper.

A statistical effect can become more credible as it becomes less useful.

Imagine a laboratory protocol producing a small but consistent shift in target rankings. Across hundreds of trials, the shift is extremely unlikely under the null. Scientifically, that could be profound. Operationally, a customer facing one urgent target does not get the average. The customer gets one noisy draw from the distribution.

The smaller the effect size, the larger this gap becomes.

This is a familiar problem outside parapsychology. A medication can produce a statistically significant average benefit that is too small to matter clinically. A financial strategy can show an edge that disappears after transaction costs. A machine-learning classifier can beat baseline by two percentage points and still be unsafe for individual diagnosis. Significance answers whether an effect is detectable. Utility asks whether it changes decisions.

Remote viewing’s later research increasingly forced this distinction.

Jessica Utts’s 1995 assessment is central because she did not argue from a handful of dramatic anecdotes. She reviewed a body of controlled research and concluded that anomalous cognition had been demonstrated to a degree that made further proof-oriented experiments unnecessary; in her view, research should turn toward mechanism and application. Whatever one thinks of that conclusion, it deserves to be represented accurately. She was making a statistical argument, not merely expressing belief in psychic experience.

Ray Hyman disagreed. He accepted that the experimental results contained statistical anomalies requiring explanation, but he did not accept that paranormal functioning was established. He emphasized possible methodological issues, the lack of truly independent replication, and the danger of treating related experiments as if they were independent confirmations.

The two positions expose the deeper question: how much methodological uncertainty can a persistent small effect survive before the effect itself becomes the simpler explanation?

There is no shortcut.

One must inspect the studies.

Were target sequences adequately randomized?

Was the person interacting with the viewer blind?

Could target information leak through file handling?

Were all planned trials included?

Were scoring methods chosen before data inspection?

Were unsuccessful subjects excluded after the fact?

Did independent laboratories reproduce the effect?

Did effect sizes remain stable when protocols changed?

The AIR review did not answer every one of these questions to universal satisfaction. It crystallized them.

The later laboratory work also made another shift: the language “anomalous cognition” became useful because it avoided pretending to know the mechanism. Remote viewing had acquired historical baggage implying literal vision at a distance. The experiments might, under some interpretations, involve precognition, telepathy, clairvoyance, or something else. If target selection occurred after a response, for example, the intuitive causal story changed entirely.

Calling the residual “anomalous cognition” was epistemically cleaner than naming an invisible channel.

But a neutral label can acquire false solidity. Once a phenomenon has a name, it begins to feel like a thing. Anomalous cognition is a category for unexplained statistical structure, not a mechanism hiding in the brain.

This matters because mechanism was one of the places the field remained weak.

A robust effect without mechanism can still be real. History provides many examples. Yet mechanisms help science discriminate artifact from discovery. If remote viewing depended on a physical interaction, researchers could ask how it scales with distance, shielding, target complexity, timing, electromagnetic environment, or neurological disruption. Different proposed mechanisms should make different predictions.

Instead, many versions of the claim were strangely indifferent to variables ordinary channels care about. Distance often appeared not to matter. Shielding did not obviously eliminate the effect. Some experiments suggested temporal displacement. These features made the phenomenon more extraordinary and harder to triangulate.

An effect unconstrained by known physics needs correspondingly strong empirical regularities.

Later work did attempt to study moderators: individual ability, target type, feedback, physiology, sender-receiver arrangements, and other factors. But the program never developed the kind of predictive model that lets an engineer say, “under these conditions the channel should improve by this much.”

Without such a model, optimization risks becoming retrospective.

Good viewers are the viewers who scored well.

Good targets are the targets that produced strong matches.

Good protocols are the protocols in which effects appeared.

These statements can all be true and still overfit the historical dataset.

The decisive test is prospective: use the identified viewers, targets, and procedures on new trials whose analysis is locked in advance.

The 1995 review arrived before preregistration became a widespread scientific norm. Today the field’s evidentiary problems are easier to name because many disciplines have lived through replication crises. Optional stopping, researcher degrees of freedom, publication bias, flexible exclusion, and post hoc subgrouping are no longer exotic accusations. They are standard threats to inference.

That contemporary perspective cuts both ways.

It makes skepticism about small surprising effects more justified.

It also makes dismissing an entire literature because “psychic phenomena are impossible” intellectually lazy. The appropriate response is the same one modern science applies elsewhere: demand transparent protocols, complete data, preregistration, independent replication, and effect-size stability.

Remote viewing is almost a perfect historical case study for why these reforms matter.

The later laboratory work deserves credit for moving toward cleaner tests. It also deserves to be evaluated with modern standards without assuming that statistical significance equals ontological revolution.

The strongest version of the proponent case after 1995 is therefore narrower than popular psychic lore and stronger than a skeptic’s joke.

There may have been a repeatable statistical anomaly in controlled free-response tasks.

The strongest skeptical case is likewise narrower and stronger than “people cannot be psychic.”

The anomaly had not been independently and methodologically isolated well enough to compel a paranormal interpretation, and it had not become an operational source reliable enough for intelligence.

Those positions are close enough to fight over and far enough apart to matter.

The government’s final decision did not require resolving them.

That is one of the most important facts in the story.

An intelligence agency can decide a phenomenon is scientifically unresolved and operationally unusable at the same time. It does not need to settle the ontology before closing the budget line.

The laboratory got cleaner.

The service never got clean enough.
