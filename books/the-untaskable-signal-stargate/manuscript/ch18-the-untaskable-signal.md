# The Untaskable Signal

A program can end without its most interesting question ending.

That is what happened to remote viewing in the United States intelligence community, at least in the public record.

By the mid-1990s, after years of shifting sponsorship, laboratory work, training, operational tasking, internal advocacy, criticism, and accumulated lore, the government had enough history to ask a mature question. Not whether a surprising demonstration had ever occurred. Not whether a statistical series could be made to depart from chance. Not whether the Soviet Union had shown interest. The mature question was whether the capability deserved to remain an intelligence service.

The answer was no.

That answer should not be inflated.

It does not prove that every remote-viewing result was ordinary noise.

It does not prove that no human ever produced information under conditions that current science cannot explain.

It does not prove that every famous session was fraudulent, cued, or reconstructed.

It proves something narrower and operationally decisive: the program did not establish a source reliable and specific enough for continued intelligence use in the form being evaluated.

This is where the title of the book comes from.

The signal, if there was one, was untaskable.

“Untaskable” does not mean literally impossible to assign a target. The archive is full of tasking. It means the alleged effect did not become a predictable service that could answer the customer’s next question with known reliability. A sensor that occasionally produces extraordinary data but does not tell you when it is functioning is not yet a sensor system. It is a research problem.

The distinction clarifies much of the historical confusion.

The early SRI experiments asked whether something anomalous could be demonstrated under sensory shielding.

Critics asked whether conventional information could have leaked through the protocol.

Later researchers asked whether cleaner experiments still produced statistical deviations.

Army and DIA units asked whether individuals could be trained and used against real targets.

Customers asked whether reports helped.

The 1995 reviewers asked whether the accumulated record justified continued support.

These were not the same experiment repeated for twenty years. They were different thresholds stacked on top of one another.

A phenomenon can cross the first threshold and fail the fifth.

An artifact can appear to cross the first and be exposed at the second.

A weak effect can survive the third and remain too small for the fourth.

An operational anecdote can delight a customer and still fail the portfolio-level fifth.

Once these possibilities are separated, the history stops looking absurd.

It looks like frontier research conducted inside institutions poorly designed to resolve frontier research cleanly.

That sentence applies far beyond psi.

Governments investigate UAP reports, unusual biological effects, foreign propulsion claims, anomalous materials, directed-energy incidents, sensor oddities, and emerging technologies under conditions of secrecy and strategic pressure. Some anomalies collapse under better instrumentation. Some become new science. Some remain ambiguous because the data are poor. Some become bureaucratic symbols detached from their evidence.

STARGATE is a calibration case for all of them.

The first lesson is that government interest is weak evidence for efficacy.

States investigate because consequences matter, because adversaries investigate, because constituencies push, because officials are curious, because a champion persists, because a small budget buys optionality, or because the possibility cannot yet be dismissed. A classified program establishes institutional attention. It does not establish that nature cooperated.

The second lesson is that ridicule is not analysis.

The fact that a claim sounds embarrassing has no bearing on its truth. Remote viewing deserved clean tests precisely because the claimed capability would have been consequential if real. The right response to an extraordinary claim is not cultural policing. It is expensive control of ordinary explanations.

The third lesson is that statistical anomaly and operational utility are different species of evidence.

A weak effect can be scientifically fascinating while useless for action. An intelligence customer needs a report that changes a decision, not merely a population-level departure from chance. If the correct fragments cannot be distinguished from incorrect fragments until after confirmation, the source has a severe operational handicap.

The fourth lesson is that secrecy changes the experiment.

It narrows criticism, fragments performance information, changes incentives, protects fragile inquiry, protects weak inquiry, amplifies anecdotes, and later turns classification markings into false authority. Any history of secret science that treats secrecy as a curtain rather than an active variable misses part of the causal system.

The fifth lesson is that rival interest can create false convergence.

American concern about Soviet parapsychology was real. That concern helped motivate U.S. investigation. But two adversaries studying the same extraordinary claim do not independently prove it. Strategic mirroring can generate parallel programs from shared uncertainty.

The sixth lesson is that primary sources need denominators.

A declassified session report can be authentic and unrepresentative. An official memorandum can faithfully record an incorrect judgment. A confirmed detail can be impressive and selected. The archive becomes scientific evidence only when cases are sampled, sequences reconstructed, and scoring rules restored.

The seventh lesson is that unexplained is allowed.

This may be the hardest one.

People feel compelled to promote residual anomalies into discoveries or demote them into errors. Sometimes the honest category is “not yet explained well enough to support the proposed mechanism.” That is not intellectual cowardice. It is a stable position when evidence warrants it.

Remote viewing may belong there at the laboratory level depending on how one weighs the later experimental literature. The operational program belongs somewhere else. It was tested as a service and did not demonstrate sufficient value.

The two verdicts can live together.

What, then, should we make of the participants who remained convinced?

We should take their testimony seriously and proportionally.

A viewer who remembers an extraordinary session may have experienced something genuinely difficult to explain. An officer who saw a transcript match a target may reasonably have updated his beliefs. A statistician who sees repeated deviations may conclude chance is inadequate. A skeptic who sees shared methodology may resist causal inflation. An intelligence customer drowning in vague reports may decide the whole thing is useless.

These people can disagree without one side being stupid.

The history becomes poorer when everyone is assigned a cartoon role.

The same generosity does not extend to claims without evidence. Stories of continuing black programs, perfect psychic assassins, remote viewers locating extraterrestrial bases, or governments hiding proven nonlocal consciousness belong in evidence class three unless documentation emerges. Their entertainment value does not upgrade them.

Likewise, patents, contracts, and classification do not establish efficacy. A patent proves a filing. A contract proves a purchase. A secret marking proves a handling decision. One of the most important disciplines in anomalous research is refusing to let documentary reality leak into physical reality without a bridge.

STARGATE is full of bridges that stop halfway.

A laboratory effect without mechanism.

A viewer impression without calibration.

A successful anecdote without denominator.

A foreign program without proof of capability.

A classified archive without a complete performance model.

A statistical argument without operational transformation.

That incompleteness is why the subject remains culturally alive decades after the budget line died.

It also makes the archive more valuable than a cleaner success story would have been.

Failure exposes assumptions.

If remote viewing had worked like radar, the methodological arguments would be historical curiosities. If it had failed instantly, the program would be a footnote. Because it lived in the unstable middle, institutions had to reveal what they thought counted as evidence. They had to decide when a weird question deserved money, how to score free-form language, how to use uncalibrated information, how to evaluate a source with unknown mechanism, and when to stop.

Those are still live problems.

Consider UAP analysis. A sensor track may be authentic while the extraordinary interpretation is not. Multiple observers may share a target without being independent. Classified interest may reflect air-defense uncertainty rather than hidden knowledge of non-human craft. A striking case may survive while population-level explanations remain mundane. The discipline learned from STARGATE is not “believe” or “debunk.” It is preserve the chain from observation to inference.

Consider anomalous health incidents. Symptoms can be real while mechanisms are disputed. Intelligence assessments can explore directed-energy hypotheses without proving them. A politically charged attribution can outrun biomedical evidence. Again: observation, model, attribution, utility.

Consider alleged exotic materials. Chain of custody matters more than story. Isotopic anomaly is not provenance. Unusual microstructure is not non-human manufacture. A government contractor’s interest does not establish origin. The STARGATE habit of separating official attention from physical proof is directly transferable.

This is why an old psychic-spy program deserves to be studied without smirking.

It is a laboratory for epistemology under pressure.

The participants were not sitting outside reality. They were inside budgets, rivalries, Cold War fear, scientific ambition, statistical argument, bureaucratic survival, personal conviction, and genuine uncertainty. Those conditions shaped what they could know.

We inherit the records after the pressure has changed.

That gives us advantages. We can compare decades. We can read failures without career consequences. We can use modern ideas about preregistration, replication, data leakage, base rates, and decision theory. We can separate the program’s existence from its claims.

We also inherit distortions. We know how the story ended. We know which sessions became famous. We encounter declassified files through search boxes rather than chronological workflow. We know remote viewing did not become a standard intelligence discipline, which makes every early believer look more foolish than he did at the time.

Hindsight is its own cue.

The fair reconstruction keeps uncertainty where it originally existed.

In 1972, investigating the claim was not obviously irrational.

In 1974, publication did not establish the phenomenon.

In the late 1970s and 1980s, foreign interest and domestic results could rationally sustain some exploration while methodological concerns accumulated.

By 1995, the burden had changed. After years of work, the question was no longer whether remote viewing deserved curiosity. It was whether curiosity had matured into capability.

It had not.

That is a failure, but not a trivial one.

The government had spent years trying to turn a controversial human anomaly into an instrument. The instrument refused to acquire a stable calibration curve. It produced enough suggestive material to remain arguable and not enough dependable material to become ordinary.

Ordinary is the hidden standard in every extraordinary technology.

The day a miracle can be scheduled, measured, insured, trained, debugged, and compared with competitors, it stops being a miracle. It becomes infrastructure.

Remote viewing never crossed that line.

The signal, if it existed, stayed on the other side.
