# Nature, 1974

On October 18, 1974, Nature published a paper that should not be remembered as a certificate.

The title was almost aggressively technical: “Information transmission under conditions of sensory shielding.” Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff, working at Stanford Research Institute, described experiments involving subjects who appeared to obtain information about targets that ordinary sensory channels should not have supplied. The paper included work with Uri Geller, distant geographical targets, and physiological experiments. Its claim was not merely that people sometimes had uncanny intuitions. The authors proposed that something deserving controlled laboratory study had survived attempts at shielding.

Publication in Nature later became part of the evidence in popular retellings. This is understandable and wrong in a precise way. A prestigious journal can establish that a paper entered serious scientific argument. It cannot establish that the paper’s interpretation survived the argument. Nature itself made unusually clear that publication was not endorsement. The editorial decision was closer to opening the specimen jar in public.

That distinction is important because remote viewing has always accumulated authority by proximity. Stanford in the institutional name. Physicists running the experiments. Intelligence agencies paying attention. Nature publishing a report. Classified stamps appearing on later documents. Each item is real. Stack them carelessly and they begin to impersonate a proof none of them supplies alone.

Still, the 1974 paper matters for more than sociology. It reveals why the subject was hard to dismiss at first contact.

The remote-viewing setup had an appealing shape. A person remained in one place while another person, or “beacon,” went to a randomly selected target location. The viewer described impressions. Later, descriptions could be compared against target sites. This was not the séance room of cultural memory. It looked like a protocol. There were target pools, distances, judges, and statistical tests. That appearance did not guarantee adequate control, but it moved the argument onto terrain where control failures could be named.

That was progress.

The history of fringe claims is full of propositions designed to escape testing. Psychic ability disappears around skeptics. Healing energy cannot be measured by ordinary instruments. Predictions are metaphorical until an event makes them literal. Remote viewing, at least in its experimental form, did something more dangerous to itself. It created records. A transcript could be wrong. A judge could be blinded. A target could be randomized. A replication could fail. A method could be criticized in print. The claim acquired handles.

The 1974 work also contained the seed of a problem that would never leave the program: the difference between resemblance and information.

Suppose a target site contains a pedestrian bridge over water. A viewer reports “a long horizontal form,” “something underneath,” “movement,” “open air,” and draws parallel lines. That may look impressive after the target is known. But how informative was it before the target was known? How many places in the target pool would accept some subset of those features? If the alternatives include a marina, a rail yard, an office plaza, a church, and a bridge, blind ranking can convert resemblance into a test. If the alternatives are the whole world, the problem changes.

A laboratory can make ambiguity measurable by constraining the alternative set. Intelligence cannot always afford that luxury.

The early SRI work often used judging schemes in which transcripts were matched against candidate targets. This is sensible. It also means the statistical object is not “viewer named the place.” It is closer to “judges could pair descriptions and targets better than expected under a defined procedure.” Those are different propositions. The second can be true while the first remains operationally weak.

This becomes easier to see with a deliberately ordinary example. Imagine five sealed photographs: a waterfall, a stadium, a church interior, a highway interchange, and a snowfield. A viewer says “large open area, curved boundaries, many repeated small verticals, a sense of crowding.” A blinded judge may rank the stadium first. Across many trials, if rankings consistently beat chance, there is something to explain. But now remove the four decoys and ask an analyst what facility in a foreign country the description identifies. The same information may be nearly worthless.

The target pool is not a detail. It is part of the instrument.

Targ and Puthoff’s paper drew attention because some reported matches seemed too good to dismiss casually. It also drew criticism because extraordinary claims make every mundane information path consequential. If a judge can infer target order from dates, transcript sequence, references to previous sessions, or other artifacts, the test ceases to isolate anomalous perception. No fraud is required. The protocol itself can leak.

David Marks and Richard Kammann would become central to that critique. Their later work argued that cues embedded in the judging materials could permit correct matching. This is the kind of criticism that both sides should want. If the effect is real, remove the cue and the effect survives. If the effect disappears, the alleged psychic channel loses explanatory work.

But scientific disputes rarely move so cleanly. The early experiments were not one frozen protocol. Methods changed. Critics and proponents disputed which flaws applied to which trials. Later researchers attempted more automated target selection, improved blinding, and formal scoring. A valid criticism of one experimental generation cannot simply be photocopied onto every later result. Conversely, improved later work cannot retroactively clean the earlier record.

This book will repeatedly refuse that temporal laundering.

In 1974, there was enough ambiguity to justify argument, not enough to justify certainty.

The paper also arrived into a political environment unusually receptive to low-probability possibilities. The United States and Soviet Union were competing not only over deployed weapons but over scientific surprise. Intelligence analysts followed lasers, particle beams, antisubmarine technologies, human performance, chemical agents, mind-control rumors, and claims that would later look ridiculous. Some were ridiculous. Some transformed warfare. The cost of not knowing which was which was part of the job.

Reports that the Soviet bloc had an organized interest in parapsychology therefore mattered independent of whether Soviet psychic research worked. U.S. intelligence documents from the 1970s cite studies of Soviet and East European parapsychology, “paraphysics,” and alleged psychokinesis work. These records prove American officials were collecting and evaluating such claims. They do not prove Soviet laboratories had operational paranormal capabilities.

The distinction sounds obvious until bureaucratic mirror effects begin.

Imagine one side funds a modest investigation because it believes the other side is investigating. The other side’s analysts detect the first side’s investigation and upgrade their own interest. The resulting documents become evidence, decades later, that both superpowers “knew something.” In reality, reciprocal uncertainty may be enough to generate convergence. Intelligence history is full of mirrors that amplify suspicion.

Remote viewing was especially suited to this because the claimed capability, if real, would attack the premise of secrecy itself. A hardened facility can stop photography. Encryption can stop interception. Distance can stop ordinary observation. But a person who can describe a target without a physical channel would make entire classes of security architecture porous. Even a weak version would be strategically weird.

The expected-value logic writes itself. A small research contract buys information about whether a potentially revolutionary collection method deserves larger investment. The downside is embarrassment and money. The upside, under the hypothesis, is access to denied targets. That asymmetry can make early exploration rational without making the underlying claim likely.

Government curiosity is therefore not evidence proportional to its exoticism.

The more consequential question is what happened after curiosity met records.

At SRI, the program produced demonstrations, experiments, contractor reports, and relationships with government sponsors. Some officials came away impressed. Others did not. Kenneth Kress, a CIA officer who later wrote a classified then declassified retrospective, described the agency’s involvement with parapsychology in terms that capture institutional ambivalence better than later mythology. The work was investigated. There were striking episodes. There were also doubts about experimental adequacy and operational utility.

The tension is visible in the kind of questions intelligence agencies asked. They were not primarily interested in metaphysics. A collection agency does not need to know whether information transfer is mediated by quantum effects, precognition, telepathy, or a category physics has not named. It needs to know whether the source is useful. If an effect can be exploited without a mechanism, intelligence may use it first and let science catch up.

This is not hypothetical. Many technologies were useful before their mechanisms were fully understood. Sailors used magnetism before Maxwell. Physicians used some drugs before molecular targets were known. Meteorologists forecast patterns before the atmosphere yielded modern computational models. “No mechanism” is not by itself a decisive objection to an empirical capability.

Remote viewing’s problem was harsher. It did not merely lack an accepted mechanism. It struggled to become a stable capability whose failures could be predicted.

A sensor with a known error distribution can be integrated into intelligence. A radar has clutter. An imaging system has resolution limits. A human source may have motives and access constraints. An analyst can weight the report because the failure modes have shape. Remote viewing often presented a less tractable problem: a session might contain evocative correct elements mixed with incorrect ones, and the customer had no independent way to know which were which until other intelligence arrived.

That converts collection into a peculiar kind of optionality. After confirmation, one can identify the hit. Before confirmation, the hit is embedded in noise.

The 1974 paper did not solve this because it was not designed to. Laboratory work asks whether there is an effect. Operational work asks whether the effect can support decisions. The same transcript can be evidence for the first and inadequate for the second.

This is where proponents and critics often talk past each other. A proponent may say, correctly within a specific dataset, that odds against chance were low. A critic may respond that no commander would act on the output. The critic has not necessarily refuted the statistic. The proponent has not necessarily established the application.

The next step in a mature field should be to build a bridge between them.

What would the bridge require?

Not a slogan about repeatability. Something more specific: prospectively frozen targets; target selection insulated from experimenters and viewers; complete transcript retention; no post hoc editing; independent judging; controls against cue leakage; public or at least adversarially reviewable analysis; enough trials to estimate individual and group performance; and, for operational use, a scoring system tied to the decisions customers actually face.

The program would eventually attempt parts of this architecture. Some later laboratory work became considerably cleaner than the early SRI demonstrations. This is why a responsible skeptical account cannot stop with the 1970s cue controversy and declare the entire later literature explained. The 1995 evaluation, whatever one thinks of its conclusion, treated a body of later work seriously enough to produce a genuine disagreement between Jessica Utts and Ray Hyman.

Yet the operational bridge remained weak.

There is a historical irony in this. The cleaner the laboratory test, the more remote viewing can become a task of discriminating structured targets under conditions optimized for statistical evaluation. The closer one moves toward actual intelligence, the messier the target space, feedback, scoring, and customer interpretation become. Scientific validity and operational realism are both desirable, but they can pull protocol design in different directions.

Nature in 1974 sits at the beginning of that fork.

The paper did not prove a new human sense. It did something more modest and historically potent: it made the claim test-shaped.

Once a claim has a target pool, a blind judge, and a probability value, it can enter institutions that know how to buy experiments. Once it enters institutions concerned with adversarial surprise, it can survive longer than ordinary academic fashion. Once parts of the work become classified, outside correction slows while insider anecdotes gain weight. Once those documents are declassified decades later, the public encounters the archive in reverse order, seeing the most dramatic surviving pages without having lived through the mundane sequence of budgets, failures, protocol disputes, and negative sessions.

That reverse order distorts the story.

A person opening the CIA reading room today can find a transcript that seems eerie in isolation. The natural impulse is to ask whether this is the page that proves it. But the proper unit of analysis is rarely the page. It is the protocol, the target set, the session series, the feedback chain, and the denominator of failures.

The 1974 publication is useful precisely because the debate around it teaches us to widen the unit.

Consider the word shielding. It sounds absolute. The subject is shielded from the target; therefore normal information transfer has been eliminated. In practice, sensory shielding is a systems property, not a room feature. The target can leak through experimenter knowledge, paperwork, timing, transcript order, judging cues, inadvertent conversation, or post-session feedback. A Faraday cage can block radio-frequency energy and still leave a human protocol full of information channels.

This became one of the field’s recurring lessons: when the alleged phenomenon is information transfer, information hygiene is the experiment.

The same lesson applies to intelligence. A viewer may be formally blind to a target while the interviewer knows the general mission. The tasker may know that the agency is looking at a submarine facility. The analyst interpreting the transcript may already have overhead imagery. Later retellings may merge the viewer’s raw description with the analyst’s interpretation. Every handoff creates opportunities for useful synthesis and for contamination.

None of this requires bad faith.

That may be the most uncomfortable part. People can be intelligent, careful, and sincere while participating in a system that generates stronger stories than evidence. Human pattern matching is not a corruption added from outside. It is one of the things intelligence analysis is for. The question is how to keep that strength from becoming a scoring artifact when the source itself is ambiguous.

Remote viewing forces the issue because the raw product is unusually underdetermined.

A satellite image of a hangar is also ambiguous, but dimensions can be measured. Shadows constrain height. Multiple passes can reveal change. Different sensors can cross-cue one another. A sketch of “a long object with a pointed end” has a much larger interpretation space. If the target later turns out to be a submarine, the match can feel uncanny. If the target is an aircraft, missile, bridge, tower, or ship, some of the same geometry may remain available.

Specificity, therefore, is not merely a stylistic virtue. It is the currency in which operational remote viewing has to pay.

The early program often celebrated correspondences because correspondences were the signal under investigation. That is reasonable at the discovery stage. But a discovery program and an intelligence capability should not share a success criterion forever.

By the time the United States had accumulated years of work, the central question should have migrated. Not “can judges sometimes detect target-related structure in transcripts?” but “can a tasker get a report that changes the probability of a live intelligence hypothesis enough to matter?”

The difference is enormous.

Suppose the prior probability that a missing person is in one of ten compounds is ten percent for each compound. A remote-viewing report strongly and prospectively favors one compound with a validated likelihood ratio large enough to move it to sixty percent. That could be useful. Suppose instead the report contains descriptors compatible with six compounds, plus one striking detail that only becomes recognizable after the rescue. That might be scientifically intriguing and operationally useless.

No mystical question has to be resolved to distinguish them.

The book’s method will repeatedly use this move: translate extraordinary claims into ordinary performance requirements. What is the false-positive rate? What is the alternative set? What is the scoring rule? What was known at the time? What would a customer have done differently? What is the prospective prediction, not the retrospective fit?

The irony is that this method gives the anomalous claim more respect, not less. It refuses to kill it with a sneer. It asks it to carry weight.

In 1974, Nature gave remote viewing a place to stand in public science.

The next twenty years would test whether that place had a road leading out of the laboratory.
