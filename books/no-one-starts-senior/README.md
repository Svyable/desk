*How AI Automates the Work That Creates Experts*

The first tasks a profession automates are often the same tasks through which a beginner learns the profession.

Organizations have a sensible reason to hand routine, first-draft, and preliminary-review work to machines: it is expensive, repetitive, and increasingly automatable. Removing it can improve speed and quality today. But those tasks were also the lower rungs of a ladder. They let novices see many cases, make inexpensive mistakes, watch senior judgment up close, acquire tacit knowledge, and earn responsibility gradually. A firm can remove the work from a job description faster than it can replace the learning hidden inside that work.

The working thesis is that automation can raise current productivity while quietly reducing the future supply of people capable of supervising the automated system, because expertise is not a stock an organization inherits forever — it is a pipeline, and pipelines fail downstream, often years after the decision that thinned them.

This is not a book that predicts a profession-wide collapse from a handful of anecdotes or job postings. It follows the pipeline across law, software engineering, medicine, finance, and the skilled trades, and it takes seriously the strongest counter-case throughout: that AI may expose beginners to more examples, better feedback, and earlier contact with hard problems than the old apprenticeship systems ever did. The book only succeeds if it can show, profession by profession, when that counter-case is true and when it is not.
