# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*No One Starts Senior* asks what happens to the formation of expertise when the tasks that used to train beginners are the first tasks a profession hands to a machine.

The strong, easy version of the argument — that AI will simply eliminate junior jobs — is not the interesting claim and is not what the evidence supports uniformly. The version worth testing is more specific:

> Automation can raise current productivity by removing routine, repetitive, first-draft, and preliminary-review work, while quietly reducing the future supply of people capable of supervising the automated system, because that routine work was also how novices built pattern recognition, judgment, and the tacit knowledge senior practitioners rely on.

The book must survive counter-evidence in both directions. If firms that automate junior work are demonstrably building equal or better replacement pathways — structured simulation, earlier exposure to ambiguous cases, faster feedback loops — the argument should say so plainly and by profession, not in a single closing caveat. If a profession shows no measurable change in entry-level hiring, promotion timelines, or skill formation, that should be reported as a limit on the thesis, not omitted.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Entry-level hiring and staffing-pyramid data:** Are law firms, banks, consultancies, software companies, and other pyramid-shaped professional organizations measurably narrowing their junior cohorts, and since when?
2. **Deliberate-practice and expertise research:** What does the cognitive-science literature actually say about how repetition, feedback, and consequence build pattern recognition and tacit judgment — and where does that literature's evidence run out?
3. **Profession-by-profession mechanism:** In law, software engineering, medicine, finance/accounting, and skilled trades, which specific tasks are being automated, and were those tasks historically load-bearing for training rather than merely for output?
4. **Skill-decay and human-factors evidence:** What is known, from aviation, medicine, and automation-heavy industries, about what happens to human supervisory performance when a task is handed to automation for an extended period?
5. **Licensing and credentialing exposure:** Do professional licensing and certification regimes assume a volume or type of hands-on experience that automation is now reducing?
6. **Counter-evidence and positive cases:** Where has automation demonstrably expanded a beginner's access to cases, feedback, or senior-level problems rather than removing their access to practice?
7. **Employer response:** Which firms, residency programs, apprenticeship systems, and bootcamps have redesigned entry-level work deliberately in response to AI, and with what documented results so far?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should actively look for evidence against the central argument.

- Automation may expose beginners to *more* cases, not fewer, if it removes only the most mechanical sub-tasks and leaves diagnosis, judgment, and revision to the human.
- Entry-level hiring contractions may be driven by macroeconomic conditions, interest rates, or cyclical overhiring rather than by AI substitution specifically; the book must be careful about attribution.
- Some "junior" work was never actually educational — it was pure toil that taught little and whose removal is a pure efficiency gain.
- Professions have absorbed earlier waves of tooling (calculators, spreadsheets, PACS imaging, legal databases, IDEs, autopilot) without a demonstrated collapse in expertise formation; the book must explain what, if anything, is different this time rather than assume it.
- Some firms are demonstrably redesigning apprenticeship deliberately and successfully; these are the strongest counter-cases and must be given real space, not a token paragraph.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Deliberate practice, tacit knowledge, and expertise formation

Ericsson-tradition deliberate-practice research and its critics (including the more recent meta-analytic pushback on the "10,000-hour" framing), situated and tacit-knowledge literature (Polanyi onward), cognitive apprenticeship research, and studies of how professionals actually learn judgment under supervision.

### II. Professional pyramids and entry-level labor markets

Law-firm associate hiring and leverage-ratio data, investment-bank and consulting analyst-class trends, junior software-engineer hiring data and job-posting indices, entry-level unemployment and underemployment statistics, and any longitudinal cohort or promotion-timeline data available.

### III. Medicine and licensed apprenticeship

Residency case-volume and duty-hour research, radiology/pathology AI-assistance studies and their effect on trainee interpretation skill, surgical simulation evidence, and nursing/allied-health training pipelines.

### IV. Software engineering

Studies and industry data on AI code-generation adoption, junior-developer hiring and onboarding changes, debugging-skill and code-comprehension research, and open-source/bootcamp pipeline effects.

### V. Skilled trades and physical apprenticeship

Registered-apprenticeship data, trade-school and union training-pipeline evidence, and the specific limits of remote/simulated instruction for embodied skill.

### VI. Human factors, automation, and skill decay

Aviation automation and manual-flying-skill research, "automation complacency" and out-of-the-loop performance literature, and any transferable findings about what happens to supervisory competence when a task moves from doing to overseeing.

### VII. Positive redesigns

Documented cases — law, medicine, software, consulting, trades — where an employer, residency program, or licensing body redesigned entry-level work around AI in a way with actual evidence of effectiveness, not just a press release.

## Source discipline

- Prefer peer-reviewed research, professional-association data (bar associations, medical boards, apprenticeship registries), government labor statistics, company disclosures, and named-author journalism with on-record sourcing.
- Do not invent a statistic, survey result, or quotation. Where a widely repeated number cannot be traced to a primary source, say so and either drop it or attribute it explicitly as a claim made by a named party.
- Distinguish a hiring *slowdown* from a hiring *collapse*, and distinguish correlation with AI adoption from causation; note competing macroeconomic explanations wherever they exist.
- Keep the "10,000 hours" framing honest — cite the original Ericsson violin-study methodology accurately and note the popularization and later critique (including Hambrick et al.'s meta-analysis) rather than repeating the rule as settled fact.
- For every profession chapter, keep a note titled "What would make this false?"
