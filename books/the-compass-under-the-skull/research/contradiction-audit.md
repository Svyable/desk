# Contradiction and Incentive Audit

## Core contradiction

The 2019 human EEG study and the 2022 human behavioral study can both be cited as evidence for magnetosensitivity, but their strongest mechanism signatures point in different directions. Wang et al. emphasize polarity sensitivity, darkness-compatible stimulation and a response pattern they interpret as inconsistent with a classic radical-pair inclination compass, favoring ferromagnetic transduction. Chae et al. emphasize blue-light dependence and radiofrequency-resonance effects, favoring radical-pair chemistry.

The manuscript does not smooth this into generic “multiple evidence.” It treats the disagreement as the main experimental opportunity.

## Alternative explanations tracked

- apparatus-specific electromagnetic or mechanical artifacts;
- analysis flexibility and small-sample instability;
- post hoc responder selection;
- subtle sensory cue leakage in behavioral paradigms;
- real magnetic sensitivity with incorrect mechanism assignment;
- two biologically separable magnetic pathways;
- population/developmental tuning to geomagnetic environment.

## Incentives and distortions

### Scientific novelty

A positive human “sixth sense” result is more publishable and more likely to receive press than a null replication. This can inflate selective visibility without implying misconduct.

### Fringe stigma

The opposite pressure also exists. Magnetoreception in humans overlaps culturally with paranormal claims, dowsing and “lost sense” rhetoric. Serious laboratories may avoid the subject, reducing independent replication. Stigma can therefore suppress both weak work and useful adversarial tests.

### Mechanism ownership

Laboratories invested in magnetite or radical-pair frameworks may preferentially design experiments around their own apparatus and favored predictions. Cross-laboratory adversarial designs reduce this problem.

### Popularization pressure

“Molecular capability,” “neural response,” “magnetic perception” and “navigation” are often collapsed in secondary coverage. The manuscript keeps these levels separate.

## Known provenance problem

Human brain magnetite has at least partly mixed origin. Endogenous-looking crystals and pollution-derived nanospheres should not be pooled into a single sensory claim.

## Claims deliberately rejected in this pass

- that humans have demonstrated conscious magnetic navigation;
- that the receptor has been anatomically located;
- that quantum mechanics makes human magnetoreception mysterious or nonlocal;
- that modern electromagnetic pollution has been shown to suppress a human magnetic sense;
- that one isolated human study establishes either magnetite or cryptochrome as the in-vivo receptor.
