# The Compass Under the Skull

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Title | The Compass Under the Skull |
| Subtitle | Human Magnetoreception and the Sense We Cannot Agree How to Have |
| Author | Sven Hardy Benson |
| Status | Drafting |
| Chapters | 18 |

The book begins with an awkward fact: two of the strongest modern experimental claims for human magnetoreception point toward different physical mechanisms. The 2019 Caltech/Tokyo EEG work reported direction-selective alpha-band responses to Earth-strength magnetic-field rotations and argued that the response pattern was most consistent with a ferromagnetic transducer such as biogenic magnetite. A 2022 behavioral study reported blue-light dependence and radiofrequency-resonance effects more naturally associated with radical-pair chemistry. Both cannot simply be treated as interchangeable proof of the same sense.

That disagreement is the subject. The reader promise is narrower than “humans have a sixth sense” and more interesting: by the end, the reader should understand exactly what has been demonstrated, what remains unreplicated or contested, why magnetite and cryptochrome models make different predictions, how animal magnetoreception earned its scientific footing, and what experiment would actually force the human question to move.

The strongest competing explanation is that one or both modern human results arise from subtle experimental artifacts, analytic flexibility, expectancy, uncontrolled cues, or false-positive structure in small and heterogeneous samples. The manuscript keeps that possibility alive throughout. A second possibility is that human tissues contain more than one magnetically responsive pathway, only one of which ever contributed to behavior. The book does not promote that synthesis beyond the evidence.

Evidence classes used throughout:

1. **Verified/documented:** peer-reviewed experiments, openly described apparatus and protocols, known geomagnetic physics, demonstrated animal magnetic behavior, human brain magnetite measurements, cryptochrome biochemistry, and public data where available.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** effects reported by competent groups that need independent replication, mechanism assignments inferred from stimulus selectivity, and historical human-orientation results whose controls or reproducibility are uneven.
3. **Disputed or unconfirmed:** strong claims of a consciously usable human compass, precise receptor anatomy in humans, universal population effects, and mechanism-specific claims not independently reproduced.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** humans may retain a degraded, subliminal, developmentally contingent, or multi-channel magnetic sensory system; the disagreement between current experiments may contain biological information rather than merely noise.
