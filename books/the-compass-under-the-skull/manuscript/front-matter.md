# The Compass Under the Skull

Human Magnetoreception and the Sense We Cannot Agree How to Have

Sven Hardy Benson

There are scientific questions that survive because nobody has looked closely enough. Human magnetoreception is stranger. People have looked, stopped looking, looked again with different instruments, and repeatedly returned with results that refuse to assemble into one respectable story.

The vulgar version is easy: perhaps humans have a sixth sense. That sentence is almost designed to make the useful parts disappear. It drags the subject toward paranormal theater, superhero language and the old appetite for discovering that civilization has somehow blinded us to an ancestral power. The experiments are less flattering and more interesting. Nobody in the strongest modern work stands in a field, closes his eyes and walks north. The signal, when it appears, is weak, statistical, conditional and often unconscious. It lives in changes in EEG alpha power, in forced-choice orientation under controlled light, in radiofrequency perturbations, in proteins borrowed from a human and expressed inside a fly. It is exactly the kind of evidence that requires patience because it cannot survive exaggeration.

The deeper problem is physical. A magnetic field at Earth strength is feeble. Biology has at least two serious ways of sensing it. One uses ferromagnetic material: tiny particles such as magnetite can experience torque or force and, in principle, couple the external field to a mechanosensory structure. The other uses spin chemistry: photoexcited radical pairs can have reaction yields influenced by weak magnetic fields, turning quantum dynamics into a biological directional cue. These are not decorative explanations for the same mystery. They respond differently to light, polarity, field inversion and weak oscillating radiofrequency fields. They should leave different experimental fingerprints.

In 2019 a Caltech-led team reported that rotations of an Earth-strength magnetic field produced repeatable alpha-band event-related desynchronization in some human participants. The response depended on the direction and geometry of the field. The authors argued that their pattern ruled against induction and the light-dependent radical-pair compass and favored a ferromagnetic transducer such as magnetite. Three years later, another group reported behavioral orientation in human subjects that depended on blue light and could be disrupted or augmented by radiofrequency fields near the relevant magnetic resonance condition. That paper argued for the opposite family of mechanism.

This is where I became interested. Not because both papers must be right. They may not be. The interesting fact is that the field has reached the stage where two serious experimental programs can claim human magnetic sensitivity and disagree about the physics underneath it. That is a better scientific situation than unanimity built from one fragile result. Contradiction creates leverage if the mechanisms make different predictions.

This book treats the contradiction as an instrument. It does not attempt to rescue every historical claim of human magnetic orientation. It does not convert magnetite found in the brain into a receptor, human cryptochrome into a compass, or an EEG response into navigation. It will also resist the opposite mistake: assuming that because the final receptor has not been anatomically identified, all magnetoreception work belongs in the cabinet with dowsing rods. Animals have made that position untenable. Magnetic orientation and magnetic-map behavior are experimentally real across diverse taxa. The open questions are how, where, and to what extent humans remain part of that biological story.

The standard I use is simple enough to be annoying. A mechanism must survive its own predictions. A receptor has to be in the right tissue, at the right scale, connected to a pathway capable of affecting behavior or neural activity. A behavioral effect has to survive sensory masking, sham conditions, preregistered analysis and independent replication. A neural signal has to survive electromagnetic artifact tests and demonstrate selectivity that means something biologically. A story about evolution has to explain both the presence of machinery and the embarrassing weakness of the apparent phenotype.

If human magnetoreception is real, we do not yet know whether we lost the useful part and retained the wire, whether the sense remains useful below awareness, whether it only appears in a minority of people, whether developmental environment tunes it, or whether more than one magnetically sensitive process survives in us. If it is not real, the route to that conclusion should be equally specific. Which result failed? Under what replication? Which artifact reproduced it? Which predicted dependency disappeared?

A good anomalous subject should get harder as evidence accumulates. This one does. That is why it is worth the trouble.
