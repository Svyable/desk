# Latitude in the Nervous System

The Earth's field is not a uniform arrow painted through the planet. Its inclination, intensity and declination vary with geography. That variation is what makes magnetic map behavior possible in some animals and what makes human developmental history experimentally interesting.

At mid-northern latitudes, the field points downward into the Earth. In much of the Southern Hemisphere it points upward relative to the local horizontal. Near the magnetic equator inclination approaches horizontal. A sensory system tuned during development could therefore encounter systematically different field statistics depending on where a person grew up.

The 2019 EEG result made this more than a geographical curiosity. The reported alpha response appeared selective for downward-directed fields characteristic of the Northern Hemisphere participants' environment, while upward-directed configurations did not evoke the same response. The authors raised the possibility of biological tuning to ecologically relevant field geometry.

That is an attractive explanation because it makes a prediction. Recruit people raised in the Southern Hemisphere and test whether the sensitivity reverses. Recruit people raised near the magnetic equator and ask whether the response broadens, weakens or follows another dimension. Recruit migrants and ask whether tuning reflects childhood environment, recent residence or both.

A developmental effect would be important for mechanism. A permanent magnetite receptor does not need developmental experience in order to experience torque, but neural calibration of its outputs might. A radical-pair receptor likewise produces chemistry without learning, yet the brain may still need to interpret its directional pattern. Sensory systems are full of this division between physical transduction and developmental calibration.

The experiment also has a built-in trap. Latitude is correlated with thousands of cultural, genetic and environmental variables. A simple comparison between national populations would be almost useless. The design needs people whose magnetic-development histories differ while other variables are measured or balanced, and it needs a specific predicted interaction with field geometry.

There is a deeper implication. If human magnetoreception varies by developmental field environment, then replication failures across laboratories may contain structure. A protocol developed in California and repeated in Australia should not automatically expect the same preferred stimulus geometry. What looks like nonreplication could be a badly specified ecological variable.

This should not become an excuse for every null result. The prediction has to be written down before the data are seen. Otherwise latitude becomes another flexible rescue.

A sensory system that has spent a lifetime under one planetary field may carry that history quietly. The question is whether we can make that history show itself on command.
