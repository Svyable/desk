# The Sense Evolution Can Leave Behind

A vestige is not a fossil. It is living biology that no longer does exactly what its ancestor once did.

The human body is full of structures whose evolutionary stories are less clean than the textbook word “vestigial” suggests. Functions can shrink, shift, split or become redundant. A magnetic sensory pathway could follow the same pattern. The receptor might remain while the behavioral program decays. A peripheral signal might still reach cortex without entering conscious spatial judgment. A molecular component might persist because it serves circadian or metabolic roles unrelated to magnetism.

This is one reason the 2019 EEG result is conceptually plausible. If the brain responds to magnetic change without awareness, the pathway could be a remnant. But plausibility is not evidence of ancestry. Neural systems respond weakly to all kinds of physical perturbations. To call something vestigial, we need homology or at least a convincing evolutionary path.

Comparative biology can help. Closely related primates are an obvious missing part of the literature. If chimpanzees, bonobos or other apes show comparable geomagnetic neural responses, the ancestral case strengthens. If distant mammals do but apes do not, a human-specific retention story becomes less likely. Ethical and practical constraints make primate experiments difficult, but noninvasive paradigms are imaginable.

Developmental data could also help. Does the putative response appear in children? Does it change with age? Is it stronger before years of exposure to artificial electromagnetic environments? Those questions are tempting and dangerous because they invite narratives about civilization suppressing an ancient capacity. The correct design is longitudinal or preregistered, not nostalgic.

Selection does not care whether a sense feels mysterious to us. If magnetic information once improved survival even slightly, a pathway could have existed. If other cues later dominated, the pathway could degrade gradually. The resulting phenotype might be exactly what current experiments suggest: inconsistent, unconscious, individually variable and difficult to recruit for useful behavior.

Or that description could simply fit a false positive.

The vestigial hypothesis becomes valuable only when it predicts patterns that artifact does not: phylogenetic continuity, developmental trajectories, heritable variation, stable individual differences and anatomical residue. Until then, “we once had a compass” is a story waiting for evidence.
