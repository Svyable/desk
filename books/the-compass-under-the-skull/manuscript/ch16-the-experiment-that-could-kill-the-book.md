# The Experiment That Could Kill the Book

A book about an unresolved sense should contain the experiment that could make its central tension disappear.

The design is conceptually simple and technically unpleasant. Recruit enough participants to estimate individual effects, stratified in advance by developmental magnetic hemisphere. Put each participant through the same three-axis coil apparatus under strict blinding. Record high-density EEG while collecting a forced-choice orientation measure. Cross four variables: magnetic polarity, inclination, retinal light condition and weak radiofrequency perturbation. Add current-matched magnetic shams and RF shams. Predeclare the primary contrasts.

The magnetite-like prediction is that a neural response can survive darkness, care about polarity and ignore resonance-frequency RF in a way a radical-pair compass should not. The radical-pair prediction is that performance or physiology should depend on appropriate illumination and show a specific vulnerability to RF conditions that alter spin dynamics, with less concern for polarity than for inclination. The multiple-pathway hypothesis predicts a dissociation between the two families of outcome.

The study should be run in at least two independent laboratories with nonidentical hardware. Analysis code should be written before unblinding and executed on both datasets. One laboratory should hold out a subset of participants for confirmatory analysis. Responder status should be defined from session one and predicted in session two.

A strong null would matter. If neither the published EEG signature nor the published light/RF behavioral signature reproduces under adequately powered preregistered conditions, this book's organizing contradiction largely dies. Historical animal work and molecular plausibility remain interesting, but there is no longer a good reason to speak of demonstrated human magnetoreception.

A partial result would also sharpen the field. Reproduced EEG with no behavioral radical-pair effect would move attention toward ferromagnetic transduction. Reproduced light/RF behavior with no polarity-sensitive EEG would do the opposite. A clean double dissociation would make the two-pathway model serious.

The experiment should also include aggressively unromantic controls: accelerometers on the coil structure, microphones, temperature sensors, electric-field probes, magnetic-field logging at the participant's head, eye tracking, pupil monitoring and recording of RF spectra. Participants should be asked afterward whether they noticed sound, vibration or light differences, but subjective reports should not define the primary outcome.

This is expensive compared with arguing in reviews and cheap compared with another decade of interpretive ambiguity.

A field becomes healthier when its most interesting book can specify how to become obsolete.
