# The Alpha Drop

The most famous graph in the 2019 human magnetoreception paper is not a compass rose. It is a fall.

Alpha-band power drops after certain magnetic-field rotations. In some participants the decrease is large enough to be visually obvious in the time-frequency plots. That image has carried a great deal of rhetorical weight because it looks like a brain reacting to something invisible.

EEG deserves more suspicion than pictures encourage. Alpha power changes for many reasons. Attention shifts. Eyes move. Drowsiness changes. A participant hears something. A cable settles. A muscle tightens. A magnetic coil induces a transient into the recording chain. If a study presents one dramatic post-stimulus dip without controls, the correct response is not wonder.

The Caltech-led group understood this problem. Their apparatus used a shielded chamber and double-wrapped coil systems that could run current in configurations producing a magnetic-field rotation or a sham condition with much of the same electrical behavior but cancellation of the field. Participants sat with eyes closed. The analysis compared post-stimulus alpha power to baseline and examined which field geometries produced the response.

The reported selectivity is the important part. A generic electromagnetic transient should not ordinarily care whether a simulated geomagnetic vector is ecologically plausible for the Northern Hemisphere. Yet the strongest alpha event-related desynchronization appeared only for specific rotations and field directions. When the vertical component was inverted, turning the field into an unnatural upward orientation, the same horizontal rotations did not produce equivalent responses.

The paper interpreted this as evidence of biological tuning. That interpretation is reasonable enough to deserve replication and not strong enough to substitute for it.

There are several nested claims here. The first is purely descriptive: under the reported protocol, the group observed statistically significant alpha-power changes associated with particular magnetic stimuli. The second is causal: the magnetic field rather than an artifact caused those changes. The third is biological: a sensory pathway produced them. The fourth is mechanistic: the pathway likely uses a ferromagnetic transducer. Each step requires additional assumptions.

One of the cleverest parts of the study was the attempt to use field symmetry as a mechanism discriminator. A radical-pair inclination compass should, in a simplified model, respond to field-axis inclination without caring about north-south polarity. A magnetite-based receptor can be polar. The human EEG response appeared to distinguish polarity. The authors argued that this favored magnetite and ruled against several alternatives.

But “ruled out” is dangerous language in a biological system whose receptor is unknown. Radical-pair mechanisms can be more complex than textbook summaries. Neural processing can transform a receptor signal. An unrecognized artifact can inherit polarity from the apparatus. The right conclusion is that the reported signature is more naturally compatible with some mechanisms than others.

The study also reported individual differences. Not every participant showed a dramatic alpha drop. Strong responders appeared to retain their response across repeated trials. This could represent genuine biological variation. It could also represent the familiar problem of selecting on the dependent variable. If the strongest responders are identified after inspecting their data, their apparent stability can be exaggerated unless the criterion is tested prospectively.

A decisive follow-up would predefine responder classification using one session and predict response magnitude in a later session before opening the data. Better still, it would move the participant to another laboratory with a different coil system and analysis pipeline. A sensory phenotype should travel with the person more reliably than an apparatus artifact does.

The data availability of the 2019 paper is a strength. The authors provided digital data and analysis scripts. Open data does not eliminate analytic flexibility, but it makes reanalysis possible. Independent teams can test whether preprocessing choices, artifact rejection, baseline selection or cluster statistics materially change the result. They can also build preregistered replication protocols from the exact stimulus geometry rather than from a popular summary.

Why has such replication not yet become routine? Human magnetoreception is technically awkward. Building a three-axis Earth-strength coil system inside adequate electromagnetic shielding is not a standard neuroscience setup. The effect is not a fashionable clinical endpoint with a large funding ecosystem. The subject carries historical baggage. A failed replication can disappear quietly; a successful one risks association with a field some departments still regard as fringe. None of these sociological facts make the 2019 result true. They help explain why the evidentiary bottleneck can persist longer than the paper's visibility would suggest.

The alpha drop should therefore be treated as a functional handle, not a verdict. It gives future experiments something quantitative to reproduce. It has timing, frequency, topography and stimulus dependencies. Those properties can be attacked.

Change the light. Add the RF perturbation used in radical-pair tests. Reverse polarity. Vary inclination independently. Slow the rotation. Increase and decrease field magnitude around geomagnetic strength. Test participants from the Southern Hemisphere with stimulus geometries matched to their developmental environment. Record eye movements and peripheral physiology. Run identical coil currents in dummy loads and phantoms to map electronic artifacts.

A good anomaly becomes smaller as it becomes better measured. “The brain senses magnetism” is too large. “A subset of participants shows a reproducible posterior alpha-power decrease 300 to 800 milliseconds after a specific Earth-strength field rotation under blinded sham-controlled conditions” is smaller and far more useful.

If that statement survives independent laboratories, the field has something real to explain. If it does not, the famous drop becomes a lesson in how beautiful selectivity can emerge from a fragile experimental system.

Either outcome is worth knowing.
