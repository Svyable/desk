# Two Mechanisms Enter the Room

By now the clean story has failed in a useful way.

The human EEG result looks, on its face, more like a polarity-sensitive ferromagnetic transducer. The human behavioral result looks, on its face, more like a light-dependent radical-pair transducer. Both cannot be the single explanation for the same single pathway unless at least one mechanistic inference is wrong.

That leaves four broad possibilities.

The first is mundane: one result is real and the other is not. This should be the default competitor because frontier fields routinely produce isolated findings that do not survive independent replication. The second is harsher: neither result is real, and the apparent mechanism signatures are elaborate artifacts generated by different apparatuses and analyses. The third is that both effects are real but one or both mechanism interpretations are wrong. The fourth is biologically richest: humans retain more than one magnetically responsive process.

Animals make the fourth possibility plausible without making it probable. Magnetite-associated receptors and radical-pair compasses need not perform the same task. One might supply polarity or map information; another might provide an inclination compass. Evolution often layers sensory cues rather than selecting one perfect transducer. But invoking two pathways before reproducing either human effect would be a classic rescue maneuver.

The only responsible way to entertain multiple pathways is to demand dissociation.

A dissociation means the two effects separate under a manipulation predicted in advance. Imagine that alpha-ERD remains present in complete darkness, survives blue-light removal, ignores resonance-frequency RF perturbation, but reverses or changes with magnetic polarity. At the same time, behavioral orientation vanishes in darkness, is restored by blue light, is disrupted by the predicted RF field, and remains indifferent to polarity while tracking inclination. If that pattern appeared repeatedly across laboratories, “two pathways” would stop being a story and become the most economical model.

The same design can kill the idea. If EEG and behavior both show identical light and RF dependencies, the simpler explanation is a shared mechanism and an overconfident original magnetite inference. If neither responds to the factorial manipulations on replication, there may be no robust human magnetic phenotype to explain.

The field badly needs this kind of adversarial collaboration. The original teams should not merely repeat their own paradigms. They should agree beforehand on which result would count against their preferred mechanism, exchange stimulus code, use matched sham conditions, and analyze blinded datasets. A laboratory associated with magnetite should be able to lose to a radical-pair prediction and vice versa.

There is precedent for this style elsewhere in science, though not enough of it. Mechanism disputes become productive when researchers stop treating their apparatus as territory. In magnetoreception the opportunity is unusually clean because the candidate mechanisms respond to different physical interventions.

The book's central thesis lives here: the contradiction between modern human magnetoreception studies is not a reason to smooth the literature into consensus and not a reason to discard the field. It is a map of experiments that have not yet been run.

The map is more valuable than certainty.
