# The Fly With a Human Protein

A fruit fly carrying a human protein is an odd witness for a human sense. It is also a useful one because it prevents anatomy, behavior and molecular capability from being confused.

In 2011 Lauren Foley, Robert Gegear and Steven Reppert asked a narrow question. Drosophila magnetosensitivity in their assay depended on cryptochrome. If the fly's own cryptochrome was removed, magnetic behavior was lost. Could a human cryptochrome substitute for the missing protein?

They expressed human CRY2 in cryptochrome-deficient flies and tested magnetic preference under controlled illumination. The human protein restored a light-dependent magnetic response in that system. The paper concluded that human CRY2 had the molecular capability to function in a light-sensitive magnetosensing pathway.

The wording matters. Capability is not deployment.

A transistor can function in a radio without proving that every device containing the transistor is a radio. Human CRY2 inserted into a fly inherits the fly's cells, signaling machinery, developmental context and behavioral assay. The experiment demonstrates that the protein can participate in magnetosensitive chemistry under those conditions. It does not show that human retinas arrange CRY2 in the necessary orientation, that the human protein is photoactive in the same physiological state, or that a signal reaches a human sensory pathway.

This is a recurring problem in frontier biology. Molecular possibility is seductive because it feels mechanistic. Once the molecule has a name, the story seems finished. In reality, receptor function is architecture plus context. Opsins are useful visual pigments because they sit in exquisitely organized photoreceptor cells connected to neural circuits. Mechanosensitive channels become hearing because the cochlea supplies amplification, geometry and frequency separation. A cryptochrome with magnetically sensitive chemistry becomes a compass only if tissue organization preserves directional information and the nervous system reads it.

The fly experiment nevertheless created a bridge that had not existed before. Human magnetoreception no longer required inventing an exotic molecule unique to us. A known human protein could support magnetic behavior in another animal. That fact became more interesting after avian CRY4 work strengthened the radical-pair model in migratory birds.

But human CRY2 is not bird CRY4. Cryptochrome families have diverged. Mammalian cryptochromes play central roles in the transcriptional feedback loops of circadian clocks. Their light sensitivity and redox states differ from insect photoreceptor cryptochromes. The fact that hCRY2 works in a transgenic fly does not erase those biochemical differences.

This is exactly why the 2022 human behavioral study is provocative. It did not merely point to cryptochrome by analogy. It reported dependencies that were intended to fingerprint radical-pair physics: blue light reaching the eyes and sensitivity to weak radiofrequency fields near a resonance condition. If those results replicate, they move the argument from “human CRY2 can do something in a fly” toward “a human behavioral effect carries signatures predicted by light-dependent spin chemistry.”

There are still several gaps. One is anatomical. Which human cells contain the relevant molecule in the relevant state? Another is optical. How much blue light reaches those cells under the experimental conditions? Another is signaling. What downstream pathway converts a small change in radical-pair yield into neural activity large enough to influence orientation? Another is specificity. Could the radiofrequency manipulation act through some unrelated mechanism or experimental cue?

A serious field does not hide these gaps. It uses them to rank the next experiments.

One could, for example, test whether the reported behavioral effect depends on retinal exposure specifically rather than blue light on the skin. Spectrally narrow illumination could map an action spectrum. Genetic variation in CRY genes might correlate with response, though such association work would require large samples and careful correction. Pharmacological manipulation of flavin redox state is conceptually interesting but biologically messy. More immediately, laboratories can reproduce the forced-choice paradigm with preregistered RF conditions and sham generators, while monitoring for auditory or vibrational cues.

The fly also warns us about the word “human.” A human protein inside another organism is not a miniature person. The result belongs in evidence class one for molecular capability and nowhere higher. The human behavioral interpretation remains a separate empirical burden.

That separation may feel pedantic until a field becomes controversial. Then it becomes the only way to keep the evidence from laundering itself. A molecular paper cites behavior. A behavior paper cites animal mechanisms. A review cites both and writes that humans possess a cryptochrome compass. A popular article drops the qualifiers entirely. Within three citation hops, possibility becomes fact.

The fly with a human protein is therefore useful for two reasons. It makes the radical-pair hypothesis harder to dismiss as chemically impossible in humans. And it teaches exactly how not to turn a mechanism into a sense before the organism has earned it.
