# A Compass Is Not a Map

A compass answers one kind of question. A map answers another.

That distinction is easy to lose because animal magnetoreception research uses both words around the same planetary field. Directional information can tell an organism how to hold a bearing. Spatial information requires gradients or combinations of field variables that vary across geography. Sea turtles, salmon and other animals appear capable of extracting map-like information from magnetic intensity and inclination. A human alpha response to a field rotation is nowhere near that claim.

The modesty is useful. Human magnetoreception does not need to recover an ancestral GPS to matter. A reproducible magnetic sensory response would already expand our understanding of human sensory biology. Identifying the receptor would solve a long-running biophysical problem. Showing two separate magnetic pathways would be stranger still.

But the evidence stops where it stops.

Verified facts include robust magnetoreception in many animals, the existence of magnetically sensitive cryptochrome chemistry in experimental systems, magnetite in human brain tissue, a published human EEG response to selected geomagnetic rotations, and a published human behavioral result with reported light and radiofrequency dependencies. Credible but incomplete evidence includes the proposed mechanism assignments and the stability of individual human responders. Disputed claims include a consciously usable human magnetic compass and any specific receptor organ not yet identified. The synthesis—that humans may retain one or more degraded magnetic pathways—is an open conjecture.

The most important change during the writing of this book was not an increase in confidence. It was a narrowing of the right question.

“Can humans sense Earth's magnetic field?” is too blunt. Some experiments say that human physiology or behavior changes under controlled magnetic manipulations. The better questions are: which effect replicates, under which geometry, through which tissue, with which physical dependency, and with what consequence?

That is a less cinematic ending than discovering a lost sense. It is also a more exciting scientific beginning.

Somewhere between a robin's retina, a bacterium's magnetosome chain, a human brain's iron particles and an alpha rhythm dropping in a shielded room is a problem that still has enough structure to be solved. We do not need to call the answer mysterious. We need to make the mechanisms disagree in public and then measure which one survives.

A compass is not a map. A signal is not a sense. A sense is not a skill.

For now, those separations are the most reliable instruments we have.
