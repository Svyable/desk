# Chapter 2 — 1780s: Philadelphia — The Country Becomes a Government

A successful revolution can hide a failed operating system for a while.

The United States entered the 1780s with the first part of its gamble accomplished. Britain had not crushed the rebellion. The states had declared independence, sustained a war, secured French assistance, and survived long enough to negotiate peace. The Treaty of Paris in 1783 recognized American independence and gave the new country an enormous territorial claim east of the Mississippi.

Victory did not answer the harder question of what, exactly, had won.

Was the United States a country with states inside it, or a league of states that sometimes acted like a country? The Articles of Confederation leaned heavily toward the second answer. Congress could conduct diplomacy, declare war, make treaties, and handle some common affairs, but it could not reliably tax individuals, regulate interstate commerce, or force states to provide the revenue it requested. The arrangement reflected revolutionary fear of concentrated authority. It also made national commitments difficult to finance and national problems difficult to coordinate.

The 1780s therefore belong to Philadelphia for a different reason than the 1770s. The first Philadelphia chapter was about making a national claim. This one is about admitting that the machinery built to carry the claim was inadequate, then risking a stronger machine.

New York has the strongest rival case. The Confederation Congress met there from 1785. It was from New York that the government under the Articles conducted the country's affairs during several crucial years. When the new Constitution took effect, New York became the first capital under that system. George Washington took the presidential oath at Federal Hall in 1789. The first Congress created executive departments, established the federal judiciary, sent constitutional amendments to the states, and began converting the Constitution's spare language into institutions.

If the decade ended with the problem of implementation, New York might win.

But the decade's decisive act happened before implementation. It happened when Americans with every reason to distrust central power decided that their first national arrangement was too weak to preserve what the Revolution had created.

They made that decision in Philadelphia.

The crisis was not one dramatic collapse. That matters. Political systems often become obsolete through accumulation rather than catastrophe. The Confederation government could function. Congress settled western land questions with ordinances of lasting importance. States governed themselves. Trade continued. Families farmed, merchants borrowed, courts sat, and towns grew. The system was not an empty shell.

It was a system whose weaknesses became increasingly expensive.

Congress struggled to obtain revenue from states. War debts remained. British troops continued to occupy frontier posts in territory the peace treaty assigned to the United States, while Britain complained that Americans were not honoring provisions affecting prewar debts and Loyalist property. States pursued trade policies that could conflict with one another. Credit was tight. Different constituencies wanted different forms of relief. The national government could make commitments without possessing all the tools needed to enforce them.

The problem was partly fiscal and partly psychological. A government that could not reliably fund itself had difficulty demonstrating permanence. Creditors had to consider not just whether American leaders wanted to pay debts but whether their institutions could produce payment. Foreign powers had to judge whether an agreement with Congress would be honored by the states. Domestic factions could interpret every proposal for stronger national authority as a possible return of the centralized coercion they had just fought.

That tension is the central political problem of the decade: how do people create enough public power to make a republic effective without creating so much that it becomes the thing they fear?

The answer was not obvious.

Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts during 1786 and 1787 became one of the events later used to demonstrate the Confederation's weakness. Farmers burdened by debt and taxes shut down courts and eventually confronted state forces. Nationalists saw the uprising as evidence that public order and republican government were vulnerable. Others saw the grievances themselves as evidence that governments could become instruments of creditors and wealthy interests. The same episode could support opposite diagnoses depending on what a person believed the greater danger to be.

That is worth preserving because the Constitutional Convention is too often narrated as if all reasonable people had recognized one technical defect and gathered to repair it.

They had not.

The men who assembled in Philadelphia in May 1787 came from states with different sizes, economies, political cultures, debts, regional interests, and expectations about the future. Rhode Island sent no delegation. The convention had been called to revise the Articles, not necessarily to replace them with an entirely new constitution. Delegates disagreed about representation, executive power, national authority, courts, commerce, slavery, and the degree to which ordinary voters should influence the institutions being built.

The setting mattered because Philadelphia offered continuity without requiring obedience to the old arrangement. The Pennsylvania State House had already become national political ground. The building carried the memory of independence, but there was no constitutional sanctity attached to the Articles simply because Congress had once met nearby. The delegates could claim to be preserving the Revolution while redesigning the structure meant to protect it.

Their most basic innovation was not a clever clause. It was a change in where authority was said to come from.

The Constitution did not present itself as a treaty among sovereign states. Its opening words spoke in the name of “We the People.” That phrase did not mean political equality as a modern reader might hear it. The electorate remained restricted by state rules. Women generally could not vote. Enslaved people were denied freedom and political agency. Native nations stood outside the constitutional community even as federal power over Indian affairs would become enormously consequential. Still, the document's theory shifted the source of legitimacy away from a compact that states alone could alter.

The national government would operate directly on individuals.

That was a profound change. Congress would gain the power to tax, borrow, regulate interstate and foreign commerce, create uniform rules in certain areas, raise armies, and pass laws necessary for carrying out enumerated powers. A separate executive would enforce federal law. A federal judiciary would interpret it. The states would retain broad authority, but they would exist inside a constitutional order that could act without asking each legislature for permission every time national action was required.

The design was full of compromises because the underlying union was full of conflicts.

The question of representation nearly broke the convention. Large states wanted influence tied more closely to population. Small states feared permanent subordination. The resulting Congress divided representation between a House based on population and a Senate in which states received equal votes. That arrangement solved one bargaining problem by creating a permanent structural advantage for residents of less populous states in one chamber.

Slavery produced compromises with consequences far more severe.

The Constitution protected the political power and economic interests of slaveholding states in several ways. The three-fifths provision increased southern representation by counting a portion of enslaved people for apportionment while denying those people political rights. The document restricted federal interference with the international slave trade until 1808. It included a fugitive-slave provision requiring the return of people held to service who escaped across state lines. The word “slavery” was largely avoided, but the institution was not absent from the design.

This is not a footnote to the creation of effective government. It was part of the price of creating that government.

The convention's achievement and its moral failure were joined. A stronger union became possible partly because delegates reached bargains that allowed human bondage to persist and gave slaveholding states additional national power. The structure that would later give the federal government the capacity to fight a civil war and abolish slavery was initially created through compromises that helped protect slavery. American institutions repeatedly contain this kind of historical reversal: a mechanism built under one distribution of power can later be used under another.

The convention also created an executive strong enough to alarm people who had just rejected monarchy. The presidency combined national visibility, military command, veto power, appointment authority, and responsibility for executing laws. Yet it was constrained by elections, fixed terms, impeachment, legislative powers, and the judiciary. The Electoral College was another compromise layered onto the problem of executive selection, shaped by federalism, population, and slavery.

None of these mechanisms came with proof that they would work.

That uncertainty is essential. Americans now live with the Constitution as inherited architecture. A senator, a president, the Supreme Court, congressional elections, federal taxation, and the hierarchy between federal and state law feel like features of an established landscape. In 1787 they were design choices in a system with no operating history.

The delegates could study ancient republics, British institutions, state constitutions, colonial experience, and political philosophy. They could not run a simulation of the United States under the proposed Constitution for fifty years and compare it with alternatives.

They had to commit under uncertainty.

The convention sent its proposed Constitution to the states, where the argument moved into ratifying conventions, newspapers, pamphlets, taverns, churches, homes, and personal correspondence. The debate was not simply between people who favored government and people who opposed it. Anti-Federalists worried that the proposed system concentrated authority too far from ordinary citizens, lacked explicit protections for individual rights, created a potentially dangerous executive, and weakened local control. Those fears were not irrational. Several became enduring themes of American politics.

Federalists argued that the union needed enough power to survive, govern commerce, maintain credit, provide security, and prevent states or factions from undermining common interests. In *The Federalist*, Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay defended the proposed system to New York readers, which is another reason New York has a legitimate claim on the decade. Their essays became one of the most influential explanations of the Constitution's design.

But the argument they were defending had been assembled in Philadelphia.

Ratification required more than intellectual victory. The Constitution specified that it would take effect among the ratifying states once nine approved it. Delaware went first in December 1787. Pennsylvania followed. The process continued into 1788 amid intense contests. New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify in June 1788. Virginia and New York followed after major debates. North Carolina and Rhode Island would join later.

The Constitution survived its first test because enough states accepted a bargain none regarded as perfect.

The demand for a bill of rights illustrates how political opposition can reshape a system even when it loses the immediate vote. Federalists had argued that enumerating rights could be unnecessary or even dangerous if it implied the government possessed powers not granted. Critics wanted explicit protections. The promise of amendments helped secure support, and the first Congress in New York took up the task. Ten amendments were ratified in 1791.

The sequence matters: Philadelphia designed, the country argued, New York began to operate, and the states continued to modify.

Why, then, does Philadelphia get the decade twice in a row?

Because American independence and American government were separate inventions, and the same city happened to host both.

The 1770s achievement was centrifugal: colonies broke from imperial authority and asserted the right to govern themselves. The 1780s achievement was partly centripetal: states that had guarded their sovereignty agreed to place real powers in a national government. The second act did not reverse the first. It exposed a tension inside it. Self-government at continental scale required some people to surrender some local discretion to institutions farther away.

Philadelphia was where revolution encountered administration.

The costs and exclusions remained visible in the city and in the country around it. Free Black communities grew even as the new Constitution strengthened protections for slaveholders. Pennsylvania had enacted gradual abolition in 1780, making the city part of a state moving away from slavery while national political bargains continued to protect the institution. That contrast would become sharper in the following decade when national officials, including George Washington, lived and governed in Philadelphia while navigating Pennsylvania's gradual abolition law.

The city therefore occupied an unstable border between political ideals and legal realities. A visitor could encounter arguments about universal liberty, a state process of gradual emancipation, national leaders who enslaved people, and a constitutional order that treated slavery as a protected interest without naming it directly.

The new government did not resolve the contradiction. It nationalized the arena in which the contradiction would continue.

The Constitution also redistributed importance among cities. New York's role as the first federal capital under the new system was real but temporary. Philadelphia would serve as the national capital during the 1790s. Washington did not yet exist as a functioning seat of government. The location of the capital itself became a bargaining chip in national politics, tied to regional interests and financial arrangements.

That mobility is a reminder that the United States had not yet developed one unquestioned center. The republic's institutions could move because the political nation was still being built through networks of cities, states, ports, farms, roads, printers, banks, and legislatures.

The Constitution's most consequential feature may have been its capacity to outlive the specific bargains of 1787 without becoming easy to alter. Amendment was possible but difficult. Elections could replace officeholders without replacing the system. Courts and legislatures could reinterpret broad language. Political parties could emerge even though the document did not design them. The presidency could become far more powerful than many delegates anticipated. Federal reach could expand through war, taxation, regulation, spending, civil rights, and administrative institutions.

The document became an operating environment.

That phrase can make the achievement sound technological, as if a constitution were neutral infrastructure. It is not. Rules advantage some strategies over others. Representation shapes whose coalition can win. Jurisdiction determines which institution gets to decide. Rights protect some forms of action from ordinary majorities. Procedures delay, accelerate, fragment, or concentrate power. The structure of government is itself a distribution of political possibility.

Philadelphia's 1787 convention chose that distribution for a country it could not foresee.

The delegates did not know about political parties as they would soon develop, mass suffrage, industrial capitalism, continental expansion to the Pacific, civil war, corporations of modern scale, radio, nuclear weapons, civil-rights litigation, administrative agencies, or digital networks. They wrote broad institutions that later generations would stretch over realities outside the designers' experience.

This is often praised as foresight. Some of it was. Some of it was ambiguity. Some of it was institutional struggle by later generations who forced old language to accommodate new claims. Durability should not be confused with perfect design.

The deeper achievement was that the country created a mechanism for conflict that did not require starting the country over every time the conflict changed.

That mechanism has failed people, excluded people, and sometimes preserved injustice for generations. It has also provided tools through which those exclusions could be challenged. The tension cannot be flattened into either constitutional worship or constitutional dismissal without losing the history.

Philadelphia in the 1780s matters because the city hosted the moment when Americans chose to make their national experiment harder to leave and more capable of acting.

The danger of that choice was obvious to people at the time. A government powerful enough to solve collective problems might become powerful enough to violate liberty. The danger of refusing the choice was also obvious. A union too weak to raise revenue, regulate shared systems, enforce commitments, or respond to crisis might cease to be a union in practice.

The Constitution did not eliminate the choice between liberty and power. It built a permanent arena in which Americans would keep arguing about the boundary.

By the end of the decade, the argument had moved north. George Washington was inaugurated in New York in 1789. Congress began filling the empty boxes of the new constitutional diagram with offices, laws, salaries, procedures, and people. The national government was finally becoming operational.

Philadelphia had done its second piece of work.

In the 1770s, it gave the country a sentence: independence.

In the 1780s, it gave the country a structure capable of making sentences into law.
