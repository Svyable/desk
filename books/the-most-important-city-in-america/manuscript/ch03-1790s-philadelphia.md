# Chapter 3 — 1790s: Philadelphia — The Republic Learns to Govern

Philadelphia did not win the 1790s because it remained the symbolic birthplace of the Revolution. Symbols decay quickly when they are asked to collect taxes.

It won because the city became the place where the new Constitution had to survive contact with ordinary government.

The federal government began under the Constitution in New York in 1789, and New York can reasonably object to losing this decade. George Washington was inaugurated there. The first Congress created the executive departments, federal courts, and the amendments that became the Bill of Rights. Alexander Hamilton began building the Treasury's financial program while the government still sat at the southern tip of Manhattan. The city was already developing the commercial and securities culture that would make it impossible to write later American history without Wall Street.

But the federal capital moved to Philadelphia in 1790, and for the rest of the decade the country's largest political experiment took place there. The experiment was not whether Americans could write an elegant constitution. They had already done that. It was whether politicians, judges, tax collectors, diplomats, soldiers, creditors, debtors, newspaper editors, voters, and ordinary residents would accept the new government's authority when its decisions became costly.

A written constitution is an invitation to implementation.

Philadelphia became the implementation city.

The move itself was the product of bargaining. The Residence Act set the permanent capital on the Potomac after a temporary period in Philadelphia. The political settlement around the location of the capital was connected to Hamilton's plan for the federal government to assume state Revolutionary War debts. The exact private conversations surrounding the bargain have been mythologized, but the larger point is clear: the physical geography of national power and the financial architecture of the new government were negotiated together.

That pairing tells us something about the republic from the beginning. Governments do not float above markets, debts, land, and regional interests. The federal government needed a home, and it needed credit. Both questions forced political actors to decide which parts of the new union would bear costs and which would gain influence.

Hamilton's financial program was among the most consequential answers. Federal assumption of state debts, funding of federal obligations, customs revenue, excise taxes, and the creation of the First Bank of the United States were intended to establish national credit and bind influential economic interests to the success of the federal government. Public debt, in Hamilton's design, was not merely a burden inherited from war. Properly managed, it could become part of the infrastructure of national power.

That claim was controversial for good reasons.

Speculators had purchased government securities from veterans and other original holders, sometimes at steep discounts. Funding those securities at favorable terms could enrich people who had bought when confidence was low. Assumption shifted obligations from states to the federal government, redistributing political and fiscal power. The Bank of the United States looked to critics like an institution that would privilege financiers and stretch constitutional authority beyond its proper boundary.

Thomas Jefferson and James Madison came to see Hamilton's program as part of a dangerous concentration of financial and political power. Hamilton and his allies saw opposition as a threat to the capacity the country needed to survive.

Philadelphia was where those disagreements stopped being private differences among founders and became the early party system.

The Constitution does not mention political parties. Many leaders of the founding generation distrusted faction. Yet the new government's policies created coalitions almost immediately. Federalists and Democratic-Republicans developed newspapers, local organizations, congressional alliances, electoral strategies, and rival interpretations of the Revolution. The argument over what the Constitution meant began before the ink of the founding era had dried.

This is why the familiar idea that parties later corrupted an originally nonpartisan republic gets the sequence wrong. The powers and ambiguities of the new system created disagreements that required organization. People had to recruit candidates, coordinate voters, distribute arguments, and identify allies. Once power existed, competition for its use became structural.

Philadelphia's printing culture made the city a particularly intense arena for that competition. Newspapers attacked officials, defended policies, spread rumor, translated foreign events into domestic faction, and made personalities part of constitutional argument. Editors could be partisan combatants rather than detached observers. The political nation began to acquire a daily rhythm of accusation and response.

The 1790s also forced the new government to decide how much dissent it could tolerate.

The Whiskey Rebellion grew from resistance to the federal excise tax on distilled spirits. The tax fell differently across regions and economic systems. In western areas, whiskey could serve as a transportable store of value and a practical medium of exchange. Opposition was not simply a philosophical protest by people who disliked taxes. It reflected the geography of a country in which cash, roads, markets, and state capacity were distributed unevenly.

When resistance in western Pennsylvania became serious, the Washington administration mobilized militia forces. The federal government demonstrated that it could enforce a law through organized coercion in a way the Confederation government had often been unable to do.

The symbolism was unavoidable. A government created by a tax rebellion against imperial authority was now marching against citizens resisting a federal tax.

The comparison was not exact. Americans had representation in the government imposing the whiskey excise. The law had been enacted under the constitutional process. But constitutional legitimacy does not make coercion disappear. It changes the argument about when coercion is authorized.

The episode showed that the new federal government possessed teeth.

It also showed why the Constitution's critics had worried about teeth in the first place.

Foreign affairs provided another test. The French Revolution transformed European politics and pulled American factions into fierce disagreement. France had been the essential ally of the American Revolution. Many Americans sympathized with the overthrow of monarchy. But war between revolutionary France and Britain created enormous risk for a young country with limited military capacity and substantial commercial ties.

Washington's administration pursued neutrality. The decision was strategic and constitutional. It required the executive branch to interpret its authority in foreign affairs, manage treaty obligations, and resist domestic pressures that could drag the country into war. The controversy surrounding the French minister Edmond-Charles Genêt, who sought to mobilize American support for French objectives, made the question immediate.

Could the United States remain neutral when its citizens had strong transatlantic political loyalties and its former ally was fighting the empire from which it had just separated?

The answer was not self-evident.

The Jay Treaty with Britain became another political explosion. Supporters saw it as a way to settle outstanding disputes, protect commerce, and avoid a war the United States was poorly positioned to fight. Opponents considered it too favorable to Britain and a betrayal of republican sympathy for France. Crowds, newspapers, and political societies turned foreign policy into mass domestic politics.

Philadelphia was not merely the place where officials signed papers. It was the city in which national policy and public opinion collided at close range.

Then disease emptied the streets.

The yellow fever epidemic of 1793 killed thousands in Philadelphia and caused many residents, including national officials, to flee. Modern readers know that yellow fever is transmitted by mosquitoes, but physicians and officials at the time lacked that understanding. Competing theories shaped responses. The epidemic exposed the limits of medical knowledge, municipal capacity, and the assumption that a capital could remain continuously functional simply because the Constitution said the government existed.

Disease transformed the capital into a test of continuity.

The federal government could disperse temporarily. Treasury operations and official correspondence could continue imperfectly. The crisis raised questions about executive authority to convene Congress elsewhere and about the practical vulnerability of concentrating government in one city. The new republic had designed branches of government. It had not designed germ theory.

The epidemic also revealed social divisions. People with money and connections were generally better positioned to leave. Those who remained included laborers, servants, sailors, free Black residents, and people whose work or poverty limited their options. Members of Philadelphia's Black community, including leaders associated with the Free African Society, provided nursing and burial assistance after claims circulated that Black people were less susceptible to the disease. Black residents died too, and some were later accused unfairly of profiteering despite their service.

A public-health disaster became another arena in which citizenship, race, labor, and reputation were contested.

The decade's most uncomfortable Philadelphia story occurred not in Congress but in the President's House.

George Washington brought enslaved people from Mount Vernon into a state whose gradual abolition law could have created legal claims to freedom after extended residence. Washington's household took steps to avoid triggering the law's residency provisions, including rotating enslaved people out of Pennsylvania. One of those people, Ona Judge, escaped from the presidential household in Philadelphia in 1796 and resisted Washington's efforts to recapture her.

The details collapse the distance between founding language and lived power.

The first president governed a republic that had announced equality while holding human beings in slavery. The capital city belonged to a state moving gradually toward abolition, but federal power and presidential status did not automatically extend that freedom to the enslaved people in the executive household. Ona Judge's escape was not a philosophical objection. It was an individual act that exposed the limits of the political order around her.

She understood freedom as something she could take more urgently than the republic was willing to grant it.

Philadelphia's free Black community grew during these years and built institutions of its own. Richard Allen and Absalom Jones emerged as important religious and civic leaders. Independent Black churches and mutual-aid organizations were not marginal to the meaning of the city. They demonstrated that people excluded from formal political power were creating civic infrastructure while the national government debated the abstract boundaries of republican citizenship nearby.

The city therefore held two forms of institution-building at once: the federal government was learning how to govern a nation, and communities denied equal membership were learning how to protect themselves within it.

The tension became even sharper at the end of the decade.

The Adams administration, facing an undeclared naval conflict with France and intense domestic opposition, supported the Alien and Sedition Acts. The laws expanded federal power over noncitizens and criminalized certain forms of criticism of the government. Federalists defended them as necessary for national security in a dangerous international environment. Republicans saw them as an assault on political liberty and an attempt to suppress opposition.

The Sedition Act turned the abstract promise of a free political press into a practical question: could the people who held federal power use criminal law against journalists and political opponents who attacked them?

The answer, for a time, was yes.

Prosecutions followed. The controversy deepened partisan conflict and helped shape the election of 1800. Jefferson and Madison's Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions advanced theories of state resistance to unconstitutional federal action, introducing constitutional arguments that would echo in dangerous and contradictory forms for decades.

Again, the new system did not eliminate conflict. It gave conflict procedures, offices, laws, and jurisdictions through which to operate.

By 1800, the republic had accumulated a remarkable set of precedents. It had established public credit. It had created a national bank. It had enforced a federal tax with military force. It had stayed out of a major European war. It had survived epidemic disruption. It had seen political parties become unavoidable. It had tested the limits of press freedom. It had conducted fiercely contested elections. It had shown that a president could become the head of one administration rather than the embodiment of national unanimity.

And it was about to do something many republics had failed to do: transfer executive power between rival political factions after an election.

The election of 1800 would be resolved only after an Electoral College tie between Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr sent the decision to the House of Representatives. The constitutional mechanism revealed flaws serious enough to produce the Twelfth Amendment a few years later. But the immediate result was a peaceful transfer from Federalist John Adams to Republican Thomas Jefferson.

The capital moved too.

Washington, the planned city on the Potomac, was ready enough to receive the federal government. Philadelphia's decade as temporary national capital ended. It did not become irrelevant. It remained a major commercial, intellectual, medical, publishing, and financial center. But the specific national concentration that made it the most important city of three consecutive decades was breaking apart.

The triple victory is not a claim that Philadelphia dominated every feature of American life from 1770 through 1799. It did not. Boston drove early revolutionary mobilization. New York grew in finance and commerce. Virginia's political leadership was enormous. Western settlements forced questions about land and security. Caribbean revolutions, European wars, Native resistance, and Atlantic markets shaped the new nation from outside any American city.

Philadelphia wins because three different national tasks happened to converge there in sequence.

First the colonies had to become a political “we.” Then the states had to build a stronger constitutional system. Then the system had to learn how to operate when policy created winners, losers, resistance, opposition, and fear.

The third task may have been the least romantic and the most necessary.

A republic becomes real when its institutions disappoint people and survive.

Philadelphia in the 1790s was full of disappointment. Farmers hated taxes. Republicans hated Federalist finance. Federalists feared radical opposition. Merchants feared war. Residents feared disease. Enslaved people had every reason to distrust political declarations of liberty. Black citizens had to build institutions because the republic's equality did not include them. Newspaper editors discovered that freedom of political attack could collide with federal prosecution.

The city did not resolve those conflicts. It proved that the new government could contain enough of them to continue.

That is a different kind of founding.

By the time the national government left Philadelphia, the Constitution was no longer a proposal from a convention hall. It had become a tax bill, a bank charter, a militia summons, a treaty, a prosecution, an election, a debt instrument, a presidential order, and a dispute over who belonged.

The republic had acquired friction.

It was finally governing.
