# The Most Important City in America

A country can have a capital without having a center.

The United States has spent most of its history proving that point. Washington is the permanent seat of the federal government, but there have been decades when the future of the country was being manufactured in Detroit, financed in New York, contested in Birmingham, routed through Chicago, provisioned in St. Louis, coded in San Francisco, or exposed to the world from a few blocks of lower Manhattan. There are moments when the formal capital matters more than anywhere else. There are other moments when it is reacting to a change that has already become real somewhere else.

This book began with a question that sounds simpler than it is: what was the most important American city in each decade of the country's first 250 years?

The difficulty arrives immediately. Important by what measure? Population would give one kind of history. Wealth would give another. Federal employment would keep returning us to Washington. Cultural influence would pull us toward New York and Los Angeles. Industrial output would favor a changing set of manufacturing centers. Military importance, migration, finance, transportation, science, political movements, communications, energy, and technology would each produce their own map.

I wanted a map that could move.

So “most important” means something narrower here. The winning city is the place where a force that changed the country became unusually concentrated during that decade, and where the consequences did not stay local. Sometimes the force is an institution: constitutional government, the federal administrative state, a financial market. Sometimes it is an infrastructure: a canal, a rail network, an assembly line, a freeway system. Sometimes it is a conflict that reveals the actual boundary of American claims: Reconstruction in New Orleans, industrial labor in Chicago, civil rights in Birmingham. Sometimes a city matters because it connects systems that had previously been separate. Sometimes it matters because a national contradiction becomes too visible there to remain politely abstract.

This is not a beauty contest. It is not a list of the best places to live. It is not even a list of the most successful cities. A city can be nationally important because something went badly there. It can become consequential because it shows the cost of a system other places are eager to copy. It can matter because power is being challenged, not merely accumulated.

The method also allows cities to return. New York appears repeatedly, which may irritate readers who hoped for a perfectly distributed road trip. Washington does too. Philadelphia wins consecutive early decades. Chicago, Detroit, and New Orleans each have more than one moment. That is intentional. If a city recaptures national importance for a genuinely different reason, forcing it off the list merely to improve geographic variety would turn a historical argument into a tourism itinerary.

The repetitions reveal something useful. American power does not simply move westward, or southward, or from government to markets, or from industry to information. It oscillates. A place can matter because it controls a physical bottleneck in one era and an institutional bottleneck in another. New York in the 1820s is not New York in the 1920s, even though both chapters involve the movement of value. Washington in the 1800s is an experiment in whether an intentionally created capital can acquire gravity; Washington in the 1930s is becoming part of daily economic life at a scale the young republic would barely have recognized.

Every choice has rivals. Boston has a powerful claim on the 1770s. Philadelphia could easily take the 1780s and 1790s as well as the 1770s. Baltimore, Cincinnati, Charleston, Memphis, Richmond, Atlanta, Cleveland, Kansas City, San Diego, Oakland, Miami, Las Vegas, Austin, Boston again, and dozens of other cities can make plausible cases for decades they do not win here. Los Angeles could take more than one. Chicago could take the 1890s on the strength of the World's Columbian Exposition alone. New York could monopolize much of the twentieth century if importance were defined broadly enough.

A useful argument should survive strong alternatives rather than pretending they do not exist. In each chapter I have therefore tried to ask why the winner matters more for this particular decade than its strongest competitors. The answer is not always that more happened there. It is often that what happened there traveled farther.

There is another rule, and it matters more.

Every chapter asks who paid for the importance.

Cities are marvelous machines for concentrating gains and losses. A port creates trade and coerced labor. A factory creates products, wages, injury, pollution, discipline, and organizing power. A railroad makes distance cheaper while reorganizing whose land and whose businesses remain connected to the national economy. A freeway makes one kind of metropolitan freedom possible while destroying neighborhoods and hardening another kind of exclusion. Financial systems direct capital toward productive uses and can transmit panic at extraordinary speed. A technology cluster creates tools used by billions and can make ordinary housing unaffordable to people who built the city before the boom.

The story of an important place becomes dishonest when importance is treated as an uncomplicated compliment.

This is especially necessary in the chapters about expansion. The United States did not spread across empty land. St. Louis could become a gateway to western settlement because a continental project of migration, military power, surveying, treaty-making, dispossession, extraction, and infrastructure was underway. Calling a city a gateway tells us who was moving through it. It says less about the people whose homelands were being crossed, subdivided, or seized. The cost belongs in the same story as the achievement because it was part of the same process.

The same is true of industrialization. Lowell's mills cannot be separated cleanly from the cotton grown under slavery. Chicago's commodity efficiency cannot be separated from the conversion of grain, animals, timber, land, labor, and information into standardized units that could move through national markets. Pittsburgh's steel cannot be admired only as engineering without noticing the workers, smoke, heat, injury, and landscape required to produce it. Detroit's assembly line made an unprecedented consumer economy possible while also making the labor process more measurable, repetitive, and controllable.

Importance is therefore a relationship. A city becomes nationally important when some combination of geography, institutions, people, capital, technology, law, culture, and timing lets it change the options available elsewhere. The city does not do this alone. Ports depend on hinterlands. Factories depend on mines and farms. Wall Street depends on savers, borrowers, regulators, and taxpayers. Silicon Valley's products depend on global supply chains, public research, immigration, customers, and physical infrastructure. Even the most powerful city is a node inside larger systems.

That is why the chapters begin with places but do not stay inside municipal limits. The city is the lens. The country is the subject.

There is also a problem at the far end of the book. Historians have one great advantage over people living through history: outcomes have occurred. A decade that looked like it belonged to one place in its third year may look completely different by its tenth. We do not have that advantage with the 2020s. The final chapter is written in 2026, four years before the decade ends. Phoenix is therefore a provisional winner, and the chapter says so. Its case is not that the future has chosen Arizona. Its case is that several of the decade's largest American questions are already colliding there: the attempt to rebuild leading-edge semiconductor manufacturing, dependence on global technological supply chains, extreme heat, water management, fast metropolitan growth, migration, housing, and the political importance of the Sunbelt.

A different city may own the decade by 2029. That possibility is not a flaw in the exercise. It is evidence of what the exercise is trying to recover from the past: uncertainty.

The people in Philadelphia in 1776 did not know whether independence would survive. The delegates who met there in 1787 did not know whether the Constitution they produced would be ratified, much less endure for centuries. New Yorkers digging and financing connections to the Erie Canal could not see every consequence of turning the interior toward their harbor. Workers gathering in Chicago in 1886 did not know how Haymarket would be remembered around the world. Black citizens of New Orleans during Reconstruction could not know how thoroughly the democratic experiments of their moment would be attacked and constrained. Nobody standing in lower Manhattan on the morning of September 11, 2001, knew what the next eight years would make of that day, or that the decade would close with another crisis radiating through institutions nearby for entirely different reasons.

Hindsight makes history look more organized than it felt.

Cities help correct that distortion because they are crowded with simultaneous realities. A national policy becomes a queue at an office. A new industry becomes a shift change. A financial innovation becomes a new building and then a new panic. Migration becomes a block of unfamiliar languages and familiar groceries. Segregation becomes an address someone cannot buy. Technological change becomes rent, traffic, a job posting, a factory gate, an empty storefront, a faster delivery, a different skyline.

The country is easiest to mythologize from far away. Up close, it has always had an address.

Over the next twenty-six chapters, that address keeps changing.
