# Chapter 11 — 1870s: New Orleans — Democracy Under Fire

The most important political question of the 1870s was not whether slavery had ended.

It had.

The question was whether freedom would acquire enough public power to survive people determined to reverse it.

New Orleans is where that question became impossible to misunderstand.

Washington has the obvious rival claim. Congress enforced Reconstruction, passed civil-rights laws, and confronted the administration of Ulysses S. Grant over the reach of federal authority. The Supreme Court began narrowing some Reconstruction-era protections. The disputed presidential election of 1876 was resolved through a national political settlement that helped end sustained federal intervention in the South. Chicago rebuilt after the Great Fire and continued becoming the country's industrial center. New York's finance and population kept growing.

But New Orleans compressed the decade's democratic possibility and its violent counterrevolution into the same streets.

The city had already shown in 1866 how dangerous interracial political change could become. A white mob and police attacked Black marchers and participants in a constitutional convention at the Mechanics' Institute, killing dozens. The violence helped strengthen northern support for congressional Reconstruction. Louisiana's 1868 constitution then created one of the era's most ambitious formal experiments in interracial democracy: Black male suffrage, broader civil equality, and integrated public education were written into the state's political order.

By the 1870s, Black men in Louisiana were voting, holding office, serving in legislatures, participating in local government, and building political institutions that slavery had made impossible only a few years earlier.

That change was not symbolic.

Political office affected taxation, schools, policing, courts, public spending, patronage, and the enforcement of civil rights. Voting altered who had to be persuaded. Integrated institutions challenged the social order that had defined racial hierarchy as normal. Formerly enslaved people were not merely being “included” in an unchanged democracy. Their participation changed what democracy could decide.

That is why the opposition became so violent.

White supremacist organizations across the South used intimidation, murder, economic coercion, and organized attacks to destroy Republican political power and Black civic participation. The Ku Klux Klan was one form. In Louisiana, the White League became an overt paramilitary force committed to restoring white Democratic rule.

New Orleans was not simply witnessing spontaneous racial conflict.

It was a city in which organized groups used force to change the outcome of constitutional politics.

The distinction matters.

Calling the violence a “riot” can imply mutual disorder without political design. Much of Reconstruction-era violence had a clear objective: reduce Black voting, destroy Republican organization, remove officeholders, intimidate communities, and make federal rights unenforceable at the local level.

Democracy was being attacked as an institution.

The 1872 Louisiana governor's election intensified the crisis. Republican William Pitt Kellogg and Democrat John McEnery both claimed victory amid accusations of fraud and irregularity. Rival governments and contested returns turned the state's political authority into a question the courts, federal officials, parties, and armed supporters all tried to answer.

Disputed elections are dangerous when participants no longer agree on who has legitimate authority to count, certify, and enforce the result.

Louisiana showed how quickly that disagreement could become military.

In 1873, white opponents of the Republican state government attempted to seize the Cabildo and state arsenal in New Orleans. Elsewhere in Louisiana, the Colfax Massacre that year demonstrated the extraordinary violence white paramilitary forces could use against Black citizens and Republican officeholders. More than a local atrocity, Colfax became part of the legal history through which federal power to prosecute racial violence would later be narrowed.

Then came September 1874.

Thousands of armed White League members gathered in New Orleans and confronted the Metropolitan Police and state militia, forces associated with the Republican government and racially integrated political order. The White League erected barricades, fought in the streets, drove government forces back, and seized key public buildings, including the state arsenal and Cabildo.

For several days, an insurgent force displaced the lawful state government in the state's capital.

The episode became known as the Battle of Liberty Place.

The name itself is a warning about historical memory.

To the White League and later white-supremacist commemorators, “liberty” meant liberation from a Reconstruction government in which Black political power and federal enforcement constrained white rule. A word associated with the Revolution was redeployed to celebrate an attempt to overthrow an interracial government.

Political language does not arrive with permanent owners.

The federal government restored Governor Kellogg's administration. President Grant sent troops, and the White League withdrew rather than fight the United States Army directly.

Federal capacity worked.

Temporarily.

That adverb defines the decade.

Washington possessed the constitutional amendments, statutes, troops, courts, and executive authority needed to defend Reconstruction governments under certain conditions. What it increasingly lacked was durable northern political willingness to keep using those tools at the scale required.

Enforcement fatigue became a political force.

Some white northerners who had supported the Union war effort opposed continued federal intervention in southern politics. Economic concerns after the Panic of 1873 shifted attention. Corruption scandals damaged Republican credibility. Racist assumptions remained widespread outside the South as well as within it. Critics portrayed Reconstruction governments as uniquely corrupt even though corruption was hardly confined to them. Calls for “home rule” could sound like restoration of local democracy while meaning the removal of federal protection from Black citizens whose local opponents were using terror.

The country began confusing exhaustion with resolution.

New Orleans exposes the mistake.

If federal troops were the only thing preventing an armed organization from overthrowing a state government, withdrawal did not return politics to a neutral local equilibrium. It changed the balance of force.

That is the difference between decentralization in theory and decentralization under unequal coercive capacity.

Black citizens understood the stakes because the consequences were immediate. Voting could mean threats against employment, tenancy, family, or life. Officeholders could be targeted. Schools could become political battlegrounds. Local police and courts could not be assumed to protect Black rights if white Democrats captured them or if officials sympathized with racial violence.

A constitutional right is only as durable as the chain of institutions between the text and the person invoking it.

Reconstruction was testing that chain.

New Orleans also complicates a simplistic picture of the postwar South as only Black and white. The city contained Creoles of color with distinct histories rooted in the French and Spanish colonial eras, migrants from the Caribbean, European immigrants, Anglo-American newcomers, and communities divided by language, class, religion, and color as well as legal race.

Some free people of color before the war had owned property, received education, and built institutions while living under a slave society that still denied them equality. Reconstruction opened new political possibilities and threatened older hierarchies among groups as well as between them.

The city's complexity made the democratic experiment unusually rich.

It also made the effort to impose a binary white-supremacist order more revealing.

Integrated public education became one of the clearest fronts. The 1868 Louisiana constitution called for public schools open without racial distinction. In practice, integration was uneven, contested, and politically vulnerable. Yet the principle was radical enough to provoke opposition because schools do more than teach children. They define who can share public institutions as equals.

The battle over a classroom can be a battle over citizenship.

The same was true of public accommodations and transportation. Black New Orleanians and Creoles of color challenged segregation through legal action and organized protest. Their efforts anticipated later civil-rights strategies. In the 1890s, New Orleans activists would deliberately test Louisiana segregation law in the case that became *Plessy v. Ferguson*.

The infrastructure of resistance outlived Reconstruction even when Reconstruction's governments fell.

That matters because historical defeat is rarely total.

The people who built churches, newspapers, schools, mutual-aid societies, political clubs, legal strategies, and family networks during the 1860s and 1870s did not vanish in 1877. Jim Crow would severely constrict Black political and social life, but later movements inherited institutions, memory, and constitutional language from the Reconstruction struggle.

New Orleans was therefore both a place of democratic loss and a place where future civil-rights claims were rehearsed.

The national legal environment was becoming less favorable.

The Supreme Court's *Slaughter-House Cases* in 1873 arose from Louisiana and gave a narrow interpretation to the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause. The dispute itself involved a state-created slaughterhouse monopoly and public-health regulation, not directly racial discrimination, but the Court's constitutional reasoning limited one potential route for federal protection of rights against state action.

Other decisions would narrow federal enforcement further.

The transformation is striking when set beside the 1860s. The Civil War amendments had created extraordinary constitutional tools. In the 1870s, courts and politics began defining how difficult those tools could be to use.

Rights expanded on paper while remedies contracted in practice.

The disputed presidential election of 1876 brought the problem to its national climax.

Rutherford B. Hayes, the Republican, and Samuel J. Tilden, the Democrat, contested electoral votes in several states, including Louisiana. Rival returns and allegations of intimidation and fraud made the election impossible to resolve through ordinary confidence in state certification. Congress created an Electoral Commission to determine the disputed votes.

Hayes became president.

The political settlement surrounding the outcome is commonly associated with the withdrawal of remaining federal troops from key southern statehouses and the end of Reconstruction. Historians debate the exact nature and enforceability of any singular “Compromise of 1877,” but the practical shift is not in doubt: sustained federal military protection of Republican governments in the South ended.

In Louisiana, Democratic control consolidated.

The White League had failed to hold the state government by force in 1874.

Its political objective was achieved a few years later through a broader collapse of Reconstruction enforcement.

That sequence is why New Orleans is the decade's most important city.

It demonstrates that a democratic system can defeat an insurrection tactically and still lose the larger political struggle if the coalition defending the system dissolves.

The lesson is more uncomfortable than a simple story of federal overreach or local self-government.

Federal intervention during Reconstruction could be heavy-handed, partisan, inconsistent, and vulnerable to corruption. Republican governments were not free of misconduct. Military presence in civil affairs creates real problems for representative government.

But withdrawal must be evaluated against the conditions left behind.

When organized racial terror is a method of political competition, removing the force restraining it does not necessarily restore democracy. It can hand democracy to the better-armed faction.

New Orleans made that mechanism visible.

The city's economy also struggled through the decade. The Panic of 1873 and its long depression hurt trade and public finance. Municipal debt and taxation became serious problems. Economic distress reinforced political anger and gave opponents of Reconstruction another way to portray governments as illegitimate or incompetent.

Material hardship is rarely politically neutral.

People often interpret falling wages, taxes, debt, or business failures through the identities and institutions already under dispute. In New Orleans, economic complaints could merge with racial and partisan campaigns against Republican rule.

The city had not recovered its antebellum commercial dominance. Railroads were changing national trade routes. The Mississippi remained essential, but New York and Chicago were increasingly powerful in the national economic network. New Orleans's importance in the 1870s was therefore political more than commercial.

That reversal from the 1810s is part of what makes the city worth revisiting.

The first New Orleans chapter was about controlling a continental gateway.

The second is about controlling citizenship.

The same streets that had once mattered because goods and armies moved through them now mattered because ballots, schools, police forces, constitutions, and racial power were being contested there.

Cities can remain important after the mechanism of importance changes.

The costs of the 1870s would last generations. As white Democrats regained control across the South, Black voting rights were progressively constrained through violence, fraud, law, and later formal disfranchisement. Segregation hardened. Public institutions were divided by race. Convict leasing and other coercive labor systems exploited the Thirteenth Amendment's punishment exception. Economic dependency reinforced political vulnerability.

The democratic opening after the Civil War narrowed dramatically.

It is tempting to describe Reconstruction as having “failed.” The phrase is too passive.

Some institutions failed to protect rights. Some northern commitments weakened. Some policies were badly designed or enforced. But interracial democracy was also deliberately attacked by people who understood that it could work well enough to threaten their power.

New Orleans is important because the attackers did not hide their objective.

They built barricades.

They seized public buildings.

They tried to replace an elected government by force.

Federal troops reversed the takeover.

Then politics removed the troops.

The sequence is almost too clear.

A country that had spent the 1860s discovering the strength of the federal state spent the 1870s deciding whether to keep using that strength to defend equal citizenship.

In New Orleans, the answer arrived before the decade ended.

The Constitution had changed.

The balance of power changed back.
