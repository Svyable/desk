# Chapter 1 — 1770s: Philadelphia — The Country Becomes an Argument

In the summer of 1776, the most important room in America was not particularly grand. It sat inside the Pennsylvania State House in Philadelphia, a brick building whose later fame would make its original purpose feel almost incidental. Delegates came and went. Windows opened into heat and city noise. Papers accumulated. Men argued over language while a war that had already begun made the argument more dangerous than literary.

Boston has the first and strongest objection to Philadelphia's claim on the decade. The Boston Massacre, the Tea Party, the punishment of Massachusetts, Lexington and Concord, Bunker Hill: the sequence that pushed resistance toward war was centered there. If the question were which city most effectively made British imperial rule untenable, Boston might win. But the 1770s were not ultimately decided by the place that made rebellion unavoidable. They were decided by the place that turned rebellion into a claim of national political existence.

That happened in Philadelphia.

The city's importance was partly geographic. It was large by colonial standards, prosperous, connected to Atlantic trade, and accessible enough to the middle and southern colonies to serve as a meeting ground. It was also politically useful. Philadelphia belonged to Pennsylvania rather than to the New England center of resistance, allowing intercolonial cooperation to look less like everyone joining Massachusetts's quarrel. The city's Quaker origins and diverse religious life did not make it politically neutral, but they contributed to a reputation for pluralism and civic argument that suited a gathering of colonies not yet accustomed to acting as one.

The First Continental Congress met in Philadelphia in 1774. The Second returned in 1775 after fighting had begun. Before there was a recognized United States government, before independence, before a national constitution, there was a congress improvising the functions of one in a city that had never been designed as a capital. It appointed George Washington commander of the Continental Army. It dealt with money, supplies, diplomacy, military administration, petitions, and the increasingly awkward question of what exactly the colonies were trying to achieve.

That awkwardness matters because independence looks cleaner in retrospect than it did in sequence. The war began before the Declaration. Many colonists who resisted Parliament were not initially committed to separating from the British Empire. Political identities overlapped. A person could resent taxation, admire British constitutional traditions, fear centralized power, depend on Atlantic commerce, distrust local radicals, and still consider himself loyal to the king. The future nation existed first as a coalition held together by immediate pressure.

Philadelphia became the place where that coalition had to speak in a single voice.

Thomas Paine's *Common Sense*, published in Philadelphia in January 1776, did not invent the case for independence, but it gave it unusual clarity and circulation. Paine's attack on monarchy and his insistence that the colonies could become something separate helped move a public argument that had been accelerating for months. The pamphlet mattered partly because Philadelphia was a printing city. Printers, newspapers, taverns, coffeehouses, docks, churches, shops, and political meetings formed an information network in which arguments moved from page to conversation and back again.

The city was not merely hosting politics. It was producing the conditions in which politics could spread.

On July 2, 1776, the Continental Congress approved the resolution for independence. Two days later it adopted the text that would become the better-known date in American memory. The Declaration of Independence did several things at once. It announced separation. It offered accusations against George III. It tried to persuade audiences at home and abroad that the rebellion belonged to a recognizable tradition of political legitimacy. And in its most durable passage, it made a claim about human equality that the society announcing it did not remotely honor in practice.

That contradiction was present in the room, not added later by hostile interpretation.

Men who approved the Declaration included enslavers. The economy of the colonies was entangled with slavery. Philadelphia itself contained free and enslaved Black people, abolitionist sentiment, slaveholding households, and a commercial world connected to goods produced by coerced labor. Native nations were treated in the Declaration's grievances not as peoples with their own political sovereignty but through the racialized language of colonial warfare. Women were excluded from formal political power. The universal language was launched from a political order that drew its boundaries of membership narrowly.

Yet the language traveled farther than the men controlling it could contain.

That is one reason Philadelphia wins the decade. A city becomes nationally important not only when it concentrates power but when it produces claims that other people can seize. The Declaration would later be read by abolitionists, suffragists, civil-rights activists, labor organizers, independence movements abroad, presidents, judges, and citizens arguing that the country had failed to meet its own announced standard. The sentence about equality became a recurring instrument of dissent precisely because it exceeded the political settlement of 1776.

The document did not win the war. Philadelphia did not command the battlefield. The city itself was vulnerable enough that the British captured it in September 1777. Congress fled. The British occupied the rebel capital through the following June. If symbolic importance were military protection, Philadelphia would have failed an obvious test.

Its capture reveals something more interesting.

The British could occupy the city without ending the rebellion because the emerging United States was not a normal European state whose government could be decapitated by taking its capital. Congress moved. The army remained in the field. State governments functioned. Networks of supply, credit, militia, local administration, and political commitment extended beyond Philadelphia. The place mattered enormously, but the cause had already become distributed.

The occupation therefore created an early distinction that would recur throughout American history: a city can be the center of a national system without containing the whole system.

During the British occupation, Philadelphia's political loyalties were tested in intimate ways. Residents had to decide whether to leave, remain, trade, hide property, cooperate, resist, or simply survive. British officers needed quarters and supplies. Civilians faced shortages and uncertainty. Loyalists could experience the occupation as restoration; Patriots could experience it as humiliation or danger. The city that had declared a universal political principle became a place where politics was reduced to who controlled a street, a house, a warehouse, a loaf of bread.

Outside the city, Washington's army spent the winter at Valley Forge. The name later became a national shorthand for sacrifice, sometimes polished into a patriotic tableau. The real importance was organizational. The Continental Army survived a brutal winter and continued its transformation into a more disciplined force. The proximity of occupied Philadelphia made the contrast visible: the British held the political capital while the rebels preserved the military institution that would eventually make occupation temporary.

When the British withdrew in 1778, Philadelphia resumed its political role. But it returned to a different country than the one it had hosted before the invasion. France had entered the war as an American ally. Independence was no longer merely a declaration by insurgent colonies; it had become part of an international conflict. The city had helped turn a domestic imperial crisis into diplomacy between states.

This is another reason the decade belongs to Philadelphia rather than Boston. Boston gave the Revolution much of its ignition. Philadelphia gave it an address from which it could become legible to the world.

The distinction can be seen in the paperwork. Revolutions are remembered through crowds, battles, dramatic speeches, and iconic moments because those are easier to paint. But a successful rebellion also requires committees, correspondence, ledgers, procurement, appointments, printing, diplomatic instructions, financial improvisation, and decisions about who is authorized to decide. Philadelphia was full of this less cinematic work. The Continental Congress was both too weak and astonishingly ambitious, trying to conduct a war among states jealous of their autonomy while lacking many of the powers a later federal government would take for granted.

The defects were already apparent. Congress could request money and troops more easily than it could compel them. States had different interests. Currency depreciated. Supply was unreliable. Military and civilian authority had to be negotiated repeatedly. The emerging national government was an argument about whether a national government could exist at all.

That argument is easy to miss if 1776 is treated as a finished founding rather than a beginning.

The Philadelphia of the 1770s was not yet the Philadelphia of the Constitutional Convention. The men of 1776 did not know that they would return eleven years later to design a stronger federal system. They were operating under emergency conditions and inherited assumptions. Their central political achievement was to claim sovereignty before they had built a durable machine for exercising it.

The city itself embodied a useful tension between local competence and national aspiration. Philadelphia had municipal routines, markets, institutions, trades, religious communities, and a population accustomed to managing urban life. Congress borrowed the city's physical and social infrastructure while attempting something with no established local template. A state house became a national chamber. Printers became conduits for continental political communication. Inns and homes absorbed delegates and messengers. A city built for commerce and provincial government became the workshop of sovereignty.

The choice came with costs that the national mythology often distributes elsewhere.

War disrupted Atlantic trade and household economies. Occupation exposed civilians to requisitioning, fear, scarcity, and disease. Political suspicion could destroy old relationships. Loyalists faced confiscation and exile. Black Philadelphians had to navigate competing claims from political actors who spoke of liberty while maintaining or tolerating slavery. Enslaved people across the colonies recognized that war might create openings for escape, military service, negotiation, or emancipation, but those openings depended on geography, policy, and circumstance. Freedom was not delivered uniformly by either side.

For Native peoples, the new political project carried dangers that were already visible. British imperial policy had at times attempted to restrain settler expansion west of the Appalachians. Many colonists understood independence partly as freedom from those constraints. A nation claiming self-government for its settlers could intensify pressure on the sovereignty and land of nations already living across the continent. The Revolution's language of liberty and the republic's appetite for expansion were not separate stories waiting for later chapters to connect them.

They began together.

Philadelphia also illustrates how cities magnify political contradiction because people who are theoretically separated encounter one another in close quarters. Wealth and poverty, enslavement and free Black life, radical politics and commercial caution, cosmopolitan trade and local prejudice, religious conviction and secular republican argument all occupied the same urban space. National principles were debated by people whose daily lives revealed how partial those principles were.

That condition made the city productive. It also made it uncomfortable.

A founding story told only through consensus would not need a city. It could happen in a field beneath a clean sky. Philadelphia is useful because cities do not permit that simplification. Somebody printed the declaration. Somebody carried it. Somebody read it aloud. Somebody disagreed. Somebody sold supplies to the army. Somebody lost business because the port was disrupted. Somebody was enslaved in a household whose owner spoke about liberty. Somebody watched troops march down a street and had to decide what the flags meant for him or her personally.

National history becomes physical in those details.

There were other consequential cities in the 1770s. New York occupied a strategic harbor and became a major military objective. Charleston was central to the southern theater and to an economy built on slavery. Williamsburg mattered to Virginia's revolutionary politics. Baltimore grew as a commercial center. And Boston's claim remains formidable because the decade's first half cannot be understood without it.

But the decade's most important transition was from resistance to nationhood, and Philadelphia was where that transition acquired language, institutions, signatures, diplomatic intent, and a physical chamber.

The word “nationhood” can overstate what existed. The new states remained jealous of one another. The Confederation that followed would be weak. British forces still held territory. Independence was not secured until the peace settlement of 1783. Even after military victory, the United States would spend years confronting the fact that declaring a country and governing one are different achievements.

That is precisely why Philadelphia's importance should not be reduced to July 4.

The city mattered because it became a repeated meeting place for people trying to turn temporary cooperation into political structure. It was where the colonies learned to operate as something more than parallel rebellions. It held the imperfect machinery through which military command, foreign diplomacy, public argument, and national claims could be coordinated. The Declaration was the most famous product of that machinery, but not the only one.

By the end of the 1770s, Americans had not solved their fundamental institutional problem. They had made it harder to retreat from it. Independence required them to answer questions that imperial membership had once answered for them: Who could tax? Who could borrow? Who spoke for the states abroad? Who controlled western lands? Who paid soldiers? Who settled disputes among states? What did citizenship mean? How much coercive power could a republic possess without reproducing the tyranny it had rejected?

Those questions would dominate the next decade.

And when the first American political system began to prove inadequate, the country would return to the same city to argue again.

Philadelphia's first victory, then, was not that it produced a perfect founding. It was that it became the place where a collection of colonies could make a claim too large for any one of them to make alone. The city converted grievance into a national proposition.

The proposition was unfinished, compromised, exclusionary, and more durable than anyone in the room could know.

That is why the first address in this 250-year journey is Philadelphia.
