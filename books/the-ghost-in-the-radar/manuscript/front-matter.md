# The Ghost in the Radar

*PALLADIUM, Electronic Deception, and the Intelligence Value of Things That Aren't There*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

## A note on evidence

There is an easy way to ruin this subject. Find a secret program that made false radar targets, put it beside a modern unexplained radar event, and let adjacency perform the argument. I am not going to do that.

Four evidence classes govern this book.

**1. Verified, declassified, or otherwise documented facts.** Government records, official histories, contemporaneous technical material, archival documents, and peer-reviewed engineering literature. These establish that a program, instrument, technique, event, or physical principle existed. They do not establish every story later attached to it.

**2. Credible but incomplete reporting or testimony.** Named participants, engineers, military personnel, intelligence officers, journalists, and historians can supply information unavailable in the surviving official record. Their testimony matters. It is still testimony. Memory, access, institutional loyalty, retrospective interpretation, and the absence of surviving records all matter too.

**3. Disputed allegations.** Claims for which the public record does not provide independent confirmation. These remain claims even when the claimant held a clearance, wore a uniform, worked for an intelligence agency, or says the confirming documents remain classified.

**4. Speculative synthesis.** My argument about what the documented pieces may mean when placed together. This is where the book takes intellectual risk. It is also where the book must be easiest to attack.

PALLADIUM itself sits unusually close to the first two categories. The strongest public description comes from CIA technical history and retrospective accounts by engineer Gene Poteat. The exact operational chronology, equipment configurations, Cuban episodes, NSA feedback architecture, and downstream technical influence must therefore be scored claim by claim rather than inherited as one indivisible legend.

The rule for modern UAP material is stricter. A historical capability class is not a case explanation. Showing that an intelligence service could manufacture a radar target in the 1960s proves possibility, not attribution. A Navy track in 2004 or 2015 does not become PALLADIUM because PALLADIUM existed. It may not be deception at all.

That distinction is the book.
