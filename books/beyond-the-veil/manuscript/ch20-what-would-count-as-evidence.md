# What Would Count as Evidence

The most useful question in a speculative argument is not whether something can be true. Almost anything can survive that question. The better question is what observation would make us believe it more, and what observation would make us believe it less.

That is where *Beyond the Veil* has to end. The book began with a narrower possibility than its title might suggest: adaptive competence can exist without sapience, and some of its component capacities can exist without a brain. Earth gives us examples. The remaining scientific question is whether those capacities can be identified precisely enough that the comparison becomes useful beyond the examples that inspired it.

Astrobiology has been forced to think unusually hard about evidence because its most important claims are vulnerable to both excitement and scarcity. A possible biosignature may arrive before anyone can travel to the world that produced it. The data may be limited to light that crossed interstellar space. A sample from another planetary body may contain structures or chemistry with several plausible histories. A public announcement can become “life discovered” while the underlying paper says something much narrower.

NASA scientists and the broader astrobiology community have spent years discussing how confidence in life-detection evidence should accumulate. One proposed Confidence of Life Detection framework was explicitly offered as a starting point rather than a final law. The durable lesson is less tidy than a numbered scale: establish that the signal is real, identify what produced the measurement, investigate contamination and abiotic alternatives, ask whether biology could plausibly produce it in the observed environment, and seek independent lines of evidence. Critics are right that a confidence ladder can create false precision, and philosophers of astrobiology are right to worry about unconceived alternatives. The strongest hypothesis on the menu may still be wrong because the real explanation has not occurred to us. Those objections do not remove the need for standards. They tell us what a standard cannot promise.

Non-sapient intellect especially needs that restraint because the phrase has a publicity problem. A bacterium changing its movement according to recent chemistry is a beautiful result, but *intellect* can make it sound like a tiny philosopher. A regenerating tissue restoring anatomy is a remarkable control problem, but *goal* can imply an internal picture of the body it intends to build. A machine can find a winning move without giving us direct evidence about subjective experience. The evidence standard has to work against the seduction of the vocabulary.

Start with the boring requirement: there has to be a reproducible phenomenon, described at the right level. If experience is said to change later behavior, repeat the experience and measure the later behavior. If a system is said to correct toward a target state, perturb that state in more than one way and measure recovery. If components are said to coordinate through a signal, interrupt the proposed channel and observe what breaks. If a material or organism is said to store information, identify a physical state whose manipulation changes a later response.

The level of description matters. “The slime mold made something efficient-looking again” is weaker than reproducing how network cost, redundancy, and route choice change when the environment changes. A microorganism showing less response after repeated exposure is less informative than evidence that the change is stimulus-specific, recovers after time, and cannot be explained by exhaustion or damage. An alien atmosphere varying after a stellar disturbance would become more interesting if repeated observations tied the forcing, the system’s history, and its recovery trajectory together in ways competing abiotic models handled poorly. A standard earns its keep by saying which part of the phenomenon must recur.

History dependence is the next hurdle, but history alone is cheap. A canyon records erosion. A crater records impact. A warmed ocean retains heat. A shape-memory alloy responds differently because of what happened to it before. None of these facts by itself is evidence of non-sapient intellect. The relevant question is whether retained state participates in adaptive control.

Bacterial chemotaxis passes a modest version of that test because molecular adaptation lets recent chemical conditions affect movement. Immunological memory passes a different version because prior exposure changes later response. Habituation in *Physarum*, where robustly demonstrated, matters because repeated exposure to an innocuous aversive condition changes subsequent behavior in ways researchers can test for specificity, persistence, and recovery. In each case the useful questions are physical: what variable carries the past forward, how long does it persist, does manipulating it alter the later response, can it be overwritten, and is it doing work for the system rather than merely leaving a trace for an observer to read? That is how “memory” stops being atmosphere and becomes mechanism.

Perturbation is even more revealing. A system becomes interesting when disturbance does not merely displace it but recruits different responses according to what has gone wrong. A pendulum returns toward equilibrium. A hot object cools. Elastic material can return toward its prior shape. Lawful restoration is common. Biological correction can be different because the response depends on what was damaged, where it happened, what resources remain, what developmental state the organism occupies, and which alternative routes are still available.

A planarian fragment missing a head does not simply relax toward a lower-energy state called “head.” Cells proliferate, differentiate, signal, change gene expression, and coordinate in a location-dependent process. Block one pathway and different errors appear; change electrical or molecular conditions and patterning can change. The evidence for adaptive correction grows when multiple perturbations reveal conditional responses rather than one fixed rebound.

This is where intervention becomes more informative than admiration. Block a route, move the nutrients, remove part of the network, reverse a gradient, alter the timing, interrupt a communication channel, repeat the challenge. Different domains require different interventions, but the logic travels. A tissue can be cut. A microbial network can be chemically altered. A machine can lose a sensor. An ecology can experience drought. A distant planet may offer only the disturbances nature happens to provide. There is no single “intelligence test” hiding here, only a family of ways to separate conditional control from a passive trajectory.

The same discipline applies to information. The word is easy to abuse because almost anything can contain information about something else. A tree ring contains information about past growth conditions; a spectrum contains information about its source; a fossil contains information about an organism. The stronger question is whether a physical state is available to the system and changes what the system does.

In bacterial chemotaxis, receptor modification participates in temporal comparison. In immune memory, changed populations and molecular states participate in altered future response. In bacterial biofilms, electrical and chemical signaling can causally influence activity across separated cells. Engineered systems use stored parameters, sensor readings, internal state, and memory to alter later policy. The substrates differ radically, but a causal middle remains necessary. If we cannot say how retained state reaches the response, we may have an interesting correlation; we have not yet earned memory-like control.

Astronomy makes that requirement harder without making it meaningless. A telescope may never identify the molecule carrying memory inside an alien organism, but a planetary-scale claim still needs a plausible pathway. If atmospheric response is the proposed output, models should identify which biological or geochemical fluxes could produce it, how quickly those fluxes can change, what energy and material budgets they require, and which environmental signals could plausibly drive the change. “The atmosphere changed, therefore the biosphere adapted” skips the part that matters.

Performance also has to be attached to an explicit problem rather than our aesthetic sense of cleverness. The Tokyo slime-mold experiment is useful because network length, transport efficiency, redundancy, and response to the layout can be compared. The network does not merely look ingenious. Its performance can be measured under stated costs.

That habit shrinks grand interpretations, which is usually healthy. A bacterium can be evaluated by movement in chemical gradients. A learning experiment can ask whether exposure changes a later response. Regeneration can be measured against anatomical recovery. A controller can be tested against faults. “Survival” is too vague to rescue every response after the fact; natural selection does not turn every short-term behavior into evidence of a global objective. Use the narrowest performance measure the experiment can support.

Flexibility matters in proportion to the strength of the claim. Specialization is not failure. The immune system does not need to play chess, and a bacterium does not need to solve a rodent maze. But if a response is called learning, it matters whether the change is confined to one stimulus or transfers to related conditions. If a system is called problem-solving, altered geometry matters. If a controller is called adaptive, a blocked familiar route is informative. Generality is not required for every form of non-sapient intellect; it tells us how broad the competence is.

This is one reason profiles are more useful than rankings. We can ask how long state persists, which perturbations a system can handle, how far a learned change transfers, how many components coordinate, how quickly performance degrades outside a familiar regime, and what the energetic cost is. A profile does not demote a specialized system merely because it is unlike a person, and it does not let one narrow success inflate into general intelligence.

Then come the alternatives. For extraterrestrial use, this may be the hardest part. Oxygen can accumulate without biology under some planetary histories. Atmospheric disequilibrium can have several causes. Materials can show memory-like hysteresis. Physical networks can optimize flows without learning. Chemical reaction systems can oscillate, self-organize, and preserve state. A machine can produce goal-directed behavior because engineers supplied the objective. These are not annoyances to clear away before the exciting interpretation begins. They are the experiment.

The phrase *unconceived alternatives* should remain uncomfortable. Even after every known false positive has been tested, an unknown one may remain, especially when an observation is unprecedented. Responsible language should expose that uncertainty rather than hide it. Say which nonbiological explanations were modeled and what their assumptions were. Say which fatigue, damage, sensitization, or fixed-response explanations were tested before calling a behavioral change learning. The grammar gets less exciting and the science gets stronger.

That is also why a serious detection is unlikely to arrive as one decisive measurement. Confidence should converge across dimensions: a spectrum, temporal behavior, environmental context, independent instrumentation, a later predicted change, perhaps another consequence expected under the same hypothesis. The claim strengthens when several observations become difficult to explain together under the alternatives, not because one signal acquires a louder adjective.

Prediction is the useful cruelty. A good explanation should risk being wrong about what happens next. If a microbial community coordinates through a particular channel, interrupting that channel should produce a specific failure. If apparent material memory is simple hysteresis, a different loading history should produce a predicted loop. If atmospheric variation reflects passive photochemistry, changed stellar forcing should propagate on the model’s timescales. If an adaptive biosphere contributes to the response, the living model eventually has to predict something the abiotic model does not. Seductive stories are plentiful after the fact. An appointment with the future is harder.

The remote astronomical case therefore deserves a higher bar than the terrestrial laboratory case. On Earth we can manipulate a biofilm, cut a planarian, alter ion channels, expose an organism repeatedly to a stimulus, move nutrients, remove components, and repeat the experiment. Interstellar astronomy usually has to wait for nature to perform the intervention. A stellar flare, seasonal cycle, volcanic episode, or changing orbital geometry may provide the forcing. The challenge is to distinguish adaptive living organization from geophysical and chemical systems that also possess memory and nonlinear dynamics.

A remote signature of non-sapient intellect would consequently require more than evidence of life. Some living system’s prior state or coordinated response would have to affect a later planetary-scale observable in a way passive models handled poorly. That is an extraordinarily demanding standard and may be unattainable with foreseeable telescopes. There is no virtue in inventing a detectable signature because the thesis would be more exciting if one existed.

The astronomical contribution may instead be a mission question. Suppose a future probe reaches an ocean world, an exoplanet environment, or another site with convincing evidence of life. After chemistry, metabolism, morphology, reproduction, ecology, and evolution, what should it ask? A non-sapient-intellect program would add temporal and perturbational questions: what changes after exposure, what state persists, whether damage is repaired, whether blockage can be routed around, whether separated components coordinate, whether a second perturbation differs because of the first, which physical state carries that difference, and what happens when the state is disrupted. These are questions about control, not alien consciousness.

The order matters. Establish that the object or process is not contamination or instrument error. Establish that the chemistry or structure is plausibly biological. Characterize response over time. Perturb when ethically and technically appropriate. Identify retained state and communication pathways. Only then should a comparative label such as non-sapient intellect enter the conversation. The label should summarize evidence rather than substitute for it.

Competence also does not settle moral status. A system could have sophisticated adaptive control and no subjective experience. A system might have subjective experience without displaying the general problem solving humans reward on tests. We do not know enough about consciousness to use non-sapient intellect as a proxy. A claim of consciousness needs its own arguments; personhood requires more; technological intelligence requires different evidence again. The categories should not ride on one another for free.

Which returns to the title. Why use *intellect* at all instead of regulation, learning, memory, control, adaptation, and basal cognition? Within their home disciplines, those terms are better. The cross-cutting term earns its keep only if comparing mechanisms reveals a useful higher-order structure: retained state alters future action, perturbations reveal selective correction, information moves across components, performance changes under experience or constraint, and control persists without a centralized conscious planner.

If those dimensions do not prove useful, the category should be retired. Future work may show that the similarities emphasized here are superficial, that *non-sapient intellect* collapses into a loose collection of homeostasis, learning, distributed control, and self-organization better studied separately, or that the astronomical extension is observationally empty. Some terrestrial examples may fail replication or receive simpler mechanistic explanations. A book should be allowed to contain the conditions under which its title becomes unnecessary. Otherwise the thesis has become a brand.

The more interesting outcome would be a common perturbation language across fields. A microbial community, regenerating tissue, soft material, autonomous machine, and alien sample could be described in terms of state retention, feedback, recovery, flexibility, transfer, coordination, and energy cost without pretending they are the same kind of thing. The profiles might cluster or separate. The word would have become a comparative map rather than a compliment.

The strongest evidence would come from elsewhere. A world would first present convincing evidence of life. Repeated observation or direct experimentation would then reveal history-dependent adaptive behavior not reducible to the abiotic environment alone: retained state changing later response, correction under perturbation, coordination through physically detectable channels, and competing explanations failing across independent tests. No voice, artifact, civilization, or proof of consciousness would be required. We would have evidence of adaptive competence organized by another evolutionary history.

Until then, the responsible position is narrower. Earth demonstrates that memory-like state, feedback control, distributed coordination, repair, learning-like behavioral change, and optimization-like performance can exist outside sapient minds. Those phenomena justify a comparative scientific question. They do not justify a cosmic conclusion. The distance between those claims is where the work belongs.

I began with a slime mold and a railway map because the image creates an immediate temptation: the organism appears to have solved something. Twenty chapters later, *appears* matters more than it did at the beginning. We have learned to ask what changed, which constraints were present, which alternatives remain, and what the experiment did not show. That habit is more valuable than the original surprise.

The same discipline should meet any future signal from the universe. A strange pattern earns another measurement. Repetition earns a model. A model should survive prediction and, where possible, intervention. Favored explanations need competitors. Claims about life need independent evidence; claims about adaptive control need more than life alone; consciousness should never be smuggled in by grammar.

The first mistake was assuming intelligence had to know that it was intelligent. The second would be assuming that, once we corrected the first mistake, everything interesting became intelligence.

The universe deserves a detector built to survive both errors.
