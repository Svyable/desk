# What Would Count as Evidence

The most useful question in a speculative argument is not, “Can this be true?”

Almost anything can survive that question.

The better question is, “What observation would make us believe it more, and what observation would make us believe it less?”

That is where *Beyond the Veil* has to end.

Not with the announcement of a new intelligence hidden throughout the cosmos.

Not with a declaration that life is smarter than science realized.

Not with a poetic expansion of mind until planets, storms, cells, machines, and minerals all disappear into one warm category.

The book began by proposing a narrower possibility: adaptive competence can exist without sapience, and some of its component capacities can exist without a brain. Earth gives us examples. The remaining scientific question is whether those capacities can be identified with enough precision that the concept becomes useful beyond the examples that inspired it.

That requires an evidence standard.

Astrobiology has been forced to think unusually hard about standards because its most important claims are vulnerable to both excitement and scarcity.

A possible biosignature may arrive before anyone can travel to the world that produced it. The data may be limited to light that crossed interstellar space. A sample from another planetary body may contain structures or chemistry with several plausible histories. A single announcement can be heard by the public as “life discovered” even when the underlying paper says something much narrower.

NASA scientists and the broader astrobiology community have spent years discussing how confidence in life-detection evidence should accumulate. One proposed Confidence of Life Detection framework was explicitly offered as a starting point rather than a final law. The larger lesson is more durable than any numbered scale: establish that the signal is real, identify what produced the measurement, investigate contamination and abiotic alternatives, ask whether biology could plausibly produce it in the observed environment, and seek independent lines of evidence.

Even that approach has critics.

A confidence ladder can create false precision. Public communication can turn a provisional level into a verdict. Philosophers of astrobiology have emphasized the problem of unconceived alternatives: the strongest hypothesis in the menu may still be wrong because the real explanation has not occurred to us.

These objections do not weaken the need for standards.

They clarify what a standard can and cannot do.

A framework cannot guarantee truth.

It can make overstatement harder.

That is exactly what non-sapient intellect needs.

The phrase has a built-in publicity problem. It sounds larger than many of the phenomena it is meant to describe. A bacterium changing its movement according to recent chemistry is a beautiful result, but *intellect* can make the result sound like a tiny philosopher. A regenerating tissue restoring anatomy is a remarkable control problem, but *goal* can make it sound as though the tissue contains an image of the body it intends to build. A machine finding a winning move can be genuinely competent while giving us no direct evidence about subjective experience.

The standard must therefore run against the seduction of the term.

The first requirement is almost boring: there has to be a reproducible phenomenon.

A striking anecdote is not enough.

A photograph that resembles a network is not enough.

A single organism doing something surprising is not enough.

A one-time correlation is not enough.

If the claim is that experience changes later behavior, repeat the experience and measure the later behavior. If the claim is that a system corrects toward a target state, perturb the state in more than one way and measure recovery. If the claim is that components coordinate through a signal, interrupt the proposed channel and observe what breaks. If the claim is that a material or organism stores information, identify a physical state whose manipulation changes later response.

This sounds obvious because it is science before it is astrobiology.

The novelty is not in the rule.

It is in applying the same rule to phenomena we are tempted to interpret differently because of how much they resemble us.

Reproducibility also needs the right level of description.

Suppose a slime mold sometimes creates an efficient path. Reproducing “something efficient-looking happened” is weaker than reproducing how network cost, redundancy, and route choice change when the environment changes.

Suppose a microorganism reduces its response after repeated exposure. Reproducing reduced response is weaker than showing that the change is stimulus-specific, recovers after time, and cannot be explained by exhaustion or damage.

Suppose an alien atmosphere varies after a stellar disturbance. Reproducing the variation is weaker than showing a repeatable relationship between the forcing, the history of the system, and a recovery trajectory that competing abiotic models fail to explain.

A standard becomes useful when it says which part of the phenomenon must recur.

The second requirement is history dependence that does causal work.

Everything has a past.

A canyon contains a record of erosion. A crater contains a record of impact. A warmed ocean retains heat. A compressed spring contains stored energy. None of these facts by itself is evidence of non-sapient intellect.

The relevant question is whether prior state changes later behavior in a way that participates in adaptive control.

Bacterial chemotaxis passes a modest version of this test because molecular adaptation lets recent chemical conditions affect movement. Immunological memory passes a different version because prior exposure changes later response. Habituation in *Physarum*, where robustly demonstrated, passes because repeated exposure to an innocuous aversive condition changes subsequent behavior and the effect has properties researchers can test for specificity, persistence, and recovery.

A shape-memory alloy also has history-dependent response.

That is why history dependence cannot stand alone.

The counterexample is useful. It prevents the definition from becoming a synonym for hysteresis.

A good evidence program therefore asks not simply whether the past matters, but how it matters.

What physical variable carries the past forward?

How long does it persist?

Does manipulating that variable alter future response?

Is the retained state relevant to a functional challenge, or is it merely a trace an outside observer can read?

Does the system weight recent and older history differently?

Can the stored state be overwritten?

These questions turn “memory” from atmosphere into mechanism.

The third requirement is selective correction under perturbation.

A system becomes more interesting when a disturbance does not merely displace it but recruits responses that tend to preserve or restore some functional organization.

The word *selective* matters.

A pendulum returns toward equilibrium after displacement. A hot object cools toward ambient temperature. A stretched elastic material returns toward its prior shape. These systems exhibit lawful restoration without adaptive control.

Biological correction can be different because the response depends on what was damaged, where the damage occurred, what resources are available, what developmental state the organism occupies, and what alternative routes remain possible.

A planarian fragment missing a head does not simply relax toward a lower-energy state called “head.” Cells proliferate, differentiate, signal, change gene expression, and coordinate in a location-dependent process. Block one pathway and different errors appear. Change electrical or molecular conditions and patterning can change.

The evidence for adaptive correction grows when multiple perturbations reveal conditional responses rather than one fixed rebound.

This is the point at which intervention becomes more informative than admiration.

Do not ask whether the system looks purposeful.

Ask what happens when purpose-looking behavior is made difficult.

Block the shortest path.

Change the nutrient distribution.

Remove part of the network.

Reverse a gradient.

Alter the timing.

Interrupt the communication channel.

Repeat the challenge.

The system’s response under failure tells us more than its performance under ideal conditions.

A publication-friendly version of the argument would be tempted to call this “the intelligence test.” It is not one test. It is a family of interventions designed to separate a conditional controller from a passive trajectory.

The distinction matters because every domain will require different perturbations. A tissue can be cut. A microbial network can be chemically altered. A machine can lose a sensor. An ecology can experience drought. A planet may only offer naturally occurring disturbances that telescopes happen to observe.

The logic travels farther than the apparatus.

The fourth requirement is a physically plausible information pathway.

*Information* is one of the easiest words to abuse because everything in science can be described as containing information about something else.

A tree ring contains information about past growth conditions. A spectrum contains information about the source that produced it. A fossil contains information about an organism. That does not mean the tree ring, spectrum, or fossil is processing information for its own purposes.

For adaptive competence, the stronger question is whether a physical state is available to the system and changes what the system does.

In bacterial chemotaxis, receptor modification participates in temporal comparison.

In immune memory, changed populations and molecular states participate in altered future response.

In bacterial biofilms, electrical and chemical signaling can causally influence activity across separated cells.

In engineered systems, stored parameters, sensor readings, internal state, and memory can influence later policy.

The substrate can differ radically.

The causal requirement should not.

If we cannot say how the retained state reaches the response, we may still have an interesting correlation, but we should not promote it into evidence of memory-like control yet.

At extraterrestrial scales this requirement becomes difficult but not meaningless.

A telescope may never identify the molecule carrying memory inside an alien organism. It may still constrain pathways at larger scales. If atmospheric response is the claimed output, models should identify which biological or geochemical fluxes could produce it, how quickly those fluxes can change, what energy and material budgets they require, and which environmental signals could plausibly drive the change.

The information pathway can be modeled at the scale we can observe even when the microscopic implementation is unknown.

A claim that skips that causal middle and jumps directly from “the atmosphere changed” to “the biosphere adapted” has not earned the word.

The fifth requirement is performance relative to an explicit problem, not to our aesthetic sense of cleverness.

The Tokyo slime-mold experiment is powerful partly because the comparison can be stated quantitatively. Researchers can measure network length, transport efficiency, redundancy, and response to the layout. The network does not merely look beautiful. It can be compared against alternative networks under specified costs.

This is a general lesson.

A system is not adaptive because it surprises us.

Adaptive to what?

What counts as success?

Under which constraints?

Compared with what baseline?

A bacterium’s behavior can be evaluated by movement in chemical gradients. A learning experiment can measure whether repeated exposure changes a later response. A regeneration experiment can measure anatomical recovery. A controller can be tested against faults. A collective can be tested under altered connectivity.

The moment the performance criterion becomes explicit, several grand interpretations usually shrink.

That is good.

A precise small claim is worth more than an atmospheric large one.

The criterion also prevents survival from becoming a universal excuse.

It is easy to say that every organism is “trying to survive.” Natural selection does favor traits that contribute to reproductive success in particular environments, but that does not mean every short-term response should be evaluated against a vague global goal called survival. A bacterium moving up an attractant gradient has a nearer measurable function. A planarian regenerating a head has a morphological outcome. An immune response can be measured against pathogen control and physiological cost.

Use the narrowest performance measure the experiment can actually support.

The sixth requirement is transfer or flexibility when the claim implies more than a fixed adaptation.

This criterion has to be used carefully because specialization is not failure. Many evolved systems are exceptionally competent within narrow domains. The immune system does not need to play chess. A bacterium does not need to solve mazes designed for rodents.

But the stronger our language becomes, the more flexibility matters.

If we call a response learning, does it change only for one stimulus or transfer to related conditions?

If we call a system problem-solving, can it handle altered geometry or only replay a pattern selected by evolution for one environment?

If we call a controller adaptive, can it find a new route when the familiar route fails?

If we call a planetary pattern evidence of regulation, does the recovery trajectory change appropriately under different disturbances?

Generality is not required for every form of non-sapient intellect.

It is evidence about how broad the competence is.

This suggests a useful way to report claims: as profiles rather than rankings.

How much history does the system retain?

How many perturbation types can it handle?

How far does a learned change transfer?

How many components coordinate?

How quickly does performance degrade outside the familiar regime?

What is the energetic cost?

A profile keeps a specialized system from being called “less intelligent” simply because it is unlike a person, while also preventing a narrow success from being marketed as general intelligence.

The seventh requirement is the serious construction of abiotic and noncognitive alternatives.

This may be the most important requirement for extraterrestrial use.

Oxygen can accumulate without biology under some planetary histories.

Atmospheric disequilibrium can have several causes.

Materials can show memory-like hysteresis.

Physical networks can optimize flows without learning.

Chemical reaction systems can oscillate, self-organize, and preserve state.

A machine can produce goal-directed behavior because engineers built the objective into it.

None of these alternatives is an annoyance to be cleared away before the exciting interpretation begins.

They are the experiment.

A claim earns confidence by surviving competitors.

The phrase *unconceived alternatives* should remain uncomfortable. Even after every known false positive has been tested, an unknown one may remain. This is especially important when the observation is unprecedented. The more unlike our training data a world is, the less confident we should be that our catalog of alternatives is complete.

This changes how evidence should be communicated.

Instead of saying, “We have ruled out nonbiological explanations,” say which explanations were modeled and what assumptions those models require.

Instead of saying, “Only life can do this,” say which known abiotic processes fail under the observed conditions and where the uncertainty remains.

Instead of saying, “The system learned,” say what changed with experience and which fatigue, damage, sensitization, or fixed-response explanations were tested.

The grammar becomes less exciting.

The science becomes stronger.

This is one reason a detection of life, intelligence, or non-sapient intellect is unlikely to arrive responsibly as a single decisive measurement.

Evidence should converge across independent dimensions.

A spectrum suggests one thing.

Temporal behavior suggests another.

Environmental context eliminates some alternatives.

A second instrument confirms the signal.

A later observation shows a predicted change.

A different measurement detects a consequence expected under the same hypothesis.

The claim becomes stronger not because one measurement grows louder, but because several measurements become harder to explain together under the alternatives.

That leads to the eighth requirement: prediction before celebration.

A good explanation should risk being wrong about the next observation.

If we think a microbial community is coordinating through a particular channel, interrupting the channel should produce a specific failure.

If we think a material’s apparent memory is simple hysteresis, changing the loading history should produce a predicted loop.

If we think an atmosphere’s variation reflects a passive photochemical cycle, a change in stellar forcing should propagate according to the model’s timescales.

If we think an adaptive biosphere contributes to the response, the living model should predict some feature the abiotic model does not.

Prediction disciplines interpretation because it forces the hypothesis to leave the observation that inspired it.

Many seductive stories are excellent at explaining what already happened.

Fewer survive an appointment with the future.

For non-sapient intellect, the remote astronomical case must be held to a higher bar than the terrestrial laboratory case.

On Earth, we can manipulate a biofilm.

We can cut a planarian.

We can alter ion channels.

We can expose an organism repeatedly to a stimulus.

We can watch a slime mold explore a maze.

We can knock out genes, change temperatures, move nutrients, remove components, and repeat the experiment.

Interstellar astronomy rarely gives us that luxury.

We cannot perturb an exoplanet on demand.

We have to wait for nature to perform the intervention.

A stellar flare may alter atmospheric chemistry.

A seasonal cycle may provide repeated forcing.

A volcanic episode may produce a change.

Orbital geometry may shift observable conditions.

The challenge is to distinguish a response that indicates adaptive living organization from geophysical and chemical systems that also possess memory and nonlinear dynamics.

This is where the hypothesis becomes difficult enough to be scientific.

A remote signature of non-sapient intellect would need more than evidence of life.

It would need evidence that some living system’s prior state or coordinated response is affecting a later planetary-scale observable in a way that passive models handle poorly.

That is an extraordinarily demanding standard.

It may be unattainable with foreseeable telescopes.

The book should say so plainly.

There is no virtue in inventing a detectable signature merely because the thesis would be more exciting if one existed.

The astronomical contribution may instead be a mission question.

Suppose a future probe reaches an ocean world, an exoplanet, or another environment with convincing evidence of life. What should it measure after the first biosignature?

The ordinary next questions will concern chemistry, metabolism, morphology, reproduction, ecology, evolution, and perhaps technology.

A non-sapient-intellect program would add temporal and perturbational questions.

What changes after exposure?

What state persists?

Does the system recover from damage?

Can it reroute around blockage?

Do separated components coordinate?

Does a second perturbation produce the same response as the first?

Does prior experience alter the second response?

Which physical state carries the change?

What happens when that state is disrupted?

These are not questions about alien consciousness.

They are questions about control.

A robotic mission could, in principle, ask some of them without ever trying to decide whether the organism has an inner life.

The evidence hierarchy on such a mission would matter.

First establish that the object or process is not contamination or instrument error.

Then establish that the chemistry or structure is plausibly biological.

Then characterize response over time.

Then perturb when ethically and technically appropriate.

Then identify retained state and communication pathways.

Only after those steps should a comparative label such as non-sapient intellect enter the conversation.

The label should summarize evidence, not substitute for it.

This distinction matters ethically as well as scientifically.

Competence does not settle moral status.

A system could have sophisticated adaptive control and no subjective experience. A system could perhaps have subjective experience without displaying the kind of general problem solving humans reward on tests. We do not know enough about consciousness to use non-sapient intellect as a proxy.

The evidence standard therefore stops before phenomenology.

That boundary is intentional.

A claim of consciousness would require its own arguments.

A claim of personhood would require more.

A claim of technological intelligence would require different evidence again.

The categories should not ride on one another for free.

This is also why the book’s title needs defending one last time.

Why use *intellect* at all?

Why not stay with regulation, learning, memory, control, adaptation, and basal cognition?

Those terms are better within their home disciplines.

The reason for a cross-cutting term is comparative.

We want to ask whether several mechanisms share a higher-order structure: retained state alters future action; perturbations reveal selective correction; information moves across components; performance changes under experience or constraint; control persists without a centralized conscious planner.

If those dimensions prove useful across biological and engineered systems, the category earns some scientific utility.

If they do not, the category should be retired.

That is a real possibility.

Perhaps future work will show that the similarities emphasized here are superficial.

Perhaps *non-sapient intellect* will collapse into a loose collection of homeostasis, learning, distributed control, and self-organization, each better studied separately.

Perhaps the astronomical extension will prove observationally empty.

Perhaps some of the strongest terrestrial examples will fail replication or receive simpler mechanistic explanations.

A book should be allowed to contain the conditions under which its title becomes unnecessary.

That is not weakness.

It is the difference between a thesis and a brand.

The positive outcome is more interesting precisely because failure is available.

Imagine that future researchers develop a common perturbation language across fields.

They measure response profiles rather than trading metaphors.

A microbial community, regenerating tissue, soft material, autonomous machine, and alien sample can all be described in terms of state retention, feedback, recovery, flexibility, transfer, coordination, and energy cost.

The profiles differ.

Some systems cluster.

Others separate.

The category stops being a word and becomes a comparative map.

That would be evidence that the book pointed toward something real.

The strongest version would go farther.

A world elsewhere would present convincing evidence of life.

Repeated observation or direct experimentation would then reveal history-dependent adaptive behavior not reducible to the abiotic environment alone. The system would preserve state, alter later response, correct under perturbation, and coordinate through physically detectable channels. Competing explanations would fail across independent tests.

No voice would be required.

No artifact.

No civilization.

No proof of consciousness.

We would have evidence of adaptive competence organized by another evolutionary history.

At that moment, the most surprising fact might not be that the universe contains another intellect.

It might be that the intellect had been there before anything on that world became the kind of being we were trained to search for.

Until then, the responsible position is narrower.

Earth demonstrates that memory-like state, feedback control, distributed coordination, repair, learning-like behavioral change, and optimization-like performance can exist outside sapient minds.

Those phenomena justify a comparative scientific question.

They do not justify a cosmic conclusion.

The distance between those two sentences is where the work belongs.

I began this book with a slime mold and a railway map because the image creates an immediate temptation.

The organism appears to have solved something.

Twenty chapters later, the word *appears* matters more than it did at the beginning.

We have learned to ask what the organism actually did.

What state changed.

What constraints were present.

What alternatives exist.

What the experiment did not show.

That habit is more valuable than the original surprise.

The same discipline should meet any future signal from the universe.

We should be difficult to impress in the right way.

Not cynical.

Not incurious.

Difficult.

A strange pattern should earn another measurement.

A repeated pattern should earn a model.

A model should earn an intervention where intervention is possible.

A favored explanation should earn competitors.

A life claim should earn independent evidence.

An intelligence claim should earn evidence about adaptive control beyond life alone.

A consciousness claim should not be smuggled in by grammar.

The veil does not lift all at once.

We replace one kind of ignorance with a narrower kind.

That is what progress often looks like.

The first mistake was assuming intelligence had to know that it was intelligent.

The second would be assuming that, once we corrected the first mistake, everything interesting became intelligence.

The universe deserves a better detector than either assumption.

So do we.
