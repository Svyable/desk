# The Civilization Bias

In 1959, Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison published a short paper with an audaciously practical title: “Searching for Interstellar Communications.”

The word *communications* mattered.

They were not proposing a general instrument for detecting every form of intelligence in the universe.

They were asking whether another technological society might use radio in a way we could detect.

That was a narrower problem.

Narrow problems are often how science begins.

Radio had several advantages. The physics was understood. Interstellar space is transparent across useful microwave frequencies. Radio astronomy had already produced instruments sensitive enough to make the question experimental. A sufficiently powerful or deliberately directed signal could stand out from many natural sources because narrowband emission could be conspicuous.

The wager was not that intelligence equals radio.

The wager was that some intelligence might build radios.

One year later, Frank Drake turned the wager into an observing program at Green Bank, West Virginia.

Project Ozma pointed an 85-foot radio telescope toward Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani, two nearby Sun-like stars, and listened around the 21-centimeter hydrogen line. The search was modest enough to sound almost quaint now: two stars, one small slice of the radio spectrum, a receiver slowly scanning a narrow range.

It found no extraterrestrial signal.

It did something more durable.

It established a style of question.

Choose an artifact that technology might produce.

Define the part of parameter space where the artifact would be detectable.

Build or adapt an instrument.

Look.

Do not ask the sky whether anybody is intelligent.

Ask whether a specified signal is present above a specified threshold under specified conditions.

This is excellent science.

It also creates a selection effect.

The instrument sees what the hypothesis makes visible.

Modern SETI inherited that bargain.

The search became associated, in public culture especially, with communicative civilizations because communication is one of the cleanest technological consequences a distant observer can hope to detect. A radio transmitter can cross light-years. A microbial mat cannot announce itself at 1420 megahertz. A slime mold can solve a maze and remain astronomically silent.

This is the civilization bias.

The phrase is not an accusation against SETI researchers.

It is a description of an observational filter.

A radio survey is biased toward systems that produce radio emissions detectable by the survey.

An optical laser search is biased toward systems producing detectable optical pulses or continuous emission.

A search for artificial atmospheric pollution is biased toward worlds whose technology produces molecules we can distinguish spectroscopically.

A search for megastructures is biased toward construction on a scale that alters starlight.

Every detector has a philosophy hidden inside its engineering.

Not a metaphysical philosophy.

A practical one.

This phenomenon would count.

That one would not.

Project Ozma’s philosophy was explicit.

A sufficiently capable society might communicate using radio.

The search did not need to know whether the society was conscious in a human way, democratic, biological, embodied, peaceful, expansionist, or curious.

It needed the society to make a detectable transmitter.

That is one reason the approach was so powerful.

It discarded almost everything cultural and psychological.

A signal either entered the receiver or it did not.

Shannon would have appreciated the move.

Meaning could be left outside the engineering layer.

The cost of that abstraction is easy to miss.

A civilization that never transmits in our direction is absent from the experiment.

A civilization that uses another frequency is absent.

A civilization whose signals are too weak is absent.

A civilization that existed a million years before the light reaching us today is absent.

A planet covered in organisms that sense, remember, repair, coordinate, and adapt but never build technology is absent by definition.

The experiment can be perfect and the target class still be narrow.

This is not a defect.

It is scope.

The mistake arrives later, when scope becomes ontology.

A search for communicative civilizations becomes “the search for intelligence.”

A null result becomes “the silence of the universe.”

A technological signature becomes the only form of intelligence worth naming.

Those translations are rhetorically smooth and scientifically expensive.

The history of the Drake equation shows how early the technological threshold entered the field.

Drake devised the equation in 1961 as an agenda for a Green Bank meeting about the prospects for detecting extraterrestrial communication. Its later terms ask, in effect, how often life develops intelligence, how often intelligent life develops detectable communication technology, and how long such civilizations remain detectable.

The equation is not wrong because it has a technological term.

That term is the point.

It estimates a target population relevant to SETI.

But notice what falls out of the multiplication.

A planet with life that never builds a transmitter contributes nothing to the final count.

A planet with elaborate non-neural cognition but no technology contributes nothing.

A biosphere with organisms capable of adaptive planning but no detectable emissions contributes nothing.

The equation is about communicative civilization, not a census of competence.

That distinction became easier to blur because the word *intelligence* carried cultural prestige.

“Search for extraterrestrial intelligence” is grander than “search for detectable products of extraterrestrial technology.”

The shorter phrase won.

The instruments remained more precise.

This is one of those cases where the scientific machinery can be more disciplined than the public language surrounding it.

Radio SETI does not literally search for a mental property.

It searches for electromagnetic patterns difficult to explain without technology.

Technosignatures made this logic explicit.

The word broadens the target beyond intentional messages.

A civilization might leak radio.

It might fire powerful radar.

It might use lasers.

Its industry might alter atmospheric chemistry.

Its propulsion systems might produce unusual emissions.

Large engineered structures could alter transit light curves.

Waste heat could change infrared spectra.

None of these requires the civilization to be trying to contact us.

The signal can be a by-product.

That is an important conceptual expansion.

Classical communication SETI asks: is somebody calling?

Technosignature research can ask: did anybody build something whose consequences reached us?

NASA’s renewed attention to technosignatures after its 2018 workshop reflects this broader landscape. Radio remains important, but current technosignature discussions include optical signals, artificial structures, atmospheric pollutants, propulsion signatures, and other evidence of advanced technology.

The field has therefore already begun escaping the narrowest version of its own civilization bias.

But it has not escaped technology.

It should not.

Technosignatures are signatures of technology.

The word does useful work by keeping the inference bounded.

A narrowband radio signal with characteristics that natural astrophysics cannot easily generate would be interesting because it suggests engineering.

A repeating optical pulse could be interesting for the same reason.

An industrial molecule in an exoplanet atmosphere could be interesting because its production might imply processes beyond known geochemistry or biology.

The inference goes from observable to technology.

Only after that would we ask what kind of beings built the technology.

This is cleaner than pretending the telescope measured intelligence directly.

It also reveals the missing middle.

Biosignature searches sit below technology.

They ask whether life has altered a world in detectable ways.

Technosignature searches sit above ordinary life.

They ask whether technology has altered signals in detectable ways.

Between them sits an enormous space of living organization.

Earth spent almost all of its biological history there.

For billions of years, this planet was inhabited and technologically silent at interstellar distances.

Microbes transformed the atmosphere.

Cells invented photosynthesis, respiration, multicellularity, immune systems, nervous systems, learning, cooperation, predation, development, regeneration, and ecological engineering.

None of this produced a radio beacon.

The fact is so obvious that its implication is easy to overlook.

Life can become astonishingly competent without becoming remotely visible as technology.

Even on a planet that eventually produces radio telescopes, the technological interval can be a thin sliver of total biological time.

NASA’s own current technosignature material makes this asymmetry explicit: a world may support life for billions of years before producing technology detectable across interstellar distance.

This is not an argument that technological civilizations are rare.

It is an argument that they are a subset.

A search aimed at the subset cannot tell us how common the larger set is unless we know the conversion rate.

We do not.

The civilization bias is therefore partly temporal.

Technosignatures privilege a phase.

A biosphere has to cross many contingent transitions before entering it.

Complex cells.

Multicellularity, perhaps.

Large nervous systems, perhaps.

Manipulative anatomy, perhaps.

Social learning.

Cumulative culture.

Energy capture.

Materials processing.

Science.

Engineering.

A motive and capacity to produce detectable artifacts.

None of these is guaranteed by the existence of adaptive competence.

Evolution has no requirement to climb toward a radio dish.

That sentence sounds like a criticism of the old ladder-of-progress picture of evolution.

It is also an observational fact.

Earth contains millions of lineages that are not failed attempts at civilization.

They are successful ways of remaining alive.

A bacterium adapted to a hydrothermal vent has not missed its appointment with astronomy.

A forest is not waiting to invent metallurgy.

The notion that intelligence culminates in technology is human because technology is what makes humans remotely detectable.

Our observability can become our definition.

This is the deepest form of the civilization bias.

We imagine the universe’s interesting systems in the image of the signal we are able to receive.

There is another side to the bias, and it deserves defense.

A search program has to choose.

No telescope can measure every possible consequence of every possible form of organization.

Technological signatures are attractive precisely because some may be less ambiguous than biological ones.

A narrowband radio carrier, a sequence with improbable modulation, or an obviously artificial transit geometry could be much harder for natural processes to mimic than oxygen or methane.

Technology can produce anomalies with low natural background.

That is a gift to detection.

If the scientific objective is to obtain decisive evidence that another technological society exists, civilization-centered signatures are rational targets.

The mistake would be demanding that they answer a different question.

This distinction matters because the term *bias* often implies error.

In statistics and measurement, a selection function can be known and useful.

A survey of bright galaxies is biased toward bright galaxies.

That does not invalidate it.

It tells us what population the survey can constrain.

Radio SETI is a survey with a complicated selection function.

It is sensitive to certain transmitter powers, frequencies, bandwidths, sky positions, distances, times, and emission geometries.

A technosignature search extends the selection function into other technologies.

A biosignature search has its own sensitivities and blind spots.

A serious theory of extraterrestrial life should keep these functions separate.

What is detectable is not the same as what exists.

The practical history of SETI makes this vivid.

Project Ozma did not ask its telescope to distinguish intelligence from non-intelligence.

It asked a receiver to distinguish a candidate signal from noise and interference.

That modesty should survive scale.

Breakthrough Listen can examine thousands of stars with bandwidth and computational power Drake did not have.

The observations are vastly deeper.

The logical form remains familiar.

At specified frequencies and sensitivities, after accounting for terrestrial interference, did a candidate engineered signal remain?

If not, the result places limits on transmitters of the searched type.

It does not place a general limit on life.

It does not place a general limit on intelligence.

It does not even place a general limit on radio technology.

A transmitter might be weaker, outside the searched band, intermittent, directional, or silent during the observation.

The experiment’s precision is exactly why the conclusion must be precise.

This is a theme the next chapter will need.

The phrase “we have heard nothing” compresses a multidimensional experiment into a human experience of silence.

Astronomers have heard a great deal.

They have found pulsars, masers, fast radio bursts, stellar flares, interference, satellites, spacecraft, radar, and countless other natural and artificial signals.

What they have not confirmed is an extraterrestrial technosignature.

That is a very different statement.

The absence is conditional.

And conditions are where science lives.

The civilization bias also appears in fiction.

Aliens arrive in ships.

They send messages.

They build monuments.

They invade, trade, teach, threaten, or observe.

A planet full of microbial ecologies does not make a character.

A distributed living network that never centralizes into persons is even harder to dramatize.

Narrative follows agency.

Search strategy can unconsciously follow narrative.

The systems easiest to imagine are not necessarily the systems easiest for evolution to produce.

This book’s purpose is not to replace the familiar alien with a more fashionable one.

A slime-mold planet is still a human image.

The point is methodological.

Start from capabilities rather than characters.

Does the system preserve state?

Does it alter response after experience?

Does it restore organization after damage?

Does it coordinate across components?

Does it exploit energy gradients?

Does it evolve?

Does it produce remotely observable consequences?

Only then ask whether there is a person hiding inside.

The same order should apply to civilization.

First detect the technology.

Then infer the builders.

This makes technosignatures stronger, not weaker.

It prevents us from overloading them with claims they do not contain.

An engineered radio signal would be monumental evidence of technology.

It would not tell us whether the builders are conscious in a way we recognize.

A large artificial structure would imply planning and construction at some level.

It would not tell us whether there is one mind, a colony, an automated industrial ecology, or a civilization long gone whose machines remain.

Technology itself can outlive sapience.

A probe can continue transmitting after its makers disappear.

An automated factory can keep operating.

A beacon can be inherited.

The observational object is still the artifact.

This possibility becomes important later when the book reaches machines that never wake up.

The civilization bias is therefore more than biological.

It assumes a relationship between technology and persons.

On Earth, that relationship is currently strong.

Humans build technology.

But even here, technological systems increasingly perform operations no individual human executes directly.

Supply chains allocate.

Markets route capital.

Software schedules infrastructure.

Autonomous systems control machines.

No one component contains the whole behavior.

A future technosignature could come from a system whose original sapient builders are absent, peripheral, or impossible to identify from the signal.

The telescope would still be right to call it technological.

It would be wrong to claim more.

There is a useful way to see the selection effect without leaving Earth.

Stand beside a radio telescope and ask what parts of human civilization it could discover if Earth were moved ten light-years away.

It would not see a hospital.

It would not see a court.

It would not see a school, a symphony, a theorem, a language, a market, or a scientific argument.

Those are among the most complicated products of human intelligence.

They are not radio observables.

The telescope might detect a sufficiently powerful radar transmitter, depending on direction and timing. A future optical observatory might infer industrial gases under favorable conditions. A distant civilization with exquisite instruments might notice atmospheric changes produced by our technology.

The observable is not a measure of cognitive richness.

It is a leak through a physical channel.

This becomes stranger as technology changes. Communication systems can become more efficient, more directional, more fiber-bound, more spread-spectrum, or otherwise less conspicuous from interstellar distance even while the civilization producing them becomes technologically richer. Other activities can make a civilization more visible: radar, power use, propulsion, atmospheric industry, large structures.

There is no reason for technological sophistication and astronomical detectability to rise together monotonically.

That breaks another hidden ladder.

Primitive life.

Complex life.

Intelligence.

Civilization.

Loud civilization.

The sequence feels natural because each stage seems to contain the previous one plus more capability. Detectability does not work that way.

A planet can become more capable and less visible in a channel we happen to monitor.

A technological species can abandon one signature and create another.

An automated system can remain detectable after its biological creators vanish.

A biosphere can be chemically loud without producing technology.

The search space therefore has two independent dimensions that public discussion often collapses: what exists and what leaks into our instruments.

SETI science is strongest when it keeps them apart.

This gives us a useful hierarchy.

Biosignature: evidence that life is a plausible cause.

Adaptive signature: evidence that history-dependent living organization may be shaping the response.

Technosignature: evidence that technology is a plausible cause.

Sapience: a much stronger psychological or organizational inference that remote data may not establish directly.

These categories can overlap.

They should not be collapsed.

The hierarchy is not a ladder of value.

A bacterium is not inferior evidence of life because it cannot build a laser.

A technosignature is not “more alive” than a biosignature.

It answers a different question.

That may be the most important repair this book can make to the word intelligence.

Human culture bundled competence, consciousness, language, civilization, and technology because we possess all of them at once.

Nature is under no obligation to keep the bundle.

Earth already pulls it apart.

The bacterium has adaptive competence without language.

The immune system has memory without recollection.

The planarian has regenerative control without centralized planning.

A nonhuman animal can solve problems without engineering.

A machine can perform complex tasks without evidence of consciousness.

A civilization can produce detectable artifacts whose signal tells us almost nothing about subjective experience.

Different capabilities separate under examination.

Search programs should reflect the separation.

That does not mean SETI must transform into a search for slime molds across the Galaxy.

SETI has a clear and valuable target.

The point is that astrobiology is larger than SETI, and intelligence may be larger than technology.

NASA’s current framing already places technosignatures within the broader continuum of evidence for life.

That is the right direction.

It lets civilization-centered searches remain precise while other programs explore biology, chemistry, atmospheres, oceans, origins, and the strange middle layers between passive matter and engineered artifacts.

Beyond the Veil belongs in that middle.

Its astronomical wager is weaker than a radio detection and harder to operationalize.

That is not a reason to inflate the claims.

It is a reason to define the observational gap.

What kinds of worlds would be alive, adaptive, and organized enough to deserve study but completely invisible to a search for technology?

Probably most inhabited worlds, if Earth’s history is any guide.

That sentence needs one final qualification.

We have one inhabited planet.

One evolutionary history cannot give a frequency.

Perhaps technological intelligence appears rapidly on most inhabited worlds.

Perhaps it almost never appears.

Perhaps radio is universal.

Perhaps no one else uses it.

Perhaps technology generally becomes more visible with time.

Perhaps mature systems become quieter.

We do not know.

The civilization bias is not a claim about what aliens are.

It is a claim about what our instruments select.

That is enough.

Once the selection function is visible, the apparent silence of the Galaxy changes meaning.

We have not searched “for intelligence” in the abstract.

We have sampled particular consequences of particular forms of technology across a tiny and uneven parameter space.

The next question is not why the universe failed to answer.

It is how much of the space we actually searched.
