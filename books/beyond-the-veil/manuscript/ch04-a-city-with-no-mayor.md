# A City With No Mayor

A bacterial biofilm can starve from the inside while food is still available at the edge. Growth creates the distribution problem: cells near the surface or perimeter have better access to nutrients, while cells deeper inside can be cut off by the colony around them.

There is no planner watching the imbalance. In *Bacillus subtilis*, part of the response turned out to be electrical.

In 2015, Arthur Prindle and colleagues reported that biofilms use potassium ion channels to transmit long-range electrical signals. Metabolic stress in interior cells can trigger potassium release. The extracellular potassium changes the membrane voltage of neighboring cells, which can contribute to a propagating wave through the community. As the wave moves, cells in different regions alter their metabolic states. Disrupt the relevant potassium channel and the coordination disappears.

This is not a metaphor imported from a nervous system, and it is not a tiny brain. A neuron uses ion channels; so does a bacterium. Shared physics does not make the cells equivalent. What the experiment establishes is narrower and more useful: local physiological state in one part of a community can alter the behavior of cells far enough away that no single bacterium needs to sample the whole colony.

The colony has a spatial problem, and the response is spatially distributed.

The phrase “city with no mayor” can mislead if it suggests that bacteria have reinvented municipal government. They have not. A city contains citizens who can represent the city, draw maps, argue about taxes, ignore signs, file lawsuits, and elect officials. A biofilm does none of those things. The resemblance is structural: many local units share an environment, their actions change that environment, and signals can carry consequences from one region into another.

That form of organization is common enough to tempt us into overgeneralization. A flock turns without a lead bird issuing a maneuver order. An ant colony reallocates work without an ant holding a spreadsheet of colony labor. A wound recruits cells without a foreman. A market changes a price without one trader calculating the complete economy. These systems are not equivalent, and distributed organization alone is not evidence of intelligence. Crystals, convection patterns, and traffic jams also emerge from local interactions.

The biofilm becomes more interesting because the interaction closes a control loop around a problem the community creates for itself. Peripheral growth can worsen nutrient access in the interior. Interior metabolic stress contributes to electrical signaling. The signal changes distant cellular activity, which can ease the pressure that helped produce the signal. Present state in one region changes future activity in another.

No bacterium needs access to a variable called “nutrient distribution across the biofilm.” Each responds to conditions and signals where it is. The larger pattern emerges from those local responses. The control is in the relation among cells, not in a privileged cell that contains the plan.

That claim can be tested causally. Break a pathway and a dimension of coordination is lost. Change the environment and the old response may no longer fit. A signal can arrive too late. A distributed system can fragment. Failure is useful because it shows that the coordination is contingent rather than an unavoidable consequence of matter arranging itself.

A second set of experiments widened the ecological reach of the mechanism. In 2017, work from the same broad research line showed that potassium-based electrical signaling from a *Bacillus subtilis* biofilm could influence bacteria that were not yet part of the community. The signal altered membrane potential and affected motility in distant cells; *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* could also be attracted.

The dramatic version would say the biofilm broadcasts an invitation. The physical version is better. Potassium changes a variable to which bacterial movement is sensitive, and activity in one community thereby changes the movement of organisms outside it. The ecological consequence looks communication-like without requiring a sender who intends a message or a receiver who understands one.

That distinction matters because the word *signal* can quietly acquire semantics. Cells release molecules or ions. Other cells possess receptors or electrical sensitivities. Gene expression changes. Motility changes. Metabolism changes. Population behavior changes. Biologists can study those causal links without deciding that the colony has a group mind.

Non-sapient intellect should not upgrade every feedback loop into cognition. Its usefulness, if it has any, comes from comparing capabilities while leaving mechanisms intact. The biofilm can coordinate metabolic state over distance. That is a capability. The mechanism gives us the boundary.

Distributed control also has limits. A fast animal moving through a dangerous environment cannot always wait for slow local interactions among tissues. Nervous systems can integrate distant sensory inputs quickly, select among competing actions, and coordinate muscles on timescales chemical diffusion alone could not match. Central structures can reduce decision latency.

The appearance of nervous systems therefore does not prove that earlier distributed control was fake. It shows that different architectures fit different problems. A biofilm does not need to steer a body away from an approaching predator in a fraction of a second. It needs to manage resources and stress across a relatively stationary community.

This is why biological competence is better pictured as a landscape than a ladder. One system can be extraordinarily good at chemical homeostasis and incapable of navigation. An immune system can preserve molecular history for years while lacking the centralized spatial model a hunting animal uses over seconds. A slime mold can remodel a transport network without anything resembling a vertebrate visual system. Capabilities come apart.

That separability matters to the astronomical wager of this book. A distant living world may be very competent at persistence and very poor at communication. It may regulate flows, exploit gradients, preserve environmental memory, and survive shocks for geological periods without producing a nervous system, much less a transmitter.

The difficulty is that distributed control is much easier to demonstrate in a laboratory than across interstellar distance. We can watch a bacterial wave, manipulate an ion channel, and measure which dimension of coordination fails. A telescope gives us outcomes: a spectrum, a light curve, a seasonal cycle, perhaps one day a coarse surface map. It rarely gives us the mechanism directly.

Imagine two distant environments with similar spatial patterning. On one, the pattern is produced by passive chemistry and transport. On the other, local living systems exchange signals and alter resource use after stress. A still image may not distinguish them. The useful information would be in dynamics: when one region is perturbed, do other regions change in a coordinated way? Does prior disturbance alter later response? Does recovery involve selective reallocation rather than simple relaxation?

Every one of those observations has nonliving false positives. Atmospheres redistribute heat. Oceans propagate waves. Forests burn differently after previous fires because fuel changed. Rock fractures redirect fluid. Physical systems can be nonlinear, hysteretic, and history-dependent without being alive.

So a planetary pattern should not be called intelligent because it moves in concert. The relevance of the biofilm is more modest. Earth demonstrates that living systems can solve coordination problems without a central controller, and that the capability can be exposed by perturbing the pathway that carries information.

That shifts the question from appearance to causal organization. What variable carries state? What coupling propagates change? Which disturbance recruits the response? What fails when the pathway is broken? Those questions are useful precisely because they do not require a mayor.

Evolution adds another reason to resist tidy stories. A biofilm is not an engineering design evaluated against one clean objective function. Growth, resilience, cooperation, competition, resource use, and reproduction can pull in different directions. A mechanism can be good enough, path-dependent, and full of compromises. There may be no single quantity the colony is optimizing.

Coordination is therefore not omniscience or perfection. It is the ability of local parts to alter one another’s behavior in ways that stabilize or reorganize a larger process under some conditions. The failure conditions belong to the description as much as the success.

This makes the biofilm a cleaner example of non-sapient competence than the city metaphor first suggests. The cells are alive. They sense local conditions, maintain themselves, alter gene expression and motility, and participate in long-range signaling and collective regulation. None of that tells us what it is like to be a bacterium, if there is anything it is like at all. Consciousness is a separate claim.

The relevant evidence is operational. Does present response depend on system state? Does information about a disturbance travel? Do components alter activity elsewhere? Does disrupting the channel degrade coordination? Does prior state change what happens next?

The biofilm clears some of those bars. It does not clear every imaginable one, and it does not need to.

This is also where the word *just* stops helping. A biofilm is just chemistry in the same sense that a jet engine is just physics and a brain is just electrochemistry. The scientific work begins after the reduction: which arrangement of matter produces the capability, and under what conditions does it fail?

If a future astrobiologist finds a world containing distributed adaptive systems, they may not announce themselves with messages. Their competence could be embodied in regulation: flows altered after stress, local activity coordinated across distance, later responses changed by retained state. Most of that may remain invisible from another star.

That possibility weakens the search program where it should be weakened. A billion biofilms do not automatically create a planetary signal. Local adaptive control can be real and astronomically silent.

The point is not to turn bacterial potassium waves into a new SETI target. It is to remove one assumption before the search moves outward: complex coordination does not wait for a brain or a command center.

The city has flows, shortages, signals, and local actors whose behavior changes conditions for others. What it does not have is a mayor.

We keep expecting one because that is how agency often looks from inside a human life.
