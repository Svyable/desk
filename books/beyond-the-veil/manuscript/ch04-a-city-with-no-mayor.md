# A City With No Mayor

A bacterial biofilm can starve from the inside while food is still available at the edge.

That sounds like bad planning.

There is no planner.

A mature biofilm is a crowded community. Cells near the surface or perimeter have better access to nutrients. Cells deeper inside can be cut off by the very success of the colony around them. Growth creates the distribution problem.

The usual language of organization encourages us to look for the office that notices.

Where is the control room?

Who sees that the interior is hungry?

Who tells the cells at the edge to slow down?

In *Bacillus subtilis*, part of the answer turned out to be electrical.

In 2015, Arthur Prindle and colleagues reported that biofilms use potassium ion channels to transmit long-range electrical signals. A metabolic trigger in stressed cells can lead to potassium release. The extracellular potassium changes the membrane voltage of neighboring cells. Those cells, in turn, participate in a propagating wave.

The wave moves through the community.

As it does, cells in different regions alter their metabolic states.

The result is coordination across a distance larger than one bacterium can span.

Delete the relevant potassium channel and the response disappears.

This is not a metaphor imported from a nervous system. The experiment measured a physical signal and perturbed the mechanism that carried it.

It is also not a tiny brain.

That qualification matters even more.

A neuron uses ion channels. A bacterium uses ion channels. The shared mechanism does not make the bacterium a neuron any more than a pump in a kidney becomes a heart because both move fluid. Evolution reuses physical possibilities. Electricity is one of them.

What matters here is narrower.

A community of cells can couple local physiological state to a signal that changes the behavior of other cells far away.

The colony has a problem distributed across space.

The solution is distributed too.

No bacterium contains a map of the whole biofilm.

No bacterium has to.

The phrase “city with no mayor” can mislead if it suggests that bacteria have reinvented municipal government. They have not. A city contains citizens capable of representing the city, arguing about it, drawing maps of it, taxing one another, ignoring signs, filing lawsuits, and electing mayors who may or may not solve the problem.

A biofilm does none of those things.

The comparison is useful only at the level of coordination.

Many local units share an environment. Their actions change that environment. Information about conditions can propagate. Local responses alter collective behavior. A large-scale pattern emerges without a component that contains the large-scale pattern as a plan.

This form of organization is common enough that we stop noticing how strange it is.

A flock turns without a lead bird issuing a maneuver order.

An ant colony reallocates workers without an ant possessing a spreadsheet of colony labor.

A wound recruits cells without a cell reading an architectural drawing of the body.

A market changes a price without a trader calculating the complete state of the economy.

These cases are not equivalent. Some include nervous systems. Some include conscious agents. Some are institutions. Some are tissues.

The resemblance is structural.

Global behavior can be produced by repeated local interactions.

That statement, by itself, is not an argument for intelligence.

A crystal grows through local interactions too.

So does a convection pattern.

So does a traffic jam.

The burden of this book is to say what makes some distributed systems more interesting than ordinary emergence.

The bacterial biofilm helps because the mechanism includes a feedback loop tied to a problem the community creates for itself.

Growth at the periphery can worsen nutrient access in the interior. Interior metabolic stress contributes to electrical signaling. The signal changes peripheral behavior. That alteration can reduce the pressure on the interior.

The present state of one region changes the future activity of another.

The system is not merely forming a pattern.

It is regulating a relationship.

That still does not require anything we should call a mind.

Control theory got along perfectly well before anyone tried to give thermostats personalities.

A system can detect deviation and alter output without containing a witness to the error.

In the biofilm, there is not even one thermostat.

There are many interacting cells, each responding to local conditions, coupled through shared chemical and electrical media.

The control is in the relation.

This is a hard idea for minds to hold because minds prefer objects.

We want intelligence to sit somewhere.

Inside a skull.

Inside a chip.

Inside a queen ant.

Inside a command center.

Inside the biggest cell.

Distributed systems frustrate that instinct. If the relevant capability belongs to the pattern of interactions, then cutting the system into pieces can destroy the very thing we are trying to locate.

Ask which water molecule contains the whirlpool.

None does.

The whirlpool is not therefore imaginary.

The difference, again, is that a whirlpool does not become non-sapient intellect merely by being collective. Organization is cheap in physics. The universe is full of structures nobody designed.

What the biofilm adds is conditional response.

A metabolic imbalance in one region affects signaling. The signaling changes distant cellular states. Genetic disruption of the channel can break the coordination. The architecture can therefore be interrogated causally.

This is stronger evidence than a beautiful pattern.

It lets us ask what the system is doing and what happens when a control pathway fails.

There is another layer.

In 2017, research from the same broad line of work showed that potassium-based electrical signaling from a *Bacillus subtilis* biofilm could influence bacteria that were not yet part of the community. The signal altered membrane potential and affected motility in distant cells. The effect was not limited to the same species; *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* could also be attracted.

Again, the careful description is better than the dramatic one.

The biofilm was not broadcasting an invitation.

The distant bacteria were not understanding a message.

Potassium altered a physical variable to which bacterial movement was sensitive.

Yet the ecological consequence looked communication-like: activity in one community changed the movement of organisms outside it.

A signal does not need semantics to matter.

Claude Shannon would have recognized the danger in that sentence.

The word *signal* is useful because a physical change in one place affects behavior elsewhere. It becomes dangerous if we quietly add a sender who intends and a receiver who interprets.

Bacterial communication often lives near that boundary.

Cells release molecules. Other cells have receptors. Gene expression changes. Motility changes. Metabolism changes. Population behavior changes.

Biologists can study the mechanisms without deciding that the colony has a group mind.

That is the discipline this book needs.

Non-sapient intellect should not be a license to upgrade every feedback loop into cognition.

It should be a way to compare capabilities while keeping mechanisms intact.

The biofilm can coordinate metabolic state over distance.

That is a capability.

The colony does not need a belief about nutrient justice.

That is a boundary.

Once the distinction is clear, a larger question appears.

Why does centralized control seem so natural to us?

Part of the answer is anatomical.

A human nervous system has a brain. Destroy enough of it and familiar forms of human agency disappear. We therefore learn, from the inside, to associate coordinated behavior with a privileged organ.

Part of the answer is institutional.

Our organizations have names for authority: chief executive, president, commander, principal, chair, mayor. Even when real control is more distributed, the organizational chart puts a box at the top.

Biology repeatedly refuses the chart.

A bacterial community has no sovereign cell.

An immune response has no lymphocyte that understands the patient.

A regenerating tissue has no foreman.

A slime mold has no central neuron because it has no neurons.

The absence of a mayor is not evidence that nothing is governing the outcome.

It may mean governance is the wrong scale of description.

Consider the electrical wave again.

No single cell needs access to the global variable “nutrient distribution across this biofilm.”

Each cell responds to conditions in its neighborhood and to signals that arrive there.

The aggregate dynamics can nevertheless act on the distribution.

This is one reason local rules are so powerful.

They reduce the information any component must possess.

A centralized planner trying to optimize a changing biological community would need an enormous sensing and communication system. Distributed control lets the world do some of the bookkeeping. A cell only has to experience the concentrations, voltages, forces, and signals available where it is.

The environment becomes part of the computation.

That phrase also requires restraint.

We are not claiming the biofilm runs an algorithm in the same sense as a laptop executing source code. We are noticing that physical dynamics can transform information about local conditions into changes in collective state.

The transformation is lawful.

It can be modeled.

It can fail.

It can have consequences for survival and growth.

Calling that “computation” may help in some contexts and obscure in others. The chapter does not need the word.

Coordination is enough.

The interesting property is that the colony can remain coherent without a complete representation of itself.

Human systems often work this way too, though for different reasons.

No one person knows the entire state of a power grid.

No one engineer knows every dependency in a large software platform.

No dispatcher knows every future condition on every road.

Large systems survive by pushing decisions downward, standardizing interfaces, propagating alerts, and allowing local responses to absorb disturbances.

Centralization returns when local information is insufficient or conflicts must be resolved.

Biology found versions of this tradeoff long before institutions named it.

But natural selection complicates any story that praises distributed control too easily.

A biofilm is not an engineering design that was evaluated against a clean objective function.

It is the product of evolutionary history.

Traits that persist need not be globally optimal. They need only have survived selection in particular environments.

A regulatory mechanism can be good enough, contingent, path-dependent, and full of compromises.

This matters because the language of intelligence often smuggles in optimization.

If a colony coordinates, we start asking what it is optimizing.

Perhaps nothing singular.

Growth in one condition may conflict with resilience in another. Cooperation can coexist with competition. A response that benefits the collective in one ecological context may impose costs on particular cells. Evolution operates across tangled levels and timescales.

There may be no one objective hidden behind the behavior.

Non-sapient intellect should therefore not be defined as optimality.

The Physarum chapters will make the same point from another direction. A network can be impressively efficient without being globally optimal. A biological solution can sit on a tradeoff frontier rather than at one perfect point.

The biofilm gives us the social version of the lesson.

Coordination is not omniscience.

It is not perfection.

It is the ability of local parts to alter one another’s behavior in ways that stabilize or reorganize a larger process.

Sometimes that process works.

Sometimes the colony fails.

Sometimes environmental change outruns the regulatory architecture.

Sometimes another organism exploits the signal.

The existence of failure is useful.

A system that could never fail would be difficult to distinguish from a law of physics.

Failure reveals the contingency of the control.

Break the potassium channel and one long-range coordination mechanism is lost.

Change the environment and the old response may no longer fit.

A feedback loop can overshoot.

A signal can arrive too late.

A distributed system can fragment.

Those are not embarrassments to the argument.

They help define it.

The competence is real because the mechanism makes a difference.

The competence is limited because the mechanism has conditions.

That is closer to intelligence as we actually know it.

Human beings are not intelligent because we always succeed. We are intelligent partly because our behavior depends flexibly on state, history, prediction, error, and correction.

The question is how far down those properties can be separated from consciousness.

Bacterial communities force the question early.

The cells are alive.

They sense.

They maintain themselves.

They respond to gradients.

They alter gene expression.

They change motility.

In communities, they can participate in long-range signaling and collective regulation.

None of that tells us what it is like to be a bacterium, if there is anything it is like at all.

The book can remain agnostic about that problem.

Consciousness is not the gate.

The gate is adaptive control.

Does the present response depend on the state of the system?

Does information about a disturbance travel?

Do components reallocate activity?

Does the system behave differently when the control pathway is removed?

Does history alter the response?

The biofilm clears some of those bars.

Not all distributed systems do.

That gives us a way to resist both anthropomorphism and reduction by insult.

Calling the colony “just chemistry” is true in the sense that every known organism is chemistry.

It is unhelpful if *just* means the organization can be ignored.

A jet engine is just physics.

A computer is just semiconductor behavior.

A brain is just electrochemistry.

The scientific work begins after the word *just*.

What arrangement of matter produces the capability?

What variables carry state?

What couplings propagate change?

What feedback makes the response adaptive rather than passive?

Those questions scale.

They are also the questions an astrobiologist may eventually need to ask.

Imagine detecting a distant environment with complex spatial patterning.

The first temptation would be to name the objects.

Cells?

Colonies?

Mineral structures?

Vortices?

Vegetation?

Suppose the resolution were too poor.

A second strategy would be to watch dynamics.

When one region is perturbed, do other regions change in a coordinated way?

Does the response depend on the history of previous disturbances?

Does recovery redistribute activity rather than simply relax toward equilibrium?

Do repeated shocks produce altered future responses?

On Earth, each of those observations has nonliving false positives.

Atmospheres redistribute heat.

Oceans propagate waves.

Forests burn differently after previous fires because fuel changed.

Rock fractures redirect fluid.

A planet is full of distributed dynamics.

So no single signature proves non-sapient intellect.

The relevance of the biofilm is not that we can see bacterial potassium waves across interstellar space.

We cannot.

It is that Earth demonstrates a category of organization our search vocabulary often hides.

Living systems can solve coordination problems without a central controller.

If we insist that intelligence must reveal itself as a message from a sender, we may miss forms whose “thinking,” if the word is allowed at all, is embodied in regulation.

There is a useful countercase to the romance of decentralization.

Some problems really do benefit from centralization.

A fast animal moving through a dangerous environment cannot always wait for a slow consensus among tissues. A nervous system can integrate distant sensory inputs quickly, select among competing actions, and coordinate muscles on a timescale that local chemical diffusion alone could not match. Central structures can compress decision latency.

The evolutionary appearance of nervous systems therefore does not show that earlier distributed control was fake.

It shows that distributed control has limits.

A bacterial biofilm does not need to steer a body away from an approaching predator in a fraction of a second. It needs to manage a different class of problems at different spatial and temporal scales. Comparing the two systems only by which one looks more like us would confuse task requirements with capability.

This gives the category another useful constraint.

Non-sapient intellect is not a ladder on which a bacterium sits on the bottom rung and a human sits at the top.

A ladder suggests one dimension.

Biological control is more like a landscape.

One system can be extraordinarily good at maintaining chemical homeostasis and incapable of navigation. Another can navigate and fail to regenerate. An immune system can preserve a molecular history across years while lacking the centralized spatial model a hunting animal uses over seconds. A slime mold can remodel a transport network while having nothing resembling a vertebrate visual system.

Capabilities come apart.

That separability is central to the astronomical wager.

A distant living world may be very competent at persistence and very poor at communication.

It may regulate flows, exploit gradients, preserve environmental memory, and survive shocks for geological periods without producing an organism that invents language.

If our concept of intelligence is bundled around the package humans happen to possess, such a world will look unintelligent by definition.

The more scientific alternative is to unbundle the package.

Coordination.

Memory.

Prediction.

Error correction.

Navigation.

Resource allocation.

Communication.

Model building.

Consciousness.

Sapience.

Technology.

These capacities can interact without being identical.

The biofilm earns attention because it separates one of them cleanly.

Coordination can exist without a nervous center.

That observation is modest enough to be true and large enough to matter.

It also helps with the false-positive problem.

Suppose a nonliving chemical system produces synchronized oscillations. Synchrony alone should not qualify. Suppose a crystal changes growth form when temperature changes. Conditional response alone should not qualify. Suppose a river network rearranges after a flood. Reconfiguration alone should not qualify.

The case becomes stronger when several features are coupled in a way that preserves function across perturbation: internal state affects a signal; the signal changes distant activity; distant activity alters the condition that produced the signal; breaking the pathway degrades the regulation; and the response operates within a living system whose components themselves maintain and reproduce the machinery.

Even then, we have not demonstrated a mind.

We have demonstrated a control architecture.

That phrase is deliberately less exciting.

Excitement is cheap.

Operational distinctions are expensive.

One reason the category of non-sapient intellect may fail is that existing biological vocabulary might already do all the work. We have homeostasis, signaling, collective behavior, physiological regulation, adaptation, learning, memory, development, and ecology. Perhaps adding *intellect* creates heat but no new light.

The only defense is comparative usefulness.

If the term helps us notice that memory in an immune system, metabolic coordination in a biofilm, positional control in a regenerating worm, and adaptive routing in a slime mold share certain control properties while differing radically in substrate, it may earn its keep.

If it merely makes bacteria sound clever, it should be discarded.

That is a falsification test for language itself.

The book does not get to protect its favorite word from evidence.

The astronomical problem becomes one of evidence across scales.

A local electrical wave is measurable in a laboratory.

A biosphere-scale pattern might be measurable remotely.

The chain between them is not automatic.

A billion biofilms do not necessarily create a planetary signal.

Most adaptive control may remain invisible at astronomical distances.

That possibility weakens the search program but not the terrestrial observation.

And the search program should be weakened where evidence demands it.

The point is not to announce a new SETI target because bacteria exchange potassium.

The point is to change the prior.

Complex adaptive coordination is not a capability that waits for brains.

It is already present in systems built from cells that never see the whole.

The city has streets.

It has flows.

It has shortages.

It has signals.

It has local actors whose behavior changes conditions for other actors.

It can coordinate activity across distance.

What it does not have is a mayor.

We keep expecting one because that is how agency looks from inside a human life.

The next chapter asks what happens when coordination is forced to reveal its objective more brutally.

Do not watch the system when everything is working.

Damage it.

Then see what it tries to restore.
