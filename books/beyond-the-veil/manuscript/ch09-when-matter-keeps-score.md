# When Matter Keeps Score

Bend a shape-memory alloy at one temperature, change the temperature, and the material can return toward a previous form.

No neuron fires.

No cell survives.

No organism learns.

The metal has a history anyway.

Its present behavior depends on a physical state produced by what happened before.

This is the false positive Chapter 7 was waiting for.

If memory is simply “the past changes the future,” matter is full of it.

Magnetic systems show hysteresis.

Porous materials can show return-point memory.

Disordered solids can encode aspects of prior shear in their structure.

Resistive devices can retain electrical states after the driving voltage is gone.

A shape-memory alloy can recover deformation through reversible phase transformation.

None of these examples needs life.

None needs intellect.

The conclusion is not that biological memory is fake.

It is that memory is physical before it is biological.

Life recruits the property.

Brains elaborate it.

Culture externalizes it.

But the universe did not wait for neurons to let history persist in matter.

This chapter asks what changes when a physical memory becomes part of an adaptive loop.

The distinction begins with hysteresis.

In ordinary usage, we expect a system's current state to be determined by current conditions.

Hysteresis violates that expectation.

Two systems exposed to the same current input can occupy different states because they arrived there through different paths.

History becomes a state variable.

This is common enough in physics that the word carries no cognitive suggestion.

A ferromagnet can retain magnetization after an external field is removed.

A porous system undergoing capillary condensation can follow different paths as pressure rises and falls.

Some such systems show return-point memory: after cycling an input through nested ranges, the material can return to states associated with earlier reversal points.

In 1993, experiments on helium condensation in porous material reported a hierarchy of return-point memory effects.

The phrase *memory effect* belongs to physics.

Nobody needed to argue that the pores had minds.

That should discipline every biological claim in the previous chapters.

A persistent state is not evidence of cognition by itself.

The same warning applies to the word *learning* when physical systems are trained through repeated cycles.

Materials can be trained.

Shape-memory alloys can acquire two-way shape-memory behavior through repeated thermomechanical treatment.

A material can respond differently after training because its microstructure has changed.

Engineers use the language without embarrassment because the mechanism is known.

The material is not a student.

The word describes controlled history dependence.

This is why anthropomorphism cannot be diagnosed by vocabulary alone.

Context matters.

Scientists can say a material remembers and mean something precise.

They can say a cell decides and mean a measurable branching process.

The danger arrives when a functional metaphor quietly expands into an ontological claim.

Memory does not imply a rememberer.

Training does not imply a learner with experience.

A goal does not imply desire.

The book’s proposed category has to survive those separations.

Non-sapient intellect cannot be defined by metaphorical words.

It has to be defined by how multiple capabilities are causally organized.

The 2022 work on sheared disordered materials makes the point beautifully.

Researchers studied how microscopic structure in disordered solids relates to prior deformation and later yielding.

The material's arrangement carried information about mechanical history.

That information could be read through later response.

The system had structure, memory, and flow.

It did not have metabolism.

It did not reproduce.

It did not regulate itself toward continued viability.

It did not search a changing environment for resources.

A physical memory can therefore satisfy the first half of our loop.

Past event.

Persistent state.

Future response.

What it lacks is the larger adaptive organization in which the remembered state is used to preserve, explore, correct, or improve something for the system.

Even that sentence needs care.

A nonliving material can be embedded in a designed controller that uses its memory adaptively.

A memristive circuit can store state and change future computation.

A shape-memory actuator can respond to temperature in a machine.

A material can become part of a robot.

The intelligence, if that word is useful at all, belongs to the organization of the whole system, not automatically to the material carrying state.

The carrier and the control architecture are different questions.

Biology sometimes hides that distinction because the carrier is already alive.

Consider bacterial chemotaxis.

The methylation state of receptors is a physical memory.

Taken out of context, methyl groups are chemistry.

Inside the chemotaxis network, the state alters sensitivity so the bacterium can compare recent concentrations and navigate gradients.

The same molecule-level persistence becomes functional because it is coupled to sensing and movement.

The memory participates in action.

This is the step from record to control.

It does not create consciousness.

It creates a feedback architecture with temporal depth.

The 1986 *E. coli* experiments make the causal role unusually clear.

Wild-type cells showed short-term temporal comparison.

Mutants defective in normal methylation and demethylation did not perform the comparison normally and failed to adapt on the same timescale.

Change the memory machinery and the behavior changes.

This is more informative than merely observing a persistent chemical mark.

The mark belongs to the capability because removing or altering it degrades the capability.

The immune system gives a second architecture.

Its memory is distributed across cells, receptors, antibodies, epigenetic programs, tissue niches, and population dynamics.

A later response can differ because the composition of the system changed after the first exposure.

Again, the physical substrate matters because it alters action.

A freezer full of stored antibodies would contain molecular records.

It would not be an immune memory system in the functional sense unless those records were integrated into a response process.

The living system remembers through what it can do later.

This formulation helps with epigenetics too.

The word *epigenetic memory* can cover several mechanisms in which gene-expression states persist through cell division or across time.

Some are central to normal development.

A cell descended from another cell can maintain a differentiated identity even though the underlying DNA sequence is largely shared with other tissues.

The persistence is physical.

Chromatin state, DNA methylation, transcriptional networks, and other mechanisms can help stabilize expression patterns.

But stable identity alone is not evidence of intellect.

A liver cell staying a liver cell is biological memory in one useful sense.

It does not thereby solve a novel problem.

The chapter should resist making every durable regulatory state part of the book's headline category.

The correct question is what the state is doing in context.

Does it merely preserve identity?

Does it change sensitivity?

Does it help recover after disturbance?

Does it enable discrimination among alternatives?

Does it generalize from prior exposure?

Does it participate in a loop whose outcome matters to the continued organization of the system?

Those questions sort memories by function without pretending one is more “real” than another.

Developmental bioelectricity creates another form of stored state.

Cells maintain voltage differences across membranes.

Groups of cells can be coupled by gap junctions and signaling pathways.

Electrical states can influence gene expression, growth, migration, and patterning.

Some experimental interventions can produce morphological effects that persist beyond the immediate stimulus.

The temptation is to call the voltage pattern a memory of the body plan.

Sometimes that is useful shorthand.

It can also outrun the evidence.

A voltage is not a picture of anatomy.

A persistent physiological state can bias later development without representing the whole organism explicitly.

Once again, disposition may be more important than content.

The tissue is changed in a way that changes what it will do.

That is enough for a control system.

The slime mold makes the same point with a substance embarrassingly easy to name.

Salt.

Repeated exposure to an aversive salt condition can alter later avoidance.

Retained sodium has been associated with persistence of the habituated state.

The organism's chemistry keeps score.

The phrase sounds almost literal.

Exposure adds material.

The material changes future response.

But the score is not sitting in a ledger.

It is the changed body.

This may be the common form of non-neural memory.

State is not stored separately from function.

State *is* altered function waiting for the next condition.

Brains add layers in which memories can become content-rich, cross-modal, and detached from the immediate environment.

Non-neural systems often reveal the simpler architecture.

Past conditions tune current dynamics.

This is why physical false positives are so valuable.

They force us to stop worshiping the storage mechanism.

A beautiful memory mechanism can exist in dead matter.

What matters for the book is what organization the memory supports.

There is a parallel in computing.

A transistor can retain a state as part of a memory cell.

A hard drive can preserve data for years.

A database can contain petabytes.

Storage capacity tells us almost nothing by itself about intelligence.

A calculator with one kilobyte can perform a useful computation.

An archive with a billion documents can do nothing until a process interprets them.

Memory is an enabling resource.

It is not the agent.

The same distinction applies to genomes.

DNA preserves information across generations.

That makes it one of the most consequential memory substrates on Earth.

But treating a genome as the organism's complete memory is misleading.

The DNA sequence is inherited, yet development also depends on cellular state, maternal contributions, spatial context, regulation, environment, and physical interactions.

The genome stores possibilities and constraints.

It does not contain a film of the organism's life.

Evolutionary memory is also unlike learning within one lifetime.

Allele frequencies can change because past environments filtered populations.

A future population therefore embodies historical information about selection.

Calling that memory can be illuminating.

It becomes confusing if we collapse evolutionary adaptation with an individual's experience-dependent learning.

Timescale matters.

Carrier matters.

Update rule matters.

The book should keep a taxonomy without turning it into a framework for its own sake.

Short-term molecular memory.

Immune memory.

Developmental state.

Structural memory.

Behavioral habituation.

Evolutionary inheritance.

Neural memory.

Institutional memory.

These phrases do not name one mechanism.

They name a family resemblance: history persists and changes future behavior of a system.

What distinguishes the members is how the past is written, how long it lasts, how it is read, and what action the reading changes.

That is the useful comparison.

The problem becomes more difficult when the memory is distributed.

Where is a biofilm's history?

Perhaps in gene expression, metabolic state, local chemistry, spatial structure, extracellular matrix, and population composition.

Where is an ecosystem's history?

In species composition, soil nutrients, seed banks, age structure, altered hydrology, learned animal behavior, pathogen prevalence, and the physical landscape.

Where is a body's history?

Everywhere.

The demand to point to one memory organ can be as misguided as demanding one mayor in a bacterial city.

Distributed systems can carry history across many substrates.

This complicates experiments.

Erase one substrate and another may compensate.

A failure to find one memory trace does not prove the system is memoryless.

Conversely, finding one persistent trace does not prove that trace caused the later behavior.

Causal manipulation matters.

Change the candidate state.

Does the response change?

Restore it.

Does the behavior return?

Block access.

Does the memory become functionally silent?

These are difficult standards.

They are what the category needs.

At astronomical distance, they become brutal.

Suppose we observe a planet whose atmosphere changes after a major perturbation and then follows a different seasonal cycle for decades.

The system keeps score.

That statement may be literally true in a physical sense.

Ocean heat content changed.

Ice cover changed.

Surface chemistry changed.

Aerosols changed.

The new trajectory depends on the past.

None of this proves life.

The planet has memory because dynamical systems have memory.

To argue for adaptive organization, we would need evidence that the stored state participates in regulated response rather than merely passive persistence.

Even then, geophysical feedback can mimic regulation.

Silicate weathering can provide long-term negative feedback on atmospheric carbon dioxide without a biosphere consciously or even biologically controlling the variable.

Oceans buffer chemistry.

Clouds alter radiation.

Nonlinear systems can restore ranges.

The astronomical standard must therefore be comparative and mechanistic where possible.

Does the response consume free energy in a structured way?

Does it reallocate resources?

Does it preserve organization across multiple kinds of disturbance?

Does previous exposure change the next response in a direction consistent with persistence rather than merely inertia?

Are there coupled chemical patterns difficult to generate through known abiotic mechanisms?

Can alternative nonliving models reproduce the observation?

No one answer will be decisive.

The ladder of evidence matters more than one magic signature.

This is where NASA's life-detection discipline becomes relevant later in the book.

A claim becomes stronger when independent lines of evidence converge and plausible abiotic alternatives are eliminated.

Non-sapient intellect should inherit that conservatism.

It cannot demand a lower evidentiary bar just because the category is novel.

If anything, it needs a higher one because the vocabulary is so easy to anthropomorphize.

Matter keeping score is the beginning of the story, not the verdict.

The physical examples also force a philosophical concession.

The boundary between memory and no memory is not the boundary between life and nonlife.

There may be no such simple boundary.

Life is assembled from properties physics already permits.

Persistence.

Hysteresis.

Self-assembly.

Catalysis.

Compartmentalization.

Feedback.

Energy dissipation.

Pattern formation.

Information storage.

None is exclusively alive.

The novelty of life lies partly in how these properties are organized together.

That is a less dramatic story than declaring that memory proves cognition.

It is scientifically richer.

A living system can take generic physical capacities and recruit them into a regime of self-maintenance, reproduction, adaptation, and open-ended change.

The same way a computer recruits semiconductor physics into logic, life recruits chemistry and mechanics into control.

The substrate does not become magical.

The organization becomes consequential.

The physics examples deserve to be more than a rhetorical warning.

Return-point memory shows how much apparent “recall” can emerge from systems with no metabolism at all.

Imagine changing an external field up and down.

The material does not always trace one reversible line.

Its path depends on previous reversals.

Nested cycles can leave signatures that are recovered when the control variable returns to earlier extrema.

From the outside, the material appears to remember where the experiment turned around.

Inside, there is no witness.

There are interacting physical degrees of freedom whose configurations depend on loading history.

The history is legible because the system is hysteretic.

Sheared disordered materials provide a more recent version of the same lesson.

Particles rearrange under repeated deformation.

The microscopic structure that remains after one sequence of shear can affect when and how the material yields later.

Researchers can extract information about prior deformation from the present organization.

That is not metaphorical memory in the loose sense of “we can infer the past somehow.”

It is a measurable relationship among prior loading, current structure, and future mechanical response.

Still no life.

This matters because the more impressive the abiotic case becomes, the stronger the biological criterion has to be.

It would be convenient if life owned history dependence.

It does not.

It would be convenient if life owned feedback.

It does not.

It would be convenient if life owned self-organization.

It does not.

It would be convenient if life owned information storage.

It does not.

The search for a magic property is probably the wrong project.

Life assembles generic properties into a regime.

Energy flows through the system.

Components are produced and repaired.

Boundaries are maintained.

Information-bearing states affect action.

Actions change future conditions.

Some changes persist.

Some are erased.

Reproduction allows the architecture itself to evolve.

The pattern becomes open-ended enough that new strategies can appear over generations and, in some systems, within a lifetime.

Non-sapient intellect, if useful, will name part of that regime rather than one ingredient.

This leads to a distinction between memory *of* a perturbation and memory *for* regulation.

The preposition sounds trivial.

It is not.

A dent in metal is memory of a collision.

A receptor methylation state can be memory for adjusting chemotactic sensitivity.

A scar is evidence of injury, but the scar may not function as a control variable for the next injury.

An immune-cell population created by exposure can actively alter the next immune response.

The same physical persistence can be archive in one context and control state in another.

The difference is functional coupling.

This also explains why energy matters.

A passive memory can simply remain.

An adaptive loop often has to spend energy to sense, maintain gradients, repair components, move, synthesize molecules, and act against disturbances.

Life is famously far from equilibrium.

That fact alone does not prove intelligence; hurricanes and flames are far from equilibrium too.

But sustained control generally requires work.

A bacterium swimming up a gradient consumes energy.

An immune response expands cells and synthesizes proteins.

A regenerating worm builds tissue.

A slime mold moves mass through tubes.

The stored state is connected to an energetic capacity to do something about the next condition.

Abiotic systems can also consume energy and show complex dissipative structure.

So energy is another necessary-looking property that fails as a standalone test.

The pattern repeats.

Every single criterion attracts a physical counterexample.

The evidence becomes persuasive only in combination.

There is a practical benefit to this frustration.

It tells us what Chapter 10 must do.

The false-positive problem cannot be handled by putting “not consciousness” in parentheses after every biological example.

It requires comparison classes.

Crystals.

Flames.

Hurricanes.

Reaction-diffusion systems.

Hysteretic materials.

Optimization by soap films.

Feedback controllers.

Machine-learning systems.

Ecological succession.

Each can mimic one or more signatures.

The book's category has to survive contact with the best of them, not caricatures.

Chapter 9 supplies the memory class.

Matter can keep score exquisitely.

Therefore a future telescope that detects history dependence should not announce intelligence.

It should update a hypothesis.

Then ask what else changes with the stored state.

Does the system use history to discriminate?

Does it alter response selectively?

Does it preserve a larger organization?

Does the response improve, or at least become fitted differently, after exposure?

Can the state be erased and the behavior reset?

Do multiple independent signatures point toward living control?

These are laboratory questions translated into astronomical aspiration.

The translation may fail.

We may never have the temporal resolution, spatial resolution, or repeated perturbations needed to ask them of exoplanets.

That is not a reason to soften the standard.

It is a reason to admit the observational limit.

A scientifically useless category is possible.

The research brief says so.

If adaptive organization cannot be distinguished remotely from ordinary physical memory and feedback, then “non-sapient intellect” may remain a terrestrial comparative concept rather than an astrobiological detection class.

That outcome would still teach us something.

It would tell us that capability and observability are different.

The universe could be full of systems whose local behavior would impress us in a laboratory and whose planets look completely ordinary from light-years away.

Detection is not existence.

The veil may be instrumental before it is philosophical.

This suggests a definition of non-sapient intellect that is more relational than material.

Do not ask whether the matter is intelligent.

Ask what loop the matter participates in.

A methylation mark inside a bacterium participates in temporal comparison and navigation.

An antibody-producing cell participates in altered response to exposure.

A bioelectric state can participate in developmental patterning.

A slime-mold chemical state can participate in habituation.

A martensitic phase in an alloy participates in shape recovery when temperature changes.

That last system can also be embedded in a designed adaptive machine.

The loop, not the substance, determines the capability.

This prevents a strange form of biological chauvinism.

Carbon chemistry is not automatically intelligent because it is alive.

Metal is not automatically unintelligent because it is dead.

The classification depends on organization and function.

That point will matter in Chapter 17, when engineered machines return.

A machine can remain non-sapient while becoming extraordinarily competent.

The same conceptual tools should work there.

Memory.

Feedback.

Goals.

Adaptation.

Model.

Action.

None alone settles consciousness.

That separability is the book's deepest wager.

The first nine chapters have now approached it from below.

A bacterium compares recent conditions.

A biofilm coordinates without a center.

A worm rebuilds anatomy after damage.

A slime mold turns exploration into structure.

An immune system changes after exposure.

Matter itself can preserve history.

The components of intelligence appear before the package we call a mind.

But the false positives appear too.

Crystals organize.

Materials remember.

Attractors look like goals.

Hysteresis looks like history-dependent choice.

Feedback looks purposeful.

Optimization appears in systems with no organism at all.

That is not a problem to solve later.

It is the problem.

If non-sapient intellect cannot distinguish itself from these cases, the term does not deserve to survive.

The next chapter is therefore an attack.

We have spent nine chapters learning how easy it is to see competence everywhere.

Now we ask how much of it is illusion.
