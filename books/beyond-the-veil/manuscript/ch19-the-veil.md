# The Veil

In 1944, two psychologists showed people a short animation containing almost nothing human.

There were geometric shapes.

A large triangle.

A small triangle.

A circle.

A rectangle with an opening.

The shapes moved.

That was enough.

Observers described chasing, fighting, hiding, bullying, rescuing, escape, fear, anger, and intent. The figures had no faces. They had no voices. They were not living. The motion carried just enough structure for people to see a social world.

The experiment by Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel has endured because it exposes something both useful and dangerous about human perception.

We are agency detectors.

We do not wait for a philosophical proof of mind before reacting to apparent pursuit or avoidance.

That is sensible.

If something moves toward you in a coordinated way, the cost of assuming agency too early can be smaller than the cost of assuming it too late.

But the detector has a selection function.

It is more sensitive to some forms of competence than others.

Fast motion reads as action.

A face reads as a center.

Speech reads as thought.

A response arriving after our question reads as dialogue.

A hand reaching toward an object reads as intention.

A system that behaves on those channels enters human social cognition almost immediately.

A tissue rebuilding itself over days does not.

A bacterial community coordinating metabolism through ion flows does not.

An immune response altered by an encounter months earlier does not.

A slime mold reconfiguring tubes does not.

A planet whose atmosphere carries a lagged ecological response across seasons certainly does not.

The veil in this book is not a mystical barrier hiding intelligence from us.

It is the structure of our own recognition.

We see some forms of agency too easily.

We see others only after measurement forces us to.

Those are opposite errors.

Anthropomorphism is the first.

We project human-like motives, feelings, or intentions onto systems because their behavior fits a familiar social pattern.

Anthropocentric blindness is the second.

We fail to recognize real adaptive competence because the system lacks the signals that normally trigger our social model.

The first error inflates.

The second erases.

A book about non-sapient intellect has to avoid both.

That is harder than simply declaring ourselves neutral.

Humans do not become neutral because we have learned the word *bias*.

We need procedures that make the bias less powerful than the evidence.

Nicholas Epley, Adam Waytz, and John Cacioppo proposed a three-factor theory of anthropomorphism built around accessible knowledge about humans, the motivation to understand and predict another agent, and the desire for social connection.

The details matter less here than the general point.

Anthropomorphism is not one childish mistake we outgrow.

It varies with what we know, what we need, and what interpretive resources are available.

When a system is uncertain and difficult to predict, human-like explanation can become tempting.

When a system speaks in fluent language, human knowledge is ready to supply a model.

When we are socially engaged with it, the model becomes easier still.

That helps explain why a talking machine can feel more mind-like than a regenerating animal even if the latter is unquestionably alive.

The interface carries different cues.

There is another useful distinction.

In a 2007 study of mind perception, Heather Gray, Kurt Gray, and Daniel Wegner found that judgments about minds were organized around two broad dimensions they called Experience and Agency.

The exact psychology should not be turned into a universal law.

The distinction is still valuable.

A system can be perceived as capable of acting, planning, remembering, or exerting control without being perceived as capable of pain, pleasure, fear, desire, or other felt experience.

That separation echoes the scientific discipline this book has tried to maintain.

Operational agency is not the same claim as consciousness.

Competence is not the same claim as experience.

The trouble is that everyday language keeps pulling them back together.

“Knows.”

“Wants.”

“Tries.”

“Decides.”

“Prefers.”

“Remembers.”

These verbs were built for creatures we encounter as whole agents.

They compress mechanism into social understanding.

When I say a friend remembered a birthday, nobody asks which synapses changed.

The person-level verb is useful because a huge amount of background evidence supports the person-level category.

When I say a bacterium remembered a chemical condition, the compression becomes dangerous.

Now the word may imply more than the experiment demonstrates.

The solution is not banning the verb.

It is unpacking it.

What state persisted?

How did that state alter later response?

What intervention disrupted it?

How long did it last?

Was the change specific?

Could passive dynamics reproduce it?

The word earns its place when the mechanism can cash the check.

This is why operational definitions are not sterile substitutes for wonder.

They preserve wonder from inflation.

If a slime mold shows habituation after repeated exposure, the interesting fact is stronger when we do not need to imagine a private feeling of boredom.

If a tissue restores pattern after injury, the control problem becomes more remarkable when the target state is inferred from perturbation rather than personified as a hidden blueprint with intentions.

If a machine diagnoses a fault, the engineering achievement survives without pretending the diagnostic routine feels concern.

Mechanism does not make the phenomenon less real.

It makes the claim more precise.

Human intuition often works in the other direction.

Once a mechanism is exposed, the capability can feel demoted.

It was not intelligence.

It was only feedback.

It was only chemistry.

It was only evolution.

It was only search.

It was only statistics.

The word *only* does philosophical work that the mechanism does not justify.

A mechanism explains how a capability occurs.

It does not automatically show that the capability was illusory.

Human memory is also mechanistic.

Human movement is biochemical.

Human reasoning is implemented physically.

We do not normally respond to an explanation of our own mechanisms by saying the behavior therefore did not happen.

The asymmetry is revealing.

For familiar minds, mechanism sits underneath recognized competence.

For unfamiliar systems, mechanism is sometimes used to erase competence.

That is part of the veil.

The opposite asymmetry appears when a system resembles us strongly enough.

Then sparse evidence can be promoted too quickly.

A fluent sentence feels like understanding.

A face-like configuration feels alive.

Coordinated motion feels intentional.

A first-person pronoun feels like a self.

These cues can be informative.

They can also be generated by systems whose underlying organization differs sharply from the human case that trained our intuition.

The correct response is not cynicism.

It is claim separation.

What behavior occurred?

What mechanism supports it?

What evidence supports generalization beyond the task?

What evidence supports memory?

What evidence supports self-modeling?

What evidence supports subjective experience?

The answers need not rise together.

This makes the veil less like a curtain and more like a filter bank.

Different cues pass through different channels.

Speed matters.

Centralization matters.

Scale matters.

Similarity to human anatomy matters.

Language matters.

Reciprocity matters.

Temporal proximity matters.

A phenomenon that responds within a second is easier to experience as an agent than one that responds over a year.

A phenomenon concentrated in one body is easier to experience as an agent than one distributed across a colony.

A phenomenon with eyes is easier to experience as an agent than one expressed through voltage gradients.

A phenomenon that acts on an object is easier to experience as an agent than one that changes a probability distribution.

None of these preferences is irrational in ordinary life.

They become liabilities when turned into scientific definitions.

Science has repeatedly learned to study phenomena that human perception does not present naturally.

Atoms are not visible to the naked eye.

Continents move too slowly for a person to watch.

Evolution operates across generations.

Fields and waves require instrumentation and theory.

Astronomy is almost entirely an exercise in inferring remote systems from compressed physical traces.

We do not reject a black hole because it lacks a face.

We build a chain from observable consequences to the entity that best explains them.

Adaptive competence deserves the same discipline.

The fact that it is psychologically legible in an animal and illegible in a tissue should not determine whether the control relation exists.

This is where Chapter 19 turns back on the entire book.

The examples were chosen partly because they strain intuition in different directions.

Physarum is visually animal-like enough to tempt us.

It moves.

It searches.

Its network changes before our eyes.

Planarian regeneration is stranger.

The animal has a body we recognize, but the competence is distributed through tissue.

Immune memory is more abstract.

The system is inside us, but no single component is the remembered self.

Bacterial chemotaxis is smaller still.

The control loop is molecular.

Shape-memory materials expose the false-positive problem by showing that nonliving matter can carry history.

Planetary feedback exposes it at the opposite scale.

A world can regulate variables without being one organism.

Machines then destabilize the boundary from another direction.

They can be built to plan, learn, search, diagnose, and control without settling the question of experience.

The sequence is not a ladder from stupid to smart.

It is a series of attacks on one shortcut.

Do not infer the category from the costume.

The costume is what the human detector notices first.

This creates what we might call a **legibility tax**.

A familiar system arrives with cognitive credit.

A human speaks and we assume a mind because the broader evidence for human minds is overwhelming.

A dog looks, moves, learns, vocalizes, and shares enough biology with us that the inference to experience feels natural.

An octopus is stranger, but behavior and nervous-system evidence still place it inside a recognizable animal problem.

A biofilm receives no such credit.

Every cognitive-sounding verb has to be earned from scratch.

That asymmetry is scientifically appropriate up to a point.

Extraordinary category shifts require evidence.

But the tax becomes unfair when the unfamiliar system is required to do more than demonstrate the actual capacity under discussion.

If the question is whether prior state changes later response, require evidence of prior state changing later response.

Do not quietly add a face requirement.

If the question is whether a distributed system corrects deviations, require a perturbation and a correction mechanism.

Do not quietly add a brain requirement.

If the question is whether a machine can plan under constraints, evaluate the planning.

Do not quietly require it to be conscious before calling the planning real.

Likewise, do not infer consciousness merely because the planning is impressive.

The symmetric standard is the escape route.

Same capacity.

Same evidentiary burden.

Different ontology held open until separate evidence arrives.

This is easier to say than practice because scientific language inherits social language.

Even technical terms accumulate metaphor.

“Cell signaling.”

“Genetic code.”

“Error correction.”

“Memory.”

“Recognition.”

“Decision.”

“Communication.”

Some metaphors become so productive that they harden into technical vocabulary.

That is not necessarily a problem.

A term can become operationally precise even if its ancestry is human-centered.

The problem appears when the metaphor does more explanatory work than the mechanism.

“Cells decide” is weak if it merely means the outcome changed.

It becomes more useful if competing states, sensed variables, regulatory interactions, and perturbation responses are specified.

The word should be the summary, not the evidence.

There is a subtler veil created by scale.

Humans tend to assign agency to entities whose boundaries are easy to see.

One animal.

One robot.

One vehicle.

One person at a keyboard.

Distributed systems resist this.

A colony can produce organized behavior without one member holding the global state.

A tissue can restore anatomy through local interactions whose result is visible only at the larger scale.

An immune response can involve changing populations, signaling networks, and long-lived states.

A market can route resources without becoming one mind.

A software service can distribute sensing and action across machines.

The agent boundary becomes a question for causal analysis rather than eyesight.

This is why “where is the intelligence?” can be a bad question.

It assumes intelligence is a substance located in a part.

Often the more useful question is “where is the control loop closed?”

Where is state stored?

Where is error detected?

Which components can alter which others?

What variable is stabilized or changed?

At what scale does the perturbation become meaningful?

The answers can cross visible boundaries.

None of this means the whole should be promoted into a person.

A distributed control system is not automatically a distributed subject.

The book has been strict about that because one romantic error can destroy the argument.

If every coordinated system becomes a mind, the word *mind* stops distinguishing anything.

The claim we need is smaller.

A capacity can belong to an organization even when no component contains a centralized representation of the whole.

That statement is ordinary in engineering.

It should not become mystical in biology.

Slow time creates another veil.

Human social cognition is tuned for interaction.

We call.

Something answers.

We reach.

Something moves.

We threaten.

Something flees.

A response within the envelope of attention feels causal and agentic.

Many biological control processes occur outside that envelope.

Plants can alter growth over days or seasons.

Immune memory can change later response after long gaps.

Development unfolds over hours, days, or years.

Ecological reorganization can persist across seasons.

Evolution can change population response across generations.

At those scales, the human observer sees snapshots.

The causal relation has to be reconstructed statistically.

That makes the evidence feel less vivid even when it is stronger.

A controlled time series can be better evidence than a dramatic video.

It is worse theater.

The same problem will dominate astronomy.

An alien adaptive signature, if one ever becomes measurable, may arrive as a difference between curves separated by years.

No creature waves.

No signal says hello.

A model with a history-bearing biological state simply predicts better than one without it.

That could be scientifically profound and emotionally quiet.

The first evidence of life beyond Earth may be quiet too.

Nature does not optimize discoveries for narrative satisfaction.

This is why the book has insisted on preserving the difference between what exists and what leaks into a detector.

Our minds are detectors as surely as telescopes are.

They have sensitivity curves.

They have false positives.

They have blind spots.

They have priors.

Heider and Simmel’s moving shapes remind us how low the threshold can be for one kind of agency inference.

The entire history of microbiology, physiology, and distributed control reminds us how high the threshold can be for another.

The scientific strategy is calibration.

Know what triggers the detector.

Then make the instrument argue with the intuition.

Anthropomorphism is not always the enemy.

Sometimes a metaphor generates a productive hypothesis.

“What is the tissue trying to restore?” can suggest a perturbation experiment.

“What does the organism remember?” can suggest a test of retained state.

“What is the colony deciding?” can suggest a search for competing collective outcomes.

The metaphor earns scientific value when it cashes out in a measurement that no longer depends on the metaphor.

The dangerous version is the one that cannot be falsified because every result is reinterpreted as hidden intention.

Then the human story has swallowed the system.

Anthropocentric skepticism has a matching dangerous version.

It says no unfamiliar system can possess a meaningful cognitive capacity because the mechanism is not the human mechanism.

That position can also become unfalsifiable.

Every demonstration is dismissed as “mere chemistry” or “mere computation.”

The criterion moves whenever the evidence arrives.

A fair science has to state the capacity first.

Then test for it across substrates.

This does not dissolve meaningful differences.

A human autobiographical memory and bacterial adaptation are vastly different phenomena.

A human plan and a spacecraft planner are vastly different.

A felt pain and an error signal are not interchangeable.

Operational comparison should sharpen these differences, not erase them.

The common dimension can be real without the endpoints being equivalent.

History dependence is one dimension.

Flexible transfer is another.

Error correction another.

Subjective experience another.

Self-modeling another.

Symbolic communication another.

Once the dimensions are separated, the pressure to decide whether the whole system is “really intelligent” becomes weaker.

That pressure is part of the veil too.

Humans like categorical nouns.

Person.

Animal.

Machine.

Mind.

Tool.

Life.

Intelligence.

Categories make action possible.

We cannot deliberate from first principles about every object.

But science often advances by discovering that a useful everyday category hides several variables.

“Intelligence” may be one of them.

The book’s proposal is not to abolish the word.

It is to make the hidden variables visible.

This has consequences for machine systems in particular.

Language can overtrigger our social detector because language is one of the strongest signs of mind in ordinary human life.

A system that answers questions, uses first-person grammar, refers to prior context, apologizes, jokes, or adapts its tone presents cues that normally come bundled with a person.

The bundle can feel intact even when the implementation is unfamiliar.

We should not respond by declaring the output meaningless.

Nor should we let fluency settle consciousness.

Measure capability as capability.

Investigate experience as a separate hypothesis.

The same symmetric caution protects us from the opposite mistake if a future conscious system looks nothing like a human conversation partner.

Unfamiliarity is not evidence of absence.

This is why the book cannot end with a confident map of which systems are conscious.

That would exceed its evidence.

The book can end with a map of claims.

Physical history dependence.

Biological adaptation.

Operational agency.

Learning.

Distributed coordination.

Technology.

Sapience.

Experience.

These claims have different tests.

They can coexist without collapsing.

The veil becomes thinner when we stop asking one cue to answer all of them.

There is a social version of the veil as well.

Scientific fields inherit target classes.

SETI historically built instruments around detectable technology.

Astrobiology builds frameworks around detectable life.

Cognitive science historically concentrated on animals and humans for excellent practical reasons.

Control theory studies regulation without requiring consciousness.

Developmental biology studies pattern without usually calling it cognition.

Each field’s language makes some questions easy to ask and others awkward.

A new category is useful only if it helps those languages meet without erasing their boundaries.

That is a demanding standard.

If *non-sapient intellect* becomes a way to relabel homeostasis, it has failed.

If it becomes a way to call every organism conscious, it has failed.

If it becomes a way to dismiss SETI for studying technology, it has failed.

If it becomes a way to romanticize planets, it has failed.

If it becomes a bridge that forces researchers to ask whether history, error correction, reconfiguration, and distributed coordination leave distinct measurable consequences, it may have earned temporary use.

Temporary is enough.

Scientific categories are tools.

They do not need souls either.

The final veil is narrative.

A person makes a good protagonist.

A civilization sends a message.

A machine wakes up.

An alien lands.

A scientist has a revelation.

These are events with characters and moments.

Distributed competence often has neither.

A lineage accumulates mechanisms.

A tissue corrects.

A community reorganizes.

A planet records the aggregate.

A model becomes incrementally better.

There may be no instant when intelligence arrives.

There may only be a growing profile of capacities.

That is unsatisfying if we want a creation scene.

It is satisfying if we want a research program.

The veil lifts by replacing dramatic thresholds with measured distinctions.

Not because thresholds never exist.

Persons may mark profound thresholds.

Sapience may require organizations that deserve their own category.

Consciousness may be a genuine transition with deep moral significance.

Technology may transform planetary observability.

The point is that none of those thresholds grants retroactive ownership of every component capacity below it.

Memory existed in useful forms before autobiography.

Control existed before deliberation.

Coordination existed before government.

Adaptive reconfiguration existed before engineering.

The lower capacities are not counterfeit because the higher ones are richer.

They are part of the history.

And perhaps part of the universe’s larger inventory.

We do not know.

That uncertainty is where the final chapter begins.

If the veil is partly ours, what observation could force us to see through it?

What would count as evidence strong enough that the category no longer depends on a writer’s metaphor?

What would make the idea fail?

The book has spent nineteen chapters collecting constraints.

The last chapter has one job.

Turn them into a standard.
