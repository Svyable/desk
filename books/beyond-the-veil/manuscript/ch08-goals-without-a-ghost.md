# Goals Without a Ghost

A thermostat is easiest to understand when the room is wrong.

The temperature falls.

The switch closes.

The heater turns on.

The temperature rises.

The switch opens.

Nothing in the box feels cold.

Nothing in the box wants seventy degrees.

Yet describing the system without reference to a target quickly becomes awkward.

In 1943, Arturo Rosenblueth, Norbert Wiener, and Julian Bigelow published “Behavior, Purpose and Teleology.” They were trying to make language such as *purpose* scientifically usable without requiring a mystical force pushing events toward the future.

Their move was behavioral.

Look at inputs and outputs.

Ask whether behavior is organized around a state.

Ask whether feedback changes action as the system approaches or departs from that state.

Purpose, in this operational sense, does not require a conscious purpose-holder.

That idea became foundational to cybernetics.

It also remains dangerous.

The danger is not that goal language is always false.

The danger is that it compresses mechanism so efficiently that the mechanism disappears.

Say that a bacterium “wants food” and gradient sensing, receptor adaptation, flagellar control, metabolism, and evolutionary history collapse into a single verb.

Say that a tissue “wants a head” and positional control, signaling, cell proliferation, apoptosis, mechanics, and bioelectric state vanish behind the grammar.

Goal language earns its place only if we can cash it back out.

What variable is being regulated?

What counts as deviation?

What sensing is available?

What actions can the system take?

What happens when the feedback pathway is broken?

How flexible is the target?

Who chose the target, if anyone?

Those questions are the ghost detector.

If the answers reduce to “the system somehow knows,” we have explained nothing.

If the answers lead to measurable mechanisms, goal-directedness can become a compact description of a real control relationship.

Biology forces this issue constantly because organisms are full of target-like behavior.

Blood glucose stays within ranges.

Body temperature is regulated.

Cells migrate toward signals.

Embryos develop reproducible structures.

Wounds close.

Planarians rebuild missing anatomy.

Birds migrate.

Bacteria climb nutrient gradients.

None of those outcomes imply that the relevant system consciously represents a goal.

The stronger claim is unnecessary.

A goal, operationally, can be a state or region of states toward which a system selectively acts across more than one disturbance.

That last phrase matters.

A ball rolling into a bowl reaches the same low point from many starting positions.

We usually do not call the ball goal-directed.

The trajectory follows passive mechanics.

A thermostat also returns a room toward a range from different starting temperatures, but it does so through sensing and an action that changes the environment.

The mechanism closes a feedback loop.

Living regulation can be more complicated still because the action set changes, the target can change, and the system can learn.

This suggests a hierarchy of evidence.

Convergence alone is weak.

Feedback is stronger.

Context-sensitive correction is stronger still.

History-dependent adjustment stronger again.

The ability to reach a viable outcome through novel disturbances adds another layer.

No one threshold turns matter into an agent.

The hierarchy is a way to keep language proportional to evidence.

The 2026 review literature on biological agency has returned to exactly this problem. Pierrick Bourrat and Andrew Barron argue that goal-directed explanations can be useful at several levels of biological organization, including cells and tissues, but they also warn that teleonomic explanations can omit important causal detail and misdirect conclusions.

That warning belongs at the center of this book.

A short explanation is not always a deep explanation.

“The tissue restores its target morphology” can summarize many observations.

It does not tell us how polarity is established, which cells carry positional information, how growth is scaled, or how repair stops.

The phrase can guide a research question.

It cannot substitute for the research.

This is one reason non-sapient intellect cannot be defined by goal language alone.

Human observers are exceptionally good at inventing purposes.

A stone can be a paperweight if somebody uses it as one.

A river “seeks” the sea in poetry.

A corporation “wants” profit even when employees disagree.

Evolution “designs” adaptations in shorthand.

A gene “wants” to copy itself in metaphor.

The grammar of goals travels farther than the evidence for agents.

We therefore need a distinction between an observer's goal and a system's regulated state.

The shortest route through a maze may be the experimenter's objective.

The slime mold may be responding to nutrient and flow dynamics that happen to produce a short route.

The coincidence can be impressive without implying that the organism encodes “shortest path” as a goal.

The same caution applies to the Tokyo rail experiment.

Researchers measured cost, efficiency, and fault tolerance.

Those were analysis dimensions chosen by humans.

The slime mold did not sign the objective function.

Its network dynamics produced a compromise that scored well on those dimensions.

That is enough.

A system can solve a problem we care about without sharing our representation of the problem.

This point becomes harder in regeneration because the target seems to belong to the organism.

A planarian fragment tends to regenerate a species-appropriate anatomy.

The resulting head is not merely what an experimenter hoped to see.

The animal itself functions differently if polarity, scale, or organ identity is wrong.

This makes target language less arbitrary.

But even here, the target is not one point.

Organisms tolerate variation.

Heads differ in size.

Cells turn over.

Bodies grow.

Development changes proportions.

The relevant goal is a viable region in a high-dimensional state space, not a photograph.

That picture of goal-directedness is closer to control engineering.

A controller does not need every internal variable to equal one exact number.

It may need variables to stay within ranges while responding to disturbances.

Living systems add nested goals.

A cell maintains membrane potential while a tissue repairs architecture while an organism searches for food while reproduction competes with immediate survival.

These objectives can conflict.

There is no reason to expect one hidden master goal to resolve them cleanly.

This is where talk of “the organism's goal” becomes most misleading.

Which goal?

Over which time horizon?

At which level?

For which component?

A starving animal may sacrifice tissue maintenance to preserve immediate function.

An immune response can damage the organism while fighting infection.

Cancer cells can be locally successful and globally catastrophic.

A developing embryo can trade one resource for another.

Natural selection can favor a trait that improves reproduction while shortening lifespan.

Biology is full of partial objectives.

The language of agency should preserve that mess.

A single optimizer is usually a fiction.

The false positive is the attractor.

Many physical systems move toward stable states.

A pendulum with friction comes to rest.

A chemical reaction approaches equilibrium.

A droplet becomes rounder because surface tension reduces area.

A magnetic material follows a hysteresis loop.

A ball settles into a valley.

These systems can return toward characteristic states after perturbation.

If “returns toward a state” is enough for goal-directedness, physics becomes crowded with goals.

That would make the word nearly useless.

Feedback helps, but even feedback is not sufficient for intellect.

Engineered machines can regulate without life.

A cruise controller adjusts throttle.

A refrigerator cycles a compressor.

A power converter changes current.

A software daemon restarts a failed process.

These systems have target-like behavior because designers gave them architecture.

They are useful counterexamples because they separate goal-directedness from consciousness cleanly.

The thermostat has a goal in an operational sense.

It does not have a ghost.

That is the chapter's title in miniature.

The biological question is different from the machine question because biological targets emerged through evolution and development rather than an engineer's specification.

But this difference should not be mystified either.

Natural selection can produce systems whose current dynamics regulate states that historically contributed to persistence and reproduction.

The organism does not need to know the evolutionary reason.

The mechanism works now.

This gives us two explanatory layers.

Why does the system possess this control architecture?

Because of development and evolutionary history.

How does the architecture act in this moment?

Through current sensing, state, and feedback.

Confusing the layers creates bad teleology.

The heart does not beat because future tissues will need oxygen.

It beats because present physiology produces contractions in a system shaped by evolution.

Yet saying the cardiovascular system serves oxygen delivery can still be a useful functional description.

Purpose language is often a map of causal organization rather than a force from the future.

The map is valuable if we remember it is a map.

This is where Rosenblueth, Wiener, and Bigelow still matter.

Their 1943 paper tried to classify behavior in terms of feedback and prediction rather than metaphysical final causes.

The historical setting matters because machines were beginning to make goal-like behavior visible in a substrate nobody could plausibly accuse of having desires.

A servo could correct.

A weapon-control system could track.

A thermostat could regulate.

The existence of these machines weakened an old linguistic shortcut.

Purposeful behavior did not require conscious intention.

Cybernetics made that distinction technologically obvious.

Biology had been living it all along.

Homeostasis is purposeful-looking because systems maintain variables against disturbance.

Development is purposeful-looking because embryos reach organized forms despite local variation.

Regeneration is purposeful-looking because missing structures can be rebuilt.

Behavior is purposeful-looking because animals alter action according to conditions.

The scientific task is to ask what kind of control each case contains.

The word *goal* should come after that analysis, not before it.

There is also a question of counterfactual flexibility.

A passive system often has one response determined tightly by current physics.

A more capable control system can reach similar outcomes through different actions depending on the disturbance.

If one road is blocked, take another.

If one pathway is inhibited, recruit another.

If one resource declines, reallocate.

This capacity to preserve an outcome through changing means is sometimes called equifinality.

It is tempting evidence for agency because the end persists while the route changes.

But even equifinality can arise from distributed dynamics with no central chooser.

The interesting property is the flexibility itself.

How wide is the set of disturbances the system can compensate for?

How different can the routes become?

Does experience expand the repertoire?

At what point does compensation fail?

These questions let goal-directedness become quantitative without pretending there is one universal agency score.

A planarian is revealing because it can recover anatomy after extraordinary perturbation.

A bacterium is revealing because it can preserve chemotactic responsiveness across changing background concentrations.

An immune system is revealing because prior exposure changes the later response.

A biofilm is revealing because local metabolic stress can alter distant activity.

Each system preserves something.

Each also fails outside a range.

The boundary of correction is part of the definition.

This is important for alien life.

A biosphere that maintains atmospheric disequilibrium can look goal-directed if we phrase the observation carelessly.

“The planet regulates its atmosphere.”

The sentence may describe a real coupled feedback.

It may also import a planet-sized agent that the evidence does not support.

Earth already teaches the danger.

Life changes atmospheric chemistry.

Geology changes atmospheric chemistry.

Oceans absorb and release gases.

Weathering feeds back on carbon dioxide.

Vegetation alters albedo and water cycles.

The whole planetary system can exhibit stabilizing and destabilizing feedbacks without a global controller.

Calling Earth an organism can be evocative.

It is not a measurement.

The astronomical version of goal-directedness will therefore need interventions or natural perturbations.

Does a variable return because passive physics pushes it there?

Or because a network of living processes senses conditions and reallocates activity?

Can we distinguish the two from remote data?

Perhaps not.

The chapter should not promise that we can.

It should define what evidence would move the claim.

One possibility is controlled diversity of response.

A passive system driven by one mechanism may have a narrow relaxation pattern.

An adaptive system might show context-sensitive correction across qualitatively different disturbances.

But nonliving systems can be nonlinear, multistable, and history-dependent too.

The false-positive problem returns immediately.

That is healthy.

Every strong criterion should attract a counterexample.

Non-sapient intellect will only survive if the criteria work together.

Memory without goal-directed regulation is not enough.

Goal-directed regulation without flexibility may be too close to fixed homeostasis.

Flexibility without history may be ordinary distributed dynamics.

Learning without persistence is not learning.

The category is conjunctive.

No single magic property carries the argument.

This also protects against a familiar mistake in artificial intelligence.

A machine can be highly capable without being conscious.

A machine can pursue a specified objective without understanding it.

A reinforcement-learning system can adjust behavior according to reward without possessing a human-like desire for reward.

A language model can produce goal-like sequences when embedded in an agent architecture without settling any question about subjective experience.

We will return to engineered systems later.

For now they offer a conceptual service.

They make it impossible to insist that goal-directed behavior and sapience are the same property.

Once those properties are separated in machines, there is no reason to fuse them again in cells.

But the machine analogy has limits.

Engineered systems inherit goals from designers.

Biological systems inherit control architectures from evolutionary and developmental processes.

Neither fact tells us what consciousness requires.

The point is narrower.

A system can be organized around error reduction without an inner witness to the error.

That observation is almost mundane in engineering.

It remains philosophically disruptive in biology because the language of life is saturated with intention.

Cells “decide” fates.

Genes are “selected.”

Organisms “strategize.”

Plants “forage.”

Immune cells “recognize.”

Bacteria “communicate.”

Many of these verbs are productive shorthand.

They become dangerous when shorthand becomes evidence.

The safeguard is always the same.

Translate the verb back into an experiment.

What changed?

What signal carried the change?

What history mattered?

What alternative response was available?

What happened when the pathway was broken?

What variable remained within range?

If the verb survives translation, keep it cautiously.

If it evaporates, discard it.

Cancer provides one of the sharpest tests of level.

A tumor can display properties that look locally goal-directed.

Cells proliferate, alter metabolism, recruit blood vessels, evade some immune pressures, and adapt to treatment.

At the level of the cancer lineage, these behaviors can preserve growth.

At the level of the organism, they destroy the system that makes that growth possible.

Which goal is the real one?

The question has no useful answer until the level is specified.

There is no single sovereign objective shared by every biological component.

Natural selection can operate across competing lineages and levels.

Cells pursue, in the purely operational sense, local survival and replication through mechanisms that can conflict with organism-level homeostasis.

An immune system can attack healthy tissue.

A placenta can negotiate aggressively with maternal physiology.

Microbes can benefit from a host while imposing costs on it.

Multicellular life is not one optimization problem.

It is a negotiated stack of control problems.

This is one reason claims that a whole organism “wants” something should be treated as compressed descriptions.

Often they are useful.

An animal seeks food.

A plant grows toward light.

A bacterium swims up an attractant gradient.

But the useful level can change with the question.

At the molecular level, receptor occupancy and signaling matter.

At the cellular level, motility matters.

At the organismal level, resource acquisition matters.

At the evolutionary level, persistence of lineages matters.

A good explanation can move among these levels without pretending they are interchangeable.

The 2026 agency literature is valuable precisely because it treats the level of organization as part of the problem rather than assuming agency belongs only to whole animals.

That does not mean every level deserves the same agent vocabulary.

It means the evidence must show a sufficiently integrated pattern of goal-directed control at the level being described.

This suggests another test: perturb the supposed goal while leaving the mechanism partly intact.

If a planarian's polarity system is altered, can it regenerate the “wrong” anatomy consistently?

If a control parameter changes, does the system pursue a different stable configuration?

If an organism switches physiological state with season or development, does its regulated range move?

A goal-like state that can itself be reset by identifiable signals looks less like an observer's arbitrary description and more like a variable implemented by the system.

Engineered control systems make this easy to see.

Change a thermostat's set point and the same sensor-actuator loop begins defending a different temperature.

The target is encoded somewhere in the controller.

Biological targets are rarely stored in one knob, but experiments can sometimes reveal analogous plasticity.

A developmental transition changes what physiology maintains.

Hormones alter metabolic priorities.

Injury changes local growth rules.

Learning changes which outcomes an animal pursues.

The target can be state-dependent.

This is stronger than a system falling into one fixed attractor.

It also creates a new false positive.

Physical systems can have multiple attractors and switch among them when parameters change.

Multistability is not agency.

The crucial difference is not the number of stable states.

It is whether state selection is coupled to sensing, history, and action in a way that supports the continuing organization of the system.

Even that criterion is not absolute.

Designed machines can satisfy it.

The category remains comparative.

Another useful test is error-specific response.

A general stress reaction says that something is wrong.

A more differentiated controller responds differently depending on what is wrong.

Low glucose triggers one set of responses.

High temperature another.

Tissue loss another.

A predator another.

The richer the mapping between deviation and corrective action, the more the system resembles a controller selecting among policies rather than a material relaxing along one path.

Again, no consciousness follows.

A lookup table can select among policies.

But differentiated correction is evidence of architecture.

It tells us there are internal distinctions that matter to future action.

This matters for the proposed astrobiological criterion.

If a distant system responds to every disturbance with the same generic rebound, passive mechanisms remain plausible.

If different disturbances produce coordinated, disturbance-specific responses that preserve some higher-level organization, the case for adaptive control becomes stronger.

A drought-like event might change one set of fluxes.

A chemical insult another.

A temperature excursion another.

Repeated exposure might alter response speed.

The pattern would still need abiotic alternatives ruled out.

But goal-directedness would be inferred from a family of counterfactuals, not from one attractive curve.

That is a more demanding standard.

It is also closer to how we infer agency in unfamiliar machines.

We do not call a door closer intelligent because it returns the door to one position.

We become more interested when a system can distinguish conditions, remember history, change strategy, and preserve performance across novel disruptions.

The word *goal* then describes the invariance underneath changing actions.

The system does different things so that something else stays acceptably the same.

That is the core of control.

It may also be the core of the non-sapient intellect category if the category survives its false positives.

This gives us an operational definition of goals without ghosts.

A candidate system is more plausibly goal-directed when it maintains or reaches a bounded set of states across multiple disturbances by using feedback that changes action according to current and, in stronger cases, prior conditions.

The definition does not mention consciousness.

It does not mention intention.

It does not mention optimization.

It does not claim the state is globally best.

It says only that some outcomes are privileged by the system's causal organization.

That may still be too broad.

A designed thermostat qualifies.

So might some chemical control networks.

That is acceptable.

Goal-directedness is one axis, not the whole category.

The aim is not to reserve a sacred word for living things.

The aim is to separate capabilities so evidence can be compared.

A thermostat can have operational goal-directedness and almost no learning.

A bacterium can combine regulation with molecular memory.

A slime mold can combine history-dependent behavior with spatial reconfiguration.

A regenerating animal can correct large anatomical perturbations through distributed control.

A human can do all of those things and also report an experience of wanting.

The packages differ.

The ghost is optional.

At least for the control problem.

This conclusion should make us more demanding, not less.

Once consciousness is removed from the definition, we lose the emotional shortcut that tells us where intelligence lives.

We have to measure.

We have to perturb.

We have to identify state variables and failure modes.

We have to distinguish targets from attractors.

We have to show that the system's own organization, not merely the observer's preference, privileges the outcome.

That is harder than saying something looks purposeful.

Good.

The book needs hard tests.

The next one concerns the substrate.

A goal can guide behavior only if the system carries enough state to compare now with what came before or with what counts as acceptable.

Where does that state live?

Sometimes in molecules.

Sometimes in cell populations.

Sometimes in voltages.

Sometimes in network structure.

Sometimes in the phase of a material that is not alive at all.

Matter keeps score.

The problem is deciding when the score belongs to an adaptive system and when it is merely physics remembering physics.
