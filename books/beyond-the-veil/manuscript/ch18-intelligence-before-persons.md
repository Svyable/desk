# Intelligence Before Persons

A person is a late arrangement of old problems.

Keep the inside different from the outside.

Find energy.

Avoid damage.

Repair damage when avoidance fails.

Compare what is happening now with what happened before.

Change behavior when the comparison changes.

Coordinate many parts without letting every part do whatever it wants.

Preserve enough continuity that tomorrow’s system can benefit from yesterday’s state.

By the time a human child learns the word *intelligence*, life has been working on these problems for billions of years.

The obvious response is that none of the old solutions is intelligence.

A membrane is not a mind.

Metabolism is not reasoning.

Homeostasis is not understanding.

Chemotaxis is not a plan.

Regeneration is not selfhood.

All true.

The mistake would be turning those distinctions into a story in which intelligence appears from nowhere only when the final package becomes familiar to us.

Chapter 18 asks for a different picture.

Do not imagine intelligence as one substance that evolution eventually invents.

Imagine a bundle of control capacities that can appear, combine, specialize, centralize, and interact across evolutionary time.

Sensing.

State retention.

Error correction.

Action selection.

Prediction.

Resource allocation.

Learning.

Communication.

Coordination.

Model building.

Counterfactual search.

Self-monitoring.

Symbolic reasoning.

No single item on that list is sufficient for sapience.

Several exist in forms far older and simpler than persons.

The evolutionary question is not, “When did intelligence switch on?”

It is, “Which capacities existed, at what scale, with what mechanism, and what became possible when they were coupled?”

This framing belongs near work sometimes called basal cognition.

Researchers in that area argue that cognition should be studied across a broader biological range than animals with large nervous systems, emphasizing capacities such as sensing, memory, decision-like control, learning, and goal-directed regulation in cells, tissues, plants, microbes, and other systems.

The program is provocative.

It is not a universal consensus.

The word *cognition* itself remains contested when applied far below nervous systems.

That controversy should not be hidden, because it reveals the exact conceptual problem this book has been circling.

One side worries that cognitive language becomes so broad that ordinary biological regulation gets renamed rather than explained.

The other side worries that brain-centered definitions make us blind to functional continuities that evolution built before neurons.

Both worries are legitimate.

The solution is not deciding which vocabulary is morally generous.

The solution is making every capacity pay rent in mechanism and experiment.

If we say a cell remembers, what physical state persists and changes later behavior?

If we say a tissue has a target morphology, what perturbations reveal correction toward that state?

If we say an organism learns, what changes with experience, how specific is the change, and does it recover or generalize?

If we say a collective coordinates, what signals connect components and what happens when the channel is disrupted?

The terms survive only when the measurements do.

This lets us talk about continuity without pretending there are no thresholds.

Human language is a threshold.

So is cumulative culture at some scale.

So is the ability to represent alternatives explicitly and discuss them with another person.

So is reflective self-awareness if it can be defined and measured.

Evolution can create genuinely new regimes when old capacities are reorganized.

Water is made from hydrogen and oxygen, but wetness is not a property of either atom alone.

Likewise, a person may possess cognitive organization that no cell in the body possesses.

Emergence is not magic.

It is also not reducible to the claim that nothing new happened.

The point is to preserve both truths.

New wholes can have new capabilities.

The parts can still carry older control capacities into the whole.

Nervous systems are a dramatic example.

A neuron is a cell with ancient cellular machinery.

A brain is not merely a pile of independent cells.

Connectivity, specialization, plasticity, recurrent activity, sensory organs, motor systems, development, and embodiment create a different level of organization.

Once those systems exist, learning can occur at speeds and scales unavailable to many non-neural organisms.

Representations can become richer.

Action can be coordinated across a mobile body.

Events separated in time and space can influence one another through dedicated circuitry.

The brain matters enormously.

“The brain was late” never meant “the brain was unnecessary.”

Late inventions can transform a system.

Writing was late too.

So was agriculture.

So was radio.

The question is what existed before the transformation.

Bacterial chemotaxis gives one answer.

A bacterium moving through a chemical gradient can compare recent concentrations over time and bias its movement accordingly.

The cell does not need a map of the environment.

It needs a mechanism by which recent history changes the probability of the next action.

That is a small capacity.

It is also one of the ingredients that more elaborate cognition uses constantly: present input interpreted against stored state.

Immune memory gives another.

After exposure, the system can respond differently to a later encounter.

The mechanism is not recollection in the autobiographical sense.

The function is still history altering future response.

Regeneration gives another.

A damaged organism or tissue does not merely continue whatever growth trajectory happened to be underway.

In many cases it changes growth and patterning in ways that restore viable anatomy.

Again, the mechanism is not a conscious body image.

The function includes error correction across many components.

Physarum adds another.

Its network can reorganize around resources and obstacles.

Repeated experience with a harmless aversive condition can alter later behavior.

Physical state can store part of that history.

No one example earns the word intelligence by itself.

Together they make the sudden-switch story harder to defend.

Evolution had access to memory-like state, feedback, error correction, and adaptive allocation before it had anything resembling a human person.

That suggests a compositional history.

Old loops become inputs to new loops.

A cell regulates ion concentrations.

Cells coordinate to regulate tissue form.

Nervous systems coordinate sensing and action across bodies.

Social groups coordinate information across individuals.

Language coordinates representations across minds.

Institutions coordinate activity across generations.

At every step, the scale changes.

So does the danger of treating the larger system as a literal mind.

A tissue can have distributed control without being conscious.

A colony can have collective behavior without containing one colony-wide point of view.

A market can allocate resources without knowing what it is doing.

An institution can retain memory in records without remembering as a person remembers.

Functional continuity does not imply phenomenal continuity.

This is why the unit of analysis matters.

Ask where the state is stored.

Ask what boundary contains the feedback loop.

Ask which perturbation the system corrects.

Ask what timescale matters.

The “agent” may be different for different capacities.

In bacterial chemotaxis, the relevant unit may be a cell and its signaling network.

In a biofilm, coordination may require the community.

In regeneration, the relevant target can span tissue larger than one cell.

In immune memory, the altered future response is distributed across populations and molecular processes.

In a social animal, memory and decision can exist both within individuals and across the group.

No rule says one natural boundary must contain every useful description.

Humans dislike this because persons provide such convenient packages.

My skin gives a visible edge.

My nervous system integrates much of my sensing and action.

My autobiography gives continuity.

My language gives report.

My legal system gives me a name.

These layers align strongly enough that “the agent” feels obvious.

Biology below persons is less polite.

Boundaries overlap.

Control loops nest.

Information crosses scales.

One cell can be both an autonomous living unit and part of a larger organism whose goals constrain it.

A tissue can correct local damage in service of body-level organization.

A microbiome can alter host physiology while containing many independent lineages.

The world is full of partially nested agents.

Calling them all minds would be reckless.

Pretending the nesting does not matter would be equally poor biology.

This is one place where Michael Levin’s bioelectric work and related basal-cognition arguments are most interesting and most vulnerable to overstatement.

The research program emphasizes that electrical signaling among cells can coordinate development and regeneration, and proposes that evolution can scale control by connecting smaller competent units into larger goal-directed networks.

That is a powerful hypothesis.

It should remain a hypothesis at the level where interpretation exceeds direct experiment.

The experiments showing that membrane-voltage manipulations can alter patterning are evidence.

The claim that bioelectric networks form a general “cognitive glue” from physiology to mind is a broader theoretical interpretation.

A responsible book needs both sentences.

The temptation is to skip from one to the other because the story is beautiful.

Beauty is not a replication.

Still, the broader idea points to an important evolutionary possibility.

A new cognitive scale may arise not by replacing all older competence, but by coordinating it.

Multicellularity makes this visible.

A multicellular organism contains cells that retain local sensing, metabolism, stress response, and internal regulation.

Development adds systems that coordinate differentiation and geometry.

Nervous systems add faster long-range signaling and specialized integration.

The higher level does not begin from dead parts.

It begins from active parts that have to be recruited, constrained, and synchronized.

This changes how we imagine the origin of minds.

The standard cartoon draws a ladder.

Single cell.

Simple animal.

Brain.

Big brain.

Human.

The ladder suggests that each lower stage is an incomplete version of the one above.

Evolution is more tangled.

Lineages branch.

Capacities are lost as well as gained.

Complexity can increase in one dimension and decrease in another.

A parasite can discard machinery its host supplies.

A social insect colony can coordinate at scale while individual workers remain behaviorally specialized.

An octopus can evolve sophisticated behavior through nervous-system architecture very different from a vertebrate’s.

A bacterium can remain a bacterium and be extraordinarily successful.

There is no obligation to become a person.

This matters for extraterrestrial life.

If we imagine intelligence as a summit, we will search for mountain climbers.

We will ask how often life evolves brains.

How often brains evolve language.

How often language evolves technology.

Those remain excellent questions.

But a capacity-based view adds others.

How often does life evolve reliable memory?

How often does it evolve distributed error correction?

How often can organisms alter behavior within a lifetime?

How often do collectives integrate information across individuals?

How often does control scale from cells to larger bodies?

How often do environmental perturbations change the future response of the system?

These questions can have answers even on worlds that never produce a person.

A planet could explore a huge region of adaptive design space while never approaching human-style sapience.

That is the astronomical consequence of intelligence before persons.

The phrase does not mean every bacterium is a tiny human.

It means the history of intelligence-like capability may begin before the history of minds we recognize.

There is another reason to prefer profiles over ladders.

Different capacities can evolve on different clocks.

Memory can improve without better locomotion.

Sensing can become exquisitely sensitive while action remains slow.

Coordination can expand across a colony while individual learning stays limited.

A nervous system can gain speed before it gains flexible representation.

Social learning can amplify behavioral repertoires without changing the genome that built the learner.

Culture can then move useful state across generations faster than genes alone.

Once these dimensions are separated, the phrase “more intelligent” becomes less informative.

More intelligent at what?

Across what timescale?

Under what perturbation?

With how much transfer to a new problem?

At what energetic cost?

With what dependence on a group?

A person is remarkable partly because many dimensions coincide.

We sense across several modalities.

We carry long memories.

We can learn rules, transfer abstractions, imagine counterfactuals, coordinate through language, and outsource memory to artifacts.

The package is dense.

Its density can fool us into thinking the capacities were born together.

Evolutionary history says otherwise even when the exact ancestry of each capacity remains uncertain.

The practical research program is therefore comparative rather than ceremonial.

Do not hunt for the first creature worthy of the word *intelligence*.

Measure how capacities are distributed across living systems.

Map which mechanisms recur and which are independent inventions.

Ask when one capacity becomes a substrate for another.

Ask when centralization helps and when distributed control is more robust.

Ask which forms of memory survive damage, dormancy, reproduction, or reorganization.

Ask how control changes when a system becomes larger than any one component’s sensing range.

Those questions can be answered without agreeing on one grand definition.

They also produce the kind of evidence an extraterrestrial theory can use.

A universal theory should be built from relationships that might survive different chemistry: state, feedback, perturbation, error, coordination, prediction, and adaptation.

The molecules can change.

The control problem remains recognizable.

This is not a claim that evolution must converge on the same solutions elsewhere.

It is a claim about how to ask.

If alien life uses unfamiliar substrates, names tied too tightly to Earth anatomy will fail first.

A feedback relation is more portable than a neuron.

A stored state is more portable than DNA methylation.

A perturbation-response curve is more portable than a slime mold.

Operational language is our best chance of carrying the question farther than the example.

There is a strong counterargument.

Perhaps bundling all these capacities under one conceptual family creates false continuity.

A bacterium’s temporal comparison, an immune system’s memory, a planarian’s regeneration, and a human’s reasoning may be so mechanistically different that calling them stages in one history tells us little.

This objection deserves weight.

Similarity of vocabulary can be manufactured.

Everything can be redescribed as “information processing.”

Everything can be said to have “goals” if survival or persistence is defined as a goal.

Everything can be said to “remember” if any state has a relaxation time.

At that point, the continuum becomes a fog.

The answer is not to abandon comparison.

It is to compare dimensions rather than identities.

Do these systems retain state?

For how long?

Through what substrate?

Does experience alter later performance?

Is the alteration specific?

Can the system generalize?

What errors does it correct?

What perturbations defeat it?

How many components must coordinate?

How far does the coordination extend?

What is the cost?

What is inherited versus acquired?

The result is not one intelligence score.

It is a profile.

Two systems can share one capacity and differ radically on another.

A bacterium and a human both use prior state in action, but the content, timescale, mechanism, and flexibility are incomparable in scale.

That is fine.

The common dimension is real without erasing the difference.

This approach also avoids another trap: treating evolution as progress toward generality.

Specialized intelligence is still intelligence-like competence when the specialization is real.

A migratory animal can solve a navigation problem it cannot explain.

An immune system can classify molecular patterns within a domain that has nothing to do with geometry.

A slime mold can reorganize a transport network without doing arithmetic.

Humans value generality because our own cognition travels across domains.

Evolution often rewards local excellence.

An alien biosphere might be full of narrow adaptive capabilities distributed among organisms and ecologies without any system combining them into a general-purpose reasoner.

That world would not be cognitively empty.

It would be differently organized.

Machines from the previous chapter make the same point from the other direction.

Engineers can assemble narrow capabilities into systems.

Perception can feed planning.

Planning can feed action.

Memory can change later policy.

Fault diagnosis can trigger recovery.

The components can be combined without a moment when personhood becomes an engineering requirement.

Evolution also assembles.

The analogy stops there; natural selection is not a designer with a blueprint.

But the compositional fact survives.

Complex capability can be built from interacting processes whose individual capacities are narrower than the whole.

At some point, perhaps, a particular organization supports subjective experience.

At some point, perhaps, self-modeling becomes rich enough to deserve a new category.

At some point, language and culture create a form of sapience with properties no earlier system possessed.

This book does not need to deny thresholds.

It needs to deny that thresholds erase ancestry.

Flight is a threshold capability.

Feathers existed before powered flight in birds.

Language is a threshold capability.

Auditory processing, vocal control, social learning, memory, and categorization have deeper histories.

Sapience can be new while its ingredients are old.

That is intelligence before persons.

There is a moral reason to be cautious here, although it is not the book’s scientific claim.

People have often used rankings of intelligence to rank the worth of humans and other animals.

A continuum can be abused as easily as a hierarchy.

Nothing in this chapter creates a moral score.

Operational competence is not moral status.

Memory span does not determine dignity.

Problem-solving performance does not determine rights.

The scientific question is how control capacities are organized.

The ethical question is what beings deserve what forms of consideration.

They can inform each other without becoming the same axis.

Keeping them separate also helps with extraterrestrial speculation.

If we detect a history-dependent biosignature, we should not rush into moral language about a planetary mind.

If we detect a technosignature, we should not assume persons like us.

If we eventually encounter organisms directly, richer evidence may force richer categories.

The correct category should arrive after the observation, not before it.

This is the habit the book has been trying to build.

Mechanism before metaphor.

Capability before character.

Observation before ontology.

A person is one possible organization of intelligence-like capacities.

A civilization is another level of organization layered on persons.

A machine can reproduce some capacities through engineering.

A biosphere can distribute them across lineages and scales.

The universe does not have to package competence in the form most legible to human social intuition.

That brings us to the veil.

Why is the person-shaped package so difficult to see around?

Why do slow systems look stupid?

Why do distributed systems look mindless until their outputs resemble ours?

Why does speech persuade us more quickly than repair?

Why does a face do more cognitive work in our imagination than a feedback loop?

The answers are partly scientific.

They are also psychological.

We are detectors built by evolution, and our detectors have a selection function too.

Before asking what the universe hides, we have to ask what our own minds filter out.
