# The Organism That Became a Map

Put food at two exits of a maze and release *Physarum polycephalum* into the corridors.

The organism spreads.

At first, that is the least surprising thing it can do.

The plasmodial form of *Physarum* is a giant single cell containing many nuclei. It extends through space as a network of tubes carrying protoplasm. Food attracts it. Unfavorable conditions repel it. The network grows, retracts, thickens, and thins.

Give such an organism a maze and it will explore more than one route.

Then something changes.

In the experiment that made the behavior famous, Toshiyuki Nakagaki, Hiroyasu Yamada, and Ágota Tóth allowed *Physarum* to occupy paths through a maze and placed food at two locations. Over time, branches that did not contribute to an efficient connection receded. The surviving organism linked the food sources through a short route.

The headline became irresistible.

Slime mold solves maze.

It is not wrong.

It is also easy to hear too much inside it.

The organism did not inspect a map from above.

It did not name the corridors.

It did not represent all possible routes and compare their lengths in a symbolic workspace.

It did not know that human beings had constructed a maze or that the experimenters considered a shorter path the correct answer.

The maze existed in the world.

The organism became part of the maze.

That may be the more important result.

A human solves a maze partly by separating representation from movement. We can stand still and imagine a path. We can draw a graph. We can mark dead ends. We can search possibilities without physically occupying each one.

*Physarum* solves differently.

It extends its body into the possibility space.

The transport network is both explorer and memory.

Routes that carry useful flow are reinforced.

Routes that do not are abandoned.

The organism does not need a model of the maze distinct from the material used to traverse it.

Its body is the model.

That sentence should not be romanticized.

A physical system embodying information is still a physical system.

The point is not that slime mold possesses a secret cartographic consciousness.

The point is that representation does not always have to look like a picture held inside a head.

Sometimes the state of the system is enough.

A bent paperclip contains information about the force that deformed it.

A tree ring contains information about growth conditions.

An immune repertoire contains information about exposure.

A slime-mold network contains information about which routes have been explored, fed, reinforced, or withdrawn.

The question is what the stored state can do next.

In *Physarum*, network state changes later movement and transport.

That makes the geometry causal.

The organism that became a map also became a machine for editing the map.

A map, in the ordinary sense, is a representation that can be consulted while the territory is absent.

That definition excludes *Physarum*.

Its network usually remains coupled to the territory it is traversing.

Food is not a dot on paper.

It is a chemical condition at a location.

A barrier is not an edge in a graph.

It is material the organism cannot or will not cross.

This makes the system look less cognitive by human standards and more interesting by ecological ones.

Most organisms do not need detachable representations of every problem they face.

A root system does not need a soil atlas before growing toward water.

A bacterium does not need a concentration map before moving up a gradient.

A migrating cell can respond to a chemical field as the field is encountered.

The world itself can supply information on demand.

Brains become useful partly when action must be separated from immediate contact.

An animal can remember a water hole after leaving it.

It can plan a route around a mountain before reaching the obstruction.

It can model a predator that is temporarily hidden.

Those are powerful capabilities.

They are not prerequisites for every kind of successful navigation.

This gives us a useful hierarchy of representation without making it a hierarchy of worth.

At one extreme, a system can be driven directly by current conditions.

A little farther along, its present structure can preserve consequences of previous conditions.

Farther still, it may contain internal states that stand in for absent features of the environment.

Eventually a system may manipulate those representations offline, compare imagined alternatives, and communicate them symbolically.

*Physarum* sits somewhere in the early part of that space.

The exact placement depends on which experiment we discuss.

Maze pruning can be explained largely through embodied exploration and adaptive transport.

Habituation requires history to remain causally active after the immediate stimulus changes.

Neither result establishes symbolic representation.

That distinction matters because the book will later ask what evidence could be visible remotely.

A planet-scale adaptive system might never build an internal model detached from its environment.

It may not need one.

If its surroundings change slowly and its body is spread through the same gradients it must regulate, direct coupling can outperform abstraction.

Human observers could misclassify such a system as mere environmental response precisely because it has no obvious boundary between organism, memory, and terrain.

The boundary is unusually blurry in *Physarum*.

Its tubes are body.

They are transport infrastructure.

They record use.

They influence future flow.

They are also temporary.

A route can disappear when it is no longer useful.

Human maps are usually cheap to keep after a journey ends.

Biological maps can be metabolically expensive.

The organism has to decide, through its dynamics, which history deserves continued material.

That is a different form of compression.

Unused branches are forgotten by being dismantled.

Useful routes are remembered by being reinforced.

Nothing guarantees that this language is the best biological description.

It is helpful because it points to a measurable relation between past flow and future structure.

The metaphor should stop there.

A tube does not remember a journey the way a traveler does.

But a traveler’s memory and a tube’s reinforcement share one abstract property: history changes the future choice set.

That property may be common long before minds are.

The Tokyo experiment made this property visible at a larger scale.

In 2010, Atsushi Tero and colleagues placed food sources in positions corresponding to cities around Tokyo and allowed *Physarum* to form a connecting network. The resulting biological network was compared with the region’s rail system.

The slime mold did not reproduce Tokyo rail line for line.

That was never the serious claim.

The researchers evaluated tradeoffs including total network cost, transport efficiency, and fault tolerance. The biological network achieved performance comparable in important respects to the engineered infrastructure, and the team derived a mathematical model inspired by the tube-adaptation dynamics.

This is where the story usually crosses from biology into mythology.

A brainless organism redesigned Tokyo.

Again, the sentence is entertaining and wrong in exactly the useful way.

The experimenters chose the food locations.

They chose the scale.

They created the substrate.

They selected what counted as cost, efficiency, and fault tolerance.

Tokyo’s actual rail network exists inside terrain, law, land ownership, politics, construction history, ridership, finance, legacy lines, disaster planning, and human neighborhoods.

The slime mold did not solve those constraints.

It solved a simplified network problem presented physically.

The simplification is not an embarrassment.

Science advances by removing variables.

The danger comes when the removed variables sneak back into the conclusion.

The proper claim is narrower and stronger.

Local biological adaptation can generate transport networks that negotiate several competing properties without centralized design.

That is already enough to trouble our intuitions about problem solving.

A network problem sounds like something that needs a planner because human network design often does.

Someone studies demand.

Someone chooses links.

Someone evaluates redundancy.

Someone pays for construction.

Someone has a spreadsheet.

In *Physarum*, the calculation is distributed through flow.

Tubes experiencing more flow can strengthen.

Underused routes can shrink.

The network’s own operation changes its structure.

Use becomes design pressure.

That is a powerful architecture because it links allocation to evidence.

The organism does not need to predict traffic before building every route.

Traffic helps determine which routes persist.

Biology frequently works this way.

Bone remodels partly in response to load.

Blood vessels respond to local signals and demand.

Neural connections change with activity.

Immune populations expand after stimulation.

None of those mechanisms are identical to slime-mold tubes.

The family resemblance is feedback between use and structure.

A designed machine often begins with architecture and then runs.

An adaptive biological system can partly design itself by running.

This can look like foresight when viewed afterward.

The network ends up economical.

The unnecessary branches are gone.

The paths appear selected.

The final state tempts us to imagine a search that happened in an invisible central place.

But the search was visible.

It was the spreading.

This is an important correction to the way intelligence is often described.

We tend to divide thought from action.

First decide.

Then act.

Brains can support that sequence, but even human behavior is messier. We probe, move, look, update, touch, ask, retreat, and let action create new information.

*Physarum* collapses the distinction almost completely.

Exploration is growth.

Evaluation is flow.

Memory is altered structure and chemistry.

Decision is differential persistence of routes.

Action and computation are the same physical process viewed at different scales.

Whether *computation* is the best word is optional.

The mechanism does not depend on it.

The larger point is that the organism can produce useful solutions without internalizing the task the way a person would.

There is also a scale problem in the word *choice*.

At a branch point, we can describe the organism as choosing one route over another.

But there may be no instant when a decision occurs.

Both routes can exist for a while.

Flow differs.

Tube diameters change.

One branch gradually becomes more important as another recedes.

The apparent choice is the end of a continuous competition among pathways.

This is unlike a person pointing left.

It may be more like a market in which one supplier slowly captures demand or a river system in which one channel deepens as another silts.

The absence of a decision moment does not erase selection among alternatives.

It changes what selection means.

That distinction may matter enormously for alien systems.

We are trained to recognize agency through discrete acts.

A hand moves.

A signal begins.

A machine switches state.

A creature turns.

Distributed organisms may choose through slow reweighting.

An option becomes thicker.

Another becomes chemically suppressed.

A route attracts more flow.

A structure persists one season longer than its competitor.

If we only look for sudden acts produced by a central controller, gradual distributed selection may remain beneath the threshold of recognition.

Time scale can hide agency as effectively as distance.

The slower the process, the easier it is to call the outcome scenery.

This gives non-sapient intellect one of its clearest cases.

The system is not merely maintaining a set point.

It is exploring a changing spatial environment.

It allocates material.

It compares possibilities indirectly through their consequences.

It abandons poor routes.

It preserves useful ones.

Its later state depends on what happened earlier.

Those properties together are harder to dismiss as passive self-organization.

Harder does not mean impossible.

There are good reasons for skepticism.

One is that many physical systems minimize quantities without being intelligent.

Soap films form surfaces that can solve geometric optimization problems.

Elastic materials settle into low-energy configurations.

Electrical currents distribute according to circuit properties.

If a clever experimenter encodes a mathematical problem into the environment, nature may produce an answer because the governing physics already embody the relevant optimization.

The result can be useful computation without useful grounds for calling the material intelligent.

This is why the slime mold’s biology matters.

The system does not merely relax once to an equilibrium.

It grows.

It senses chemical conditions.

It redistributes internal transport.

Its tubular architecture changes with use.

It can recover, explore, and modify future response.

In later work, researchers found evidence of habituation: repeated exposure to an aversive but harmless substance could reduce avoidance, with responsiveness returning after the stimulus was withdrawn.

That result pushes beyond path formation.

Experience altered future behavior.

The 2016 habituation experiments were designed to distinguish the change from simple fatigue or sensory adaptation, including tests of stimulus specificity and spontaneous recovery.

This does not prove a mind.

It does make the word *learning* less metaphorical.

Learning, at its minimum, is a relatively lasting change in behavior resulting from experience.

A nervous system is one mechanism for producing it.

Not necessarily the only one.

Research on *Physarum* has even explored where such a memory could reside.

A 2019 study reported that slime molds habituated to salt could retain the altered response through a dormant stage and that retained salt itself was associated with the memory-like state. The authors described a circulating memory based on absorbed chemical.

This is almost offensively physical compared with ordinary human intuitions about memory.

Good.

Memory has always been physical.

Brains simply hide the physics behind experience.

In a slime mold, the substrate can be easier to see.

A chemical accumulated during exposure changes what the organism does later.

No recollection is required.

The system carries the past forward in matter.

The beauty of that result is also its warning.

If a memory can be implemented by retained salt, then calling memory evidence of a mind is plainly too strong.

The word names a functional relationship before it names an experience.

Past condition.

Persistent physical change.

Altered future response.

That sequence may be enough.

This is why *Physarum* belongs in a book about the search for intelligence beyond sapience.

It forces a choice.

Either reserve words such as learning, memory, choice, and problem solving for systems that resemble human psychology, or define the terms operationally enough that unfamiliar substrates can qualify.

The first option protects intuition.

The second is more useful scientifically.

Operational definitions can still be demanding.

A random change is not learning.

A temporary chemical effect is not necessarily memory.

A path that happens to be short is not evidence of optimization.

A network that copies one successful geometry under one experimental setup does not establish a general problem-solving capacity.

Each claim has to survive alternative explanations.

The maze experiment should therefore be read as one behavior in a larger portfolio, not as a magic trick that settles the case.

The organism explores and prunes routes.

The rail-network experiment shows a tradeoff among cost, efficiency, and resilience in one constructed environment.

Habituation work suggests experience-dependent behavioral change.

Chemical work proposes a substrate for persistence.

Together the evidence becomes more interesting than any viral photograph.

The most important counterargument may be the mathematical model itself.

Tero and colleagues showed that core features of adaptive network formation could be captured by relatively simple rules.

To someone searching for intelligence, that can feel deflationary.

If simple local equations can reproduce the behavior, perhaps there was never anything intelligent to explain.

That conclusion would be too fast.

A simple rule can generate a complex capability.

The existence of a concise mechanism tells us how the capability works, not whether the capability is real.

Bird flight did not stop being flight when aerodynamics became mathematical.

Memory does not stop being memory when molecular mechanisms are identified.

If a network can adaptively allocate resources through local flux-sensitive rules, the simplicity of the rule is an engineering virtue.

The deeper question is whether the resulting system meets the operational criteria we care about.

Does history affect behavior?

Yes, in some tested contexts.

Does the system reconfigure after environmental change?

Yes.

Does it allocate resources among competing routes?

Yes.

Does it always find the globally optimal solution?

No.

That last answer matters.

Biological intelligence is often sold to us through spectacular demonstrations and then judged by a standard no organism could meet.

Humans do not always find shortest paths.

Animals make costly mistakes.

Organizations lock into bad infrastructure.

Learning can produce maladaptive habits.

A slime mold should not need omniscience to count as competent.

The 2010 Tokyo work explicitly involved tradeoffs.

A network with minimal total length may be fragile.

A highly redundant network may be expensive.

A very direct network may require too much material.

There is no universal best design without specifying priorities and constraints.

The organism lands on one compromise generated by its dynamics.

That is more biologically plausible than global perfection.

It is also more relevant to extraterrestrial life.

Real environments will not present one objective at a time.

A living network might need to gather resources, avoid toxins, survive damage, reproduce, and remain adaptable to change.

The best structure for one demand can be poor for another.

Non-sapient intellect, if the category holds, should be expected to look satisficing and path-dependent.

Evolution builds systems that persist, not proofs that win optimization contests.

The slime mold’s mistakes are therefore part of the evidence.

It can overexplore.

It can be guided by attractants into arrangements the experimenter chose.

Its performance depends on environmental geometry and parameters.

Biologically inspired algorithms derived from its behavior can produce suboptimal solutions, and later engineering work has shown that extra control can improve on some networks generated by pure Physarum-like adaptation.

The natural process is not a universal oracle.

Good.

Oracles are usually a sign that a story has outrun the science.

A limited adaptive mechanism is more useful.

It gives us something to compare.

Imagine an alien organism spread across a mineral surface.

It has no head.

No eyes.

No central processor.

It grows toward chemical gradients and retracts from harmful conditions.

Internal flows redistribute material.

Repeated exposure changes later sensitivity.

Network geometry records which routes have mattered.

Would we call it intelligent?

At close range, perhaps.

At telescope range, we might not even call it alive.

This is the observational problem waiting behind the terrestrial science.

The capabilities that look most intelligence-like in *Physarum* are dynamic.

You have to watch the network form.

Change the food.

Add a barrier.

Repeat a stimulus.

Measure whether past exposure changes the next response.

A still image loses the argument.

Astronomy is full of still images.

Even time-series observations compress extraordinary systems into a few variables.

We may see atmospheric composition, brightness, spectra, rotation, seasonal change, perhaps surface maps for the nearest worlds in some future era.

We are unlikely to watch a slime mold cross an alien maze.

So why care?

Because categories shape what measurements are worth building.

If intelligence is defined as technological communication, we build instruments for signals.

If life is defined through known biochemistry, we build instruments for familiar molecules.

If adaptive organization itself becomes a target, we ask different questions.

Can we detect response to perturbation?

Can we distinguish regulation from passive relaxation?

Can we observe changes that imply stored state?

Can spatial flows reorganize in ways that preserve function?

Those questions may lead nowhere.

They may collapse back into biosignature science.

They may prove impossible at interstellar distance.

The book should be prepared for that outcome.

But the Earth case has already done its conceptual work.

A map does not have to exist inside a mind.

A body can become the representation.

A route can be evaluated by being used.

A memory can be chemical.

A solution can emerge through the same material that explores the problem.

The maze-solving headline survives, but in a changed form.

The slime mold does not solve the maze like us.

That is exactly why it matters.

If extraterrestrial intelligence exists below sapience, the mistake will not be that we failed to recognize another human.

It will be that we only knew how to recognize human-shaped answers.
