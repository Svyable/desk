# Memory Without a Mind

An *Escherichia coli* cell swimming through water cannot see the gradient it is trying to climb.

It is too small.

The cell is not stretched across enough space to compare the concentration of an attractant at its front with the concentration at its back in the way a long animal might compare two sensors. At bacterial scale, the useful difference lies mostly in time.

Was the world better a moment ago?

Is it better now?

The bacterium swims, samples, and changes the probability that it will keep moving in roughly the same direction or tumble into a new one.

That requires memory.

Not memory in the autobiographical sense.

A bacterium does not need to recall where it had lunch.

It needs its present response to contain a trace of the recent past.

In 1986, James Segall, Steven Block, and Howard Berg tested this problem directly in *E. coli*. Their experiments showed that wild-type cells make short-term temporal comparisons extending several seconds into the past. The response did not depend only on the concentration at one instant. Recent stimulation was weighted against earlier stimulation.

Mutants defective in the receptor methylation system lost that normal short-term comparison and adapted abnormally.

The mechanism has since become one of the best-studied examples of molecular memory in biology.

Chemotaxis receptors change their methylation state as stimulation continues. That state alters how the signaling pathway responds to later conditions. The cell therefore carries a moving biochemical record of what it has just experienced.

The record is short.

That does not make it less real.

A four-second memory can be enough for a bacterium whose world changes over micrometers.

Human intuition makes duration look like importance because our own useful memories can last decades. Biology has no obligation to preserve that scale.

Memory should be defined by what it does.

A prior state leaves a physical trace.

The trace persists after the event that created it.

The trace changes a later response.

That is the minimal structure.

Everything beyond it is implementation.

Brains use changing synapses, molecular cascades, network states, recurrent activity, altered gene expression, and many other mechanisms.

Immune systems use changing populations of cells and durable molecular programs.

A slime mold can carry consequences of repeated exposure through its chemistry.

A bacterium can alter receptor methylation.

A regenerating worm may preserve a consequence of training through the loss and reconstruction of a head.

The substrates are so different that grouping them under one word feels reckless.

That discomfort is useful.

It forces us to ask whether the word *memory* names a mechanism or a relationship through time.

If memory required the same mechanism everywhere, even two parts of a human nervous system might fail the test.

Biologists already use the term functionally.

Immunological memory is the clearest case because nobody mistakes it for recollection.

After an immune system encounters a pathogen or vaccine, some of its cellular and molecular populations change. A later encounter can produce a faster, larger, or otherwise altered response.

The system has history.

The history is stored in material.

Memory B cells and T cells do not sit inside the body remembering a fever.

The future response depends on the past anyway.

This distinction helps remove a category error that follows us through the book.

Experience is one thing.

State dependence is another.

A conscious memory includes state dependence, but state dependence does not imply consciousness.

The human package makes the two arrive together so often that language welds them.

Biology pries them apart.

A receptor can carry history without experience.

A population can carry history without a narrator.

A tissue can carry history without a brain.

This is why the phrase *memory without a mind* is not meant as a paradox.

It is a reminder that minds are one architecture for storing consequential history.

They are not the definition of consequential history.

The hard part is preventing the category from becoming trivial.

A footprint stores information about a person who walked through mud.

A crater stores information about an impact.

A bent branch stores information about a load.

A sediment layer stores information about conditions when it formed.

If every persistent consequence counts as memory, the universe remembers almost everything.

That definition would not help us identify non-sapient intellect.

The book needs a stricter test.

The stored state must participate in later regulation, choice among responses, or changed performance.

The footprint does not use its shape to modify how it reacts to the next walker.

The bacterium does use receptor state to modify future movement.

That difference is small in wording and large in mechanism.

Memory becomes interesting when it closes a loop.

Past event.

Persistent state.

Future behavior.

That loop appears at many scales.

Consider bacterial chemotaxis again.

The cell is not trying to preserve a permanent record.

Permanent memory would be a disadvantage.

A bacterium swimming through a gradient needs to forget.

If the receptor state held yesterday's concentration too strongly, the comparison would be useless. The relevant past is the recent past.

Memory has a timescale matched to a task.

This is an important correction to the cultural idea that better memory means more retention.

Sometimes useful memory is rapid erasure.

Adaptation in the chemotaxis system lets the bacterium reset sensitivity as background concentration changes. The cell can keep detecting differences rather than saturating on an absolute level.

The memory is therefore inseparable from forgetting.

One molecular process modifies the current response based on recent history and then continually rewrites that history as the organism moves.

A brain does this too, only with more machinery and more layers.

The similarity should not be inflated into equivalence.

A bacterium is not doing episodic recall.

It is performing temporal comparison.

The mechanism earns the narrow word *memory* because prior input alters present responsiveness through a persistent molecular state.

That is enough.

The slime mold offers a different boundary.

In habituation experiments, *Physarum polycephalum* repeatedly exposed to an innocuous but aversive substance became less avoidant. The change was stimulus-specific enough and recoverable enough after withdrawal to satisfy criteria the researchers used to distinguish habituation from simple fatigue.

Later work investigated a physical substrate for that persistence.

Slime molds habituated to salt could retain altered behavior through a dormant stage, and retained sodium was associated with the memory-like state.

A chemical the organism had absorbed became part of what its future behavior depended on.

The result almost seems designed to offend people who think memory should be ethereal.

It is salt.

Or at least, salt is part of the proposed mechanism in that experimental system.

That is a useful humiliation.

Every known memory is physical.

Human experience makes the physicality easy to forget because we encounter memory from the inside.

We experience the recalled face, not the receptor trafficking.

We experience the melody, not the synaptic changes.

A slime mold has no obligation to hide the substrate from us.

The planarian case is harder.

Tal Shomrat and Michael Levin trained planarians in an automated environment, then removed their heads. After the animals regenerated new heads and brains, prior training affected later performance in a savings-style test.

The result is provocative because the obvious candidate storage organ had been replaced.

It is also precisely the kind of finding that should make a careful writer slower.

The experiment does not prove that memory was stored in body tissue outside the brain.

It shows that a consequence of prior experience survived a regenerative interval and affected later behavior.

Possible explanations include persistent changes in remaining nervous tissue, non-neural biochemical or epigenetic state, effects on the way the new nervous system developed, or combinations of mechanisms.

The result is evidence for persistence.

The storage claim remains open.

This distinction is the whole discipline.

Memory is often inferred from behavior because the state itself cannot be read directly.

If an organism learns faster the second time, responds differently after prior exposure, or alters a choice after an interval, the scientist infers that some physical consequence persisted.

The next question is where.

Then how.

Then whether the apparent memory could be explained by something simpler.

The vocabulary should follow that order.

Observed difference first.

Mechanism second.

Grand interpretation last.

The 2026 literature on learning and memory in microorganisms makes this problem explicit. Researchers are increasingly willing to test habituation, associative-like behavior, and persistent state in organisms without nervous systems, while arguments continue over terminology, controls, and whether some celebrated examples meet the standards used in animal-learning research.

That disagreement is not a weakness in the field.

It is what a boundary science should look like.

If every unusual response were immediately accepted as cognition, the category would dissolve.

If every non-neural mechanism were rejected by definition, the experiment would be pointless.

Operational criteria let evidence do the work.

The same standard should apply across substrates.

Did the response change because of experience?

How long did the change last?

Was it specific to the stimulus?

Did it recover after withdrawal?

Could fatigue explain it?

Could damage explain it?

Could simple receptor saturation explain it?

Could selection among individuals explain an apparent population memory?

Could the effect be reproduced?

These questions are more useful than asking whether the organism “really remembers.”

The phrase *really remembers* usually smuggles in a human prototype.

Science can ask narrower questions.

The narrower questions travel farther.

They also expose a problem for extraterrestrial search.

Memory is easiest to establish when we can perturb a system repeatedly.

Expose.

Wait.

Re-expose.

Compare.

At interstellar distance we may not control the stimulus.

We may have to rely on natural experiments.

A stellar flare.

A season.

A volcanic event.

An impact.

A drought-like shift in atmospheric or surface conditions.

If a distant living system exists, its response may contain history.

But geology contains history too.

So does climate.

An ocean remembers heat because heat content persists.

An ice sheet remembers previous temperatures through its geometry.

A soil remembers rainfall through moisture.

A magnetic material can show hysteresis.

None of those facts imply life.

Memory alone will not save the astronomical thesis.

The question is whether memory is coupled to adaptive regulation.

Does the stored state change a later response in a way that preserves organization, improves performance, reallocates resources, or alters sensitivity?

Even that is not sufficient by itself.

Nonliving control systems can be designed to do the same.

A thermostat stores a setting.

A feedback controller integrates error.

A machine can retain state.

The category we are building is not “anything with memory.”

It is a constellation of capabilities embedded in living or life-like adaptive organization.

The word *embedded* matters.

Memory is useful because it changes what the system can do.

A bacterial chemotaxis pathway without adaptation cannot compare the recent past normally.

An immune system without durable responsive populations loses part of its second-response advantage.

A slime mold without persistent consequences of exposure cannot habituate in the same sense.

The memory is not an archive beside the system.

It is part of the system.

This is also true of human culture at a larger scale.

An institution remembers through records, procedures, norms, people, buildings, software, and habits.

No one document contains the whole organizational past.

Yet a previous accident can change future engineering standards.

A failed launch can alter checklists.

A financial crisis can change capital rules.

The memory becomes visible when behavior differs later.

Institutions remind us that storage and retrieval are separate problems.

An archive can contain a fact nobody uses.

A database can preserve a record no process consults.

A forgotten lesson can remain perfectly stored.

Biology has the same distinction.

A molecular state is only functional memory for a particular question if it can influence a later process.

Information can persist without being retrieved.

Retrieval can fail even when storage survives.

That point is especially important for the planarian result.

If some consequence of training survives outside the regenerated brain, the new nervous system still has to become coupled to that consequence in a way that alters behavior.

Persistence alone would not explain the savings effect.

The body has to make the history causally available.

This turns memory into a relation among storage, access, and action.

Human computer language tempts us to imagine a neat address where a bit sits until fetched.

Biological memory is often messier.

A concentration changes a signaling threshold.

A cell population changes the probability of a response.

A chromatin state changes which genes are easier to express.

A network topology changes how activity propagates.

A tissue voltage pattern changes developmental behavior.

A tube diameter changes future flow.

The “record” may be identical to the system's changed disposition.

There may be nothing to read except the changed behavior itself.

This is another place where minds mislead us.

Conscious recall makes memory feel like content.

I remember a room.

I remember a sentence.

I remember a person.

The memory appears to have an object.

Many non-neural memories may be closer to calibration than content.

The system has become different because something happened.

It does not need to contain a detachable representation of what happened.

The bacterial receptor does not store a narrative of the attractant.

Its methylation state changes sensitivity.

The immune system does not store a picture of the pathogen.

Its cellular and molecular composition changes the next encounter.

The distinction between content memory and dispositional memory may help keep the book out of trouble.

Non-sapient intellect does not require the first.

The second can already support adaptive behavior.

There is one more trap hidden in population-level memory.

A population can appear to remember even when no individual cell does.

Suppose exposure kills the most sensitive organisms and leaves resistant ones behind. The second exposure then produces a different response. History has changed the population, but the mechanism is selection, not within-individual learning.

That distinction is familiar in antibiotic resistance.

A bacterial population can become harder to kill because resistant variants survive and reproduce. Calling the population “trained” may be rhetorically tempting, but it would conceal the causal story.

The same problem appears in immune populations, microbial communities, and even experiments on habituation.

Did each individual alter its response?

Did the composition of the population change?

Did the environment change?

Did the stimulus become physically weaker?

Did the organisms become tired, damaged, or depleted?

A valid memory experiment has to separate these possibilities.

This is why habituation criteria are stricter than “response got smaller.”

A reduced response can come from sensory fatigue.

It can come from motor fatigue.

It can come from injury.

It can come from adaptation at a receptor that simply resets sensitivity.

It can come from selection.

Habituation is stronger when the response recovers after time, when a different stimulus can restore responsiveness, when the change shows some specificity, and when the organism remains capable of performing the behavior.

The details vary among systems.

The principle does not.

A memory claim is a causal claim about why present behavior differs.

That principle helps sort bacterial chemotaxis too.

Chemotactic adaptation is sometimes described as memory and sometimes as adaptation.

Both words can be right at different levels.

The methylation state acts as a memory variable because it carries information about prior stimulation into the present signaling response.

The functional consequence is adaptation because sensitivity is reset as background conditions persist.

Memory is the mechanism's temporal property.

Adaptation is the behavior of the larger control loop.

The terms do not compete.

They answer different questions.

This is useful because biological language often turns into arguments over labels when the mechanisms are actually agreed upon.

Does a bacterium “remember”?

If the question means “does a prior concentration alter current signaling through a persisting molecular state,” yes.

If it means “does the cell have a recollective experience of the prior concentration,” the experiment says nothing of the kind.

A precise answer can be both more permissive and more conservative than intuition.

It permits memory where no brain exists.

It refuses experience where no evidence exists.

The same two-step discipline belongs with immune memory.

A memory B cell can survive for years, but persistence alone is not the whole immune phenomenon.

Re-exposure can trigger clonal expansion, antibody production, affinity-shaped responses, and coordination among cell types.

The stored state matters because it changes a future control process.

Yet the immune system can also misremember in a functional sense.

Prior exposure can bias later responses in ways that are unhelpful.

Cross-reactivity can cause trouble.

Immune memory can contribute to pathology.

A memory is not intelligent because it improves outcomes every time.

It is intelligent-like only in the narrower sense that history modifies future discrimination and response.

That negative case matters.

Books about adaptation tend to define the capability by successful examples.

But a system with memory can become trapped by its past.

A learned avoidance can persist after a threat disappears.

An immune response can overreact.

A developmental state can become difficult to reverse.

An institution can preserve a procedure long after the condition that created it is gone.

History dependence is a resource and a liability.

Forgetting is not failure.

It is part of control.

A useful memory system has to solve at least three problems.

What deserves to be written?

How long should it persist?

When should it stop influencing behavior?

The bacterial answer is often seconds.

The immune answer can be years.

Developmental identity may persist across many cell divisions.

Evolutionary information can persist across geological time.

There is no universally correct retention period.

The timescale has to fit the hazard and the cost of being wrong.

That principle may become important when we imagine alien life.

A world with very slow environmental change might favor memories encoded in structure and population composition.

A world with violent, frequent fluctuations might reward faster resetting.

A system that never forgets can become brittle.

A system that forgets instantly cannot learn.

Memory is therefore not just storage.

It is a policy for allowing the past to remain relevant.

No mind is required for the policy to be implemented physically.

This gives us a better way to think about the evolutionary transition toward nervous systems.

A brain did not introduce history dependence into a previously amnesiac living world.

Life already had ways to let the past alter the future.

What nervous systems did was multiply the speed, capacity, integration, flexibility, and possible content of that process.

A neural animal can remember locations, individuals, threats, sequences, perhaps social relationships.

A bacterium needs a few seconds of chemical history.

Both solve temporal problems.

The gap between them is enormous.

The continuity is still real.

That continuity should not be converted into a ladder.

An immune memory can last longer than many conscious memories and still lack experience.

A bacterial memory can be exquisitely matched to a task and contain almost no transferable content.

A slime mold can show habituation without planning.

Different systems inhabit different coordinates of a capability space.

The useful questions become empirical.

How long?

Stored where?

Updated by what?

Retrieved how?

Changes which behavior?

At what cost?

Under what conditions does it fail?

Those questions can be asked without deciding whether the system has a mind.

That is exactly what makes them useful beyond Earth.

If alien life is found, consciousness may be the hardest property to infer.

History dependence could be easier.

Not easy.

Easier.

We might observe that a system exposed to a recurring perturbation changes its later response.

We might infer persistent state.

We might build a case for adaptive memory long before we could say anything about experience.

That would already move us beyond a static biosignature.

It would tell us that the system is not merely there.

It has a past that matters.

The next problem is more dangerous.

Memory tells us that history can shape response.

It does not tell us what counts as success.

For that, we need a word science has distrusted for good reason.

Goal.
