# Beyond the Scale

For most of these drugs' commercial history, the number that mattered most — to regulators, to insurers, to the patients themselves — was the one on the bathroom scale. That began to change decisively on November 11, 2023, when the New England Journal of Medicine published the results of a trial that had nothing to do with weight loss as its primary goal, even though weight loss was one of the things it measured along the way.

The trial, called SELECT and led by A. Michael Lincoff, enrolled 17,604 adults across 804 sites in 41 countries, every one of them 45 or older, every one of them with a body mass index of 27 or above, and every one of them already living with established cardiovascular disease — but, critically, none of them living with diabetes, the condition every earlier major GLP-1 trial had either required or centered around. Participants were randomized to receive either semaglutide 2.4mg, the Wegovy dose, or placebo, and followed for a median of roughly forty months. The trial's primary endpoint was not weight lost; it was MACE, a composite measure standard in cardiology research standing for major adverse cardiovascular events — cardiovascular death, non-fatal heart attack, or non-fatal stroke, counted together as a single outcome. Semaglutide reduced that composite risk by 20 percent relative to placebo, a result striking enough, in a trial population selected specifically because their hearts were already at elevated risk, that it moved semaglutide out of the category of "diabetes and weight-loss drug" and into the category of drugs cardiologists themselves now had independent reason to prescribe, entirely apart from any interest in a patient's weight.

It is worth understanding why a cardiovascular-outcomes trial, rather than a weight-loss trial, carries this much additional evidentiary weight within the medical and regulatory establishment, because the distinction reflects something specific about how clinical medicine ranks different kinds of evidence. A drug's effect on body weight, however dramatic, is what researchers call a surrogate endpoint — a measurable outcome assumed, on reasonable biological grounds, to predict some more important outcome the trial did not directly measure, in this case reduced cardiovascular risk and reduced mortality. Pharmaceutical history contains a number of cautionary examples in which a drug moved a surrogate endpoint impressively while ultimately failing to improve, or even worsening, the harder outcome that endpoint was supposed to predict, which is why regulators and cardiologists alike treat a trial measuring the harder outcome directly, as SELECT did with actual heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular deaths rather than pounds lost, as categorically stronger evidence than even the most impressive surrogate-endpoint result. STEP 1's 14.9 percent weight-loss figure was always going to be read, by a cautious cardiologist, as suggestive rather than conclusive proof that the drug improved a patient's actual health outcomes rather than merely a number on a scale. SELECT closed that gap directly, and its significance ripples backward through several chapters this book has already covered. This is the trial that gave the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services its opening to begin covering these drugs under Part D in March 2024, not because Congress had revisited its two-decade-old statutory exclusion of weight-loss medication, but because SELECT had proven the same drug did something Congress had never excluded: reduce the risk of a heart attack or stroke in a patient who already had heart disease. It is the trial that let obesity medicine specialists argue, with a rigor the field had rarely been able to marshal before, that treating obesity pharmacologically was not a matter of appearance or lifestyle preference but a matter of the same kind of measurable, mortality-relevant outcome cardiology had always organized itself around. And it reframed, for physicians and patients alike, what these drugs were actually treating: not simply a number on a scale, but a cluster of interconnected risks — cardiovascular, metabolic, and, as the following approvals would demonstrate, respiratory — that had clustered around obesity all along, without a single drug capable of addressing more than one or two of them at once until semaglutide's trial program began working systematically through each one in turn.

Eli Lilly's tirzepatide followed a parallel expansion of its own, arriving at a different destination. Having already secured approval for both type 2 diabetes, under the Mounjaro brand, and chronic weight management, under the Zepbound brand, the company pursued and won a further FDA approval for Zepbound as the first and only prescription medicine specifically approved to treat moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity — a condition in which excess tissue around the airway causes repeated breathing interruptions during sleep, historically managed through continuous positive airway pressure machines, oral appliances, or, in severe cases, surgery, none of which addressed the underlying weight-related cause of the airway obstruction in the way a drug capable of substantially reducing that excess tissue directly could. The approval mattered for reasons beyond the sleep apnea diagnosis itself: obstructive sleep apnea is strongly linked to hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and daytime cognitive impairment, meaning a drug that treated it directly, rather than merely managing its nightly symptoms through a machine a large share of patients struggle to use consistently, carried the same kind of broader, compounding health benefit the SELECT trial had demonstrated for cardiovascular risk.

The alcohol-use-disorder research this book examined in its earlier chapter on the beverage industry belongs in this same expanding category, even though its commercial stakes ran through a different industry entirely. The federally funded trials showing semaglutide reducing alcohol craving and consumption in patients with alcohol use disorder — one small Phase 2 trial published in JAMA Psychiatry in February 2025, a larger international trial in patients with comorbid obesity published in The Lancet — represent the same underlying pattern showing up in a third, unrelated clinical domain: a drug developed to manage blood sugar, discovered almost by accident to suppress appetite, now being tested deliberately and systematically against conditions as different from diabetes as cardiovascular risk, sleep-disordered breathing, and substance use disorder, all traceable back to the same reward-circuit and appetite-regulation mechanisms this book's earlier chapters worked to explain in physiological terms.

Researchers have, unsurprisingly, begun extending that same investigative logic further still, into conditions where the GLP-1 receptor's role is considerably less well established and the evidence considerably more preliminary. Chronic kidney disease, a condition that frequently accompanies both diabetes and obesity and that shares much of the same underlying inflammatory and metabolic biology these drugs already act on, is an area of active clinical investigation, as are early-stage questions about whether GLP-1 receptor activity in the brain might have some protective relevance to neurodegenerative disease. This book treats both of those research directions as exactly what they currently are — genuinely active areas of scientific inquiry, not established indications with FDA-approved labels or randomized trial results of the scale and rigor this chapter has been able to report for cardiovascular risk, sleep apnea, and alcohol use disorder — and declines to attach specific numbers or claimed outcomes to either that this book's own sourcing cannot support. The honest state of the science, as of this writing, is that a drug class discovered through one narrow clinical observation about a desert lizard's venom has now been shown, through rigorous, large-scale, randomized evidence, to meaningfully affect at least four distinct and previously separate areas of human health — blood sugar, body weight, cardiovascular risk, and sleep-disordered breathing — with credible preliminary evidence pointing toward a fifth, addiction, and open, unresolved questions extending into several more.

That expanding list of legitimate, evidence-backed indications is precisely what makes the cultural and financial enthusiasm surrounding these drugs difficult to dismiss as simple hype, even as this book's own earlier chapters have documented genuine excess in how that enthusiasm got priced, marketed, and sold. A drug that only helped people lose weight would already have justified the scale of attention it has received; a drug that also measurably reduces heart attacks, treats a serious sleep disorder at its physiological root, and shows credible early evidence of blunting addictive cravings is doing something considerably closer to what earlier generations of medicine reserved for genuinely transformative discoveries — penicillin's effect on infection, the polio vaccine's effect on a disease that had terrified a generation of parents — than to an ordinary, incremental pharmaceutical advance. Whether that comparison ultimately holds up depends heavily on the questions this book turns to next: how the people whose bodies and identities these drugs are reshaping most directly, and whose skepticism this book has, until now, largely deferred, actually experience a culture that has decided, with striking speed, that the condition many of them had spent years being told to simply try harder against was, all along, a matter of chemistry a weekly injection could fix.
