# The Weight of Being Watched

The empirical research on weight stigma itself, conducted well before these drugs existed, gives Tovar's and the other critics' concerns in this chapter a considerably firmer evidentiary foundation than a purely philosophical objection would carry on its own. Decades of published research in social psychology and public health, much of it associated with researchers like Rebecca Puhl at the University of Connecticut's Rudd Center for Food Policy and Obesity, has documented that weight stigma — discriminatory treatment and negative social attitudes directed specifically at people in larger bodies — is itself independently associated with worse physical and mental health outcomes, including outcomes related to disordered eating, depression, and even, in some studies, the very metabolic markers obesity treatment is meant to improve, through chronic-stress pathways largely independent of body weight itself. That research base matters enormously for how this chapter's central tension should be read: it is not simply a matter of critics objecting to an effective treatment on principle, but a matter of critics pointing to a well-documented, separate causal pathway — stigma itself — that a drug treating body weight alone does not address and, absent real cultural change, cannot address, no matter how effectively it manages the underlying physiological condition.

Virgie Tovar has spent her career arguing, as a fat-acceptance activist and the author of the 2018 book You Have the Right to Remain Fat, that the culture's treatment of larger bodies is a matter of prejudice rather than health, and when the wave of GLP-1 enthusiasm crested through 2023 and 2024, she did not soften that argument to meet the moment. In a Substack essay titled "The Ozempicalypse," published in April 2024, she called the cultural embrace of these drugs "alarming... terrifying," and went further than most critics were willing to in print: "It's eugenics," she wrote, arguing that "a group of people with power have decided that a certain quality possessed by a more marginalized group of people does not meet their standard and seek to erase that quality." It is a deliberately uncomfortable framing, one that asks a reader who has watched these drugs reduce cardiovascular risk and treat sleep apnea in rigorous randomized trials to also sit with the possibility that the same medical enthusiasm carries within it an older, less scientifically defensible impulse — a cultural preference for thinness dressed up, not for the first time in the history of weight-loss medicine, in the more respectable language of health.

It is worth taking Tovar's specific historical comparison seriously rather than treating it as rhetorical excess, because the fat-acceptance movement she represents has a genuine and well-documented history of being right about specific medical claims the mainstream culture later had to walk back. The diet industry of the late twentieth century promoted numerous weight-loss interventions later shown to be ineffective or actively harmful — the fen-phen combination that led directly to the Medicare and Medicaid coverage exclusion examined earlier in this book chief among them — while the broader culture spent those same decades treating body size as a straightforward matter of willpower the science did not actually support. That history does not, on its own, prove that today's skepticism about GLP-1 drugs will be similarly vindicated; the clinical trial evidence behind these drugs is considerably more rigorous than anything supporting the diet industry's earlier claims, and this book's own earlier chapters have tried to take that rigor seriously on its own terms. But it does mean that a movement with a track record of catching real problems mainstream medicine missed deserves to be heard as a substantive critic rather than dismissed as a reflexive objector to any medical intervention touching body weight, which is too often how commentary from fat-acceptance advocates gets characterized in more casual press coverage of this subject.

It is worth being specific about what "eugenics," the term Tovar deliberately reached for, is actually claiming, because the word carries enough historical weight that using it loosely would do a disservice to both the argument and the history it invokes. Eugenics, as an organized movement, sought to engineer a population's traits through selective reproduction and, in its most coercive twentieth-century forms, through forced sterilization and other state violence directed at people deemed biologically undesirable. Tovar's argument is not that GLP-1 drugs constitute that kind of coercive state program; it is a narrower claim about cultural preference operating through medicine — that a society already primed to prefer thinness has found, in an effective appetite-suppressing drug, a new and more medically legitimate-seeming vehicle for enforcing that preference, without the underlying preference itself having been examined or justified on its own terms. That narrower reading is more defensible than the word "eugenics" alone might suggest to a reader encountering it out of context, and this book has tried to present it in that more precise form rather than either amplifying or flattening the deliberately provocative language Tovar herself chose to use.

Tovar's argument sits at one end of a spectrum of professional and advocacy-community skepticism this book owes its readers a full hearing of, not as a counterweight inserted for the sake of balance but because the concerns raised across this spectrum are, in several cases, backed by exactly the kind of credentialed clinical expertise this book has relied on throughout its earlier, more triumphant chapters. The National Eating Disorders Association maintains a standing resource specifically addressing these drugs, cautioning that GLP-1 medications "can trigger or exacerbate eating disorder behaviors, especially in people with a history of disordered eating, body dysmorphia, or weight stigma," and calling for pre-prescribing screening and better provider education given how little long-term research on this specific risk currently exists. Samantha DeCaro, a psychologist and the director of clinical outreach and education at the Renfrew Center, a national eating-disorder treatment network, has been more specific about the mechanism of concern in interviews given to National Geographic and other outlets: because larger-bodied patients are so often praised, by friends, family, and even their own physicians, for losing weight regardless of the method or its underlying psychological cost, she has warned that "a lot of times these symptoms get lost in diet culture" — meaning a patient sliding into disordered eating patterns alongside their GLP-1 use may find that the culture's automatic celebration of their smaller body actively obscures the warning signs a clinician, or a worried family member, might otherwise catch. A 2024 peer-reviewed paper in the International Journal of Eating Disorders, co-authored by Sarah Bartel and Susan McElroy, examined the same intersection from within the clinical literature itself, treating GLP-1 use in eating-disorder populations as a genuinely under-studied risk rather than a settled question in either direction.

The disability-rights framing that sits adjacent to fat-acceptance advocacy, though not always identical to it, raises a further question this book's earlier chapters have not directly addressed: whether obesity itself should be understood, in part, through the same social model of disability that disability-rights advocates have applied to other conditions — the idea that a body's impairment is often less disabling on its own than the environment built without that body in mind, from chairs too narrow to seatbelts too short to doorways too tight. Under that framing, a large share of what makes a larger body genuinely difficult to live with in contemporary society reflects a built environment and a set of social attitudes designed around a narrower range of bodies, rather than reflecting an inherent, unavoidable medical burden the body itself imposes independent of that environment. A drug that changes the body rather than the environment, on this view, treats the more tractable variable rather than the more fundamental one — a critique structurally similar to arguments disability advocates have long made about other medical interventions offered as a substitute for environmental accommodation, and one this book thinks deserves to sit alongside the more explicitly stigma-focused critiques already examined, even where the two framings do not always fully agree with each other about what follows from the underlying observation.

Physicians within mainstream, non-advocacy medicine have raised versions of the same concern, working from inside rather than outside the profession prescribing these drugs. Mara Gordon, a family physician in Camden, New Jersey, responded directly to Oprah Winfrey's primetime special in an NPR op-ed published March 23, 2024, under a headline that captured her argument plainly: "No Oprah, Ozempic alone can't fix our culture of fat shaming." Her point was not that the drug does not work, or that patients should feel ashamed for using it, but that an effective medication, however genuinely transformative for an individual patient's health, does not by itself resolve the underlying cultural machinery of weight stigma that had made those same patients' lives harder for years before any prescription existed — a machinery this book's earlier chapter on Ozempic-era celebrity culture, with its filler booms and its coined vocabulary for facial hollowing, arguably illustrated rather than dismantled. A subsequent physician editorial, published in American Family Physician in October 2024 under the title "Prescribing GLP-1 Agonists for Weight Loss: Wrestling With Our Philosophical Angst," by Meera Rao, Allen Shaughnessy, and Rebecca Sokol, urged family doctors specifically not to "embrace a weight-centric approach" merely because an effective new tool had become available, and to "acknowledge the dissonance of living in a diet-entrenched society" even while writing the prescriptions their patients were asking for — an unusually candid piece of professional self-examination, published in a mainstream clinical journal rather than an advocacy outlet, arguing that the arrival of an effective drug does not automatically settle the harder question of whether the culture's underlying relationship to body size was ever the right frame to begin with.

What makes this cluster of concerns difficult to simply weigh against the drugs' clinical benefits and declare resolved in one direction or the other is that the concerns operate on genuinely different registers, several of which can all be true simultaneously without contradicting each other. It can be true, as the SELECT trial demonstrated with considerable rigor, that semaglutide measurably reduces cardiovascular risk in patients with obesity and existing heart disease. It can also be true, as the National Eating Disorders Association and Samantha DeCaro have both warned from direct clinical experience, that the same drug can trigger or worsen disordered eating in a vulnerable subset of patients whose relationship to food and body image was already fragile before they started it, and that a culture primed to celebrate any weight loss as an unambiguous good makes that particular harm harder to catch early. It can be true that obesity, as a matter of population-level cardiovascular and metabolic risk, genuinely benefits from effective medical treatment, and also true, as Virgie Tovar's sharper framing insists, that the specific cultural enthusiasm surrounding these drugs — the Oscars joke, the filler boom, the wardrobe-replacement cycle this book's retail chapter documented — reflects a longstanding aesthetic preference for thinness that predates and exceeds any legitimate health rationale, riding along on the coattails of genuine medical progress rather than being fully explained by it. This book does not think a reader is required to resolve that tension by picking a single side. It thinks the tension itself is the honest state of the evidence, and that a book willing to spend an entire chapter on Novo Nordisk's market capitalization owes at least as much space to the people whose bodies, and whose decades of accumulated experience navigating a culture that judged those bodies, are the actual subject matter underneath all of that market capitalization.

There is a narrower, related question this chapter has deliberately kept separate from the broader stigma argument, because it deserves its own precision rather than being folded into a single undifferentiated critique: whether the specific institutional advocacy organizations built to represent people in larger bodies, such as the National Association to Advance Fat Acceptance, have taken a formal, published position on these drugs distinct from the individual voices already quoted here. NAAFA maintains policy pages addressing weight-loss drugs and obesity research as part of its standing advocacy work, though this book's own research process was unable to independently verify the precise, current wording of that policy with the same confidence it has applied to the other quoted sources in this chapter, and declines to characterize its specific contents beyond noting that the organization exists, has weighed in publicly on this territory before, and represents a constituency this chapter's argument would be incomplete without acknowledging directly, even where this book cannot quote its exact current language with full confidence.

What all of these voices share, whatever they disagree about, is a refusal to let the drugs' genuine clinical success stand in for a settled verdict on the culture surrounding them — and that refusal deserves to sit directly alongside this book's next and final piece of unfinished business: the strongest version of the case against the entire GLP-1 phenomenon as it has actually unfolded in practice, assembled not from any single critic but from the economists, physicians, and journalists who have each, from different angles, argued that the triumphant version of this story this book has largely told so far leaves out more than it should.
