# The Shot Heard Round the World

Jimmy Kimmel stood on the stage of the Dolby Theatre on March 12, 2023, hosting the 95th Academy Awards, and delivered a line that would end up quoted more often than almost anything else said at that ceremony, including any of the speeches given by that night's actual winners. Looking out at a room full of some of the most photographed people on the planet, he said: "Everybody looks so great. When I look around this room, I can't help but wonder, 'Is Ozempic right for me?'" The audience laughed, the clip circulated within hours, and a drug most Americans had, eighteen months earlier, never heard of settled permanently into the culture's shared vocabulary — not as a diabetes medication, which is what its FDA approval actually said it was, but as a punchline about thinness, celebrity, and a kind of quiet mass transformation everyone in that room could apparently see happening to everyone else, without anyone quite wanting to say so directly.

Kimmel was not, strictly speaking, first. Five months earlier, on October 2, 2022, Elon Musk had replied to a comment about his own changed physique on social media with a single word — "Fasting" — and then, in a follow-up, two more: "And Wegovy." It was a small disclosure by the standards of what would follow, buried in a reply thread rather than announced from a stage, but it landed at a moment when the underlying phenomenon — a genuinely new class of drug, working through a genuinely novel mechanism, producing weight loss at a scale obesity medicine had rarely if ever achieved through pharmacology alone — was still mostly confined to endocrinology conferences, pharmaceutical earnings calls, and a quiet, word-of-mouth network of doctors and patients who had noticed something happening off-label, ahead of any marketing campaign built to announce it. By the time Kimmel made his joke, that quiet phase was already ending. Within another year, Chelsea Handler would tell a podcast audience she "didn't even know" she had been on the drug, put there, as she explained it, by an anti-aging doctor who "just hands it out to anybody." Oprah Winfrey would host a primetime special about the shame she said she had spent decades carrying over her own weight, and the relief, even redemption, she found in a drug that finally offered a chemical answer to a struggle she had spent a public career failing to solve through willpower alone. South Park would devote an entire hour-long special to the phenomenon, following its most famously appetite-driven character on a quest for the drug, closing with a pledge — delivered with the show's usual mixture of sincerity and mockery — to never make fun of people's weight again.

The celebrity disclosures that followed Musk's and preceded Kimmel's joke were not uniform in tone, and the differences between them mattered more than they might have seemed at the time. Chelsea Handler told a podcast audience in late January 2023 that she "didn't even know" she had been taking the drug, describing an "anti-aging doctor" who, in her account, "just hands it out to anybody" — a framing that cast early access as an open secret among the wealthy rather than a deliberate medical choice. Amy Schumer took the opposite tack, admitting on television that she had tried the drug herself a year earlier and stopped because she "felt so sick" she could not keep up with her young son, and then, in the same interview, criticizing other celebrities for what she called lying about their own use. Sharon Osbourne started the drug in December 2022, lost roughly forty-two pounds within four months, and would spend much of the following two years publicly walking that transformation back, telling interviewers she thought she had "gone too far" and now looked "too gaunt." Three famous people, three entirely different relationships to the same medication, playing out in public within roughly a year of each other — concealment, confession, and regret, each treated by the surrounding media as equally newsworthy, none of them quite settling the underlying question of what it meant that a drug this consequential had entered public life first through gossip rather than through any public health campaign designed to introduce it responsibly.

That the conversation started this way, rather than through a slower, more clinically framed rollout, is itself worth noting, because obesity medicine had been trying for a decade to have a different and more serious version of exactly this conversation. The American Medical Association formally classified obesity as a disease in 2013, a vote that was contested even within the AMA's own house of delegates and that took years to meaningfully change how insurers, employers, and much of the public actually treated the condition in practice. The GLP-1 drugs did not create the argument that obesity deserved to be treated as a chronic disease rather than a failure of discipline; that argument, and the physicians who had spent careers making it against considerable public skepticism, predates Ozempic by a generation. What these drugs did was hand that argument a piece of evidence — a molecule that worked, dramatically and repeatably, on a biological pathway rather than through willpower — concrete enough that it could not be waved away the way a decade of AMA position statements largely had been. It is one of the stranger features of this whole story that the evidence arrived first as a celebrity punchline and only afterward, more slowly, as the disease-model vindication obesity medicine had been waiting for.

This book is not primarily a book about celebrity culture, though celebrity culture appears in it, because it is one of the more visible surfaces on which a much larger shift became briefly legible to people who were not otherwise paying close attention to pharmaceutical trial data or corporate earnings calls. The working thesis of this book is that a molecule discovered almost by accident, in a desert lizard's venom and in a scientist's careful decade of peptide chemistry, has done something genuinely rare in economic history: it has changed, at a population scale and within the space of roughly four years, what a meaningful share of people in wealthy countries actually want to eat, drink, and buy, and in doing so has begun reordering the economics of an extraordinary range of industries that had, for generations, been built on the assumption that human appetite was a fixed, exploitable constant rather than a variable a weekly injection could substantially turn down.

The scale of that reordering is easier to state than to fully absorb. A Danish insulin manufacturer whose entire commercial identity, a decade ago, rested on unglamorous diabetes therapies became, for a few days in September 2023, worth more than the gross domestic product of the country that founded it — before losing roughly half that valuation over the following two years, once a corporate rival with a differently engineered molecule proved able to out-compete it. Walmart's own U.S. chief executive told analysts, on an earnings call in October 2023, that the company could measure a "slight pullback in overall basket" among customers using these drugs — fewer units, slightly fewer calories, a change small enough to sound almost trivial in a single sentence and significant enough that food and beverage companies across the country spent the following three years reformulating products, resizing portions, and rewriting investor presentations in response. A prominent fund manager sold his firm's entire stake in one of the world's largest spirits companies, citing these drugs by name as a structural risk to an industry built on a habit they appear, in early but real clinical evidence, to blunt. Bariatric surgery volumes fell by nearly a quarter in two years. A plus-size clothing retailer posted a double-digit sales decline and announced dozens of store closures. Airlines, gyms, cruise lines, and dermatology practices all found themselves, in different ways and to different degrees, recalculating around a population that a weekly injection was quietly making lighter, hungrier for different things, and less predictable in ways their business models had not been built to anticipate.

It is worth pausing on how unusual this is as a matter of economic history before moving further into the specifics. Entire sectors of the modern consumer economy were built, over the second half of the twentieth century, on an implicit assumption that human appetite was a roughly fixed and fairly predictable input — something marketing could redirect from one brand to another, something portion sizes could nudge upward gradually over decades without much resistance, but not something that would simply shrink, in aggregate, across tens of millions of people within a few years. The snack food industry, the casual dining industry, the beer, wine, and spirits industry, the diet industry built paradoxically around the assumption that people would keep failing at diets and need to keep buying new ones — all of it rested on that assumption of a roughly stable, exploitable appetite, the way a power utility rests on the assumption that a city's electricity demand will grow steadily rather than suddenly, structurally decline. A drug capable of turning that appetite down by a meaningful percentage, durably, for a large and growing share of the population, is not merely a new pharmaceutical product competing for market share within an existing category. It is closer to a shock to the demand curve itself, arriving with no warning to any of the industries built downstream of the assumption it violated.

None of this happened because anyone set out to reorder the economics of appetite. It happened because a molecule Novo Nordisk's own scientists had originally developed to help the pancreas of a person with type 2 diabetes release insulin at the right moment turned out, almost as an afterthought buried in a decade-old laboratory observation about mice eating less, to be extraordinarily good at making a person feel full — full enough, in trial after trial, to lose more weight than diet and exercise counseling alone had reliably produced in the entire prior history of obesity medicine. That the drug's most commercially explosive effect arrived as an unplanned discovery rather than a designed outcome matters for how this book tells the story that follows. This is not a story about a single company's brilliant strategic foresight, though both Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly displayed real institutional patience and real scientific ambition in bringing these molecules to market over decades of unglamorous research. It is a story about how quickly an economy built around a set of assumptions can be forced to rewrite itself once one of those assumptions — that human appetite, whatever its psychological and social complexity, was not something a single weekly prescription could substantially and durably override — stopped holding for a large and rapidly growing share of the population able to access the drug.

That word, access, carries most of the book's second half on its own, because the story this book tells is not only about a scientific and cultural transformation; it is, in equal measure, about a strikingly uneven economic one. The same drug that costs a few dollars to manufacture, by independent academic estimate, has carried a list price above nine hundred dollars a month in the United States for years, and above thirteen hundred dollars a month for its weight-loss-specific formulation, while selling in Denmark, the country where it was invented, for a small fraction of that figure. A United States senator spent the better part of two years investigating why, hauling the manufacturer's chief executive before a congressional hearing to ask, more or less directly, why Americans were being charged so much more than everyone else in the world for a drug their own country's scientists had done so much of the foundational research to make possible. Medicare, for two decades, was legally barred from covering these drugs for weight loss at all, a statutory relic of a 2003 law shaped partly by an earlier generation's diet-drug scandal, until a landmark 2023 cardiovascular trial gave regulators an opening — treat the heart, not the waistline — to begin, cautiously and incompletely, working around a restriction Congress itself had never revisited. Insurers and employers, watching their own pharmacy budgets balloon by hundreds of millions of dollars in a single year, have spent the past three years lurching between generous new coverage and abrupt, headline-making retreats from it, leaving millions of patients uncertain, year to year, whether the drug that changed their relationship with food would still be covered by January.

This book follows that full arc in roughly the order it actually happened, rather than jumping straight to the part of the story a reader already knows from the news. It opens with the science, because almost no one telling this story in real time bothered to: a gastroenterologist's curiosity about a lizard's venom, a Bronx VA biochemist who had to license his own discovery to a start-up after the government that employed him declined to patent it, and a Rockefeller University chemist whose identification of the exact bioactive fragment of GLP-1 took less than five years to accomplish and more than four decades to receive its due credit for. It follows Novo Nordisk's institutional patience in pursuing that biology across three successive drugs, and Eli Lilly's competing bet on a differently engineered molecule, through to the clinical trials — STEP for semaglutide, SURMOUNT for tirzepatide, and eventually SELECT, the cardiovascular trial that would do as much as any single piece of evidence to change how obesity itself was classified — large enough to force genuine reconsideration of what pharmacology could do for a condition medicine had mostly treated, for generations, as a matter of insufficient discipline.

From there the book turns to consequences, moving outward from the drugmakers themselves into every industry their success touched: a market euphoria and correction that briefly made one Danish company worth more than its own country's economy before losing roughly half that value within two years; a supply shortage severe enough to spawn a grey market in compounded and, in some documented cases, counterfeit product; a wave of reformulation and anxious earnings-call hedging across the food, beverage, retail, fitness, and travel industries; and a pricing fight that pulled in a sitting United States senator, a Danish parliament, and eventually the White House itself. It closes by turning the lens back onto the people the whole story is actually about — the patients navigating muscle loss, weight regain, unexpected pregnancies, and a culture that has not yet decided whether their transformation deserves celebration or suspicion — and by giving the strongest skeptical case against this book's own optimism a full chapter rather than a caveat, before looking ahead to a pipeline of even more powerful successor molecules already moving through trials, priced against a decade in which the drugs that started this story will, by most patent-timeline estimates, still be years away from facing generic competition in the one country paying the most for them.

This book was written in 2026, close enough to the events it describes that some of its own numbers will already look dated by the time a reader in 2027 or later encounters them — a genuine hazard for a book about a story still actively unfolding, and one this book has tried to manage by being explicit, throughout, about the date a given price, market share, or coverage policy was accurate as of, rather than presenting a moving target as a fixed conclusion. The choice to write about this subject now, rather than waiting for the dust to settle, reflects a judgment about what kind of book this needed to be. A history written a decade from now, once patents have expired, once the safety profile has another ten years of data behind it, once the pricing fights have resolved one way or another, will be able to write with a confidence this book cannot claim. It will also have lost something this book still has: the ability to show a reader what it actually felt like to live through the uncertainty, the euphoria, the backlash, and the unresolved arguments in something close to real time, before anyone involved knew how the story would end.

A note on method, offered here rather than buried in an appendix, because the subject matter makes it unusually necessary. Every clinical trial figure in this book comes from a peer-reviewed publication or a primary regulatory source, and this book tries throughout to distinguish a trial's own reported result from the marketing built on top of it — a distinction the pharmaceutical industry's own communications do not always maintain as carefully as a reader deserves. Every dollar figure, market-capitalization number, and corporate earnings-call quote is drawn from a named, dated source, and where two credible sources disagree on a specific figure — an international price comparison, a market-cap peak, an employer's exact cost exposure — this book says so rather than picking whichever number tells the cleanest story. Nearly every major fact in this book was funded, tested, or reported by a party with some stake in how the story gets told: a manufacturer funding its own trial, an insurer disclosing its own losses, a critic building a case against an industry whose profits she has spent a career questioning. This book has tried to keep that interest visible rather than invisible throughout, and to give the strongest version of the skeptical case — on safety, on cost, on what these drugs might mean for how society treats bodies that do not conform to a narrower standard — a full hearing of its own, in a chapter built for exactly that purpose rather than a caveat scattered thin across the whole. The story starts, as these things often do, somewhere considerably less glamorous than an Oscars stage: in the venom of a lizard that eats only a few times a year, and the questions a curious gastroenterologist asked about why that venom did something to a guinea pig's pancreas that nothing else in his laboratory freezer could do.
