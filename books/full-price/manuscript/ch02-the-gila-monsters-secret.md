# The Gila Monster's Secret

Every drug discovery story eventually has to answer a question its own tidiest version tends to skip: how does a researcher decide a strange result is worth pursuing, rather than one of the dozens of odd, unexplained readings any working laboratory generates in an ordinary week and quietly sets aside. Raufman's guinea pig pancreas assay could easily have produced its unusual amylin-release pattern and gone unremarked, filed as a curiosity in a lab notebook rather than followed to its source, if Raufman himself had not had the specific combination of curiosity and available time to ask why a single venom, among dozens tested, behaved so differently from the rest. That kind of decision — which anomaly to chase and which to let go — is one of the least visible and least celebrated parts of how science actually advances, made dozens of times in any working laboratory for every one that eventually produces something as consequential as this one did. The Gila monster is not built for a world of regular meals. It is a thick, black-and-orange-beaded lizard of the Sonoran and Mojave deserts, slow-moving, venomous, and capable of eating perhaps five or six times a year — gorging on a nest of eggs or a newborn rabbit, storing the fat in its tail, and then doing very little until hunger returns weeks or months later. A physiology built around feast and famine on that scale needs a different relationship to appetite and blood sugar than an animal that eats every few hours, and for most of the twentieth century nobody with a reason to study human metabolism had any occasion to wonder what that relationship might be. The lizard was a curiosity, mildly dangerous to careless hikers, not a research subject.

That changed because a gastroenterologist named Jean-Pierre Raufman happened to be interested in venom. In the early 1980s, Raufman was working at the National Institutes of Health, running assays on guinea pig pancreas tissue — a standard way, at the time, of testing whether a given substance provoked the pancreas into releasing its digestive enzymes. Venoms were useful test substances because so many of them are built from peptides, small chains of amino acids that can mimic or block a body's own signaling molecules with unusual precision; snake, spider, and lizard venoms had already yielded a run of important laboratory tools and eventually drugs, from ACE inhibitors developed out of pit viper venom to a menu of ion-channel blockers derived from cone snails. Raufman ran Gila monster venom through the same guinea pig assay he used on everything else and got a result that did not look like the others. Most venoms provoked a fast, sharp burst of pancreatic secretion. Gila monster venom provoked something slower and much larger: a prolonged, sustained release of amylase, the enzyme the pancreas uses to break down starch, well past the point where the response to other venoms had already tapered off. Something in the venom was activating a pathway the guinea pig pancreas did not normally sustain for that long, and Raufman wanted to know what.

Venom had already earned its place in pharmacology's toolkit by the time Raufman ran his assay, and it is worth understanding why before following his particular result. A snake, spider, or lizard venom is, from a chemist's point of view, a concentrated library of peptides that evolution has spent millions of years refining for a single purpose: disabling another animal's physiology as precisely and as efficiently as possible. That precision is exactly what makes venom useful to a researcher looking for a drug candidate, because a peptide that can shut down a rabbit's nervous system with terrifying specificity is also, potentially, a peptide that can be redirected, with the right modification, toward a much gentler and more useful end. Captopril, one of the first ACE inhibitors and still a foundational blood-pressure medication decades after its 1981 approval, was developed from a peptide found in the venom of the Brazilian pit viper. Ziconotide, marketed as Prialt, a powerful non-opioid pain medication, was derived from the venom of a cone snail. Raufman's guinea pig pancreas assay sat squarely inside that established tradition of venom prospecting; what made his result worth pursuing was simply that Gila monster venom did not behave like the venoms surrounding it in his laboratory freezer.

The person who answered that question was John Eng, a biochemist who had trained as a postdoctoral fellow under Rosalyn Yalow — the Nobel laureate who developed radioimmunoassay, a technique for measuring minute concentrations of hormones in blood, and who had built her career largely at the Bronx Veterans Affairs Medical Center rather than a more conventionally prestigious research university. Eng worked at that same VA hospital, and it was there, across a run of experiments through the late 1980s and into the early 1990s, that he isolated and characterized the specific peptide in Gila monster venom responsible for the effect Raufman had noticed. He named it exendin-4. What made exendin-4 worth naming, rather than simply cataloguing as one more venom component, was what Eng found when he compared its structure to hormones already known in human biology: it closely resembled glucagon-like peptide-1, or GLP-1, a gut hormone that the body releases after eating to stimulate insulin secretion from the pancreas. Human GLP-1 was already understood, by the early 1990s, to be a promising target for diabetes treatment, for an elegant reason — unlike insulin itself or the older sulfonylurea drugs that force the pancreas to release insulin regardless of blood sugar level, GLP-1 only stimulates insulin release when blood glucose is already elevated, which meant a GLP-1-based drug should in principle lower blood sugar without the constant risk of overcorrecting into dangerous hypoglycemia. The problem was durability. Native human GLP-1, once released into the bloodstream, is degraded by an enzyme called DPP-4 in about two minutes — long enough to do its local job after a meal, useless as an injectable drug that a patient could take once and expect to work for hours, let alone days.

Exendin-4 did not have that problem. It resembled GLP-1 closely enough to activate the same receptor, but its own protein sequence happened to lack the particular vulnerability that let DPP-4 chew through native human GLP-1 so quickly, so it lingered in the bloodstream for hours rather than minutes. A desert lizard that eats a handful of times a year had, for reasons that had nothing to do with human diabetes and everything to do with its own hunting and hibernation cycle, evolved a version of a mammalian gut hormone that lasted far longer than the mammalian original. Eng understood immediately what that durability might be worth to a pharmaceutical program built around GLP-1 biology, and he tried to get the Department of Veterans Affairs to patent the discovery. The VA declined. Eng, rather than letting the finding die in a filing cabinet, licensed it instead to Amylin Pharmaceuticals, a small biotechnology start-up in San Diego that was willing to bet on a molecule with no obvious institutional backer behind it.

It is worth dwelling for a moment on what Yalow's own career, and the institution where she and Eng both worked, says about how this particular thread of the discovery unfolded outside the more conventionally prestigious channels much of American biomedical research runs through. Yalow had developed radioimmunoassay — a technique sensitive enough to measure hormone concentrations in blood at levels previously undetectable, work that would win her the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine — while affiliated with the Bronx VA rather than one of the university medical centers that more typically produce Nobel laureates, partly because the opportunities open to a woman physicist of her generation at those more prestigious institutions were narrower than her talent warranted. The VA hospital where she built her career became, in turn, a place where a biochemist like Eng could pursue an unusual venom-derived peptide without needing to justify the detour to a department chair more focused on grant-funded, mainstream research directions. The Gila monster itself deserves a slightly fuller introduction than a single opening scene can provide, because the specifics of its biology are not incidental color; they are the reason its venom happened to contain a peptide durable enough to matter. Heloderma suspectum is one of only a small number of venomous lizard species, native to the Sonoran, Mojave, and Chihuahuan deserts of the American Southwest and northern Mexico, and it is a famously slow, deliberate animal — capable of surviving on stored tail fat between meals that can be separated by months, an adaptation that requires an unusually robust and unusually well-regulated metabolic and digestive system to make each rare, large meal count. That evolutionary pressure toward metabolic efficiency and durability, rather than the rapid, high-turnover metabolism of an animal that eats daily, is a plausible reason the lizard's own version of a GLP-1-like hormone would have evolved toward greater stability in the bloodstream than the human version, adapted instead to a body that eats often and has less evolutionary reason to keep a satiety-signaling hormone circulating for very long. None of the scientists in this chapter needed to fully resolve that evolutionary question to make use of its result; Eng needed only to notice that the peptide was more durable, and to understand what that durability could be worth.

Government-funded research infrastructure, unglamorous and rarely credited in the popular retelling of a scientific breakthrough, shows up at nearly every turn in this particular story — the VA in the Bronx, the National Institutes of Health where Raufman first ran his assay, the university hospitals in Toronto and Boston where Drucker's and Habener's labs operated on public and philanthropic funding for decades before a private company turned any of it into a marketable drug. That infrastructure rarely gets a line in a pharmaceutical company's own account of its own innovation, and this book means to keep it visible rather than let the corporate chapters that follow crowd it out.

Amylin spent the better part of a decade turning exendin-4 into a synthetic drug, eventually partnering with Eli Lilly to bring it to market as exenatide, sold under the brand name Byetta. The Food and Drug Administration approved it on April 28, 2005, and Amylin and Lilly announced the approval jointly the following day — the first GLP-1-based drug ever cleared for use in humans, a full twelve years before Ozempic would receive the same kind of approval for the same underlying receptor. Byetta was approved narrowly, as a twice-daily injectable treatment for type 2 diabetes, to be used alongside other medications when those alone were not controlling blood sugar. Nothing in its approved label mentioned weight loss. And yet within roughly six months of its 2005 launch, physicians and patients were already reporting, informally and then in published case series, that people taking Byetta for their blood sugar were losing more weight than diabetes drugs typically produced — noticeably more, and without patients deliberately dieting to achieve it. Byetta started getting prescribed off-label, in a limited and cautious way given its twice-daily injection schedule and its meaningful gastrointestinal side effects, to people whose primary complaint was their weight rather than their blood sugar. It was a small, mostly unremarked-upon preview, playing out in exam rooms rather than headlines, of a pattern that would eventually reshape entire industries once a longer-acting, better-tolerated version of the same biology arrived. Nobody organizing Amylin's or Lilly's marketing around Byetta in 2005 was building toward that outcome. The drug was approved to help failing pancreases release insulin at the right moment. The weight loss was, as far as the label and the sales force were concerned, an unadvertised bonus that patients noticed before the industry decided what to do with it.

Byetta's limitations, in hindsight, are as instructive as its breakthrough. It required twice-daily injection, timed around meals, a dosing burden considerably heavier than anything a patient managing a single weekly Ozempic pen would later face, and it carried gastrointestinal side effects — nausea foremost among them — significant enough that a meaningful share of patients stopped taking it before they had used it long enough to see its full effect. Its weight-loss effect, while real and while genuinely surprising to the physicians who first noticed it, was modest by the standards later semaglutide and tirzepatide trials would set: a few percentage points of body weight in typical use, not the double-digit results STEP and SURMOUNT would eventually report. Byetta proved the underlying biological principle — that a durable GLP-1 receptor agonist could meaningfully suppress appetite as a side effect of managing blood sugar — without yet delivering a drug convenient or tolerable enough to build a mass consumer market around. That gap between proof of principle and a genuinely market-ready product is where the next two decades of this story actually live, carried forward less by any single dramatic discovery than by the incremental, unglamorous work of making an already-proven mechanism easier to tolerate, easier to dose, and easier for an ordinary patient to simply keep taking.

Eng's route to exendin-4 ran through a desert lizard and a VA hospital lab that a Nobel laureate happened to have made respectable. A second, largely independent route to the same underlying biology ran through Harvard Medical School and a research fellowship that began, in 1984, as something else entirely. Daniel Drucker arrived at Harvard that year to join the laboratory of Joel Habener, an endocrinologist, with the expectation that he would work on thyroid disease — Habener's lab had an active thyroid research program at the time, and that program was the reason Drucker had come. By the time Drucker actually started, the thyroid project had been dropped, and Habener redirected his new fellow instead toward an unrelated question the lab had also been chasing: the structure and function of the glucagon gene, the genetic template that, through a process called differential post-translational processing, gives rise to several distinct hormone products depending on which tissue is doing the processing — among them, eventually, GLP-1 itself. Drucker had not chosen glucagon biology out of any particular conviction that it mattered more than thyroid disease. He inherited it because a colleague's project had ended and a fellow needed something to work on, which is a more accurate account of how a great deal of foundational science actually gets started than the origin stories retold after the fact tend to admit.

What Drucker built from that reassignment, over the following four decades, was one of the more complete bodies of work in the entire GLP-1 field — not a single discovery but a long accumulation of receptor characterization, degradation-pathway mapping, and evidence for effects of GLP-1 signaling well beyond the pancreas, conducted first at Harvard and then, after he moved to Canada, at his own laboratory at the University of Toronto's Banting and Best Diabetes Centre — an institution whose name traces directly back to the same 1921 insulin discovery that would, a century later and by a very different route, also give Novo Nordisk its founding purpose — and later as a senior scientist at the Lunenfeld-Tanenbaum Research Institute at Mount Sinai Hospital in Toronto. Across that span, Drucker's laboratory worked out, piece by piece, questions that would only later reveal their commercial stakes: which tissues actually carry GLP-1 receptors, how the receptor's signal is transmitted once GLP-1 binds it, how the hormone is degraded by DPP-4, and how each of those mechanisms might be exploited or protected against by a synthetic analogue. It is the kind of research that rarely produces a single dramatic headline in the year it is published, and that instead accumulates, paper after paper, into the working knowledge a pharmaceutical company's drug-development team draws on without always crediting the specific study that supplied a given fact. Novo Nordisk eventually endowed a chair in his name at Toronto, a fact worth noting for what it says about how thoroughly the pharmaceutical industry came to depend on the basic science Drucker's lab kept producing, decades before that science turned into a product anyone outside a small research community had heard of. By his own account, Drucker's path crossed the same desert-lizard biology that had occupied Eng and Raufman: he obtained a Gila monster himself at one point and analyzed its venom directly, independently confirming that its GLP-1-like hormone was active at the mammalian GLP-1 receptor while remaining structurally distinct from the native peptide either species produced on its own — the same durability-through-difference that had made exendin-4 useful to Eng a continent away. Two research programs, run by people who were not collaborating in any close sense, kept arriving at variations on the same insight: whatever it was about GLP-1 signaling that made it worth harnessing, a molecule that resisted the body's own two-minute clock on the native hormone would be the one that actually worked as medicine.

None of this, in the 1980s or through most of the 1990s, looked like the beginning of a story about obesity, or about a drug so culturally dominant that its brand name would become a stand-in verb for an entire category of medicine. It looked like diabetes research, conducted by scientists whose primary audience was other scientists, funded by grants written to justify incremental progress on a well-defined biochemical question. Eng was trying to explain an odd result in a guinea pig assay. Drucker was trying to make sense of a gene his lab had been handed almost by accident. The Gila monster was not a metaphor anyone had reason to reach for yet; it was simply the animal whose venom happened to contain the most convenient natural example of a hormone the body degrades too quickly to use as medicine. Recognition, when it eventually came, arrived on a timeline that says something about how science's reward system is built. The Breakthrough Prize in Life Sciences, established in 2013 by a group of technology entrepreneurs specifically to award sums closer to those of the Nobel Prize to researchers the more traditional prize committees had not yet reached, is sometimes described as biomedical science's own attempt to fix a recognition gap the older institutions were too slow to close. Drucker was recognized for the accumulated weight of that work in 2025, sharing the Breakthrough Prize in Life Sciences with four other researchers for the discovery and development of GLP-1 biology — an award that arrived only after the drugs built on that biology had already reordered how tens of millions of people ate, how several of the world's largest food and beverage companies talked to their investors, and how at least one small Nordic insulin manufacturer became, for a while, worth more than the entire economy of the country that hosted it. The award recognized forty years of unglamorous, structural, receptor-by-receptor work. The world had already started paying attention to the consequences before it had fully worked out who to credit for the cause. That gap between commercial consequence and scientific credit is not unique to Drucker's story, and in his case it eventually closed, if slowly. It closed considerably more slowly, and along a more visibly unjust path, for the scientist whose own contribution sat even closer to the molecular heart of the drugs this book is about — the person who first isolated, from proglucagon's larger and more ambiguous protein sequence, the exact fragment responsible for the effect Eng's Gila monster venom had merely imitated. That story, and the four decades it took to correct, is where this book turns next.
