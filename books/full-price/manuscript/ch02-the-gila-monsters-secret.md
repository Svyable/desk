# The Gila Monster's Secret

The Gila monster is not built for a world of regular meals. It is a thick, black-and-orange-beaded lizard of the Sonoran and Mojave deserts, slow-moving, venomous, and capable of eating perhaps five or six times a year — gorging on a nest of eggs or a newborn rabbit, storing the fat in its tail, and then doing very little until hunger returns weeks or months later. A physiology built around feast and famine on that scale needs a different relationship to appetite and blood sugar than an animal that eats every few hours, and for most of the twentieth century nobody with a reason to study human metabolism had any occasion to wonder what that relationship might be. The lizard was a curiosity, mildly dangerous to careless hikers, not a research subject.

That changed because a gastroenterologist named Jean-Pierre Raufman happened to be interested in venom. In the early 1980s, Raufman was working at the National Institutes of Health, running assays on guinea pig pancreas tissue — a standard way, at the time, of testing whether a given substance provoked the pancreas into releasing its digestive enzymes. Venoms were useful test substances because so many of them are built from peptides, small chains of amino acids that can mimic or block a body's own signaling molecules with unusual precision; snake, spider, and lizard venoms had already yielded a run of important laboratory tools and eventually drugs, from ACE inhibitors developed out of pit viper venom to a menu of ion-channel blockers derived from cone snails. Raufman ran Gila monster venom through the same guinea pig assay he used on everything else and got a result that did not look like the others. Most venoms provoked a fast, sharp burst of pancreatic secretion. Gila monster venom provoked something slower and much larger: a prolonged, sustained release of amylase, the enzyme the pancreas uses to break down starch, well past the point where the response to other venoms had already tapered off. Something in the venom was activating a pathway the guinea pig pancreas did not normally sustain for that long, and Raufman wanted to know what.

The person who answered that question was John Eng, a biochemist who had trained as a postdoctoral fellow under Rosalyn Yalow — the Nobel laureate who developed radioimmunoassay, a technique for measuring minute concentrations of hormones in blood, and who had built her career largely at the Bronx Veterans Affairs Medical Center rather than a more conventionally prestigious research university. Eng worked at that same VA hospital, and it was there, across a run of experiments through the late 1980s and into the early 1990s, that he isolated and characterized the specific peptide in Gila monster venom responsible for the effect Raufman had noticed. He named it exendin-4. What made exendin-4 worth naming, rather than simply cataloguing as one more venom component, was what Eng found when he compared its structure to hormones already known in human biology: it closely resembled glucagon-like peptide-1, or GLP-1, a gut hormone that the body releases after eating to stimulate insulin secretion from the pancreas. Human GLP-1 was already understood, by the early 1990s, to be a promising target for diabetes treatment, for an elegant reason — unlike insulin itself or the older sulfonylurea drugs that force the pancreas to release insulin regardless of blood sugar level, GLP-1 only stimulates insulin release when blood glucose is already elevated, which meant a GLP-1-based drug should in principle lower blood sugar without the constant risk of overcorrecting into dangerous hypoglycemia. The problem was durability. Native human GLP-1, once released into the bloodstream, is degraded by an enzyme called DPP-4 in about two minutes — long enough to do its local job after a meal, useless as an injectable drug that a patient could take once and expect to work for hours, let alone days.

Exendin-4 did not have that problem. It resembled GLP-1 closely enough to activate the same receptor, but its own protein sequence happened to lack the particular vulnerability that let DPP-4 chew through native human GLP-1 so quickly, so it lingered in the bloodstream for hours rather than minutes. A desert lizard that eats a handful of times a year had, for reasons that had nothing to do with human diabetes and everything to do with its own hunting and hibernation cycle, evolved a version of a mammalian gut hormone that lasted far longer than the mammalian original. Eng understood immediately what that durability might be worth to a pharmaceutical program built around GLP-1 biology, and he tried to get the Department of Veterans Affairs to patent the discovery. The VA declined. Eng, rather than letting the finding die in a filing cabinet, licensed it instead to Amylin Pharmaceuticals, a small biotechnology start-up in San Diego that was willing to bet on a molecule with no obvious institutional backer behind it.

Amylin spent the better part of a decade turning exendin-4 into a synthetic drug, eventually partnering with Eli Lilly to bring it to market as exenatide, sold under the brand name Byetta. The Food and Drug Administration approved it on April 28, 2005, and Amylin and Lilly announced the approval jointly the following day — the first GLP-1-based drug ever cleared for use in humans, a full twelve years before Ozempic would receive the same kind of approval for the same underlying receptor. Byetta was approved narrowly, as a twice-daily injectable treatment for type 2 diabetes, to be used alongside other medications when those alone were not controlling blood sugar. Nothing in its approved label mentioned weight loss. And yet within roughly six months of its 2005 launch, physicians and patients were already reporting, informally and then in published case series, that people taking Byetta for their blood sugar were losing more weight than diabetes drugs typically produced — noticeably more, and without patients deliberately dieting to achieve it. Byetta started getting prescribed off-label, in a limited and cautious way given its twice-daily injection schedule and its meaningful gastrointestinal side effects, to people whose primary complaint was their weight rather than their blood sugar. It was a small, mostly unremarked-upon preview, playing out in exam rooms rather than headlines, of a pattern that would eventually reshape entire industries once a longer-acting, better-tolerated version of the same biology arrived. Nobody organizing Amylin's or Lilly's marketing around Byetta in 2005 was building toward that outcome. The drug was approved to help failing pancreases release insulin at the right moment. The weight loss was, as far as the label and the sales force were concerned, an unadvertised bonus that patients noticed before the industry decided what to do with it.

Eng's route to exendin-4 ran through a desert lizard and a VA hospital lab that a Nobel laureate happened to have made respectable. A second, largely independent route to the same underlying biology ran through Harvard Medical School and a research fellowship that began, in 1984, as something else entirely. Daniel Drucker arrived at Harvard that year to join the laboratory of Joel Habener, an endocrinologist, with the expectation that he would work on thyroid disease — Habener's lab had an active thyroid research program at the time, and that program was the reason Drucker had come. By the time Drucker actually started, the thyroid project had been dropped, and Habener redirected his new fellow instead toward an unrelated question the lab had also been chasing: the structure and function of the glucagon gene, the genetic template that, through a process called differential post-translational processing, gives rise to several distinct hormone products depending on which tissue is doing the processing — among them, eventually, GLP-1 itself. Drucker had not chosen glucagon biology out of any particular conviction that it mattered more than thyroid disease. He inherited it because a colleague's project had ended and a fellow needed something to work on, which is a more accurate account of how a great deal of foundational science actually gets started than the origin stories retold after the fact tend to admit.

What Drucker built from that reassignment, over the following four decades, was one of the more complete bodies of work in the entire GLP-1 field — not a single discovery but a long accumulation of receptor characterization, degradation-pathway mapping, and evidence for effects of GLP-1 signaling well beyond the pancreas, conducted first at Harvard and then, after he moved to Canada, at his own laboratory at the University of Toronto's Banting and Best Diabetes Centre, and later as a senior scientist at the Lunenfeld-Tanenbaum Research Institute at Mount Sinai Hospital in Toronto. Novo Nordisk eventually endowed a chair in his name at Toronto, a fact worth noting for what it says about how thoroughly the pharmaceutical industry came to depend on the basic science Drucker's lab kept producing, decades before that science turned into a product anyone outside a small research community had heard of. By his own account, Drucker's path crossed the same desert-lizard biology that had occupied Eng and Raufman: he obtained a Gila monster himself at one point and analyzed its venom directly, independently confirming that its GLP-1-like hormone was active at the mammalian GLP-1 receptor while remaining structurally distinct from the native peptide either species produced on its own — the same durability-through-difference that had made exendin-4 useful to Eng a continent away. Two research programs, run by people who were not collaborating in any close sense, kept arriving at variations on the same insight: whatever it was about GLP-1 signaling that made it worth harnessing, a molecule that resisted the body's own two-minute clock on the native hormone would be the one that actually worked as medicine.

None of this, in the 1980s or through most of the 1990s, looked like the beginning of a story about obesity, or about a drug so culturally dominant that its brand name would become a stand-in verb for an entire category of medicine. It looked like diabetes research, conducted by scientists whose primary audience was other scientists, funded by grants written to justify incremental progress on a well-defined biochemical question. Eng was trying to explain an odd result in a guinea pig assay. Drucker was trying to make sense of a gene his lab had been handed almost by accident. The Gila monster was not a metaphor anyone had reason to reach for yet; it was simply the animal whose venom happened to contain the most convenient natural example of a hormone the body degrades too quickly to use as medicine. Drucker was recognized for the accumulated weight of that work in 2025, sharing the Breakthrough Prize in Life Sciences with four other researchers for the discovery and development of GLP-1 biology — an award that arrived only after the drugs built on that biology had already reordered how tens of millions of people ate, how several of the world's largest food and beverage companies talked to their investors, and how at least one small Nordic insulin manufacturer became, for a while, worth more than the entire economy of the country that hosted it. The award recognized forty years of unglamorous, structural, receptor-by-receptor work. The world had already started paying attention to the consequences before it had fully worked out who to credit for the cause — a pattern that becomes considerably sharper, and considerably less comfortable, in the story of the scientist most responsible for identifying which fragment of GLP-1 actually did the work in human blood.
