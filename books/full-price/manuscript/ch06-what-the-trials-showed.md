# What the Trials Showed

Clinical trials of this scale and duration are themselves a considerable undertaking, worth describing briefly for a reader who has never had reason to think closely about what actually happens inside a modern Phase 3 drug trial. Running a randomized, placebo-controlled study across nearly two thousand participants for sixty-eight consecutive weeks requires dozens of clinical trial sites, typically spread across multiple countries to ensure the resulting data generalizes beyond any single population's genetics, diet, and healthcare context; a small army of coordinators tracking each participant's weight, blood work, side effects, and adherence at regular intervals; a data safety monitoring board, independent of the drug's manufacturer, empowered to halt the trial early if the emerging data suggested unacceptable harm; and a statistical analysis plan locked in place before the trial begins, specifying exactly which outcomes will be measured and how, precisely so that a company cannot, after the fact, comb through the resulting data for whichever positive finding happens to reach statistical significance. STEP 1's own scale — nearly two thousand participants, more than a year of continuous dosing, a placebo comparator group receiving the same lifestyle counseling as the treatment group — reflects an investment easily running into the tens of millions of dollars, made years before Novo Nordisk could know whether the drug would clear the FDA's bar for approval at all, let alone become the commercial phenomenon it eventually did.

The trial that turned semaglutide from a diabetes drug with an interesting off-label reputation into the drug that would eventually reorder several industries was called STEP 1, an acronym for Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with obesity, and it was designed, in the most basic sense, to answer a question obesity medicine had been asking without a satisfying answer for decades: how much weight can a drug, rather than diet and exercise alone, actually take off a person who has struggled with obesity, and how safely can it do so.

STEP 1 enrolled 1,961 adults, none of whom had diabetes, all with either a body mass index of 30 or above or a BMI of 27 or above accompanied by at least one weight-related health condition. Participants were randomized, roughly two to one, between semaglutide at the 2.4-milligram weekly dose that would eventually become Wegovy's approved dose, and a placebo injection, with everyone in the trial also receiving lifestyle counseling on diet and physical activity — a design choice that meant the trial was testing the drug's effect on top of, not instead of, the conventional advice obesity medicine had always offered. The trial ran 68 weeks. It was funded by Novo Nordisk, as essentially every major trial in this book's clinical chapters was funded by the manufacturer whose drug it tested, a point worth flagging plainly rather than passing over, because it means every efficacy number that follows should be read as coming from an interested party's own trial program, even where the results were later published in a peer-reviewed journal and, in several cases, corroborated by independent replication.

The results, published in the New England Journal of Medicine on March 18, 2021, under the authorship of a multinational group of investigators led by John P.H. Wilding and including Rachel Batterham, Melanie Davies, Thomas Wadden, Julio Rosenstock, and Robert Kushner among others, showed a mean body weight reduction of 14.9 percent in the semaglutide group, against 2.4 percent in the placebo group — an estimated treatment difference of roughly 12.4 percentage points, translating in practical terms to an average of somewhere around 28 additional pounds lost compared to lifestyle counseling alone. It is worth being specific about what a trial of this design can and cannot claim, since the distinction matters for how confidently a reader should treat the headline number. STEP 1 was randomized and placebo-controlled, the gold standard for establishing that an observed effect is attributable to the drug rather than to chance, to the placebo effect of simply being enrolled in a monitored weight-loss study, or to the lifestyle counseling every participant received regardless of which injection they were given. It was not, however, a trial designed to isolate semaglutide's effect from the effect of that counseling entirely, since both study arms received the same dietary and activity guidance throughout — meaning the reported 12.4-percentage-point treatment difference between drug and placebo is the cleanest available estimate of what the drug itself adds on top of standard lifestyle advice, not a claim that the drug alone, absent any counseling at all, would produce an identical result. It was also, like nearly every major trial examined in this book, funded and run by the drug's own manufacturer, a standard and fully disclosed arrangement in pharmaceutical research but one that means the trial's design choices — which population to enroll, which comparator to use, which secondary outcomes to measure and publish — were made by a party with a direct commercial stake in how favorably the results would read.

Those numbers sat well outside the range obesity medicine had previously been able to offer through pharmacology. Older prescription weight-loss drugs — phentermine-topiramate, naltrexone-bupropion, orlistat — had typically produced average losses in the mid-single digits as a percentage of body weight, numbers clinically meaningful for some patients but rarely dramatic enough to compete, in most physicians' or patients' minds, with the more invasive but historically more effective option of bariatric surgery. A drug producing close to 15 percent average weight loss, delivered by a once-weekly injection a patient could self-administer at home, was a different category of intervention entirely, closer in magnitude to what surgery could achieve without the surgical risk, recovery time, or irreversibility that came with it.

STEP 1 was not, in fact, a single trial standing alone; it was the flagship result of a much larger clinical program that ran to at least eight numbered STEP trials, each testing semaglutide against a different comparator or in a different population — against a different weight-loss drug in one trial, in adolescents in another, in patients with type 2 diabetes in a third, withdrawn and restarted in a fourth to study durability directly. That breadth mattered for the FDA's ultimate approval decision in a way the headline STEP 1 number alone does not fully convey: regulators evaluating a new drug for a chronic, long-term-use condition like obesity want evidence the effect holds across different patient populations and different clinical contexts, not merely in the single, most favorable population a company might have hand-picked for its primary registration trial. The STEP program's breadth, run in parallel across multiple patient populations simultaneously, reflects the scale of investment a company confident in a molecule's underlying value is willing to make well before that molecule has earned a single dollar of approved-indication revenue — a bet Novo Nordisk was, by 2021, in an unusually strong position to make, given how the earlier off-label enthusiasm this book's previous chapter documented had already signaled the scale of the commercial opportunity waiting on the other side of a successful approval.

The FDA approved semaglutide 2.4mg for chronic weight management on June 4, 2021, under the brand name Wegovy, citing the STEP program as the basis for approval and describing it, in Novo Nordisk's own announcement of the decision, as the first once-weekly GLP-1 therapy specifically approved for weight management. What that approval did, beyond simply putting a new SKU on pharmacy shelves, was give formal medical and regulatory legitimacy to a use case that had, for the better part of four years, existed mostly off-label and mostly informally — turning word-of-mouth discovery into an approved indication a physician could prescribe openly and, at least in principle, an insurer could be asked to cover.

The mechanism behind those numbers, briefly touched on in the previous chapter through the more intuitive language patients had developed to describe their own experience, has a more precise physiological description worth spelling out here. GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide stimulate insulin secretion from the pancreas, but only when blood glucose is already elevated — a glucose-dependent mechanism that explains why these drugs rarely cause the dangerous blood-sugar crashes associated with insulin itself or with older diabetes drugs like sulfonylureas that force insulin release regardless of the body's actual glucose level. They suppress glucagon, the hormone that raises blood sugar, further reinforcing that glucose-stabilizing effect. In the stomach, they relax the gastric fundus and slow the rate of gastric emptying substantially, meaning a meal's worth of food remains in the stomach and continues signaling fullness for far longer than it would unmedicated. And in the brain, receptors concentrated in the hypothalamus and brainstem, reached both through direct circulation and through vagal nerve signaling from the gut, dampen the neural circuitry responsible for hunger and, in a separate but related pathway, for food-related reward and craving. None of these individual effects was entirely unknown before Wegovy's approval; each had been documented in smaller pieces across decades of incretin physiology research, much of it traceable to the foundational work this book has already followed from Svetlana Mojsov's peptide synthesis through Daniel Drucker's receptor biology. What STEP 1 did was demonstrate, at a scale and with a rigor no earlier trial in this drug class had achieved, how large an effect all of those mechanisms working together could produce in real patients over more than a year of treatment.

The reception among obesity medicine specialists went beyond ordinary enthusiasm for a strong trial result. For decades, a meaningful current within the field had argued, against considerable public skepticism, that obesity functioned as a chronic disease with a strong physiological and hormonal basis rather than as a simple deficit of willpower or discipline correctable through diet advice alone — a position the American Medical Association had formally endorsed by classifying obesity as a disease in 2013, well before STEP 1 gave that classification a matching pharmacological tool. STEP 1's results were read, within that professional community, as vindication of the chronic-disease model in a very concrete sense: a drug that acted on a specific, identifiable hormonal pathway had produced weight loss at a scale diet and exercise counseling essentially never achieved on its own in trial populations, strongly suggesting that whatever had been keeping STEP 1's participants at a higher body weight before the trial began was not simply a matter of insufficiently rigorous behavioral effort. That reframing carried real consequences beyond the academic literature — for how physicians talked to patients about weight, for how insurers would eventually be pressured to think about coverage, and for how patients themselves would come to understand their own relationship to hunger, appetite, and body weight, questions this book returns to at length in its later chapters on stigma, culture, and the philosophical question of what has actually changed about being a person with an appetite.

The reclassification also had a practical, professional dimension beyond the theoretical argument about chronic disease. Obesity medicine had, for decades, occupied an uncertain place within the broader medical profession — a field many physicians entered as a subspecialty after training in internal medicine, endocrinology, or family practice, often treated with less institutional prestige than fields built around more dramatic or more technically demanding interventions, and frequently underfunded relative to the scale of the population it served. STEP 1's results, and the wave of prescribing and public attention that followed Wegovy's approval, changed that professional standing considerably. Obesity medicine fellowship programs saw rising interest from residents choosing subspecialty training, pharmaceutical companies redirected substantial research funding toward the field, and medical journals that had historically given obesity research a comparatively modest share of coverage began treating it as a lead story — a shift in institutional attention that, whatever else it produced, gave obesity medicine specialists considerably more resources and considerably more of a public hearing than the field had commanded in the decades before a single trial result made the underlying science impossible to keep treating as a secondary concern.

It is worth pausing, before moving on to the corporate rivalry Wegovy's success provoked, to hold onto two qualifications the trial data itself makes plain, because both will matter considerably later in this book. First, STEP 1's headline number describes an average; individual results varied substantially, and a meaningful minority of participants lost far less weight than the mean, or in rare cases did not respond to the drug in a clinically significant way at all, a variability real-world prescribing has continued to bear out. Second, and more consequentially, STEP 1 was not a trial of a finite treatment course with a durable, lifelong result; it was a trial of continuous, ongoing weekly injection over 68 weeks, and a companion extension study following participants after they stopped the drug found that the weight came back, substantially, once treatment ended — a finding this book examines in full later, because it sits at the center of the argument, made forcefully by both the drug's manufacturers and its sharpest critics for very different reasons, that these drugs function less like a course of antibiotics and more like a blood pressure medication: a treatment for a chronic condition that, once started, a patient is expected to continue more or less indefinitely.

That expectation of indefinite use is, on its own, one of the more commercially significant facts in this entire book, and Eli Lilly understood it as clearly as Novo Nordisk did. A drug patients take once and are cured does not build a market the way a drug patients take every week for the rest of their lives does, and by the time Wegovy's approval confirmed the scale of demand a genuinely effective weight-loss injection could generate, Lilly had already spent years developing an answer of its own — a molecule built on a different, more ambitious pharmacological bet than the single-receptor approach that had carried Novo Nordisk this far.
