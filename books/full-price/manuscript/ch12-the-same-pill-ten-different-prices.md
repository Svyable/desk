# The Same Pill, Ten Different Prices

Senator Bernie Sanders, chairman of the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee, opened a formal investigation into Novo Nordisk's American pricing on April 24, 2024, sending a letter to the company demanding an explanation for a gap that, once laid out in a single table, was hard to read as anything but arbitrary. Ozempic, the committee's subsequent report found, listed at roughly $969 a month in the United States, against $155 in Canada, $122 in Denmark — Novo Nordisk's own home country — and figures in the range of $60 to $100 in Germany, depending on formulation and pack size. Wegovy listed at $1,349 a month in the United States, against figures in the range of $185 to $265 in Canada, $186 in Denmark, and $92 to $140 in the United Kingdom. The precise German and Canadian figures varied somewhat depending on which formulation and which analyst's methodology one consulted, a reminder that international drug-price comparisons are messier in practice than a single clean chart suggests. What did not vary, across every version of the comparison anyone produced, was the basic shape of the gap: the same molecule, manufactured in the same factories, cost Americans somewhere between four and ten times what it cost patients in almost every other wealthy country on earth.

The committee's investigation escalated through the following months. Sanders threatened to subpoena Novo Nordisk's chief executive, Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen, after the company initially resisted sending him to testify; Jørgensen agreed to appear, and the subpoena threat was dropped. The hearing itself took place on September 24, 2024, under the official title "Why Is Novo Nordisk Charging Americans with Diabetes and Obesity Outrageously High Prices for Ozempic and Wegovy?" — a title that left little ambiguity about the committee's own conclusion before a single witness had spoken. Sanders, addressing Jørgensen directly on the hearing floor, delivered the line that both CNBC and NBC Washington would run as their own headlines within hours: "All we are saying, Mr. Jørgensen, is treat the American people the same way that you treat people all over the world. Stop ripping us off." Jørgensen, for his part, did not dispute the underlying price figures; he pointed instead to the structure of the American pharmaceutical distribution system itself, arguing that a substantial share of the gap between Novo Nordisk's list price and what insurers actually paid after rebates was consumed by intermediaries — pharmacy benefit managers, insurers, and the rebate-driven formulary system built around them — rather than flowing to the manufacturer as pure profit, while stopping short of committing, at the hearing itself, to any specific reduction in the company's American list prices.

Sanders left the hearing with something concrete beyond a viral quote: written commitments, which he announced from the dais, from the three largest pharmacy benefit managers in the country — UnitedHealth's Optum Rx, CVS Health's Caremark, and Cigna's Express Scripts — agreeing that if Novo Nordisk meaningfully cut its list prices, the PBMs would not use that cut as an excuse to restrict formulary access, and would in fact expand coverage rather than narrow it. It was a modest, conditional promise, but it mattered, because one of the more persistent arguments the pharmaceutical industry had made against unilateral price cuts was that PBMs, which negotiate rebates calculated as a percentage or fixed amount off a drug's list price, had their own structural incentive to prefer a higher list price paired with a larger rebate over a genuinely lower price with nothing to rebate — an incentive critics argued helped explain why list prices kept climbing even as the rebate-adjusted "net price" insurers actually paid often moved in a different direction.

The academic case that the gap between American list prices and any defensible manufacturing cost was not merely large but almost absurdly so came from a March 2024 study led by Melissa Barber, a postdoctoral health economist then at Yale School of Medicine, working with co-authors at King's College Hospital London and Harvard Medical School, published in JAMA Network Open in coordination with the Doctors Without Borders Access Campaign. Barber's team calculated that semaglutide could be manufactured profitably — including a reasonable margin for the manufacturer — for somewhere between 89 cents and $4.73 per month. Told of the finding by NBC News, Sanders summarized it for a general audience as evidence that Ozempic could be produced for less than $100 a month; Barber herself, quoted by Fortune, put the underlying point more bluntly: "The profit margin is immense." A companion finding from the same research group, examining long-acting insulin pens — the drug class Novo Nordisk's entire corporate history had originally been built around, a full century earlier — found a similar pattern: an estimated cost-based fair price around $1.30 per pen, against actual charged prices ranging from $2.98 in South Africa and $7.88 in India to $28.40 in the United States.

Denmark's own government, it turned out, had been fighting a smaller-scale version of the same battle against its most famous corporate export. A KFF Health News investigation, headlined "How Little Denmark Got Homegrown Giant Novo Nordisk To Lower Ozempic Prices," documented that Novo Nordisk had cut Ozempic's price within Denmark itself by roughly 34 percent, to about $130 a month — itself only around 15 percent of the drug's American list price — after Danish public outcry over the strain the drug's popularity was putting on the country's public health budget. Bloomberg reported separately, in May 2024, that Denmark had gone further and actually reduced its public subsidies for Ozempic domestically, because demand and associated public spending had grown faster than the health system's budget could comfortably absorb — a genuinely striking data point for this book's argument, because it means that even in the one country where Novo Nordisk might reasonably be expected to price its own signature drug as a matter of national pride, the government still found the drug too expensive to subsidize without limit, at a price that remained, even after the domestic cut, a small fraction of what American patients were paying for the identical product.

The pressure Sanders had applied in 2024 did not immediately produce a price cut, and it would be misleading to draw a straight causal line from a single Senate hearing to what eventually followed, more than a year later, under different corporate leadership entirely. Jørgensen, the executive who had testified before Sanders, was pushed out of Novo Nordisk's top job in a boardroom dispute in 2025, replaced in August of that year by Maziar "Mike" Doustdar — the same leadership transition examined in this book's chapter on the company's market-value collapse, driven considerably more by competitive pressure from Eli Lilly's better-performing drug and Novo Nordisk's own disappointing next-generation trial results than by any single congressional hearing. What can be said with more confidence is that the pressure accumulated across multiple channels at once, through 2025 and into 2026 — congressional scrutiny, competitive threat, a shrinking market share, a battered stock price, and a new chief executive with every incentive to make a decisive gesture distinguishing his tenure from his predecessor's — and that those channels converged, within roughly eighteen months of the Sanders hearing, on two significant moves.

The first came from the White House rather than from either manufacturer directly. On November 6, 2025, President Trump announced a negotiated deal with both Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk, styled after the "most-favored-nation" pricing concept the administration had pushed across several drug categories, and built around a new government-run purchasing platform called TrumpRx, slated to launch the following January. Under the deal's terms, starting doses of the existing injectable drugs — Wegovy and Zepbound — would launch on the new platform at $350 a month, with that price scheduled to decline to $245 over the following two years. Medicare beneficiaries prescribed the drugs for obesity accompanied by a related health condition would pay a $50 monthly copay, a mechanism that achieved, through direct negotiation rather than through the CMS rulemaking process the Trump administration had itself declined to finalize the previous year, at least a partial version of the broader Medicare obesity-coverage expansion that rule would have provided. Future oral obesity pills from both companies, still pending FDA approval at the time of the announcement, were priced under the same deal at a starting $149 a month across Medicare, Medicaid, and the TrumpRx platform alike. In exchange, both companies received a three-year exemption from pharmaceutical tariffs the administration had separately been pursuing — a trade concession that gave the price concessions a clear, if unstated, commercial rationale beyond simple goodwill.

The second move came from Novo Nordisk alone, and went further than the TrumpRx deal's terms had required. On February 24, 2026, the company announced it would cut its U.S. list prices for Wegovy, Ozempic, and its oral diabetes drug Rybelsus to $675 a month across the board, effective January 1, 2027 — roughly a 50 percent reduction for Wegovy from its earlier $1,349 list price, and roughly a 35 percent reduction for Ozempic. Jamey Millar, Novo Nordisk's executive vice president of U.S. operations, framed the decision in the company's own announcement as a response to scale rather than to political pressure specifically: "Lowering the list price of Wegovy and Ozempic is the best approach to address the unprecedented opportunity to help more than 100 million people living with obesity and over 35 million people with type 2 diabetes in the United States." Whatever the internal calculus behind that framing, the practical effect, arriving roughly seventeen months after Sanders had told Jørgensen to his face to stop treating Americans differently from everyone else, was a company narrowing, though by no means eliminating, the exact gap the senator's investigation had spent two years documenting.

None of this resolved the underlying tension driving the international comparison in the first place: a country whose National Institutes of Health, university laboratories, and Veterans Affairs hospitals had funded or hosted a meaningful share of the foundational science this book's earlier chapters traced — Rosalyn Yalow's radioimmunoassay technique, John Eng's isolation of exendin-4 at the Bronx VA, decades of NIH-supported incretin research — was also the country paying, even after the 2026 cut, several times what patients in Germany, Denmark, or Canada paid for the resulting drugs. That gap sits at the center of an argument this book's later chapters take up directly: whether a pricing system that lets the country doing the most to fund the underlying research also pay the most for the resulting product is a defensible way to finance pharmaceutical innovation, or simply the predictable outcome of a market with far less price regulation than every other wealthy country has been willing to accept. Before that argument, though, the same drugs whose pricing occupied a Senate committee for the better part of two years were already reshaping an entirely different set of numbers — the ones showing up in grocery-store scanner data, brewery earnings calls, and the quarterly reports of companies that had never expected a diabetes drug to become their single most-discussed competitive threat.
