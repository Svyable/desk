# Who Pays

It is worth understanding the basic mechanics of how a drug's price actually moves through the American healthcare system before following the North Carolina story in detail, because the gap between a drug's advertised list price and what any particular payer actually spends is where much of this chapter's real complexity lives. A manufacturer sets a list price, sometimes called the wholesale acquisition cost, but very few payers — insurers, employers, or government programs — actually pay that full amount. Pharmacy benefit managers, private intermediaries that most large employers and insurers hire to manage their prescription drug benefits, negotiate rebates directly with manufacturers, effectively a retroactive discount off the list price in exchange for favorable formulary placement that steers patient volume toward that manufacturer's product over a competitor's. The size of that rebate is typically a closely guarded trade secret, meaning the actual "net price" a plan pays after rebates can differ enormously, and often invisibly to an outside observer, from the sticker price a patient sees quoted in the news. This opacity is precisely what made North Carolina's own internal deliberations so unusually revealing to outside observers: because the state plan is a public entity, its board's internal debate over the exact dollar value of its own rebate arrangement, and the cost consequences of losing it, played out with a degree of visibility most private-sector coverage disputes never receive.

Dale Folwell, the North Carolina state treasurer, chairs the board that oversees the North Carolina State Health Plan, which covers roughly three-quarters of a million teachers, state employees, retirees, and their dependents. On January 27, 2024, that board voted 4 to 3 to stop covering GLP-1 drugs for weight loss, effective April 1 of that year. More than 23,000 plan members were using Wegovy or Saxenda at the time of the vote, at a net cost — after manufacturer rebates — of more than $800 per member per month, a figure Folwell's office projected would push the plan's total spending on these drugs past $170 million in 2024 alone, and past $1 billion over six years if the plan continued covering them without any cap. The math behind that projection carried a second, less obvious wrinkle: dropping the drugs from the plan's formulary meant losing the roughly 40 percent rebate Novo Nordisk and the plan's pharmacy benefit manager had negotiated, so simply continuing coverage without a rebate agreement would have cost the plan considerably more than continuing it with one — a detail that shaped the board's internal debate as much as the underlying question of whether the state should be paying for weight-loss medication at all.

The North Carolina decision became, almost immediately, the marquee example of a fight playing out in slower motion across employer and government health plans throughout the country: a genuinely effective drug, arriving at a genuinely unaffordable aggregate price, forcing institutions responsible for large risk pools to choose between extending coverage their budgets could not sustain at scale, or withdrawing coverage from patients for whom the drug was, by any reasonable clinical standard, working. The reversal came less than two years later. By late 2025, the North Carolina State Health Plan was asking the state legislature to fund reinstating the coverage it had voted to end, and North Carolina Medicaid, in a separate but related process, updated its own coverage for GLP-1 drugs prescribed for non-weight-loss indications in November 2025 before announcing in December 2025 that it would reinstitute coverage of the drugs for weight management specifically — a fuller circle than most policy reversals complete in under two years, and a sign of how genuinely unsettled the underlying cost-versus-benefit calculation remained even among the people whose job it was to make it.

North Carolina's specific numbers were unusually well publicized, but the underlying pattern showed up across employer health coverage nationally. The Kaiser Family Foundation's annual Employer Health Benefits Survey found that in 2024, 18 percent of large firms — those with 200 or more workers — covered GLP-1 drugs for weight loss, rising to 28 percent among the largest firms, those with 5,000 or more employees. By 2025, that overall figure had risen to roughly 20 percent, with coverage climbing sharply by firm size: 16 percent among firms with 200 to 999 workers, 30 percent among firms with 1,000 to 4,999 workers, and 43 percent among firms with 5,000 or more workers — up from 28 percent the year before. Then the trend reversed. The Business Group on Health's 2026 survey of large employers found the share offering GLP-1 coverage for obesity had fallen from 72 percent in 2025 to 60 percent in 2026, with nearly eight in ten employers reporting that GLP-1 spending was materially driving up their overall health-care costs, and a meaningful share of those still covering the drugs expressing uncertainty about whether they would maintain that coverage into 2027. The consulting firm Mercer separately found that 6 percent of large employers dropped weight-loss drug coverage outright in 2026, with another 5 percent actively planning or considering the same move for 2027.

It is worth pausing on why employer-sponsored insurance in particular became the site of such visible, repeated whiplash on this specific drug class, when it has generally absorbed the cost of other expensive new drug categories — biologic treatments for autoimmune disease, for instance, or the newer generation of cancer immunotherapies — with considerably less public back-and-forth. Part of the answer is sheer population scale: the Kaiser Family Foundation's estimate of roughly 50 million eligible American adults in employer plans dwarfs the patient population for almost any other single expensive drug class, meaning even a modest uptake rate translates into aggregate costs large enough to move an employer's entire benefits budget in a single renewal cycle, in a way a rare-disease drug serving a few thousand patients nationally never could. Part of the answer is that obesity, unlike most conditions triggering an expensive biologic prescription, sits astride an unresolved cultural argument about whether it is fully a medical condition or partly a matter of individual choice — an argument this book's later chapter on stigma and disability advocacy examines directly — which gave employers and plan administrators a rhetorical opening to treat coverage as optional or reversible in a way they would rarely feel comfortable doing with, say, chemotherapy. And part of the answer is timing: these drugs arrived at a moment when employer health costs were already rising sharply across the board, making a genuinely large new expense line an unusually convenient target for a benefits committee looking for somewhere to make a visible cut.

Individual institutional decisions, layered on top of those survey trends, put faces and dollar figures on the abstraction. Mayo Clinic imposed a $20,000 lifetime cap on weight-loss drug coverage within its own employee health plan. Ascension, one of the country's largest Catholic health systems, dropped weight-loss coverage entirely for its 139,000 employees in July 2023, citing cost-effectiveness and safety concerns. Fairview Health Services limited its own coverage to employees with a body mass index of 40 or above, a considerably narrower threshold than the FDA's approved label, citing an anticipated $10 million annual cost if it extended coverage more broadly. On the insurer side rather than the employer side, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan reported a $350 million increase in its own GLP-1-related drug costs in 2023 alone; Blue Cross Blue Shield of Massachusetts recorded a $400 million operating loss in 2024 that it attributed substantially to GLP-1 spending, disclosing that five GLP-1 manufacturers together accounted for more than $300 million of its pharmacy spending that year, roughly a fifth of its total pharmacy budget, after that spending had doubled year over year. The Kaiser Family Foundation has estimated that roughly 50 million American adults enrolled in employer health plans meet the clinical eligibility criteria that would qualify them for one of these drugs under its approved label — a population large enough that even a modest uptake rate translates into the kind of budget-moving numbers Blue Cross Blue Shield of Massachusetts and dozens of employers like North Carolina's state plan were confronting directly.

The federal government's own relationship to this coverage question ran through an even longer and stranger history, because Medicare had been legally barred from covering weight-loss drugs at all since long before Ozempic or Wegovy existed. The Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003, the law that created Medicare's Part D prescription drug benefit, statutorily excluded from coverage any drug used for "anorexia, weight loss, or weight gain" — a category drawn from an equivalent, older Medicaid exclusion, and shaped, according to the historical context most policy analysts attach to it, partly by the shadow of the fenfluramine-phentermine, or "fen-phen," diet-drug scandal of the 1990s, in which a popular weight-loss combination was pulled from the market after being linked to serious heart-valve damage. Congress, writing Part D's rules in the early 2000s, effectively classified weight-loss drugs as a lifestyle or cosmetic category rather than a medically necessary one, a classification that the GLP-1 drugs' own extraordinary clinical results would eventually make look badly outdated, without Congress ever formally revisiting the underlying statute.

The opening that let Medicare begin covering these drugs at all came, tellingly, not through obesity itself but through the heart. In March 2024, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services issued guidance clarifying that an anti-obesity medication receiving FDA approval for an additional, separate medically accepted indication — something other than weight loss on its own — could be covered under Part D for that specific indication, even while remaining excluded when prescribed for weight loss alone. That clarification followed directly from a landmark clinical trial result: the SELECT trial, led by A. Michael Lincoff and published in the New England Journal of Medicine in November and December 2023, had randomized 17,604 adults age 45 or older, with established cardiovascular disease and a body mass index of 27 or above but without diabetes, to semaglutide or placebo, and found a 20 percent reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events — cardiovascular death, non-fatal heart attack, non-fatal stroke — among those taking the drug, alongside continued weight loss over the trial's roughly four-year follow-up. The FDA approved Wegovy for that cardiovascular risk-reduction indication on the strength of SELECT's data, and CMS's March 2024 guidance meant that, for the first time, a Medicare beneficiary with established heart disease could have Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus, or Mounjaro covered under Part D — not because they needed to lose weight, in the statute's own framing, but because a large randomized trial had proven the drug also protected their heart. Individual Part D plans still retained discretion over whether to actually place these drugs on their formularies, and coverage for weight loss alone remained excluded by the letter of the law, but the SELECT trial had, in effect, found a side door into a federal program the plain language of a twenty-year-old statute had locked from the front.

The political dynamics surrounding the 2024 proposed rule are worth drawing out explicitly, because they illustrate a recurring feature of how expensive new drug categories get absorbed, or fail to get absorbed, into American public insurance programs. A rule this consequential arrived in the closing months of an outgoing presidential administration, a timing pattern common enough in American administrative law to have its own informal name — a "midnight rule" — and one that leaves such regulations especially vulnerable to reversal by an incoming administration of a different party, since the normal, multi-year rulemaking process that produced the original proposal can be undone through a comparatively faster review and non-finalization by whoever holds office when the comment period closes and the final rule is due. That vulnerability is precisely what played out here: a Democratic administration's CMS proposed the obesity-coverage expansion in its final months in office, and the incoming Republican administration, inheriting the unfinished rulemaking process, chose not to complete it — a sequence of events that reflects normal, unremarkable administrative transition procedure as much as it reflects any specific judgment either administration made about the underlying merits of Medicare obesity coverage, though press coverage at the time often framed the reversal in more starkly ideological terms than the procedural reality alone would fully justify.

A more ambitious attempt to walk through that door fully rather than partially came in November 2024, when the outgoing Biden administration's CMS proposed a rule that would have reinterpreted the statute to stop excluding anti-obesity medications from Part D and Medicaid when prescribed for obesity itself, on the theory that obesity is properly understood as a chronic disease in its own right rather than a condition requiring some secondary, non-obesity justification to treat. CMS's own fact sheet estimated the change would cost the federal government $24.8 billion over ten years in Part D spending and $14.8 billion over ten years in Medicaid spending. The Congressional Budget Office, working independently, put the net ten-year cost at roughly $35 billion, projecting that 12.5 million Medicare beneficiaries would become newly eligible for an anti-obesity medication in the rule's first year alone, at an average federal cost per user starting around $5,600 and gradually declining toward $4,300 by the early 2030s as competition and manufacturing scale brought prices down. The Trump administration, in office by the time the rule was due for finalization, declined to adopt it: the final Contract Year 2026 Medicare Advantage and Part D rule, issued in April 2025, explicitly excluded the anti-obesity-medication coverage expansion, leaving Medicare and Medicaid coverage of these drugs available, as before, only through the SELECT-trial-shaped side door of a qualifying non-obesity indication rather than through any broad, statute-level recognition of obesity itself as Medicare's business to treat.

That was not, it turned out, the end of the story, but the mechanism that eventually widened Medicare's coverage further was strikingly different from the regulatory rulemaking process Washington had just watched fail — a negotiated deal between the White House and the two manufacturers themselves, tied explicitly to tariff policy rather than to any reinterpretation of the 2003 statute, arriving in November 2025 alongside an equally consequential shift in how these drugs were priced not just for American patients, but relative to patients paying dramatically less for the identical product in every other wealthy country on earth. That international gap — the reason a U.S. senator felt compelled to subpoena a foreign pharmaceutical executive, and the reason Novo Nordisk itself, under new leadership, eventually chose to cut its own list prices by nearly half — is where this book turns next.
