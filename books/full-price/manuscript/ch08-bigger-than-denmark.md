# Bigger Than Denmark

For a few days in early September 2023, Novo Nordisk was worth more than any other publicly listed company in Europe — more than LVMH, the French luxury conglomerate that had held the title, more than any bank, any oil major, any technology firm on the continent. On September 5, 2023, Novo Nordisk's market capitalization closed at roughly 2.96 trillion Danish kroner, on the order of $428 billion, edging past LVMH's roughly $416 billion. A company whose entire founding story, a century earlier, had been about securing Nordic manufacturing rights to a hormone discovered in Toronto, so that a physiologist's wife with diabetes could be treated with something more reliable than whatever crude extract was available, was now, by market value, larger than the maker of Louis Vuitton handbags and Moët champagne. Commentators reached for the most legible comparison available and found it in Denmark's own economy: Novo Nordisk's market capitalization, by mid-2023, exceeded the entire gross domestic product of the country that hosted it — roughly $418 billion in market value against an IMF-projected 2023 Danish GDP of about $405.6 billion. A single pharmaceutical company, built around two drugs whose names had only recently entered everyday conversation, was worth more than everything else Denmark's 5.9 million people produced in a year, combined.

The growth driving that valuation was not abstract. In the first half of 2023, Ozempic sales rose 58 percent year over year; Wegovy sales, still working through the supply constraints examined in a later chapter, rose 367 percent. Novo Nordisk's overall profit for the same six-month period climbed 43 percent, to roughly 39.24 billion Danish kroner, on the order of $5.6 billion, and the company's stock had already risen about 40 percent year to date by the time it briefly took the European crown from LVMH. Investors were not pricing in a mature, steady-state pharmaceutical business; they were pricing in a company that appeared, at that specific moment, to have a near-monopoly position in the earliest innings of a drug category multiple Wall Street analysts were beginning to describe, with escalating confidence through 2023 and 2024, as capable of reaching well past $100 billion in annual global sales within a decade.

The mechanics of that valuation are worth spelling out for a reader unfamiliar with how equity markets price a growth story rather than simply a company's current earnings, because they explain both the speed of Novo Nordisk's rise and the severity of its later fall. A stock's price reflects not merely what a company is earning today but the market's collective, constantly updating guess about how much larger those earnings will grow, and how confidently that growth can be relied upon. When Novo Nordisk's 2023 sales data showed Wegovy revenue climbing 367 percent in a single half-year period, with clear evidence that demand was being rationed by supply constraints rather than limited by any weakening of underlying interest, investors reasonably extrapolated that trajectory forward, and competed to own a piece of a company whose most important product appeared to be supply-constrained rather than demand-constrained — ordinarily one of the most favorable positions a consumer or pharmaceutical business can occupy, since it implies years of pricing power and volume growth still ahead before the market even approaches saturation. That same mechanism, run in reverse, is what made the CagriSema disappointment in December 2024 so violently punishing to the stock: a single trial result that fell several percentage points short of an expectation the market had already priced in as close to certain forced a rapid, simultaneous downward revision of both Novo Nordisk's competitive position and its future growth trajectory, and equity markets tend to move faster and further in that kind of rapid repricing than the underlying change in the company's actual, current business would justify on its own.

The stock kept climbing for close to another year, reaching an all-time high around June 25, 2024, with Novo Nordisk's Copenhagen-listed shares closing near 135.66 kroner and its American depositary receipts touching roughly $148.15. By multiple measures, including a CNBC chart analysis of the company's trajectory, Novo Nordisk's peak market value approached $615 billion. That peak marked the top of a run that had, by any ordinary standard of pharmaceutical-company valuation, been extraordinary — a company whose stock had multiplied several times over in the space of a few years, built almost entirely on the commercial success of a molecule most of the industry had regarded, less than a decade earlier, as a solid but unremarkable diabetes therapy.

What followed the peak was a correction sharp and sustained enough to function, in retrospect, as a case study in how quickly a pharmaceutical market built on a single company's continued dominance can reprice once that dominance stops looking assured. The first serious blow landed on December 20, 2024, when Novo Nordisk released topline results from REDEFINE-1, a Phase 3 trial of CagriSema, the company's next-generation combination drug pairing semaglutide with cagrilintide, an amylin-receptor agonist intended to push weight-loss results meaningfully beyond what semaglutide alone could achieve. The trial delivered average weight loss of about 20.4 percent — a genuinely strong result by any historical standard in obesity medicine, but well short of the roughly 25 percent figure investors and analysts had been led to expect as Novo Nordisk's answer to Lilly's more potent tirzepatide. Novo Nordisk's shares fell roughly 20 percent in a single trading session. Shareholders later filed suit alleging the company had not adequately disclosed that the trial used a flexible dosing protocol under which only 57 percent of participants actually reached the maximum tested dose, a design detail that, critics argued, made the headline result harder to interpret and easier to have anticipated as disappointing than the market's reaction suggested investors had understood going in.

The CagriSema disappointment did not resolve itself with a single bad quarter. On March 10, 2025, and again on March 24, 2025, further trial data — this time from CagriSema's diabetes-population program, showing roughly 15.7 percent weight loss after 68 weeks, again below the roughly 25 percent target investors had been anchored to — triggered additional share declines. Through 2025, Novo Nordisk cut its own financial guidance four separate times, an unusually high frequency of downward revision for a company of its size, as its share of the American GLP-1 market slipped to roughly 40 percent against Eli Lilly's roughly 60 percent, a reversal of the dominant position Novo Nordisk had held only a couple of years earlier, driven partly by tirzepatide's stronger head-to-head efficacy data and partly by accumulating real-world prescribing patterns that favored Lilly's drug once physicians and patients had two well-established options to choose between rather than one.

The company's leadership paid the price its investors were, by then, openly demanding. In May 2025, Novo Nordisk announced that Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen — the CEO who had presided over the company's extraordinary rise, and who had personally testified before the United States Senate the previous September defending the company's American pricing, a confrontation examined in detail later in this book — would step down, a departure press coverage described at the time as a surprise abdication rather than a planned succession. Maziar "Mike" Doustdar, previously the company's head of international operations, was named president and chief executive, effective August 7, 2025. Under Doustdar's leadership, Novo Nordisk moved quickly to cut costs to match a growth trajectory that had clearly disappointed the market's earlier expectations: on September 10, 2025, the company announced roughly nine thousand job cuts, about 11 percent of its global workforce of 78,400, with five thousand of those cuts falling in Denmark itself — a country where Novo Nordisk's role as employer, taxpayer, and source of national economic pride had made the company's fortunes a matter of unusually direct public interest even before its stock had briefly outweighed the national economy. The company targeted roughly 8 billion Danish kroner, on the order of $1.26 billion, in annual savings by the end of 2026, and cut its own operating profit growth guidance to a range of 4 to 10 percent, down from an earlier 10 to 16 percent. As if to underline how far the pendulum had swung, Novo Nordisk shares fell a further 15 percent on February 23, 2026, after a head-to-head trial showed Eli Lilly's next-generation candidate outperforming Novo Nordisk's own new drug in development — the same competitive dynamic that had once worked in Novo Nordisk's favor against slower-moving rivals now working decisively against it.

By the time Novo Nordisk's market value had fallen from its roughly $615 billion peak to somewhere in the neighborhood of $310 billion — a decline on the order of 50 percent from the top, and a fall of about 54 percent in the stock price specifically from its June 2024 high — the company had lost, along with roughly half its market capitalization, the "most valuable company in Europe" title it had held so briefly back in September 2023, ceding it back to a rotating cast of rivals including, once again, LVMH. Eli Lilly, meanwhile, was moving in the opposite direction. On November 21, 2025, Eli Lilly became the first pharmaceutical or healthcare company in history to reach a $1 trillion market capitalization, its shares closing at $1,059.70 — more than double the value of the next-largest pharmaceutical company, Johnson & Johnson, then worth roughly $491 billion. The company that had entered the GLP-1 story as a co-marketing partner on a Gila-monster-derived drug two decades earlier, and that had spent years developing a molecule built on a structurally different, dual-receptor bet than its Danish rival, had become, by a wide margin, the most valuable healthcare company that had ever existed.

The whiplash between Novo Nordisk's 2023 ascent and its 2024-2026 correction, set against Eli Lilly's steady climb to a milestone no pharmaceutical company had ever reached before, offers a useful corrective to any account of the GLP-1 drug class that treats its commercial success as a smooth, foreordained story. The underlying clinical demand for these drugs — millions of people whose blood sugar, cardiovascular risk, and quality of life genuinely improved on them — did not waver through any of this. What moved, sometimes by tens of billions of dollars in a single trading session, was investors' assessment of which specific company would capture the largest share of that demand, at what margin, against what competition, and how durably. That assessment turned out to be considerably less stable than the underlying medicine it was meant to be pricing, a volatility that becomes still more striking once the supply chain strained to keep pace with that demand is examined directly — the shortages, the counterfeits, and the grey market that formed in the gap between how fast these drugs became famous and how fast either company could actually manufacture enough of them.
