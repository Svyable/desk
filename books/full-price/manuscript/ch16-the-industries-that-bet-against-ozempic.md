# The Industries That Bet Against Ozempic

American bariatric surgery volumes peaked above 230,000 procedures in 2022 and fell to just over 177,000 by 2024 — a decline of roughly 23 percent in two years, dropping the annual total below 200,000 for the first time this decade. A study conducted by researchers at Mass General Brigham and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, published through STAT News reporting on October 25, 2024, traced the two trends against each other directly: GLP-1 obesity prescriptions more than doubled between 2022 and 2023, rising 105.6 percent, while the metabolic and bariatric surgery rate fell 8.7 percent over the identical period. The mix of surgeries performed shifted as well, in a direction consistent with a patient population increasingly choosing surgery only once medication alone had not been enough: sleeve gastrectomy, generally considered a less invasive option, fell from 64 percent to 58 percent of all procedures, while gastric bypass, typically reserved for more severe or more complicated cases, rose from 28 to 33 percent. The pattern was not confined to the United States. The Brazilian Society of Bariatric and Metabolic Surgery reported an 18 percent drop in procedures in 2024 compared with the year before, evidence that whatever was reshaping the calculation between drug and scalpel in American operating rooms was showing up in other health systems too.

The economics underlying that shift, from the perspective of a health system rather than an individual patient, are more ambiguous than either a pure substitution story or a pure complementary-treatment story suggests. Bariatric surgery has historically been a one-time cost, typically in the range of tens of thousands of dollars including the procedure, hospitalization, and follow-up care, after which a health plan's ongoing financial exposure to that specific patient's obesity treatment largely ends. A GLP-1 prescription, by contrast, is an ongoing monthly cost that, per the chronic-disease framing examined in this book's later chapter on weight regain, a patient is expected to continue indefinitely — meaning that even where medication produces a comparable or superior clinical outcome to surgery, it does so through a completely different cost structure, one that a payer evaluating the two options over a five- or ten-year horizon might reasonably calculate very differently depending on how long a given patient is actually expected to remain on the drug, how the price of that drug is expected to move as competition and patent expiration reshape the market, and how the health system chooses to price the surgical alternative's own follow-up complications and revision-surgery risk. This is exactly the kind of long-horizon actuarial calculation the insurers and employers examined in this book's earlier chapter on coverage battles have been forced to make in real time, often with only a few years of real-world data to work from, for a decision with consequences that will not fully resolve for a decade or more.

It would be a mistake, though, to read those numbers as proof that GLP-1 drugs have made bariatric surgery obsolete, and the same Mass General Brigham research offers the clearest corrective available: across the full population of patients with obesity, only about 0.51 percent received either a GLP-1 medication or bariatric surgery during the study period — a figure so small it implies the overwhelming majority of people who would clinically qualify for either treatment were, as of the data available, receiving neither. The story these numbers tell is not one of substitution completed, but of substitution beginning, unevenly and from a very low base, inside a much larger population of eligible patients that both the pharmaceutical and surgical sides of obesity treatment remain, collectively, far from reaching. Bariatric surgery retains real advantages the drugs have not matched — a one-time intervention rather than an indefinite prescription, effects that in many patients persist well beyond what happens when a GLP-1 drug is discontinued, and, for patients whose obesity has progressed to a severity or duration where medication alone produces insufficient results, an option medication cannot yet fully replace. What has changed is the order in which patients and physicians now typically consider the two options, with medication increasingly the first line, and surgery increasingly a second line reserved for patients medication alone did not adequately serve — a genuine shift in clinical practice, even if the underlying surgical volume decline reflects only a modest fraction of the total eligible population making that switch.

The bariatric surgery profession's own response to its shrinking procedure volume offers a useful parallel to the aesthetic-medicine industry's rapid adaptation examined in this book's earlier chapter on Ozempic-era celebrity culture. Bariatric surgeons, rather than treating the arrival of an effective pharmaceutical alternative purely as competitive threat, increasingly positioned themselves as partners in a combined treatment pathway — prescribing or co-managing GLP-1 medication for patients before surgery to reduce operative risk in the highest-weight patients, and after surgery for patients whose weight loss plateaued or partially reversed, a pattern surgical societies began formally incorporating into updated clinical guidelines. That repositioning, from pure competitor to complementary partner, reflects a genuinely available professional strategy that a fitness company or a plus-size retailer, examined later in this chapter, had a much harder time replicating, because a surgeon's clinical relationship with a patient can absorb a new pharmaceutical tool into an existing treatment framework in a way a retailer selling a specific range of clothing sizes, or a hardware-and-subscription fitness company built around a specific piece of exercise equipment, structurally cannot.

Peloton's stock, meanwhile, had fallen roughly 96 percent from its pandemic-era peak by the time analysts and journalists began routinely asking whether GLP-1 drugs deserved some share of the blame. The honest answer, based on the available reporting, is that they deserve a share, but a considerably smaller one than the company's more dramatic headline numbers might suggest on their own: Peloton's collapse is attributable overwhelmingly to the ordinary, well-documented unwinding of pandemic-era demand — a company that sold an enormous volume of connected exercise equipment to a captive, homebound customer base, followed by a painful normalization once gyms reopened, offices called workers back, and the company was left holding excess inventory and a subscriber base that had grown far faster than it could sustainably retain. GLP-1 drugs are, at most, one contributing headwind layered on top of that larger and better-established story, not its primary cause. What is more interesting than the causal-attribution debate is how directly the company's current leadership has chosen to respond to the drugs rather than simply hoping the trend passes. Under chief executive Peter Stern, Peloton has built a customer-acquisition strategy specifically oriented around GLP-1 users, adding Yoga Sculpt and Pilates instructors to its platform aimed at a population trying to preserve lean muscle mass while losing weight on the drugs — a real and clinically grounded concern this book examines directly in its next chapter, given that twenty to fifty percent of the total weight lost on these medications, depending on the specific study and population, can consist of lean tissue rather than fat. An industry analysis from the investment bank Harrison Co. estimated GLP-1 adoption could add roughly $6.8 billion to the total U.S. fitness-club market — an increase of around 20 percent over a roughly $35 billion base — reasoning that patients who have committed to a weekly injection and a genuinely transformed relationship with food tend, once they start, to be considerably more likely to also commit to and sustain a structured exercise program than the general population has historically proven to be. Peloton's own connected-fitness subscriber base, still above 2.5 million as of its most recent reporting, suggests the company retains a meaningful audience to sell that specific pivot to, whatever the causes of its earlier, much larger collapse.

The broader fitness industry's relationship to this trend runs somewhat counter to the more straightforward disruption narrative Peloton's own struggles might suggest. Traditional gym chains, rather than treating a smaller-appetite membership base as a threat to visit frequency or retention, have generally welcomed the Harrison Co. analysis's underlying logic: patients who have already committed to a weekly injection and a fundamentally changed relationship with food, the reasoning goes, have demonstrated exactly the kind of sustained behavioral commitment that predicts long-term gym membership retention, a population historically hard for the fitness industry to identify and market to before they had already proven that commitment through some other channel first. Several national gym chains began exploring formal partnerships and referral relationships with telehealth GLP-1 prescribers during 2025, treating the two industries as complements serving the same underlying patient journey rather than as competitors for the same discretionary health spending — a strategic posture considerably closer to the bariatric-surgery profession's partnership model than to the more adversarial framing that dominated early press coverage of "Ozempic versus the gym industry."

Nowhere has the shift been more immediately and visibly commercial than in plus-size apparel retail, where Torrid — a specialty chain built entirely around women's clothing in sizes 10 through 30 — became the industry's clearest single case study. The company reported a 14.3 percent year-over-year sales decline for the fourth quarter of its 2025 fiscal year, alongside a net loss of $8.1 million, up sharply from a $3 million loss the year before, and announced plans to close 30 stores in the first half of 2026, following 151 store closures the company had already completed in prior years. Torrid's own public response has been notably measured rather than a wholesale strategic reversal — the company has continued to serve its existing customer base rather than pivoting its size range or core positioning, even as its topline numbers absorbed the direct commercial consequence of a shrinking pool of customers who, in aggregate, needed the specific range of larger sizes the store had been built entirely to serve. Industry analysts at the retail consultancy Impact Analytics estimated that GLP-1-driven demand shifts had put more than $5 billion of plus-size inventory across the broader apparel sector at risk of going unsold, as shoppers moving into smaller sizes returned merchandise or simply stopped buying in the ranges they no longer needed. Circana, a market research firm tracking the category, found that roughly 23 percent of American households were using a GLP-1 medication as of September 2025, up four percentage points from the year before, and that roughly 80 percent of GLP-1 users in its survey anticipated needing to buy new clothing specifically because of body-size changes the drug had produced — a genuinely enormous, drug-driven wardrobe-replacement cycle playing out simultaneously with the inventory writedown crisis it was creating on the supply side of the same industry.

Three industries, three different relationships to the same underlying shift: bariatric surgery ceding a meaningful, though still modest, share of its patient pipeline to a pharmaceutical alternative that arrived faster and less invasively than surgery ever could; a fitness company whose larger collapse had almost nothing to do with these drugs nonetheless building a genuine second act around serving the specific physiological needs of patients taking them; and a retail chain built entirely around a body-size range now shrinking, in aggregate, among a meaningful share of its own customer base, absorbing the kind of direct, hard-to-hedge commercial damage that a snack company reformulating its Mac and Cheese, or an airline quietly ending an accommodation policy, could each still plausibly treat as a manageable adjustment rather than an existential threat. None of that damage, though, addresses what these drugs are actually doing inside the bodies of the people taking them — a question this book has, so far, examined mostly through its visible economic consequences, and that deserves its own direct reckoning with the medicine itself: what, precisely, is being lost along with the weight, and what happens to a person's body, and a person's fertility, when the appetite that regulated both for their entire adult life is switched substantially off.
