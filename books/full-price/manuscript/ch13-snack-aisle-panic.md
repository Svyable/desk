# Snack Aisle Panic

The packaged-food industry's underlying business model, worth describing briefly before following its response to these drugs, has for decades depended on a handful of well-understood growth levers: expanding into new product categories, increasing prices ahead of inflation where the market would bear it, growing volume by encouraging existing customers to buy and consume more, and, perhaps most quietly important, engineering products specifically to be maximally craveable — optimizing salt, sugar, and fat ratios, along with texture and mouthfeel, to encourage consumption beyond simple nutritional need, a practice food scientists and public health researchers have documented and criticized for decades under the general heading of hyper-palatability. That last lever, more than any of the others, is the one these drugs threaten most directly, because it depends on exactly the kind of reward-circuit-driven overconsumption this book's earlier chapters described GLP-1 receptor agonists as blunting. A company whose growth strategy for half a century has rested partly on making a snack difficult to stop eating once started is facing something closer to a direct mechanistic challenge to its core competitive advantage than a simple shift in consumer taste, which helps explain why even a modest, single-digit-percentage effect on basket size generated the outsized anxiety and rapid reformulation response this chapter documents, considerably out of proportion to what a comparably sized shift in, say, brand preference or private-label competition would typically provoke.

Retail data science, largely invisible to an ordinary shopper filling a cart, made Furner's comment possible in a way worth briefly explaining, since the underlying capability is itself a fairly recent development in how large retailers understand their own customers. Walmart, like most major grocery retailers, has for years operated loyalty programs and payment-linked purchase tracking systems that let the company associate a given household's shopping basket with other data it holds about that household, including, increasingly, third-party health and pharmacy data available through data-sharing partnerships and the retailer's own in-house pharmacy operations. That infrastructure, built originally to power routine marketing and inventory optimization, turned out to double as an unusually precise instrument for detecting exactly the kind of behavioral shift this chapter describes, well before any published academic study like the Cornell research examined later in this chapter existed to confirm it independently — a reminder that some of the earliest, most consequential evidence in this entire book's economic story came not from a peer-reviewed journal but from a retailer's own internal data warehouse.

John Furner, then chief executive of Walmart's U.S. business, was not trying to make headlines when he answered an analyst's question about weight-loss drugs on an October 4, 2023 earnings call. He was describing what the company's own anonymized shopper data showed when it compared the buying habits of customers filling GLP-1 prescriptions against everyone else: "We definitely do see a slight change compared to the total population, we do see a slight pullback in overall basket," he said, characterizing the effect as "just less units, slightly less calories." It was a modest, hedged sentence, delivered by the country's largest retailer about a trend it was still just beginning to measure. It was also, within hours, the single most-quoted piece of evidence that an entire consumer economy built around the assumption of a relatively stable national appetite was about to have that assumption tested directly, in real purchasing data, by the largest retailer positioned to see it first.

What Furner's comment captured in miniature, the rest of the American food and beverage industry spent the following three years working out in fuller and more anxious detail. General Mills chief executive Jeff Harmening, speaking at the CAGNY investor conference — an annual gathering, formally the Consumer Analyst Group of New York, where major food companies present their outlook to Wall Street — offered one of the more direct executive assessments of the category's likely trajectory: "We expect GLP-1 and other anti-obesity drugs to have a lasting influence in the food and nutrition landscape, nudging some consumers towards smaller portions and more nutrient-dense protein and fiber-forward foods." On a subsequent earnings call the following March, Harmening was careful to separate that longer-run structural view from any specific quarter's sales miss, telling analysts flatly, when pressed on whether GLP-1 drugs explained a particular soft period, "That's not what we see in this environment" — a distinction between believing something is reshaping an entire category over years and believing it explains this quarter's numbers that several food-company executives found themselves drawing, in one form or another, as the narrative around these drugs threatened to become an all-purpose explanation reporters and analysts alike reached for whenever any packaged-food company's growth disappointed.

PepsiCo's own public position shifted more visibly over time than almost any other major food company's. On a July 2024 earnings call, chief executive Ramon Laguarta downplayed the drugs' significance bluntly: "I don't think GLP at this point … has a material impact in our category for sure," adding, "It's not impacting us." By a February 2026 call, roughly nineteen months later, his tone had moved considerably closer to Harmening's structural framing: "I think there are more opportunities than threats, but there are both," he told analysts, noting that households using GLP-1 drugs "engage in our category but they do it in smaller portions" — a description that pointed toward smaller, more frequent purchases and reformulated multipack sizes rather than a wholesale collapse in demand for PepsiCo's snack and beverage brands. The company's own uncertainty about how seriously to treat the question produced one of the stranger moments in this entire corporate reckoning: on PepsiCo's Q1 2025 call, held during a "Bring Your Kid to Work Day" event, thirteen-year-old Milena Gajrawala — daughter of a Jefferies analyst covering the company — asked Laguarta directly about the coming launch of oral GLP-1 medications and what it would mean for PepsiCo's business. Laguarta's answer, that consumers "eating less quantities" kept Pepsi's brands relevant "in their repertoire" through smaller and multipack portion formats, was substantively the same answer he was giving institutional analysts on every other call that year, delivered instead to a middle schooler, a small but telling sign of how thoroughly the question had become one every food-industry executive expected to be asked, by anyone in the room.

It is worth naming the strategic bind these companies found themselves in more explicitly, because it explains why the public commentary from Harmening, Laguarta, and their peers so often sounded simultaneously reassuring and uneasy within the same earnings call. A packaged-food company cannot simply announce, to its own shareholders, that a meaningful share of its historical growth strategy — larger portion sizes, more frequent snacking occasions, an ever-expanding menu of impulse-purchase products engineered for craving rather than nutrition — is now working against a structural, drug-driven shift in how a growing share of its customer base experiences hunger in the first place, without inviting exactly the kind of valuation discount investors had already begun applying to Novo Nordisk's own stock once its growth story stalled. At the same time, dismissing the trend too confidently, the way Laguarta had in July 2024, risked looking naive if the underlying shift proved larger and more durable than a single quarter's data could yet show — which is roughly what happened to his own position by early 2026. The result was an entire sector of corporate communication calibrated, quarter after quarter, to acknowledge just enough risk to seem credible without acknowledging so much that it spooked the same investors the company needed to keep believing in its stock.

Investor relations departments across the packaged-food sector developed, through this period, a genuinely new item of standard preparation for every earnings call: a rehearsed, carefully calibrated answer to the now-inevitable analyst question about GLP-1 exposure, delivered with enough acknowledgment of the trend to seem credible and enough hedging to avoid alarming shareholders further than the sector's already-battered valuations warranted. That this became a predictable, almost ritualized feature of quarterly reporting across an entire industry sector — Wall Street analysts routinely asking, company executives routinely answering with some version of the same careful hedge — is itself a measure of how thoroughly a single molecule's cultural and clinical rise had reshaped the routine business of corporate communications for companies whose core products had nothing directly to do with pharmaceuticals at all.

The reformulation wave also touched the grocery retail side of the industry, not merely the branded manufacturers stocking its shelves. Supermarket chains, watching the same shopper-level data Walmart's John Furner had described publicly, began experimenting with dedicated in-store signage and curated end-cap displays grouping "GLP-1 friendly" products together, an early and still-evolving version of the kind of category management retailers have long used to guide shoppers toward higher-margin or strategically important products, now redirected toward an entirely new organizing principle built around a drug rather than a traditional food category like "organic" or "gluten-free." Trade shows and industry conferences serving the packaged-food sector reflected the same shift in institutional attention this chapter has already traced through individual earnings calls. Panels and keynote sessions devoted specifically to "the GLP-1 consumer" or "formulating for the new appetite economy" became a fixture of major food-industry gatherings through 2024 and 2025, drawing attendance from product developers, marketing executives, and supply-chain planners across companies of every size, evidence that the response this chapter has documented at the largest, most closely watched public companies was mirrored, with less fanfare, across the much larger and more fragmented universe of smaller food manufacturers and private-label suppliers feeding the same retail shelves.

Store brand and private-label lines followed the same pattern the national brands were setting, with several major retailers introducing their own store-brand high-protein, high-fiber product lines within roughly a year of the trend becoming visible in scanner data — a faster private-label response than grocery retail typically manages for a new dietary trend, reflecting how much competitive pressure the category was under to be seen responding rather than lagging.

The industry's product response ran well ahead of any single executive's public framing. Conagra Brands relabeled roughly two dozen of its Healthy Choice frozen meals as "GLP-1 friendly," leaning into its existing portfolio of protein-forward products including Slim Jim and various nut and seed snacks. Nestlé launched an entire line of portion-controlled, protein- and fiber-rich frozen meals aimed explicitly at patients on the drugs, betting that a population eating meaningfully less per sitting would still want that smaller meal to be nutritionally dense rather than simply scaled down. Kraft Heinz began shipping a reformulated macaroni and cheese, marketed as "PowerMac," built around 17 grams of protein and 6 grams of fiber per serving against the traditional product's much thinner nutritional profile, part of a broader $600 million investment the company's new chief executive, Carlos Abrams-Rivera, announced to revive core brands including Oscar Mayer — an investment trade press connected to the broader GLP-1-driven reformulation wave, though without a specific, on-the-record Kraft Heinz executive quote naming the drugs directly as the reason. Across the industry, NielsenIQ tracked GLP-1-friendly food sales rising 11.2 percent over a trailing 52-week period, evidence that "smaller, higher-protein, higher-fiber" had moved, within a remarkably short window, from a niche dietary trend into a genuine and growing product category major manufacturers were competing to own.

The most rigorous independent evidence that any of this reflected a real, measurable shift in household behavior — rather than simply anxious executive pattern-matching against a headline-friendly narrative — came from a study conducted by Cornell University researchers Sylvia Hristakeva and Jūra Liaukonytė together with Leo Feler, published in the Journal of Marketing Research in its December 2025 issue and drawing on a Numerator consumer panel of roughly 150,000 households. The study found that households with at least one GLP-1 user cut their overall grocery spending by an average of 5.3 percent within six months of starting the drug, with high-income households cutting spending by nearly 9 percent — a detail worth sitting with, because it suggests the effect was strongest precisely among the households with the most discretionary spending to cut in the first place, rather than evenly distributed across the income spectrum. Savory snacks fell roughly 10 to 11 percent within those households; sweets, baked goods, cookies, and staples including bread, meat, and eggs also declined, while purchases of yogurt, fresh fruit, nutrition bars, and meat snacks rose — a substitution pattern that tracked closely with the "smaller portions, more protein and fiber" shift Harmening had described in more general terms at CAGNY. The same study found reduced spending at limited-service and fast-food restaurants among GLP-1 households, a finding echoed by a Morgan Stanley survey from April 2024 that found most people on weight-loss drugs reported spending less on restaurants and takeout specifically, rather than simply redistributing that spending elsewhere within the food category.

Commodity markets further upstream from any individual food company's earnings call registered a quieter version of the same anxiety. Agricultural economists and commodity analysts began, through 2024, publishing early research on whether sustained, population-scale reductions in caloric intake could eventually show up in demand for the underlying agricultural commodities — corn, wheat, sugar, vegetable oils — that packaged food ultimately derives from, a question with genuine long-run significance for farmers, commodity traders, and agricultural policy well beyond the retail food companies this chapter has focused on most closely. That research remained, as of this book's writing, considerably more speculative and considerably harder to isolate from the many other factors driving agricultural commodity prices — weather, geopolitics, biofuel policy, currency movements — than the more immediate, more directly observable effects on grocery basket composition this chapter has documented with greater confidence. It is included here not because the evidence is yet strong enough to draw a firm conclusion, but because it illustrates how far down the economic chain a sufficiently large shift in aggregate human appetite could plausibly travel, from a pharmacy counter to a commodities trading floor, if the trend documented throughout this chapter continues at anything like its recent pace.

The scale investors were pricing into that shift kept climbing through 2024 and into 2025, before a modest but instructive downward revision the following year. Morgan Stanley's research division projected the global market for these drugs could reach roughly $190 billion by 2035 — up $40 billion from an earlier estimate — against total global sales of roughly $79 billion in 2025, with a base case projecting around 55 million American users by 2035 and roughly 30 percent of the American obese or diabetic population, alongside about 10 percent internationally, ultimately treated, up from roughly 6 percent domestically and 2 percent internationally at the time of the projection. Goldman Sachs's own published estimates moved in a similarly striking arc: $100 billion by 2030 in an October 2023 note, revised up to $130 billion by May 2024, revised back down to $95 billion by May 2025 as the bank concluded the anti-obesity drug market "may prove smaller than expected" than its own earlier enthusiasm had assumed, before climbing again to $114 billion by June 2026 on the strength of faster-than-expected adoption of the newly approved oral pill and stronger demand outside the United States. That volatility in a single bank's own published forecasts, more than any individual number within it, is itself the most honest evidence available of how genuinely uncertain even the most sophisticated financial analysts remained about exactly how large this market would ultimately become — a volatility this book's readers, having already watched Novo Nordisk's market capitalization swing by hundreds of billions of dollars across the same period, should by now find entirely familiar.

It is worth asking what this reformulation wave actually costs a food company to execute, since reformulating a product line is neither instantaneous nor free, whatever the speed of the announcements might suggest. Changing a packaged food's core recipe requires new ingredient sourcing, new supplier relationships in many cases, reformulation testing to ensure the new version still meets taste and shelf-stability standards consumers expect from an established brand, updated nutritional labeling and regulatory review, and a marketing relaunch to communicate the change to a customer base that may or may not welcome a familiar product tasting or feeling different. That real cost, layered onto an industry already managing the input-cost inflation and consumer-spending pressure examined throughout the broader economic conversation these companies were having with investors, is part of why the response looked more like a gradual, portfolio-wide tilt toward protein and fiber across many existing brands rather than a wholesale abandonment of the traditional snack and convenience-food categories that still made up the overwhelming majority of these companies' revenue. No major food company examined in this chapter walked away from its core snack business; each simply began building a second, faster-growing wing of that business explicitly oriented around the smaller, more nutrient-dense eating pattern GLP-1 users were displaying — a hedge against the possibility that the shift proves durable, without a bet large enough to meaningfully cannibalize a still-dominant existing revenue base if it does not.

Restaurant chains, sitting adjacent to but distinct from the packaged-food companies this chapter has focused on, faced a related but structurally different version of the same challenge. A fast-food or casual-dining chain's revenue depends directly on transaction frequency and average order size in a way a grocery-aisle packaged-goods company's more diversified portfolio does not; a customer who visits half as often, or who orders a smaller meal each visit, hits a restaurant's revenue model more directly than the same behavioral shift hits a food conglomerate selling dozens of different products across many different consumption occasions. Several major restaurant chains began, through 2024 and into 2025, introducing smaller portion options and protein-forward menu items explicitly marketed toward health-conscious and, in some cases, explicitly GLP-1-aware customers, mirroring the packaged-food industry's own reformulation wave but with less room to hedge the bet across a broad existing product portfolio, since a restaurant chain's menu is a considerably more concentrated and more quickly adjustable asset than a food conglomerate's entire multi-brand product line.

Grocery shelves and snack aisles were, however, only the most visible and most heavily reported front in this reordering of consumer demand. A parallel and in some ways more existential version of the same conversation was unfolding, more quietly and more defensively, in an industry whose entire product exists to be consumed in quantities well beyond nutritional necessity, and whose executives, unlike Kraft Heinz's or General Mills's, could not simply reformulate their way toward smaller, higher-protein portions without changing what the product fundamentally was.
