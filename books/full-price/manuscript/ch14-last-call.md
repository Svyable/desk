# Last Call

Terry Smith runs Fundsmith, one of Britain's largest and most closely watched equity funds, and when he sold his firm's entire stake in Diageo, the London-listed conglomerate behind Johnnie Walker, Smirnoff, Guinness, and Captain Morgan, he did not do it quietly. He wrote, in comments that circulated widely through financial media, that he suspected "the entire drinks sector is in the early stages of being impacted negatively by weight-loss drugs," and went further than most analysts covering the sector were yet willing to go, predicting these same drugs would eventually be used to treat alcoholism directly — turning what had started as an unplanned side effect of a diabetes medication into a deliberate clinical tool aimed squarely at one of his own former holding's core markets. A single fund manager's decision to exit a position is not, on its own, proof of anything beyond that manager's own judgment. But Smith's public reasoning, offered by someone whose job depends on being right about exactly this kind of structural risk before the broader market has priced it in, crystallized an anxiety that had been building across the alcohol industry for the better part of two years, well before it showed up clearly in any single quarter's sales figures.

Diageo's own numbers, when the strain finally became visible in its reporting, told a genuinely difficult story, though the company's leadership was notably reluctant to attribute it primarily to the same drug class Smith had named. Under new chief executive Dave Lewis, who replaced Debra Crew, Diageo's first-half fiscal 2026 results showed sales down 2.3 percent over the trailing six months, with the damage concentrated heavily in tequila — Don Julio down 20.9 percent, Casamigos down 30.9 percent in the U.S. market specifically. Lewis, on the earnings call reporting those results, pushed back directly against the framing that GLP-1 drugs were the primary culprit, telling analysts that people should "forget all the noise about alcohol-free and GLP-1 drugs" and attributing the weakness instead to reduced consumer disposable income rather than to any structural shift in drinking behavior, whether generational or pharmacologically induced. It is a genuinely useful corrective to hold alongside Terry Smith's more alarmed reading: the chief executive actually running one of the industry's largest companies, with access to considerably more granular internal data than any outside fund manager, was arguing publicly that the GLP-1 story had become a convenient explanation reporters and short-sellers alike reached for, ahead of harder evidence that it explained a meaningful share of the industry's actual, and real, sales weakness.

Constellation Brands, the American company behind Corona and Modelo, staked out a position closer to Lewis's skepticism than to Smith's alarm, using its own internal research to argue the near-term financial risk was limited. According to figures the company has circulated, only around 1 percent of the U.S. population held an active GLP-1 prescription at the time of its analysis, and of more than 2.6 million GLP-1-related conversations the company tracked across social media, only about 2 percent mentioned reduced alcohol consumption at all — a data point Constellation used to argue that whatever effect these drugs were having on drinking behavior remained, for now, a minority phenomenon within an already-small population of drug users, rather than the broad structural threat Smith's public statements implied. Notably, Constellation's own beer volumes continued growing even as the GLP-1-skeptic thesis gained currency among some analysts, a divergence the company and several outside analysts used to argue that beer, as a category, sat somewhat more insulated from the effect than wine or spirits — a distinction this book cannot fully resolve given how recent and how contested the underlying data remains, but one worth noting as a genuine complication for any simple version of the "GLP-1s are killing alcohol demand" narrative.

Independent estimates of the drugs' likely cumulative effect on the alcohol industry sat, characteristically for this entire subject, in a wide and only loosely reconciled range. FTI Consulting, a corporate advisory firm, projected that GLP-1 adoption could reduce cumulative U.S. alcohol consumption by roughly 2.5 percent through 2035 — a meaningful number for an industry that competes on thin single-digit growth or decline in any given year, but a far cry from the kind of existential threat some of the more alarmed financial commentary implied. Survey data circulating separately in trade press suggested that roughly one in five patients on a GLP-1 drug reported reducing their alcohol consumption after starting treatment, a figure that, if broadly representative, would help explain why an industry executive like Lewis might genuinely believe the effect remained too small, relative to the industry's much larger exposure to inflation and discretionary-spending pressure, to be the primary story — even as a fund manager like Smith, positioning years ahead of where the data currently sits rather than reacting to where it sits today, treated the same early, partial evidence as sufficient reason to exit the position altogether.

Whatever the eventual scale of the effect on the beverage industry's bottom line, the underlying clinical mechanism behind it is considerably better established than the market debate over its financial magnitude might suggest, because the same neurological pathway responsible for quieting "food noise" — the intrusive, looping preoccupation with food this book's earlier chapters traced through patient testimony and hypothalamic and reward-circuit physiology alike — appears not to be limited to food specifically. A randomized, double-blind Phase 2 trial, involving researchers from the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, part of the National Institutes of Health, and published in JAMA Psychiatry on February 12, 2025, tested weekly semaglutide against placebo in 48 adults with alcohol use disorder who were not, at the time of the trial, seeking treatment for it. Over nine weeks of dose escalation, semaglutide reduced the amount of alcohol participants consumed in a controlled laboratory self-administration task, reduced several separate measures of weekly alcohol consumption, and produced a statistically significant reduction in weekly alcohol craving compared to placebo. George Koob, the NIAAA's director, described the finding as "consistent with previous studies showing that GLP-1s might be an effective treatment for alcohol use disorder" — a notably measured, hedged endorsement from the federal government's own lead alcohol-research institute, rather than an overstated claim of a breakthrough cure. A separate, larger international trial, led by Anders Fink-Jensen at Copenhagen University Hospital with NIH co-investigators and published in The Lancet, tested weekly semaglutide over 26 weeks specifically in patients who had both alcohol use disorder and comorbid obesity, and again found larger reductions in total monthly alcohol consumption, drinks per drinking day, self-reported craving, and measures of harmful use among patients on the drug compared to placebo.

The tension between Lewis's skepticism and Smith's alarm also reflects two fundamentally different jobs looking at the same incomplete evidence. A chief executive running a company with tens of thousands of employees, a global supply chain, and a fiduciary duty to defend his own strategy to shareholders every quarter has strong institutional incentives to wait for conclusive data before conceding that a structural threat is real, since prematurely validating a bearish narrative about his own industry can itself depress the stock he is responsible for. A fund manager deciding whether to hold or sell a position has the opposite incentive: the whole point of active portfolio management is to move ahead of a trend before it is fully confirmed in the data, since waiting for certainty means missing the price move that certainty would have already priced in. Both men, in other words, may be behaving entirely rationally within their respective roles even while reaching opposite public conclusions about the same underlying phenomenon — a reminder, for a reader trying to extract a single confident verdict from this chapter, that corporate and investor commentary on an unfolding trend is never a neutral read of the evidence so much as a read filtered through what each speaker's job requires them to say and when.

Read together, the industry-level anxiety and the clinical trial evidence point toward a genuinely open question rather than a settled one, and this book means to leave it exactly that open rather than resolving it more confidently than the underlying evidence currently allows. The mechanism is real: GLP-1 receptor activity appears, in controlled trials run by credible, federally funded researchers, to blunt alcohol craving and consumption through a reward-pathway effect that operates independently of any deliberate weight-loss intention, in patients who in some cases were not even trying to drink less when the trial began. Whether that mechanism, playing out across tens of millions of prescriptions rather than a few dozen carefully monitored trial participants, will eventually produce the kind of measurable, industry-reshaping decline in alcohol sales that Terry Smith bet his fund's Diageo position on, or will remain, as Dave Lewis argued to his own investors, a comparatively minor factor swamped by ordinary macroeconomic pressure on discretionary spending, is a question this book's own vantage point in 2026 cannot yet definitively answer. What can be said with more confidence is that an industry whose entire commercial model depends on a habit these drugs appear, even in early and incomplete evidence, to genuinely blunt has been forced to take the possibility seriously enough to reshape its own earnings-call talking points, its own equity research coverage, and, in at least one prominent and well-publicized case, an experienced fund manager's actual portfolio — a level of institutional attention that, whatever the eventual data shows, has already become one more piece of evidence for this book's broader argument: that very few consumer industries built on the assumption of a stable, exploitable human appetite have escaped at least having to ask themselves the question these drugs raise.

That same question, reframed around physical rather than caloric or alcoholic weight, was being asked with unusual literalness by an industry whose entire cost structure runs on a single, carefully measured number: how much a human body weighs, and how much fuel it takes to move that body through the air.
