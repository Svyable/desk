# Copenhagen's Big Bet

Novo Nordisk's relationship with the hormone that would eventually make it, briefly, the most valuable company in Europe began with a different hormone entirely, and a family whose stake in that earlier discovery was personal rather than purely commercial. In 1923, August Krogh, a University of Copenhagen physiologist who had won the 1920 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on capillary regulation, traveled to Toronto with his wife, Marie. Marie Krogh had type 2 diabetes. Frederick Banting and Charles Best had, two years earlier, isolated insulin at the University of Toronto, and the Kroghs went to secure the rights to produce it for Denmark and the wider Nordic region. What they founded on returning home, Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium, worked alongside Hans Christian Hagedorn, a Danish physician who had joined the effort. August Krogh's own scientific credentials — the Nobel Prize had recognized his discovery of the mechanism by which the body regulates blood flow through capillaries during exercise, work that had nothing to do with diabetes — gave the new venture an unusual degree of institutional legitimacy from its very first year, established him, by the time he and Marie traveled to Toronto, as one of the most respected physiologists in Europe, and made it considerably easier for a small Danish laboratory to secure production rights to a discovery as commercially and medically significant as insulin had immediately proven to be. A rival firm, Novo Terapeutisk Laboratorium, was founded separately by two brothers, Harald and Thorvald Pedersen, on February 16, 1925, after a dispute with the Nordisk group — Harald Pedersen had worked as an engineer for Nordisk before the split, and the two resulting companies spent the following six decades as direct competitors within Denmark's small domestic insulin market, each building its own manufacturing capacity, its own sales force, and its own research program, in a rivalry considerably more personal and more geographically compressed than most pharmaceutical competition, given that both companies operated for most of that period within a short distance of each other in greater Copenhagen. The two competitors ran parallel, separately, manufacturing insulin for most of the twentieth century, until they merged in 1989 to form Novo Nordisk A/S — a company whose entire institutional identity, from its founding moment, was built around a single hormonal system: the body's management of blood sugar.

That institutional memory is worth holding onto, because it explains something about why Novo Nordisk, rather than a larger and more diversified pharmaceutical company, was the organization willing to spend decades and enormous sums chasing a molecule most of the industry regarded, for most of that time, as a promising but narrow diabetes therapy rather than a category-defining drug. A company with insulin in its founding story, run substantially by people whose careers were built inside metabolic disease, had a kind of institutional patience for incretin biology that a broader pharmaceutical conglomerate, answering to shareholders expecting returns across dozens of unrelated disease areas, might not have extended to the same research program for as long.

The person most responsible for turning that patience into an actual drug was Lotte Bjerre Knudsen, a chemical engineering graduate of the Technical University of Denmark who joined Novo Nordisk as a student and stayed for the whole of the run that followed. Around 1995 and 1996, Knudsen and colleagues working with GLP-1 receptor agonists in animal models noticed something that went beyond the insulin-secretion effect the field had already been chasing since Mojsov's and Habener's foundational work in the 1980s: the mice were eating less. That observation, easy to miss or dismiss as noise in a study designed to measure blood sugar rather than appetite, became the seed of an entirely different commercial thesis than the one Novo Nordisk had originally been pursuing. Knudsen and a collaborator, Richard DiMarchi, filed foundational patents on the appetite-suppressing potential of GLP-1 agonism through the 1990s, years before any drug built on that insight would reach a pharmacy shelf.

The technical problem standing between that laboratory observation and a usable medicine was the same one that had confronted John Eng a few years earlier on the other side of the Atlantic: native human GLP-1 lasts about two minutes in the bloodstream before an enzyme called DPP-4 degrades it, nowhere near long enough for a once-daily, let alone once-weekly, injectable drug. Where Eng's answer had come from a Gila monster's venom — a natural peptide that happened to resist the same degradation pathway — Knudsen's team engineered a synthetic solution instead. They attached a fatty-acid side chain to the GLP-1 molecule, a modification that lets the resulting drug bind reversibly to albumin, the most abundant protein in blood plasma. Bound to albumin, the modified peptide is shielded from both DPP-4 degradation and renal clearance, dramatically extending how long it circulates before the body clears it. The underlying idea is not unlike attaching a small anchor to something that would otherwise wash away quickly in a current: the fatty-acid chain does not change what GLP-1 does once it reaches its receptor, but it changes how long the molecule survives the trip there, giving the drug's designers a way to control duration almost independently of the biological activity itself. It is a considerably more laborious and more purely synthetic solution than the one nature had already handed John Eng in a Gila monster's venom, but it had one significant advantage exendin-4 could never fully match: because it started from the native human GLP-1 sequence rather than a lizard's version of a related hormone, a drug built this way could be tuned, generation after generation, toward whatever balance of potency, duration, and tolerability the company's clinical data suggested it needed next, without being bound to whatever the reptile's own evolutionary history happened to have already fixed in place. This fatty-acylation strategy, refined across successive drug candidates, is the single technical innovation underlying the entire GLP-1 drug franchise that followed — the difference between a hormone the body flushes in minutes and a drug a patient can inject once a week.

Knudsen's own career, worth pausing on briefly given how thoroughly the rest of this book depends on the molecules she spent decades developing, followed an unusually singular path for the pharmaceutical industry, where researchers more commonly move between companies, therapeutic areas, or academia and industry several times across a career. She joined Novo Nordisk directly from her chemical-engineering studies at the Technical University of Denmark and remained there for the whole of the run this chapter describes, rising eventually to chief scientific advisor — a title that captures, more than any specific job description could, the degree to which the company's entire GLP-1 program came to run through her judgment across three successive drug generations, from Victoza through Ozempic to Wegovy, a continuity of institutional memory that a company changing scientific leadership more frequently would have had a much harder time sustaining across the fifteen-plus years this particular research program actually took to pay off.

The first drug built on that innovation was liraglutide, requiring once-daily dosing rather than the once-weekly interval its successor would eventually achieve, and the Food and Drug Administration approved it for type 2 diabetes on January 25, 2010, under the brand name Victoza — the first once-daily human GLP-1 analogue to reach the American market. Victoza sold well as a diabetes drug and, like Byetta before it, generated the same quiet pattern of unplanned weight loss among patients using it, reinforcing what Knudsen's mouse studies had already suggested more than a decade earlier. But liraglutide's daily injection schedule and its comparatively modest weight-loss effect, on the order of a few percentage points of body weight in typical use, kept it from becoming anything more than a solid, unremarkable entry in a crowded diabetes-drug market. What Novo Nordisk needed, if the appetite-suppression thesis Knudsen had staked her career on was going to become a commercial reality rather than a research footnote, was a molecule potent and durable enough to produce weight loss too large to explain away as a side effect — and convenient enough, in its dosing schedule, that patients would actually keep taking it.

Knudsen's team screened roughly four thousand candidate molecules in pursuit of that next step, according to her own account of the program published years later. What emerged from that search was semaglutide: a further-refined fatty-acid-modified GLP-1 analogue with a half-life extended enough to support once-weekly dosing, a full week between injections rather than a full day. The Food and Drug Administration approved semaglutide for type 2 diabetes on December 5, 2017, under the brand name Ozempic, following an FDA Advisory Committee vote in its favor two months earlier and a clinical program, called SUSTAIN, that had run across multiple trials and thousands of patients. Novo Nordisk's own press release announcing the approval framed Ozempic as a next-generation diabetes drug, one weekly injection replacing what had been, for many patients on Victoza, a daily one. Nothing in that December 2017 announcement suggested the company understood it was introducing what would, within roughly five years, become one of the most culturally significant drugs of the decade — a name repurposed as an internet verb, a punchline at the Academy Awards, a subject of congressional testimony, and briefly the product responsible for making its manufacturer worth more than the economy of the country that had founded it a century earlier. Financial analysts covering Novo Nordisk at the time treated the approval with a similar lack of drama: Ozempic entered a diabetes-drug market already crowded with competing GLP-1 agonists, competing insulin products, and an older generation of oral diabetes medications, and the trade and financial press covering the approval in December 2017 framed it, correctly by the information available at the time, as an incremental improvement in dosing convenience for an existing drug class rather than as the opening act of a story that would eventually reorder food, beverage, retail, and travel industries an ordinary diabetes-drug launch had no obvious reason to touch. That gap between how a genuinely consequential drug is actually received at the moment of its approval and how it comes to be understood only years later, once its full range of effects has become visible, recurs throughout this book, and Ozempic's own unremarkable 2017 debut is as clean an illustration of it as this story offers anywhere.

The drug that would carry the weight-loss thesis explicitly, rather than as an unlabeled side effect patients and physicians discovered informally, arrived three and a half years later. On June 4, 2021, the FDA approved semaglutide again, this time at a higher 2.4-milligram dose and under a different brand name — Wegovy — specifically for chronic weight management in adults with obesity or with overweight accompanied by a weight-related health condition. It was the first once-weekly GLP-1 drug ever approved specifically for weight loss rather than diabetes, built on a clinical trial program called STEP that will occupy the next chapter of this book in its own right, because the numbers that program produced were large enough to change how obesity itself was understood within clinical medicine — not merely a lifestyle condition responsive to diet and exercise, but a disease with a specific hormonal lever that a drug could pull with a precision diet and exercise counseling alone had never reliably achieved.

It is worth counting the years plainly, because the total is easy to lose inside a story usually told in headlines rather than in the actual pace of pharmaceutical development. From Knudsen's mid-1990s laboratory observation about mice eating less, to Victoza's 2010 approval, to Ozempic's 2017 approval, to Wegovy's 2021 approval, the company's GLP-1 program ran for roughly a quarter of a century before it produced the drug that would make Novo Nordisk a household name — a span longer than the working careers of many of the scientists and executives who eventually got to see it pay off, and a reminder that the "overnight sensation" framing so much of the press coverage around Ozempic's 2022 and 2023 breakout would eventually apply to it was, from the company's own internal vantage point, closer to the final act of a multi-decade research bet than to any sudden discovery.

What is easy to lose, looking back from the vantage point of Ozempic's cultural saturation and Wegovy's role in a multi-hundred-billion-dollar pharmaceutical market, is how long and how uncertain the path to that point actually was, and how many points along the way could plausibly have ended differently. Novo Nordisk could have abandoned the GLP-1 program after liraglutide's modest commercial performance, redirecting the company's research budget toward better-understood diabetes mechanisms with clearer regulatory paths — a decision that would have looked, at almost any point before roughly 2021, like ordinary prudent portfolio management rather than a historic miscalculation, since nothing about Victoza's sales performance alone would have flagged the much larger opportunity still waiting a decade further down the same research line. Knudsen's 1990s mouse observation could have been dismissed as an artifact of a specific study design rather than pursued through a decade and a half of further development, the way countless similarly promising but ultimately unreplicated laboratory findings are quietly abandoned across the pharmaceutical industry every year. A rival firm with a similarly patient, metabolically focused research culture could have arrived at an equivalent molecule first, changing which company's name became the cultural shorthand this book's opening chapter traced through an Oscars joke and a billionaire's tweet. None of that happened, and the reason it did not happen traces back to a specific set of institutional and individual choices, several of them made decades before anyone involved could have known how large the eventual payoff would be: a Danish physiologist's family history with diabetes in the 1920s, a merger between two rival insulin manufacturers in 1989, and one engineer's willingness to keep chasing an offhand observation about appetite in laboratory mice through years of unglamorous, incremental drug development before anyone outside a narrow research community had reason to care.

Denmark's own relationship to Novo Nordisk's success is worth pausing on before this chapter turns to Indianapolis, because it sets up a tension this book's later chapters on pricing return to directly. The Danish state itself has historically held a significant ownership stake in the company through the Novo Nordisk Foundation, a philanthropic entity that controls the majority of voting shares in Novo Holdings, the parent company that in turn controls Novo Nordisk — an unusual corporate structure by American standards, in which the foundation's charitable mission, funding scientific and humanitarian causes across Denmark and beyond, is directly bankrolled by the same drug sales this book has spent its chapters examining from every commercial angle. That structure means Novo Nordisk's success has never been merely a matter of shareholder return in the way a purely public American pharmaceutical giant's would be; it has functioned, for decades, as a quiet but substantial engine of Danish civic philanthropy, funding scientific research, cultural institutions, and social causes across the country in a way that gave the company's later, more visible role in the Danish national economy — briefly outweighing the country's own GDP, as the following chapter examines — a somewhat different civic valence than an equivalent American pharmaceutical windfall might have carried, tangled up as it was with an existing, decades-old tradition of channeling the company's profits back into a form of national public benefit.

Eli Lilly, watching this unfold from Indianapolis, was not standing still. The American pharmaceutical company that had co-marketed Byetta with Amylin back in 2005 — the first GLP-1 drug of any kind, built on exendin-4's Gila-monster origins rather than Knudsen's engineered fatty-acid chemistry — had its own incretin research program running in parallel to Novo Nordisk's, and it had reached a different technical conclusion about how to build the next generation of the drug class. Where Novo Nordisk had built its commercial empire on a single receptor, refined generation after generation into an increasingly potent and durable molecule, Lilly's scientists had begun asking a different question: what would happen if a single drug could activate two separate incretin receptors at once, rather than one. The answer to that question, and the corporate rivalry it set in motion between Copenhagen and Indianapolis, is where this book turns next.
