# Back Matter

## A Note on Evidence

This book rests on peer-reviewed clinical trial publications, FDA approval records and safety communications, company and government press releases, congressional hearing records and committee reports, court filings and multidistrict litigation dockets, and reporting from established news organizations, rather than on anonymous sources or private access. Every clinical figure, dollar amount, and quotation in this book is attributed to a named source in the text itself, and a detailed research trail — organized chapter by chapter, with source names, dates, and explicit notes on what each source does and does not establish — is kept separately in `research/README.md` and `research/source-ledger.csv`, so the narrative can read cleanly while the claims underneath it remain checkable.

Several limits are worth stating directly, because this book has tried to name them throughout rather than only here. Nearly every clinical trial cited in this book was funded, wholly or in part, by the manufacturer whose drug it tested — a standard and disclosed feature of pharmaceutical research, but one a reader deserves to keep in view rather than have obscured. Where an international price, a market-capitalization figure, or a cost estimate varied meaningfully between credible sources, this book has tried to say so rather than silently choosing whichever number told the cleaner story. Litigation examined in this book's later chapters — the gastroparesis and NAION multidistrict proceedings chief among them — remained active and unresolved as this book was completed in the second half of 2026; where a case's ultimate outcome was not yet known, this book has tried to say so rather than imply a resolution that had not occurred. This is also, more than most books this Desk has produced, a book about an unfinished story: an oral pill newly launched, a pipeline of successor molecules still moving through trials, a pricing fight still working itself out between Copenhagen, Indianapolis, and Washington as these pages went to press. Figures describing "current" prices, market shares, and coverage policies reflect the best information available at the time of writing and should be expected to have moved, in some cases considerably, by the time a reader encounters them.

## Acknowledgments

This book owes its existence to people whose patient, often uncredited work made an unlikely scientific story possible to tell at all: John Eng and Jean-Pierre Raufman, who followed an odd result in a guinea pig assay to a Gila monster's venom; Daniel Drucker, Joel Habener, and Jens Juul Holst, whose decades of incretin physiology research supplied the science this entire drug class was built from; and Svetlana Mojsov, whose identification of GLP-1's active fragment sits at the molecular center of every drug this book examines, and whose long fight for recognition this book has tried to tell honestly rather than as a footnote. It owes a further debt to the investigative journalists, congressional staff, and independent researchers — at STAT News, Reuters, KFF, the Congressional Budget Office, and elsewhere — who did the harder work of tracing what these drugs actually cost, who actually profited, and who was actually left out, long before any of it had settled into a story with a clear shape. None of them are responsible for how their work has been synthesized here, or for any error that synthesis may have introduced.
