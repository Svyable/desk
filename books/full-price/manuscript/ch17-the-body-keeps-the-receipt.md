# The Body Keeps the Receipt

The clinical response to the muscle-loss concern has moved considerably faster than the underlying long-term outcome data actually supports, which is itself worth treating as a pattern rather than an isolated observation. Obesity medicine specialists and the companies developing these drugs alike began, within roughly two years of the SURMOUNT-1 body-composition findings, recommending resistance training and adequate protein intake as standard companion guidance for any patient starting a GLP-1 medication, and at least one manufacturer began exploring a combination approach pairing a GLP-1 or dual-agonist drug with a separate compound designed specifically to preserve or build muscle mass during the resulting weight loss — treating lean-mass preservation as a genuine unmet need within the drug class's own future development pipeline rather than as a settled, adequately managed side effect. That a serious research and clinical response emerged this quickly is a reasonable sign the field is taking the concern seriously; it is also worth noting that "taking a concern seriously" and "having conclusive long-term data on how well the proposed solutions actually work" remain two different things, and this book's own vantage point in 2026 sits closer to the beginning of that research response than to its resolution.

It is worth naming plainly what this chapter is actually asking a reader to hold in mind simultaneously, because the tension is real rather than merely rhetorical. Every earlier chapter in this book's account of the clinical science — the STEP trials, the SURMOUNT trials, the SELECT cardiovascular result — described genuine, rigorously documented benefit, benefit this book has no interest in minimizing or explaining away. This chapter's job is different: to take seriously what else is true about a body that has lost a large share of its weight quickly, through a mechanism powerful enough to override appetite regulation that took a lifetime to establish, without treating that seriousness as an attack on the drugs' genuine value. A reader can hold both facts without contradiction — that these medications represent one of the more significant advances in metabolic medicine in decades, and that a body undergoing this scale of rapid change is undergoing real physiological stress worth monitoring, managing, and, where the evidence supports it, worth being honestly cautious about, particularly for populations, like older adults already at risk of frailty, where the calculus of benefit and risk looks different than it does for a younger, otherwise healthy patient losing weight to reduce a documented cardiovascular risk.

A body losing weight does not lose only fat, whatever the marketing built around these drugs sometimes implies by showing before-and-after photographs without much explanation of what changed beneath the skin. A sub-study within the SURMOUNT-1 trial, the pivotal tirzepatide obesity trial examined earlier in this book, used dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scanning — a precise imaging technique capable of distinguishing fat mass from lean mass rather than measuring only total body weight — on 160 of the trial's 2,539 participants, and published its findings in Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism under lead author M. Look and colleagues. The results, at 72 weeks of treatment, showed fat mass falling by 33.9 percent while lean mass fell by 10.9 percent — meaning roughly three-quarters of the total weight participants lost was fat, and roughly one-quarter was lean tissue, a ratio that held up consistently across subgroups defined by sex, age, and total amount of weight lost. That ratio is broadly comparable to the lean-mass loss seen in diet-only weight loss studied in the wider obesity literature; these drugs do not appear to disproportionately target muscle compared to ordinary caloric restriction. But comparable is not the same as negligible, and the absolute quantity of lean tissue lost, given how much total weight some patients on these drugs are losing, has become a genuine and growing concern within obesity medicine and geriatrics specifically, distinct from the older diet-and-exercise literature these drugs are otherwise often compared against.

It is worth being precise about what the SURMOUNT-1 body-composition sub-study can and cannot tell a reader trying to decide how seriously to weigh this concern against the drugs' other benefits. A DXA scan measures total lean mass, which includes muscle but also organ tissue, connective tissue, and body water, meaning the 10.9 percent figure is not a pure measure of muscle strength or functional capacity lost — a distinction the underlying literature is careful about even where popular coverage of the finding sometimes is not. It is also, as the study's authors themselves noted, a ratio broadly comparable to what diet-only weight loss produces in the wider obesity literature, which complicates any simple narrative in which these drugs are uniquely or unusually damaging to muscle compared to older, non-pharmacological weight-loss methods. What the finding does establish clearly is that GLP-1-driven weight loss is not selectively fat loss the way some early marketing and enthusiastic press coverage implied; a body losing three-quarters of its weight as fat and a quarter as lean tissue, at the scale of weight loss these drugs commonly produce, is losing a genuinely substantial absolute quantity of muscle even if the underlying percentage split resembles what a sustained calorie-restricted diet would have produced on its own.

The concern sharpens considerably for older patients. Sarcopenia — the age-related, progressive loss of muscle mass and strength — already affects roughly half of adults over eighty even before any deliberate weight loss intervention, and an editorial published in Annals of Internal Medicine, together with a 2025 scoping review in Current Nutrition Reports, has warned that GLP-1-driven muscle loss could meaningfully worsen that existing vulnerability in older patients, raising the risk of falls, fractures, and functional disability in a population where muscle mass is not merely a matter of appearance but a direct predictor of independence and mortality. The same body of literature raises a second, related concern that compounds the first: weight cycling, the repeated pattern of losing weight on a GLP-1 drug, regaining it after stopping, and potentially restarting treatment again, appears to disproportionately rebuild fat rather than muscle during each regain phase — meaning a patient who cycles on and off these medications multiple times over years could, in principle, end up with a body composition worse than either continuous treatment or no treatment at all, trading a smaller number on the scale for a body proportionally lower in the muscle mass that supports metabolic health, strength, and mobility as that same patient ages.

That regain pattern is not a hypothetical risk; it is a documented and, for the drugs' manufacturers, somewhat inconvenient finding from the very trial that established these medications' headline efficacy numbers in the first place. The STEP 1 extension study, published in Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism in 2022, followed participants after they stopped taking semaglutide at the conclusion of the original 68-week trial, during which the treatment group had lost an average of 17.3 percent of body weight against 2.0 percent for placebo. One year after discontinuing the drug, participants who had been on semaglutide had regained roughly two-thirds of the weight they had lost — 11.6 percentage points of the original 17.3 percent — leaving a net loss of only 5.6 percent from their original starting weight by the 120-week mark, with blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar measurements reverting substantially back toward their pre-treatment baseline alongside the regained weight. It is one of the more important and least celebrated numbers in this entire book, because it explains, more clearly than any marketing material or investor presentation, why these drugs function commercially the way they do: not as a course of treatment with a defined endpoint, but as an ongoing prescription a patient is expected, on the current evidence, to continue more or less indefinitely if the benefit is meant to last.

Novo Nordisk's own public materials describe obesity plainly as "a multifaceted metabolic chronic disease" the company has been "dedicated to" treating "for more than 30 years," language chosen deliberately to frame indefinite use not as a design flaw but as the appropriate, medically sound response to a chronic condition — no different, in this framing, from a patient with hypertension who is expected to keep taking blood pressure medication for life rather than treating a normalized blood pressure reading as a cure. That framing has real clinical merit; obesity medicine specialists broadly agree that treating obesity as a chronic condition requiring sustained management, rather than a temporary problem a course of treatment permanently resolves, better reflects the underlying biology than the older, harsher cultural assumption that willpower alone should be sufficient and permanent. It also happens to describe, with unusual precision, the exact business model that makes these drugs so valuable to the companies selling them. A Reuters investigation into the industry's marketing practices reported that Novo Nordisk had paid roughly $25.8 million to American medical professionals over the prior decade to promote Wegovy and Saxenda, and quoted one physician paid to promote the drugs describing obesity as a condition that "should be treated as aggressively as other chronic diseases such as high blood pressure or diabetes — with lifelong prescriptions," adding, in language that reads rather differently depending on whether one approaches it as a statement of clinical fact or as a marketing talking point from a paid promoter: "We are going to have to use these medications for as long as the body wants to have obesity." Both readings can be true simultaneously — the chronic-disease framing can be medically sound and also happen to be extraordinarily good for a manufacturer's recurring revenue — and this book does not think a reader is obligated to pick only one.

The weight-regain finding also complicates, in ways worth spelling out plainly, the market-size projections examined earlier in this book. An analyst modeling the total addressable GLP-1 market by assuming patients who start the drug simply keep taking it, and keep paying for it, indefinitely is modeling a considerably larger and more durable revenue stream than the STEP 1 extension data, combined with the real-world persistence figures this book's later chapter on skeptical evidence examines directly, actually supports. It also means the aggregate economic disruption this book has traced across the food, alcohol, and retail industries is itself contingent on sustained adherence in a way none of those industries can fully bank on: a food company that reformulated its snack lineup on the assumption that a meaningful share of the population would remain on these drugs indefinitely is making a bet about drug persistence, not merely about drug efficacy, and the STEP 1 extension data gives that bet a considerably shakier foundation than the drugs' headline weight-loss numbers alone would suggest.

A separate and considerably less anticipated consequence of these drugs' effect on the body surfaced through an entirely different channel than clinical trial data: unplanned pregnancies, widely nicknamed "Ozempic babies" once the pattern spread across Facebook groups, Reddit threads, and TikTok through early 2024, and covered directly by the Washington Post on April 5, 2024, under the headline "Women using Ozempic and similar drugs are reporting unexpected pregnancies." Two genuinely distinct mechanisms sit behind the phenomenon, and conflating them, as much informal social-media discussion of "Ozempic babies" tended to do, obscures more than it explains. The first is a documented, FDA-labeled drug interaction specific to tirzepatide — the molecule behind Mounjaro and Zepbound, not semaglutide's Ozempic and Wegovy — whose approved label states plainly that the drug "may reduce the efficacy of oral hormonal contraceptives due to delayed gastric emptying," with the effect largest after the first dose and after each subsequent dose increase, and diminishing over time as the body adjusts; the label instructs patients relying on oral contraceptives to switch to a non-oral method or add a barrier method for four weeks following initiation and following each dose escalation. Semaglutide is not expected to produce the same meaningful interaction with oral contraceptive absorption, a distinction multiple outlets, including Healthline's own reporting on the phenomenon, took care to draw even as popular discussion tended to attach the "Ozempic babies" label to both drugs indiscriently.

The second, independent mechanism applies more broadly across the drug class and has nothing to do with contraceptive interaction at all: significant weight loss, on its own, can correct anovulation and restore hormonal balance in patients whose obesity had been suppressing ovulation, including patients with polycystic ovary syndrome, a condition strongly linked to both obesity and infertility. For those patients, a pregnancy following GLP-1-driven weight loss is not a contraceptive failure caused by the drug at all, but a straightforward, medically unsurprising consequence of a body regaining reproductive function it had lost to excess weight in the first place — a phenomenon obesity medicine has documented for decades in patients who lost significant weight through any method, bariatric surgery included, and simply had not been observed at this particular scale and speed until a drug capable of producing that much weight loss that quickly became available to so many people at once. Both mechanisms point toward the same broader lesson this chapter has traced across muscle, metabolism, and fertility alike: a drug capable of overriding a body's appetite regulation as thoroughly and as quickly as these medications do is, almost by definition, capable of disturbing other systems the body had built around that same appetite and that same body weight over years or decades, in ways clinical trials focused primarily on measuring pounds lost were not originally designed to fully anticipate.

What none of this evidence resolves, and what this book does not think any single chapter should pretend to resolve on its own, is the harder question sitting underneath all of it: whether a drug this effective at overriding appetite, prescribed to a population this large, for a duration this indefinite, is a triumph of modern pharmacology or a experiment whose full consequences — on muscle, on fertility, on the psychology of a species whose relationship with food has never before been this directly and durably modifiable by injection — simply have not had enough time to fully reveal themselves. That question becomes even more pointed once these drugs' effects are examined beyond weight loss altogether, in the growing list of conditions researchers are now testing them against, from heart disease to sleep apnea to addiction itself.
