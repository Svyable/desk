# The Shortage

Wegovy first appeared on the FDA's official drug shortage list in March 2022, less than a year after its approval. Ozempic followed in August 2022. Both entries were, in their own dry regulatory language, an acknowledgment of something that had become obvious to anyone trying to fill a prescription at an American pharmacy by that point: demand for these drugs had outrun every projection either manufacturer had built its production capacity around, and it was going to keep doing so for years.

Part of what drove that gap was the diversion this book's earlier chapters have already traced — patients seeking semaglutide specifically for weight loss, often off-label before Wegovy's own approval gave that use a dedicated product, competing for the same limited supply as patients using Ozempic to manage their diabetes. One frequently cited estimate put the increase in semaglutide prescription fills at 442 percent over the relevant period, a surge driven substantially by that off-label weight-loss demand rather than by any comparable growth in the underlying diabetic population the drug had originally been manufactured to serve. The practical consequence, for a diabetic patient whose blood sugar, cardiovascular risk, and kidney function genuinely depended on uninterrupted access to the drug, was a pharmacy counter that increasingly could not guarantee it — a supply crunch that fell hardest on exactly the patients the drug's original label had been written for, displaced by demand from patients using it for a purpose the manufacturer had not yet built enough factories to support.

Into that gap stepped compounding pharmacies, a long-established and, within limits, legal corner of American pharmacy practice that allows licensed compounders to prepare customized versions of a drug when the FDA-approved commercial product is in short supply, is medically unsuitable for a particular patient in its standard form, or is otherwise unavailable. Federal law recognizes two tiers of compounder: so-called 503A pharmacies, typically smaller operations compounding drugs for individual, named patients on a prescription-by-prescription basis, and 503B outsourcing facilities, larger operations permitted to compound in bulk under somewhat different regulatory oversight. Both tiers expanded rapidly into semaglutide and tirzepatide compounding once the shortage designations made that expansion legally permissible, and the compounded product that resulted — typically priced dramatically below the branded drugs' list price, sold directly to patients through a fast-growing wave of telehealth platforms built specifically around weight-loss prescribing — became, for a period of roughly two years, the primary way a meaningful share of American patients without insurance coverage or with insurers unwilling to pay the branded price actually accessed a GLP-1 drug at all.

That access came with real risk attached, risk the FDA documented directly rather than only inferring. Compounded semaglutide is not held to the same manufacturing standardization as the branded, FDA-approved pens, and the agency's MedWatch safety-reporting system began collecting adverse-event reports tied specifically to compounded product: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fainting, headache, dehydration, and in more serious cases acute pancreatitis and gallstones requiring hospitalization. Some of the documented error patterns were mechanical rather than pharmacological — patients self-administering from multidose vials, a format many had no prior experience handling, at doses many times larger than intended, or clinicians miscalculating the conversion between a prescribed milligram dose and the corresponding volume to draw up from a given vial's concentration. In one documented case, a clinician prescribed twenty units of a compounded product where two units had been intended, and three separate patients ended up receiving roughly ten times their intended dose, with resulting nausea and vomiting. The FDA's alert on the issue noted that many of the affected patients had no prior experience with self-injection at all, and that compounded-product concentrations, unlike the standardized branded pens, could vary meaningfully from one compounder to the next — a patient switching compounders, or receiving a new batch from the same one, had no guarantee the dosing math that had worked safely before would still apply.

Counterfeiting compounded a supply crisis that compounding had already complicated. In December 2023, the FDA and Novo Nordisk jointly warned that counterfeit Ozempic 1-milligram injection pens had entered the legitimate American drug supply chain — not merely grey-market compounded product sold outside ordinary pharmacy channels, but pens designed to be mistaken for the genuine, FDA-approved article, complete with counterfeit needles, labels, patient information inserts, and cartons. The affected lot, identified by lot number NAR0074 and serial number 430834149057, prompted thousands of units to be pulled from circulation; the FDA reported it was aware of five adverse events tied to the counterfeit lot, none serious, consistent with Ozempic's already-known side-effect profile. A similar counterfeit-warning cycle recurred in April 2025, evidence that the underlying vulnerability — genuine demand for an expensive, hard-to-obtain drug, moving through a supply chain not built to withstand that level of pressure — had not fully resolved even as the manufacturers ramped up production.

That ramp-up eventually did catch up to demand, at least by the FDA's own formal accounting. The agency determined the tirzepatide shortage resolved on December 19, 2024, granting compounders a grace period of enforcement discretion — sixty days for 503A pharmacies, ninety for 503B outsourcing facilities — before compounded tirzepatide production would need to stop. The semaglutide shortage took slightly longer to resolve formally: the FDA issued a thirteen-page declaratory order on February 21, 2025, based on information from Novo Nordisk together with input from patients, providers, and compounders, again setting enforcement-discretion deadlines, April 22, 2025 for 503A pharmacies and May 22, 2025 for 503B outsourcing facilities, after which compounded semaglutide production would need to end. The Outsourcing Facilities Association, representing compounders whose businesses depended substantially on continued semaglutide and tirzepatide compounding, filed federal lawsuits challenging both determinations; courts denied preliminary injunctions in each case, and the deadlines held. The FDA subsequently proposed excluding semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide from its so-called Bulks List, the formal roster of substances 503B outsourcing facilities are permitted to compound in the absence of a shortage — a move that, if finalized, would foreclose any regulatory path back to large-scale compounding of these drugs even in a future supply disruption.

What the shortage's official end did not do was end the underlying tension between the branded manufacturers and the compounding industry that had grown up to fill the gap they had left open. Novo Nordisk, by mid-2025, had filed more than 130 lawsuits across roughly 40 states against telehealth companies and compounding pharmacies over compounded semaglutide sales, winning permanent injunctions against multiple defendants along the way. Eli Lilly filed a parallel wave of suits protecting Mounjaro and Zepbound, and later extended similar litigation to protect retatrutide, its next-generation candidate still working through clinical trials rather than commercial sale, after peptide vendors and compounding operations began selling versions of the unapproved molecule directly to consumers anyway — a preview of a problem this book's closing chapters return to, as an entire pipeline of increasingly potent successor drugs moves through development against a backdrop of demand the legitimate, FDA-regulated supply chain has already once proven unable to satisfy on its own.

The litigation cut in more than one direction. Novo Nordisk filed a patent-infringement suit against the telehealth company Hims & Hers in February 2026 over its compounded semaglutide sales; within weeks, the two companies instead announced a commercial collaboration, and Novo Nordisk dropped the suit — a reversal that followed an earlier, shorter-lived partnership between the same two companies in 2025, terminated within days after Novo Nordisk accused Hims & Hers of failing to adhere to the law in how it was marketing compounded product. And in January 2026, a compounding pharmacy called Strive Compounding, based in Arizona, filed its own antitrust complaint against both Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk in federal court in the Western District of Texas, alleging that the two drugmakers had used exclusive telehealth partnership agreements to systematically lock independent compounders out of the market rather than competing on price and access alone. The complaint's core allegation — that the companies most responsible for bringing these drugs to market were also, once the acute shortage had passed, using their market power to foreclose the grey-market channel that had kept many patients supplied through the worst of it — captures something genuinely unresolved about this period of the drugs' history: a supply crisis real enough to produce documented patient harm from mis-dosed compounded product, met by a compounding response that, whatever its risks, gave a meaningful number of otherwise-priced-out patients access to a medicine the branded manufacturers could not yet supply at the scale demand required, followed by a crackdown that served the manufacturers' commercial interests and the FDA's manufacturing-standardization concerns simultaneously, whether or not those two interests were actually pointing toward the same outcome for the patients caught in between.
