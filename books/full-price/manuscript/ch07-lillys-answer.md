# Lilly's Answer

Eli Lilly's relationship with the GLP-1 drug class predates Novo Nordisk's Ozempic-driven ascent by more than a decade. Lilly had co-marketed Byetta with Amylin Pharmaceuticals back in 2005, the small San Diego biotech that had licensed exenatide from John Eng after the Bronx VA declined to patent his discovery — a partnership that gave Lilly an early, ground-level education in incretin biology, in the mechanics of gastric emptying and glucose-dependent insulin secretion, and in the commercial reality that patients on these drugs tended to lose more weight than anyone had designed the drugs to produce. Lilly's own subsequent incretin research program did not simply try to build a better single-receptor GLP-1 drug to compete head-on with Novo Nordisk's steadily improving lineage from Victoza through Ozempic to Wegovy. It pursued a structurally different bet: a molecule engineered to activate two separate incretin receptors at once, rather than one.

The molecule that emerged from that research, internally coded LY3298176 through its development and eventually named tirzepatide, is a synthetic 39-amino-acid peptide built on the structural backbone of a different native hormone, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, or GIP — a molecule secreted in the gut alongside GLP-1 and long understood to play some role in glucose regulation, though its precise contribution had been harder to isolate and had drawn less commercial attention than GLP-1 itself. Lilly's chemists engineered GLP-1 receptor activity into that GIP backbone, and added the same kind of fatty-acid modification Novo Nordisk's team had used to extend semaglutide's half-life, producing a single molecule capable of activating both the GIP and GLP-1 receptors simultaneously, dosed once weekly like its Novo Nordisk rival. Peer-reviewed pharmacology work describing the molecule, published by Kim Coskun and colleagues in 2018, characterized tirzepatide's activity at each receptor as deliberately imbalanced rather than symmetric: the drug behaves more like native GIP at the GIP receptor while showing a biased pattern of signaling at the GLP-1 receptor, a combination the original research team hypothesized could produce additive or even synergistic effects on insulin secretion, on the metabolic behavior of fat tissue, and on appetite suppression, beyond what either receptor activated alone had achieved.

The FDA approved tirzepatide for type 2 diabetes on May 13, 2022, under the brand name Mounjaro, describing it in Lilly's own investor announcement as the first and only approved GIP and GLP-1 receptor co-agonist — a genuine "first" in the regulatory sense, distinct in mechanism from anything Novo Nordisk had brought to market, built on a clinical program called SURPASS that had run across five separate trials, more than six thousand patients, and sites in twenty-four countries. As with Byetta, Victoza, and Ozempic before it, Mounjaro's diabetes-focused approval did not take long to generate the same familiar secondary story: patients losing more weight on the drug than a diabetes medication was designed to produce, this time at a scale that made the earlier off-label weight-loss patterns around Ozempic look modest by comparison. Lilly moved to formalize that use case considerably faster than Novo Nordisk's roughly three-and-a-half-year gap between Ozempic and Wegovy had done. The FDA approved tirzepatide for chronic weight management on November 8, 2023, under the brand name Zepbound, for adults with obesity or with overweight accompanied by a weight-related condition — roughly eighteen months after Mounjaro's diabetes approval, a considerably tighter interval than Novo Nordisk had managed with its own two products, and a sign of how directly Lilly was now racing its Danish rival rather than following at a comfortable distance behind it.

The choice to build tirzepatide around a dual-receptor mechanism rather than simply refining single-receptor GLP-1 chemistry further, as Novo Nordisk had done across three successive drug generations, reflected a genuinely different scientific bet about where the biology's remaining potential actually lay. By the mid-2010s, when Lilly's tirzepatide program was taking shape, GLP-1 receptor agonism on its own was reasonably well understood, and the marginal returns to further refining a single-receptor molecule — squeezing out slightly better potency, slightly longer duration — were beginning to look more incremental than transformative. GIP, the second hormone tirzepatide's chemists built into the same molecule, had a murkier reputation within the field; some earlier research had actually suggested GIP signaling might work against weight loss under certain conditions rather than support it, a genuine scientific controversy Lilly's own researchers had to work through and ultimately resolve in tirzepatide's favor before the company could commit fully to the dual-agonist strategy. That the bet paid off as decisively as SURMOUNT's data eventually showed was not, in other words, an obvious or a safe scientific choice at the time Lilly's chemists made it; it was closer to a genuine gamble on a still-contested piece of hormone physiology, one that could plausibly have produced a worse drug than a single-receptor competitor rather than a better one, and that only the resulting trial data could actually settle.

The clinical trial data underlying Zepbound's approval, drawn from a program called SURMOUNT, gave Lilly a genuine efficacy edge over Wegovy that the company was not shy about publicizing. SURMOUNT-1, the pivotal trial, enrolled 2,539 participants with obesity or overweight and no diabetes, and found that participants on the highest tested dose, 15 milligrams weekly, lost an average of 20.9 percent of body weight over 72 weeks, against 3.1 percent for placebo — results that, across the three doses tested in the trial, ranged from roughly 16.0 to 22.5 percent, comfortably ahead of the 14.9 percent STEP 1 had reported for semaglutide, though it is worth noting the two trials were not run head-to-head against each other and differed somewhat in design and population, a caveat that matters more to a careful reader of the underlying science than it typically did to the marketing built on top of it.

Lilly eventually did run the head-to-head comparison directly, in a trial called SURMOUNT-5, and the results, when they arrived, gave the company's competitive claim considerably more rigor than cross-trial comparison alone could provide. The trial randomized 751 participants without diabetes across 32 sites in the United States and Puerto Rico to receive either maximum-tolerated-dose tirzepatide or maximum-tolerated-dose semaglutide over 72 weeks. Tirzepatide produced 20.2 percent average weight loss against semaglutide's 13.7 percent — a relative advantage on the order of 47 percent more weight lost with the Lilly drug — and tirzepatide patients also showed a larger reduction in waist circumference, roughly 20 centimeters against roughly 15, and a considerably higher share of patients reaching the more ambitious threshold of losing a quarter or more of their starting body weight, 31.6 percent on tirzepatide against a meaningfully smaller share on semaglutide. For a drug category whose entire commercial and clinical value proposition rests on a single number — how much weight does the drug actually take off, on average, over the course of roughly a year and a half of treatment — SURMOUNT-5 gave Lilly a genuine, trial-verified claim to having built the more effective molecule, one built on a dual-receptor mechanism its rival's single-receptor chemistry could not straightforwardly replicate without an entirely new drug development program of its own.

Lilly's own institutional history with incretin drugs gave it a genuine head start in translating the tirzepatide science into manufacturing and commercial infrastructure, even though the company had trailed Novo Nordisk badly through the Victoza and early Ozempic years. Having co-marketed Byetta with Amylin back in 2005 and having later acquired Amylin outright in 2012 after its independent commercial performance disappointed, Lilly already possessed two decades of accumulated experience manufacturing injectable incretin peptides at scale, running the specific kind of large, multinational diabetes trial programs the SURPASS and SURMOUNT efforts would eventually require, and navigating the FDA's review process for this exact drug class, before tirzepatide's own development program had produced a single Phase 3 result. That accumulated institutional knowledge does not fully explain why Lilly's dual-receptor bet paid off scientifically — the GIP-and-GLP-1 co-agonism approach could easily have underperformed a well-optimized single-receptor drug, and Novo Nordisk's own decades of iterative refinement on the GLP-1 receptor alone were not a foolish strategy in hindsight, merely the one that ultimately proved less potent once a genuine head-to-head comparison existed. But it helps explain why Lilly was positioned to move as quickly as it did once tirzepatide's early data looked promising, compressing the gap between its diabetes and obesity approvals to roughly eighteen months where Novo Nordisk had needed closer to three and a half years to do the same with semaglutide.

That efficacy gap, once it became visible first through cross-trial comparison and then through SURMOUNT-5's direct data, reshaped the competitive dynamic between the two companies in ways that ripple through much of the rest of this book — through the market-capitalization swings examined in the next chapter, through the prescribing-share shifts among physicians and patients choosing between the two drugs, and through the pricing and access battles examined later, in which Lilly's stronger efficacy data became a bargaining chip in negotiations with insurers and, eventually, with the federal government itself. It is worth being precise about what that efficacy gap does and does not mean, because both the pharmaceutical industry's marketing apparatus and the financial press covering it have, at various points, been prone to overstating its clinical significance. A roughly six-percentage-point difference in average weight loss between two drugs that both produce dramatically more weight loss than anything obesity medicine had previously offered is a meaningful clinical distinction, one worth a physician and patient discussing directly when choosing between the two. It is not, on its own, evidence that semaglutide is an inferior or obsolete drug; STEP 1's 14.9 percent average, on its own terms and without reference to what came after it, remains one of the largest pharmacologically achieved weight-loss results in the history of obesity medicine, and millions of patients have done well on Wegovy specifically, including some who did not tolerate or respond as well to tirzepatide's somewhat more pronounced gastrointestinal side-effect profile. The competition between the two drugs, in other words, produced a genuine clinical advance — patients now have two structurally different, independently effective options rather than one — even as it also produced, as the following chapters trace in detail, a financial rivalry intense enough to swing tens of billions of dollars in market value in a matter of days, depending on which company's latest trial readout landed better than the other's.

By late 2023, with Mounjaro and Ozempic both established diabetes drugs and Zepbound and Wegovy both freshly approved for weight management, the two companies were no longer racing to prove that GLP-1-based pharmacology could meaningfully treat obesity — that argument had been settled, decisively, by the STEP and SURMOUNT trial data alike. They were racing, instead, for a rapidly expanding and extraordinarily lucrative market that both Wall Street analysts and the companies' own investor materials had begun describing in the tens and eventually hundreds of billions of dollars, a race that would send Novo Nordisk's market capitalization briefly past the entire economic output of the country that had founded it, and then, within roughly eighteen months, send that same valuation into one of the steepest and most instructive corrections in recent pharmaceutical history.
