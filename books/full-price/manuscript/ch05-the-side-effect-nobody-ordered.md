# The Side Effect Nobody Ordered

For the first three and a half years of its life on the American market, Ozempic was, on paper, an unremarkable addition to a crowded field of type 2 diabetes drugs — a once-weekly injection, similar in mechanism to the once-daily Victoza that preceded it, prescribed by endocrinologists and primary care physicians to patients whose blood sugar needed better control than diet, metformin, or older drug classes were achieving. Nothing about its FDA label, approved in December 2017 on the strength of the SUSTAIN trial program, mentioned weight loss as an intended outcome. And yet the same pattern that had appeared with Byetta a dozen years earlier — patients on a GLP-1 drug losing more weight than a diabetes medication was expected to produce — showed up again with Ozempic, at a scale endocrinologists had not seen before, because semaglutide was simply a more potent, longer-lasting molecule than exenatide or liraglutide had been.

Endocrinologists prescribing Ozempic through this period were, in effect, running an uncontrolled natural experiment their own patients had opted into without any of the structure a formal clinical trial would have imposed. A physician managing a patient's type 2 diabetes routinely tracks that patient's weight at every visit as a matter of course, alongside blood glucose and the other standard metrics of metabolic health, which meant the weight-loss pattern showing up on Ozempic was not a subtle signal buried in survey data but something visible, office visit after office visit, on the same scale a nurse had been using for years. Several endocrinology practices began keeping informal, unpublished tallies of exactly this pattern well before any peer-reviewed literature caught up to describe it systematically, comparing notes with colleagues at professional conferences in the way clinical medicine has always generated its earliest hypotheses, long before those hypotheses become the subject of a funded trial. That informal, pre-publication phase of medical discovery rarely makes it into the official history of a drug, which tends to date a finding to its first peer-reviewed appearance rather than to the months or years of accumulating clinical observation that made researchers confident enough to design the trial in the first place — but it is where Ozempic's reputation as a weight-loss drug, rather than merely a diabetes drug with a helpful side effect, actually began.

The mechanism, once physiologists worked out how to describe it in terms patients could recognize in their own experience, turned on the drug's effect on several linked systems at once rather than any single lever. GLP-1 receptor agonists slow gastric emptying, meaning food sits in the stomach relaxing its usual rhythm of contraction and release, which prolongs the physical sensation of fullness after a meal considerably longer than an unmedicated stomach would sustain it. They act on receptors in the hypothalamus and brainstem, and on the vagal nerve pathways that carry signals between gut and brain, in ways that blunt hunger signaling directly rather than only through the stomach's mechanical fullness. And they appear to act on the mesolimbic reward circuitry that drives cravings and food-seeking behavior in the first place — not merely making a patient feel full sooner, but making the anticipatory pull toward food, the thing that makes a person think about a snack twenty minutes before they are actually hungry for one, noticeably quieter. Patients did not have clinical language for that third effect when they first started noticing it. What they had, instead, was an experience: the compulsive, looping mental preoccupation with food that many people carry through an ordinary day — what to eat next, whether the thing in the pantry was still there, what dessert might be available later — simply stopped, or dropped to a fraction of its previous volume, within weeks of starting the drug.

That experience eventually acquired a name, "food noise," a term that had circulated in occasional internet use as early as 2006 according to search-trend data but that surged into wide, common usage only in 2022 and 2023, once large numbers of people taking Ozempic or Wegovy for the first time were reaching for some way to describe what had changed. It is not a formal diagnostic or physiological term; no textbook chapter on hypothalamic appetite regulation used the phrase before patients coined it for themselves. But it did real explanatory work, because it captured something clinical language had struggled to convey: that for many people who had spent years or decades experiencing hunger and food-seeking as a kind of constant background static, the drugs' most startling effect was not the number on the scale but the sudden, unfamiliar silence where that static used to be. Patient testimonials describing the sensation spread heavily on TikTok and other platforms through 2022 and 2023, turning what had begun as an off-label discovery passed between doctors and patients in exam rooms into a broadly shared cultural vocabulary, well before most of the people using the term had any occasion to read a clinical trial report.

The off-label prescribing pattern that developed in Ozempic's first several years on the market followed a familiar, if legally and ethically fraught, path in American medicine: a drug approved for one indication, quietly prescribed by physicians for a related but formally unapproved purpose, on the strength of clinical judgment and patient demand rather than a matching FDA label. It is a legal practice — physicians retain broad discretion to prescribe an approved drug for a use beyond its labeled indication, based on their clinical judgment — but it is one insurers are typically far less willing to pay for, since insurance coverage generally follows the label rather than the prescriber's judgment. That mismatch meant that in the years before Wegovy's 2021 approval gave weight-loss use an FDA-sanctioned home of its own, patients seeking Ozempic specifically for weight loss were often paying the drug's full list price out of pocket, or securing a diabetes diagnosis and a covered prescription through channels not originally intended to reach them, or relying on physicians willing to prescribe off-label and patients willing to absorb the cost themselves.

The physicians prescribing Ozempic off-label during this period were navigating a genuinely uncomfortable professional position, one worth describing plainly because it shaped how cautiously and how unevenly the drug's early weight-loss reputation actually spread. Prescribing an approved drug for an unapproved purpose is legal and common throughout American medicine, but it puts the liability and the judgment call squarely on the individual physician rather than on any institutional guidance, and it typically means the patient pays out of pocket for a drug that, at Ozempic's list price, few households could absorb comfortably as a discretionary monthly expense. Physicians willing to prescribe this way tended to cluster in concierge and cash-pay practices serving wealthier patients, in medical-aesthetics and "wellness" clinics adjacent to the same industry already selling Botox and dermal fillers, and among specialists in obesity medicine who had spent careers frustrated by how few effective tools they had to offer and were willing to prescribe ahead of a formal weight-loss indication once the anecdotal evidence in their own patient panels became hard to ignore. That clustering had a real and lasting consequence for who got early access to a transformative drug and who did not: the same off-label period that built Ozempic's cultural mystique among the wealthy and famous was, simultaneously, invisible to the much larger population of patients whose primary care physician either did not know the drug could be used this way, would not take on the liability of prescribing outside its label, or served a patient population for whom an uncovered nine-hundred-dollar monthly prescription was never a realistic option regardless of what their doctor might have been willing to write.

The word spread the way word tends to spread about anything that visibly and quickly changes how a person looks: through direct observation among people who knew each other, well before any institutional channel — a marketing campaign, a physician's professional society, a public health announcement — organized or amplified it. A colleague noticeably thinner after a few months. A relative at a holiday gathering, changed enough that people who had not seen them recently commented on it. By the time Elon Musk replied to a comment about his own changed physique on social media in October 2022, naming Wegovy alongside intermittent fasting as part of what he had been doing, the drug's off-label reputation for producing this kind of visible, relatively fast transformation had already been building for the better part of two years among people paying closer attention than most of the country yet was. His disclosure did not create the phenomenon; it accelerated an already-building wave of public curiosity by putting a specific, famous name and a specific brand name in the same sentence, at a moment when the underlying medical story — a genuinely new and genuinely more effective class of obesity treatment, working through a genuinely novel mechanism — was still mostly confined to endocrinology conferences and pharmaceutical earnings calls rather than everyday conversation.

Patient communities online accelerated that informal discovery process considerably faster than the medical literature could track it. Reddit forums and Facebook groups organized specifically around Ozempic and, later, Wegovy and Mounjaro use began accumulating tens and eventually hundreds of thousands of members through 2022 and 2023, functioning as a kind of crowdsourced, unblinded, uncontrolled clinical registry — patients comparing dosing schedules, side-effect management strategies, and subjective experiences of appetite suppression in granular, day-to-day detail no formal trial protocol would have captured. That patient-generated knowledge base filled a real gap institutional medicine was slow to address, particularly around practical questions a 68-week clinical trial protocol had little reason to study closely: how to manage nausea during dose escalation, what foods became harder or easier to tolerate, how the drug interacted with alcohol, how patients experienced the psychological adjustment of no longer thinking about food the way they had for years or decades. It also, inevitably, spread misinformation alongside genuine insight, unfiltered by any clinical review, in the way patient communities organized around any dramatic new medical intervention tend to do — a tension this book's later chapter on eating-disorder risk and disability advocacy returns to directly, since some of the same online spaces that helped patients manage real side effects also normalized use patterns clinicians would come to view as genuinely risky.

What the informal, word-of-mouth phase of Ozempic's spread produced, well before Wegovy's own approval formalized the weight-loss use case, was a kind of natural experiment in unmet demand: a drug whose most culturally significant effect was not the one its manufacturer had originally designed a marketing campaign around, discovered independently by patients and physicians working outside the label, spreading through informal networks faster than the regulatory and insurance systems built to manage prescription drugs were designed to absorb. Manufacturing capacity for Ozempic had been planned around projected demand for a diabetes drug competing in an already crowded field, not around the possibility that a meaningful fraction of a national population might want it for an entirely different, unapproved purpose. The gap between that planning assumption and the demand that actually materialized would, within a few years, produce a shortage crisis serious enough to draw FDA intervention, congressional attention, and a wave of grey-market compounding that this book examines later in its own right. But the shortage was a downstream consequence of something that happened first and more quietly: a drug doing something for patients that its own approved label did not describe, discovered by the people taking it before it was ever formally studied for that purpose, and spreading through the same informal channels — a doctor's office, a family gathering, a stranger's social media post — that have always carried news of a genuinely new kind of medicine faster than any institution built to regulate one.

Pharmacies themselves became an unplanned early-warning system for what was coming. Independent pharmacists filling Ozempic prescriptions through 2021 and into 2022 began noticing a pattern their own dispensing records made hard to miss: a rising share of prescriptions carried diagnosis codes or prior-authorization paperwork that did not quite match the patients they were serving, alongside a steadily growing number of cash-pay transactions for a drug primarily designed to be billed through insurance. Some of that gap reflected legitimate off-label prescribing by physicians willing to write the prescription and patients willing to pay for it themselves; some of it, pharmacists and industry observers would later note, reflected a less clean picture, in which the line between a genuine diabetes diagnosis and a diagnosis coded partly to secure insurance coverage for what a patient and physician both understood to be primarily a weight-loss treatment had become considerably blurrier than either the drug's label or its billing codes were designed to accommodate. None of this constituted fraud in any serious sense the FDA or federal prosecutors treated as an enforcement priority during this period; it was simply what an in-demand, off-label-attractive drug does to a prescribing and billing system built around the assumption that a drug's approved indication and its actual use would remain reasonably well aligned.

That informal phase did not last. What had been word-of-mouth discovery, spreading unevenly and mostly off-label, was about to collide with a clinical trial result large enough, and clean enough, that neither the FDA nor the rest of the medical establishment could treat the phenomenon as anecdotal any longer. The number at the center of that result was 14.9 percent — the average share of body weight participants lost on semaglutide over sixty-eight weeks in a trial called STEP 1, published in March 2021, a result so far outside the range obesity medicine had previously been able to promise that it forced a genuine reclassification of what a drug in this category could be expected to do.
