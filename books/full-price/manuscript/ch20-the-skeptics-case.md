# The Skeptics' Case

It is worth stating plainly, before working through each specific line of skepticism this chapter assembles, what kind of chapter this is not trying to be. This is not a rebuttal chapter, structured to raise each concern only to knock it down and reassure a reader that the earlier, more optimistic chapters of this book were right all along. Nor is it a chapter built to relitigate the entire book from a position of hindsight bias, treating every prior finding as suspect simply because a critic somewhere has questioned it. It is, instead, an attempt to give the strongest, most carefully sourced version of each major skeptical argument the space a book covering this subject seriously actually owes it — space proportional to the credentials of the people making each argument and the quality of the evidence behind it, not proportional to how comfortably each argument fits alongside this book's own earlier, more triumphant narrative arc. Some of what follows will sit uneasily against earlier chapters. This book has tried, throughout, to let that discomfort stand rather than resolve it prematurely.

Johann Hari took the drugs himself before he wrote a book arguing readers should be considerably more cautious about them than the cultural mood of 2023 and 2024 generally allowed. Magic Pill: The Extraordinary Benefits and Disturbing Risks of the New Weight-Loss Drugs, published in 2024, does not deny that semaglutide worked for him, or that the clinical trial evidence this book has spent its earlier chapters documenting is real. Hari's argument is narrower and, in its own way, harder to dismiss: that the entire category of evidence available about these drugs, however large and however rigorous, still covers only a few years of population-scale use, and that pharmaceutical history offers a specific and sobering precedent for what can happen when a drug class looks safe across that short a window. He draws the comparison explicitly to antipsychotic medications, whose links to serious long-term risks including dementia were not discovered until years, in some cases decades, after the drugs had already become a normal part of psychiatric practice. Because the STEP 1 extension data examined in this book's earlier chapter shows most patients regaining the bulk of their lost weight within a year of stopping, Hari's book argues that most people who start these drugs are, in practice, committing to lifelong use of a molecule whose true multi-decade safety profile — not its two- or three-year trial profile, however impressive — simply is not yet knowable by anyone, including the companies selling it.

It is worth being fair to Hari's underlying methodology, and to the broader genre of long-term-risk skepticism he represents, since dismissing it purely because the FDA's own investigation reached a reassuring conclusion on one specific question would understate what actually remains unknown. A regulatory finding that a drug does not cause a specific, named adverse effect researchers had specifically gone looking for is meaningfully different from a comprehensive guarantee that no unknown long-term effect exists at all; the entire premise of Hari's antipsychotic comparison is that the dementia risk eventually identified in that drug class was not the risk anyone was specifically screening for during the drugs' early years of widespread use, and only became visible once enough patients had been on the medication for enough decades for a pattern absent from any shorter-term dataset to finally emerge. Nothing in the FDA's suicidal-ideation review, however methodologically sound, addresses that broader category of concern, because no dataset currently in existence could yet address it — the oldest continuously treated GLP-1 patients, even counting from Byetta's 2005 approval, have been on some version of this drug class for barely two decades, and the drugs at the center of this book's own story have been in truly mass use for less than five years. The regulatory record on safety, where it exists, has moved in a direction that complicates rather than confirms Hari's most alarming worry, though it has not eliminated the underlying uncertainty his argument rests on. The FDA opened a formal investigation into reports of suicidal ideation associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists in July 2023, following a wave of postmarketing adverse-event reports, and issued a drug safety communication in January 2024 concluding that its preliminary review of clinical trial and postmarketing data did not find evidence that the drugs cause suicidal thoughts or actions — while noting explicitly that the underlying risk estimate remained uncertain given the small number of cases available to analyze. The agency followed up with a considerably larger meta-analysis spanning 91 placebo-controlled trials and 107,910 patients, comparing 60,338 patients on a GLP-1 drug against 47,572 on placebo, and again found no increased risk of psychiatric adverse events. On January 13, 2026, on the strength of that accumulated evidence, the FDA formally requested that Novo Nordisk, Eli Lilly, and the other manufacturers remove the suicidal-ideation and behavior warning from their drugs' labeling entirely — as close to a regulatory all-clear on this specific concern as the agency's own process allows, arrived at only after roughly two and a half years of investigation rather than dismissed reflexively at the first sign of a worrying signal.

It is worth pausing on why the suicidal-ideation question generated this much sustained regulatory attention in the first place, given that the FDA's eventual conclusion was reassuring. Antidepressant medications carry a similar, well-known boxed warning about suicidal ideation in younger patients, added after a wave of postmarketing reports in the early 2000s prompted an FDA review broadly similar in structure to the one these drugs underwent two decades later — a review that, unlike this one, did find enough of a signal to warrant a lasting label change. Regulators reviewing any drug that affects mood, appetite, or reward circuitry as directly as GLP-1 receptor agonists do have learned, from that earlier episode, to take patient-reported psychiatric adverse events seriously even when the initial evidence is thin and the number of reports small relative to the total prescribing population, because the cost of dismissing a genuine signal prematurely is considerably higher than the cost of investigating a false alarm thoroughly. That the GLP-1 investigation ultimately concluded differently than the antidepressant review does not mean the two and a half years of FDA scrutiny were unwarranted; it means the review process worked roughly as designed, taking a plausible biological concern — a drug acting directly on brain reward and appetite circuitry could, in principle, affect mood regulation through pathways not yet fully understood — seriously enough to investigate rigorously before reaching a conclusion, rather than dismissing it reflexively at the outset because the manufacturer's own commercial interest in a negative finding was so obvious.

Two other safety concerns have moved through the legal system rather than resolving through FDA review, and remain genuinely unsettled as this book goes to print. Personal-injury lawsuits alleging gastroparesis, bowel obstruction, and related gastrointestinal injuries from GLP-1 drugs were consolidated by the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation into a single federal proceeding, MDL No. 3094, centralized before Judge Karen S. Marston in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania following a transfer order dated February 2, 2024. By August 2026, roughly 3,928 individual actions were pending within that consolidated litigation. In August 2025, Judge Marston largely denied the manufacturers' motion to dismiss the case outright, allowing the core claims to proceed toward trial, while separately ruling that plaintiffs would need to support their gastroparesis claims with objective gastric-emptying test results rather than symptom self-report alone — a ruling that will make some claims considerably harder to sustain than the volume of filings alone might suggest, without foreclosing the underlying litigation. A second, more recent concern followed a different path entirely: a retrospective study led by Dr. Joseph Rizzo at Mass Eye and Ear, affiliated with Harvard Medical School, and published in JAMA Ophthalmology in July 2024, found that diabetic patients on semaglutide were roughly four times more likely to be diagnosed with a rare eye condition called NAION — non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, a cause of sudden, often permanent vision loss — than diabetic patients on other medications, and that overweight or obese patients on semaglutide were roughly seven times more likely to receive the same diagnosis than similar patients on other weight-loss treatments. The study's authors and the major ophthalmology societies that reviewed it were careful to note that a retrospective cohort study of this kind can establish an association without proving the drug actually causes the condition, but the finding was serious enough to prompt its own multidistrict litigation, established by a December 2025 transfer order and, like the gastroparesis cases, centralized before Judge Marston, with the number of pending actions climbing through 2026 as more patients came forward.

The gap between trial persistence and real-world persistence deserves a closer examination of its likely causes, since "patients simply stop wanting to take an effective drug" is a less complete explanation than the raw discontinuation numbers alone might suggest. Cost is almost certainly the single largest driver: a patient facing the full weight of this book's earlier chapter on list pricing and inconsistent insurance coverage, watching a monthly bill fluctuate or disappear entirely as an employer's benefits committee revisits its own coverage decision, has an obvious and entirely rational reason to stop a medication a clinical trial participant, often receiving the drug free of charge as a condition of enrollment, never had to weigh. Side effects that a closely monitored trial population, supported by frequent clinical contact and dose-titration guidance, might tolerate through a difficult adjustment period can prove harder to push through without that same level of support in ordinary practice. And the psychological experience of a drug that must be taken indefinitely, examined directly in this book's earlier chapter on weight regain, may simply wear on patients differently than a trial's defined, time-limited protocol ever tested — a chronic commitment is a different thing to sustain than a bounded research obligation, however similar the actual injection or pill might be from one week to the next.

A different, less dramatic but arguably more consequential kind of skepticism concerns not whether the drugs are safe, but whether the real world actually resembles the clinical trials well enough for their headline efficacy numbers to hold up outside carefully monitored study conditions. A study by Prime Therapeutics, a pharmacy benefit manager, tracking a cohort of roughly 17.9 million average monthly commercially insured members from January 2021 through June 2024, found that one-year persistence on GLP-1 drugs among patients starting them without a diabetes diagnosis rose from just 33.2 percent in 2021 to 58.6 percent by the first half of 2024 — a genuine improvement, but one that still means, even at its best, more than four in ten patients had stopped taking the drug within a year. Other academic studies of specific obesity-clinic populations have found even starker attrition, with one dataset showing discontinuation rates climbing from 14 percent at three months to 50 percent by twelve months, and the wider range of reported one-year discontinuation across different studies and payer populations running anywhere from roughly 37 percent to more than 80 percent depending on the population studied. That gap between trial-level persistence — participants in STEP 1 and SURMOUNT-1 received close monitoring, structured support, and in many cases free drug supply for the duration of a defined 68- or 72-week study — and real-world persistence, where patients face cost, side effects, and none of a clinical trial's built-in structure, matters enormously for how the population-level economic and public-health projections examined throughout this book should actually be read. A market-size estimate or a Medicare cost projection built on the assumption that patients starting these drugs keep taking them indefinitely, the way the "chronic disease" framing this book's earlier chapter described implies they should, may be systematically overstating both the health benefit and the long-run financial exposure, if a meaningful share of real-world patients are, in practice, stopping well before that framing assumes.

The financial and structural critique runs in a related but distinct direction. Writing in Forbes in October 2025, journalist Sindhya Valloppillil argued that "the GLP-1 economy is crashing," describing the healthcare delivery infrastructure built around these drugs' distribution as "cracking under the weight of demand," with real-world impact "constrained by high drop-off rates, muscle loss, and financial costs for payers" — a critique that draws directly on the persistence data and lean-mass findings this book has already examined, synthesized into a broader argument that the investment enthusiasm surrounding these drugs outran the underlying commercial reality considerably faster than most of the bullish 2023-era market projections assumed. Richard Eskow, writing in The Nation, pressed a related but more explicitly political argument: that publicly funded science — the NIH-supported incretin research this book traced through Svetlana Mojsov's and Daniel Drucker's decades of work, and John Eng's research at a Veterans Affairs hospital — had been patented and priced steeply enough by the private companies that commercialized it to worsen medical inflation and leave millions of eligible patients untreated, warning of what he called a widening "medical caste system" between patients who could access the drugs and patients who could not.

Senator Bernie Sanders's own Senate HELP Committee report, released May 15, 2024, gave that structural critique its most concrete and most alarming numbers. The report projected that if half of American adults with obesity took a Wegovy-class drug at the prices prevailing at the time, the annual cost could reach $411 billion — a figure larger than the entire United States retail prescription drug market's total spending in 2022, which stood at roughly $406 billion — with Medicare and Medicaid alone potentially facing up to $166 billion of that cost each year. The same report calculated that the United States could save as much as $317 billion annually if Novo Nordisk charged American patients the same price it charged patients in Denmark, a comparison this book's earlier chapter on international pricing has already examined in detail. Those projections should be read against the more optimistic financial framing found elsewhere in this book — Goldman Sachs's own market-size estimates, which oscillated between $95 billion and $130 billion by 2030 across different published notes, and economist arguments, made publicly by figures including Emily Oster, that fears about the budgetary impact of broader access have been overstated relative to the offsetting healthcare savings broader treatment might eventually produce. This book does not think either side of that argument has definitively won; it thinks a reader deserves both the scale of the potential cost and the scale of the potential offsetting benefit stated plainly, rather than only whichever number better fits a preferred conclusion.

It is worth asking, finally, what a reasonable synthesis of this chapter's evidence should actually recommend to a patient or a policymaker trying to make a real decision rather than simply weigh abstract considerations. For an individual patient with a clear, evidence-backed indication — type 2 diabetes, obesity with a cardiovascular risk factor SELECT's population resembles, diagnosed sleep apnea — the accumulated weight of the clinical trial evidence this book has documented across its earlier chapters remains genuinely favorable, and this chapter's skepticism should be read as a call for informed, eyes-open use rather than avoidance. For a health system or an insurer weighing whether and how broadly to fund access, the cost projections and real-world persistence data examined in this chapter argue for a more cautious, more targeted approach than either the most optimistic pharmaceutical-industry framing or the broadest possible universal-access advocacy would suggest — coverage prioritized toward patients with the clearest, most rigorously demonstrated indications, with the difficult work of actually bringing prices down, examined throughout this book's earlier pricing chapters, treated as at least as urgent a policy priority as expanding the population eligible for coverage at today's prices. Neither of those conclusions is especially satisfying as a rhetorical matter; both are, this book believes, more honest than the more totalizing verdicts — miracle drug, dangerous overreach — that have dominated the public conversation this chapter has tried to complicate.

No single critic assembled in this chapter has made a complete, unified case against these drugs; that composite quality is itself worth naming honestly rather than smoothing over, because it reflects something true about where the genuine uncertainty in this story actually sits. A journalist who took the drug himself warns about unknown long-term risk. A federal regulator, after two and a half years of investigation, clears the drugs of one specific and widely feared safety concern while leaving two others in active litigation. A pharmacy benefit manager's own data shows real-world patients stopping far more often than trial participants did. A United States senator's own committee produces a cost projection large enough to strain the entire federal drug budget. None of these critiques, individually, overturns the clinical evidence this book's earlier chapters documented in careful detail — the STEP and SURMOUNT trials' weight-loss results, the SELECT trial's cardiovascular benefit, the sleep apnea approval, the credible early evidence on alcohol use disorder. Together, they constitute the strongest honest case available that this book's own earlier enthusiasm, however well grounded in real trial data, has been telling only part of the story — and that the part left out, concerning cost, durability, real-world adherence, and genuinely unresolved long-term safety questions, deserves exactly the same rigor this book has applied to the science that made these drugs famous in the first place.
