# The Woman They Forgot

Svetlana Mojsov arrived at Rockefeller University in the 1970s to study under Bruce Merrifield, the chemist whose invention of solid-phase peptide synthesis — a way of building peptide chains link by link on a solid support, rather than assembling them in solution — had already won him the kind of reputation that draws ambitious graduate students to a lab. Merrifield would go on to receive the 1984 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for that work. Mojsov's own contribution, over the following decade, would prove at least as consequential to human health, and would take more than forty years to be publicly credited at anything close to its actual weight.

By the 1980s, Mojsov had moved into the orbit of Joel Habener's laboratory, then at Massachusetts General Hospital, working as an independent investigator rather than a formal member of his group — a distinction that would matter later, when institutional credit got parceled out along lines of formal lab membership rather than along lines of who had actually done the work. The problem in front of the field at that point was not whether the glucagon gene produced more than one hormone; that much was becoming clear. The problem was which fragment of the resulting proglucagon molecule was the biologically active one — which specific, truncated piece of the larger protein actually bound the receptor and stimulated insulin release, and which pieces were inert byproducts of the same processing pathway. Mojsov's training under Merrifield gave her a tool almost nobody else studying glucagon biology had at hand: she could synthesize each candidate fragment herself, precisely, rather than relying only on material isolated from tissue, and test each one directly. Working through the possibilities, she identified the specific form — glucagon-like peptide-1, residues 7 through 36, amidated at one end, generally written as GLP-1(7-36)amide — that carried the insulin-stimulating activity the field had been chasing. It is, in essentially unmodified form, the molecular backbone every subsequent GLP-1 drug has been built from: Victoza, Ozempic, Wegovy, and the tirzepatide and retatrutide molecules that followed it are all, at their core, engineered variations on the fragment Mojsov isolated by chemical synthesis in the 1980s.

What happened next is the part of the story that took decades to become public in anything like full detail. Mojsov was, by her own later account and by the reporting that eventually surfaced it, left off or minimized in the credit structure that formed around the discovery — omitted or downplayed in some of the retrospective framing other scientists in the field gave to the work, and excluded from the initial shape of patent claims she eventually had to fight, successfully, to be added to as a named co-inventor. She kept her lab notebooks. She kept correcting the record in scientific venues as opportunities arose, rather than letting a simplified, two-man version of the discovery calcify into the field's accepted history. It did not fully work, for a long time. The clearest evidence of how thoroughly the credit had settled into a pattern that excluded her came in 2021, when the Canada Gairdner International Award — one of biomedical science's most prestigious prizes, and a frequent predictor of eventual Nobel recognition — went to Habener, to Daniel Drucker at the University of Toronto, and to Jens Juul Holst at the University of Copenhagen, for their work on GLP-1 biology and its therapeutic application. It was the third time in four years the same trio had been honored together for essentially the same body of work, following the 2017 Harrington Prize and the 2020 Warren Alpert Foundation Prize. Mojsov's name did not appear on any of the three.

The scientific journal Science published an investigative account of Mojsov's exclusion in 2023, under the headline "Her work paved the way for blockbuster obesity drugs. Now, she's fighting for recognition," followed in September 2024 by a second piece, "Lasker awardee Svetlana Mojsov describes a yearlong journey out of obscurity," after the announcement finally came that would begin to change the record. STAT News covered the same territory in a September 2023 piece headlined, with unusual directness for a science-journalism outlet, "The Ozempic revolution is rooted in the work of Svetlana Mojsov, yet she's been edged out of the story." What these accounts documented, in aggregate, was not a single act of exclusion but a slow accretion of it — awards that quietly settled on a two- or three-person version of events, retrospective essays and prize citations that repeated whichever earlier framing had already been established, media profiles of the "GLP-1 story" that interviewed the same well-credentialed men each time because they were the names that kept turning up in the previous round of citations. Erasure of this kind rarely happens as a single deliberate act. It happens because credit compounds, and once a name has been left off the list a few times, leaving it off again requires no fresh decision at all — it only requires following precedent.

The correction, when it came, arrived in stages rather than all at once, which is itself worth sitting with rather than smoothing into a tidier redemption narrative. On September 19, 2024, the Lasker Foundation announced that the Lasker~DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award — one of American medicine's most prestigious honors, sometimes called "America's Nobels" for its record of predicting the actual Nobel Prize — would go to Svetlana Mojsov, Joel Habener, and Lotte Bjerre Knudsen, the Novo Nordisk chief scientific advisor whose engineering work had turned GLP-1 biology into an actual drug. Daniel Drucker, notably, was not on that particular citation; the award recognized a different, overlapping configuration of the people responsible for the science and its translation into medicine. Rockefeller University's own account of the award, published on its news site, described the arc plainly in its headline: "Her scientific breakthrough took 5 years. Getting credit took decades." The Lasker recognition was followed by a fuller convergence: the 2024 Princess of Asturias Award for Scientific and Technical Research went to a five-person group — Drucker, Jeffrey Friedman, the discoverer of leptin, Habener, Holst, and Mojsov together — the first major prize to fold all of them into a single citation. In April 2026, the King Faisal Prize in Medicine named Mojsov among its laureates as well.

It is tempting, looking backward from a moment when Mojsov's name finally appears reliably alongside the men she worked among, to treat the story as a triumphant if belated correction — science getting it right eventually, the arc bending, in Martin Luther King's phrase, toward justice, however slowly. That framing is not wrong, exactly, but it understates what the delay actually cost, and to whom. For roughly three decades, the scientific establishment's answer to the question "who discovered the mechanism behind what would become the most commercially significant drug class of the early twenty-first century" was incomplete in a specific, patterned way: it kept naming men working at well-resourced institutions and repeatedly omitting the woman whose synthesis work had identified the specific active molecule. That pattern is not unique to GLP-1 research. It echoes Rosalyn Yalow's own long fight for recognition a generation earlier — the same Yalow who trained John Eng at the Bronx VA, and who had to build an unusual career at a VA hospital partly because more prestigious institutions offered a research physicist with her background fewer paths forward than her work deserved. It echoes Jocelyn Bell Burnell's exclusion from the 1974 Nobel Prize for the discovery of pulsars, a prize that instead went to her graduate supervisor. Mojsov's story sits inside a long, well-documented pattern in which women who do foundational bench science are more likely than their male colleagues to see that work folded into someone else's narrative, particularly when the someone else already occupies a more senior or more institutionally legible position.

Mojsov herself, by the accounts published after her recognition finally arrived, did not describe the delay as an abstraction. She had kept correcting the scientific record for years with what both Science profiles describe as a kind of patient, documented persistence — not public complaint so much as insistence, repeated in the venues where scientific credit is actually adjudicated, that the record be accurate. That insistence is, in its own way, a second scientific contribution layered on top of the first: a demonstration, available to any young researcher paying attention, that getting the underlying biology right and getting the credit right are two separate battles, and that neither one is guaranteed to happen just because the other did. She remains a research affiliate at Rockefeller University, in the same institution where the discovery began, and the molecule she identified in the 1980s is now injected weekly by tens of millions of people who have, for the most part, never heard her name — a gap this book means, in its own small way, to close.

The broader lesson the Mojsov story offers this book is not really about individual credit at all, though the individual injustice deserves to be named plainly rather than folded into a footnote. It is a caution about how origin stories get simplified as they travel from laboratory to boardroom to marketing copy to magazine profile, each retelling shedding a little more of the actual complexity and a little more of the actual cast. Novo Nordisk's own public account of its GLP-1 program, and much of the press coverage that followed its commercial success, tends to compress four decades of overlapping, sometimes uncredited work by dozens of scientists into a cleaner story about corporate vision and one or two named discoverers. That compression is not unique to this drug class, and it is not necessarily malicious; it is simply how institutions and journalism both tend to tell stories, because a cleaner story travels farther. But a book that means to take the science of these drugs seriously owes its subject the fuller, messier version — the guinea pig assay that almost didn't get followed up, the fellowship reassigned after a different project fell through, the chemist who had to fight for four decades to have her name attached to the molecule she was the first to isolate. What Copenhagen did with that molecule once Mojsov, Habener, Drucker, and Holst had identified and characterized it — how a mid-sized Danish insulin manufacturer turned a laboratory finding into the fastest-growing drug franchise in pharmaceutical history — is where the story goes next.
