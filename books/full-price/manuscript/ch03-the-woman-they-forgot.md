# The Woman They Forgot

Svetlana Mojsov arrived at Rockefeller University in the 1970s to study under Bruce Merrifield, the chemist whose invention of solid-phase peptide synthesis — a way of building peptide chains link by link on a solid support, rather than assembling them in solution — had already won him the kind of reputation that draws ambitious graduate students to a lab. Merrifield would go on to receive the 1984 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for that work. Mojsov's own contribution, over the following decade, would prove at least as consequential to human health, and would take more than forty years to be publicly credited at anything close to its actual weight.

The distinction between an "independent investigator" and a formal laboratory member, worth pausing on since it recurs throughout this chapter's account of how her credit was later minimized, reflects a genuinely ambiguous position within academic science's usual hierarchy — closer in status to a senior collaborator than to a graduate student or postdoctoral fellow working directly under a principal investigator's supervision, but without the kind of institutional title, own funded laboratory, or independent grant portfolio that would have made her contribution to a joint project unambiguous to anyone assembling a retrospective account of who did what. That ambiguity, structural rather than personal, is precisely the kind of gap institutional credit-assigning processes — prize committees, textbook authors, corporate historians — tend to resolve in favor of whoever already holds the more legible, more senior title, regardless of whose specific bench work actually produced the finding in question. By the 1980s, Mojsov had moved into the orbit of Joel Habener's laboratory, then at Massachusetts General Hospital, working as an independent investigator rather than a formal member of his group — a distinction that would matter later, when institutional credit got parceled out along lines of formal lab membership rather than along lines of who had actually done the work. The problem in front of the field at that point was not whether the glucagon gene produced more than one hormone; that much was becoming clear. The problem was which fragment of the resulting proglucagon molecule was the biologically active one — which specific, truncated piece of the larger protein actually bound the receptor and stimulated insulin release, and which pieces were inert byproducts of the same processing pathway. Mojsov's training under Merrifield gave her a tool almost nobody else studying glucagon biology had at hand: she could synthesize each candidate fragment herself, precisely, rather than relying only on material isolated from tissue, and test each one directly. The task required a specific and, at the time, rare combination of skills. Proglucagon, the larger precursor protein encoded by the glucagon gene, is cut apart differently depending on which tissue is doing the cutting — a process biochemists call differential post-translational processing — yielding several distinct hormone fragments from the same underlying genetic template, only one of which was responsible for the insulin-stimulating effect researchers were chasing. Simply isolating small quantities of each candidate fragment from animal tissue, the more conventional approach available to most labs at the time, made it difficult to obtain enough pure, precisely defined material to test rigorously. Mojsov's training under Merrifield let her instead synthesize each candidate fragment from scratch, one amino acid link at a time, in whatever quantity and whatever exact sequence she wanted to test — including, crucially, testing whether the fragment needed to end in a free carboxylic acid group or in an amidated form, a seemingly small chemical distinction that turns out to matter enormously for how tightly a peptide binds its receptor and how long it survives in circulation before degrading. Working through the possibilities, she identified the specific form — glucagon-like peptide-1, residues 7 through 36, amidated at one end, generally written as GLP-1(7-36)amide — that carried the insulin-stimulating activity the field had been chasing. It is, in essentially unmodified form, the molecular backbone every subsequent GLP-1 drug has been built from: Victoza, Ozempic, Wegovy, and the tirzepatide and retatrutide molecules that followed it are all, at their core, engineered variations on the fragment Mojsov isolated by chemical synthesis in the 1980s.

What happened next is the part of the story that took decades to become public in anything like full detail. Mojsov was, by her own later account and by the reporting that eventually surfaced it, left off or minimized in the credit structure that formed around the discovery — omitted or downplayed in some of the retrospective framing other scientists in the field gave to the work, and excluded from the initial shape of patent claims she eventually had to fight, successfully, to be added to as a named co-inventor. She kept her lab notebooks. She kept correcting the record in scientific venues as opportunities arose, rather than letting a simplified, two-man version of the discovery calcify into the field's accepted history. It did not fully work, for a long time. The clearest evidence of how thoroughly the credit had settled into a pattern that excluded her came in 2021, when the Canada Gairdner International Award — one of biomedical science's most prestigious prizes, and a frequent predictor of eventual Nobel recognition — went to Habener, to Daniel Drucker at the University of Toronto, and to Jens Juul Holst at the University of Copenhagen, for their work on GLP-1 biology and its therapeutic application. It was the third time in four years the same trio had been honored together for essentially the same body of work, following the 2017 Harrington Prize and the 2020 Warren Alpert Foundation Prize. Mojsov's name did not appear on any of the three.

The scientific journal Science published an investigative account of Mojsov's exclusion in 2023, under the headline "Her work paved the way for blockbuster obesity drugs. Now, she's fighting for recognition," followed in September 2024 by a second piece, "Lasker awardee Svetlana Mojsov describes a yearlong journey out of obscurity," after the announcement finally came that would begin to change the record. STAT News covered the same territory in a September 2023 piece headlined, with unusual directness for a science-journalism outlet, "The Ozempic revolution is rooted in the work of Svetlana Mojsov, yet she's been edged out of the story." What these accounts documented, in aggregate, was not a single act of exclusion but a slow accretion of it — awards that quietly settled on a two- or three-person version of events, retrospective essays and prize citations that repeated whichever earlier framing had already been established, media profiles of the "GLP-1 story" that interviewed the same well-credentialed men each time because they were the names that kept turning up in the previous round of citations. Erasure of this kind rarely happens as a single deliberate act. It happens because credit compounds, and once a name has been left off the list a few times, leaving it off again requires no fresh decision at all — it only requires following precedent. A prize committee assembling a shortlist looks, reasonably enough, at who has already been recognized for the underlying work; a journalist assigned to write about the "GLP-1 story" looks for the names that keep turning up in earlier coverage and in earlier prize citations; a textbook author summarizing the field's history cites the papers and reviews that are already considered canonical, most of which were themselves written by, or in close collaboration with, the researchers the field had already decided were the discovery's central figures. Each of these decisions, taken individually, looks like ordinary professional diligence rather than exclusion. Taken together, across three prizes in four years and dozens of retrospective articles and review papers, they produced something that functioned exactly like exclusion regardless of any individual actor's intent — a self-reinforcing citation network that kept referring back to itself, with no single point at which anyone could be said to have made an active decision to leave Mojsov out, and no single point, either, at which correcting the record would have required anything more than one prize committee, or one journalist, deciding to ask a slightly different question about who had actually done the work.

It is worth being precise about what this meant in commercial terms, and not only in terms of scientific reputation, because the two are more entangled in this story than in most. By the time Mojsov's exclusion from the 2021 Gairdner Award became a matter of public record, Novo Nordisk's own account of its GLP-1 program — repeated in investor presentations, in press coverage of the company's extraordinary stock rise, and in profiles of Lotte Bjerre Knudsen's engineering work examined in the next chapter — had already settled into a version of events that began, more or less, with the company's own 1990s research program, treating the foundational 1980s biology as background rather than as the specific, traceable contribution of an identifiable scientist whose name the company's own materials rarely mentioned. None of this reflects any specific bad faith on Novo Nordisk's part; a pharmaceutical company's investor materials are not, and are not expected to be, a comprehensive history of academic science. But it does mean that the version of the GLP-1 origin story that reached the largest audience — through earnings calls, through business journalism covering Novo Nordisk's rise, through the general cultural conversation this book's opening chapter traced through Kimmel's joke and Musk's tweet — was, for years, a version with a specific and traceable gap in it, and that gap happened to be exactly the same shape as the gap in the prize citations.

The correction, when it came, arrived in stages rather than all at once, which is itself worth sitting with rather than smoothing into a tidier redemption narrative. Progress on a story like this one rarely moves in a straight line even after the underlying facts have become public; institutions that award prizes tend to move cautiously, wary of appearing to overcorrect, wary of the awkwardness of revisiting a citation they themselves issued only a few years earlier, and often simply slow, in the ordinary bureaucratic sense, to update a committee's working assumptions about who belongs on a shortlist. The gap between the Science and STAT investigations surfacing the exclusion in 2023 and the Lasker Foundation's own announcement the following year is short by the standards of institutional change generally, and still long enough that a great many of the readers who first encountered Mojsov's name through that reporting had already, by the time the Lasker announcement arrived, spent the better part of a year wondering whether anything concrete would actually follow the journalism. On September 19, 2024, the Lasker Foundation announced that the Lasker~DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award — one of American medicine's most prestigious honors, sometimes called "America's Nobels" for its record of predicting the actual Nobel Prize — would go to Svetlana Mojsov, Joel Habener, and Lotte Bjerre Knudsen, the Novo Nordisk chief scientific advisor whose engineering work had turned GLP-1 biology into an actual drug. Daniel Drucker, notably, was not on that particular citation; the award recognized a different, overlapping configuration of the people responsible for the science and its translation into medicine. Rockefeller University's own account of the award, published on its news site, described the arc plainly in its headline: "Her scientific breakthrough took 5 years. Getting credit took decades." The Lasker recognition was followed by a fuller convergence: the 2024 Princess of Asturias Award for Scientific and Technical Research went to a five-person group — Drucker, Jeffrey Friedman, the discoverer of leptin, Habener, Holst, and Mojsov together — the first major prize to fold all of them into a single citation. In April 2026, the King Faisal Prize in Medicine named Mojsov among its laureates as well.

It is tempting, looking backward from a moment when Mojsov's name finally appears reliably alongside the men she worked among, to treat the story as a triumphant if belated correction — science getting it right eventually, the arc bending, in Martin Luther King's phrase, toward justice, however slowly. That framing is not wrong, exactly, but it understates what the delay actually cost, and to whom. For roughly three decades, the scientific establishment's answer to the question "who discovered the mechanism behind what would become the most commercially significant drug class of the early twenty-first century" was incomplete in a specific, patterned way: it kept naming men working at well-resourced institutions and repeatedly omitting the woman whose synthesis work had identified the specific active molecule. That pattern is not unique to GLP-1 research. It echoes Rosalyn Yalow's own long fight for recognition a generation earlier — the same Yalow who trained John Eng at the Bronx VA, and who had to build an unusual career at a VA hospital partly because more prestigious institutions offered a research physicist with her background fewer paths forward than her work deserved; Yalow did eventually win the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, but only after her male collaborator on the radioimmunoassay work, Solomon Berson, had died, leaving her the sole credited survivor of a partnership the field had, for years, treated as more equal in name than in actual recognition. It echoes Jocelyn Bell Burnell's exclusion from the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery of pulsars — a prize that instead went to her graduate supervisor, Antony Hewish, and the Cambridge radio astronomer Martin Ryle, for work built substantially on data Bell Burnell herself had gathered and on a signal she herself had first identified as anomalous rather than dismissed as noise. Mojsov's story sits inside that same long, well-documented pattern, one researchers who study gender and scientific credit have given its own name — the Matilda effect, after the nineteenth-century suffragist Matilda Joslyn Gage, who first wrote about the systematic tendency to attribute women's scientific contributions to male colleagues — in which women who do foundational bench science are more likely than their male colleagues to see that work folded into someone else's narrative, particularly when the someone else already occupies a more senior or more institutionally legible position, and particularly when the discovery in question does not become obviously, retrospectively important until years or decades after the credit has already settled.

The eventual convergence of credit, once it began, moved with a speed that makes the preceding decades look even starker by comparison. Within roughly nineteen months of the 2024 Lasker announcement, Mojsov had been named to the five-person Princess of Asturias Award alongside Drucker, Habener, Holst, and leptin's discoverer Jeffrey Friedman, and had received the King Faisal Prize in Medicine as well — a compressed run of recognition that, had it been distributed across the same three-decade span the exclusion itself spanned, would have looked like an entirely ordinary, unremarkable scientific career being honored roughly on schedule. That it instead arrived all at once, in the final years of a career that had already run most of its course, is itself part of what this book means by the cost of the delay: recognition deferred long enough stops functioning as recognition in the ordinary sense, arriving too late to shape the opportunities, the funding, and the institutional standing a scientist's reputation is supposed to build across an active career, and arriving instead as something closer to a correction to the historical record — valuable, and hard-won, but no longer capable of doing the work earlier, contemporaneous credit would have done.

Mojsov herself, by the accounts published after her recognition finally arrived, did not describe the delay as an abstraction. She had kept correcting the scientific record for years with what both Science profiles describe as a kind of patient, documented persistence — not public complaint so much as insistence, repeated in the venues where scientific credit is actually adjudicated, that the record be accurate. That distinction between complaint and insistence matters, because it describes a mode of self-advocacy considerably harder for an outside observer to notice or reward than a public grievance would have been. A scientist who writes a pointed letter to a journal editor, or corrects a misattributed citation in her own subsequent papers, or declines to let a retrospective review article stand uncorrected, is doing work that almost never generates a headline of its own; it generates, at best, a quiet footnote correction three or four steps removed from any audience beyond a handful of specialists already reading the primary literature closely enough to notice the change. Mojsov did that work for decades, in a research culture that had little formal mechanism for rewarding it and, if the pattern of prizes through 2021 is any guide, little apparent inclination to notice it either. It took an outside institution — first Science, then STAT, working as journalists rather than as scientists, applying the different standards of evidence and narrative construction that investigative reporting rather than laboratory science runs on — to translate four decades of quiet, internal correction into a story visible enough that a prize committee finally could not credibly leave her name off the list a fourth time. That insistence is, in its own way, a second scientific contribution layered on top of the first: a demonstration, available to any young researcher paying attention, that getting the underlying biology right and getting the credit right are two separate battles, and that neither one is guaranteed to happen just because the other did. She remains a research affiliate at Rockefeller University, in the same institution where the discovery began, and the molecule she identified in the 1980s is now injected weekly by tens of millions of people who have, for the most part, never heard her name — a gap this book means, in its own small way, to close.

The broader lesson the Mojsov story offers this book is not really about individual credit at all, though the individual injustice deserves to be named plainly rather than folded into a footnote. It is a caution about how origin stories get simplified as they travel from laboratory to boardroom to marketing copy to magazine profile, each retelling shedding a little more of the actual complexity and a little more of the actual cast. Novo Nordisk's own public account of its GLP-1 program, and much of the press coverage that followed its commercial success, tends to compress four decades of overlapping, sometimes uncredited work by dozens of scientists into a cleaner story about corporate vision and one or two named discoverers. That compression is not unique to this drug class, and it is not necessarily malicious; it is simply how institutions and journalism both tend to tell stories, because a cleaner story travels farther. But a book that means to take the science of these drugs seriously owes its subject the fuller, messier version — the guinea pig assay that almost didn't get followed up, the fellowship reassigned after a different project fell through, the chemist who had to fight for four decades to have her name attached to the molecule she was the first to isolate. It is worth asking, before leaving Mojsov's story for Copenhagen's, what a different outcome might have looked like — not as an exercise in idle counterfactual speculation, but because the answer clarifies what was actually at stake in getting the credit right the first time. Scientific credit is not merely a matter of personal recognition, however much personal recognition rightly matters on its own terms; it is also one of the primary mechanisms by which funding agencies, universities, and pharmaceutical companies decide whose future research to back, whose laboratory to staff and equip, and whose name belongs on the next grant application or the next corporate advisory board. A scientist correctly credited early in her career accumulates resources, visibility, and institutional standing that compound over decades, the same way Drucker's own credited standing let him build a laboratory that trained the next generation of incretin researchers and secure a named, endowed chair from the very company whose flagship drug his work had helped make possible. A scientist whose comparable contribution goes uncredited for decades does not merely miss out on a prize ceremony; she plausibly misses out on the accumulated institutional weight that prize recognition typically confers along the way — the grants that go more easily to a recognized name, the collaborations offered more readily to a scientist other researchers already know to credit, the students drawn to a lab whose founder's contribution to a landmark discovery is common knowledge rather than still being litigated in the pages of Science. What Copenhagen did with the molecule Mojsov, Habener, Drucker, and Holst had identified and characterized — how a mid-sized Danish insulin manufacturer turned a laboratory finding into the fastest-growing drug franchise in pharmaceutical history — is where the story goes next, carried forward by an engineer whose own name would spend far less time waiting for the credit her work earned.
