# The Forty-Four-Hour Week

When the Fair Labor Standards Act took effect on October 24, 1938, its national minimum wage was twenty-five cents an hour and its initial maximum workweek was forty-four hours for covered workers.

Those numbers are small enough to sound primitive and familiar enough to sound inevitable.

Neither impression is useful.

The law was enacted after years of depression, strikes, court fights, failed legislation, constitutional uncertainty, and political conflict over whether the federal government had any legitimate business setting minimum conditions inside private employment. Franklin Roosevelt signed it on June 25, 1938. The statute restricted oppressive child labor, established wage and hour requirements, and created an enforcement role for the federal government.

It initially covered only about one-fifth of the labor force.

That limit is the key to understanding the achievement honestly.

The law did not create a universal floor under American work. Agricultural workers and many domestic workers were excluded, categories that contained large numbers of Black workers, especially in the South. Other occupations and enterprises fell outside coverage. The original wage was low. Maximum-hours rules worked through overtime requirements and coverage definitions that would be amended repeatedly.

The floor was partial.

It was still a floor.

Before federal wage-and-hour law became ordinary, employment conditions were largely determined through individual bargaining, collective bargaining, state law, custom, labor scarcity, employer policy, and the unequal power people brought to those arrangements. States had experimented with minimum-wage laws, often focused on women and minors, but courts sometimes struck them down under constitutional doctrines protecting freedom of contract.

The Depression changed the political environment without resolving the constitutional problem overnight.

Mass unemployment weakened workers' bargaining power. Employers facing collapsed demand cut wages and hours. The New Deal attempted multiple forms of industrial regulation. The National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 supported industry codes covering wages, hours, and competition, but the Supreme Court invalidated the law in 1935.

The federal government had to find a more durable route.

The Fair Labor Standards Act used federal authority over interstate commerce and related production to establish standards for covered workers. By 1938 the Supreme Court's constitutional approach to economic regulation had shifted, creating more room for federal and state labor rules.

A workplace rule had become part of national market regulation.

That conceptual move is under-appreciated.

A minimum wage is often debated as though it concerns only the transaction between one employer and one employee. Federal law treated labor standards as a feature of competition among firms across state lines. If one factory paid extremely low wages or used oppressive child labor, reformers argued, it could undercut employers operating under higher standards elsewhere. The market could reward the jurisdiction with the weakest floor.

National rules changed the terms of competition.

This resembles food regulation three decades earlier. A national market can make state-by-state standards porous. Goods cross borders. Employers compete across jurisdictions. Federal law enters not simply because Washington prefers uniformity but because the activity being regulated has become economically integrated.

Labor itself may be local while the product is national.

The forty-four-hour week also reveals how strange modern time rules once were.

Industrial capitalism had made clock time central to work. Factories coordinated workers around shifts. Railroads standardized time zones. Offices, mines, mills, stores, and transport systems organized labor in measurable blocks. Once hours could be precisely scheduled and recorded, the question of how many hours constituted an ordinary week became politically contestable.

A forty-hour week is not a fact of nature.

It is an institution built from law, bargaining, productivity, culture, and economic development.

The FLSA stepped the maximum down from forty-four hours to forty over its early years for covered workers. Overtime premiums above the threshold encouraged employers either to pay more for long hours or distribute work differently. The law therefore influenced not just wages but the price of time.

Time outside work acquired a regulatory value.

Labor unions had fought for shorter hours long before the New Deal. The eight-hour day was one of the nineteenth-century labor movement's most durable demands. Strikes and campaigns made hours a political issue. The Haymarket affair of 1886 occurred in the context of the eight-hour movement. Industries adopted shorter schedules unevenly through bargaining and employer policy.

The federal statute did not invent the aspiration.

It converted decades of labor conflict into a national baseline for covered work.

That distinction matters because reform is often credited to presidents who sign laws rather than to movements that make the laws politically imaginable. Roosevelt and Labor Secretary Frances Perkins were central. So were union organizers, workers, progressive reformers, state experiments, economists, legislators, and generations of people who had already fought over child labor and hours.

Institutions have prehistories.

Perkins's role is especially revealing. As secretary of labor, she had spent years working on workplace safety and labor reform before joining Roosevelt's cabinet. The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire of 1911 had deeply influenced her career. By 1938 she was one of the most experienced labor-policy figures in the administration.

The law therefore did not emerge from sudden presidential compassion during the Depression.

It came from accumulated administrative knowledge meeting a political opening.

The child-labor provisions carried their own long history. Reformers had campaigned against children working in mines, mills, factories, and other dangerous settings. Congress had passed earlier federal child-labor laws that the Supreme Court invalidated. A proposed constitutional amendment giving Congress explicit child-labor power failed to achieve ratification.

The FLSA finally established durable federal restrictions through the commerce power.

Again, coverage mattered. Agricultural work and family employment retained different rules. Child labor did not disappear. But the national government acquired a lasting enforcement mechanism against categories of oppressive child labor in interstate commerce.

A reform that had repeatedly failed became administrative routine.

That routine changed childhood itself.

School attendance laws, rising incomes, changing family economics, urbanization, technological change, and state regulation all contributed to the decline of child labor. The FLSA was not the single cause. Yet federal restrictions reinforced a social expectation that a child's ordinary place was school rather than industrial production.

Expectations can become so strong that later violations feel like aberrations rather than standard business practice.

That is institutional success operating through culture.

The exclusions show the darker side of how those expectations were distributed.

Southern lawmakers had strong incentives to preserve low-wage agricultural and domestic labor systems that disproportionately employed Black workers. The New Deal coalition depended on powerful segregationist Democrats in Congress. Major labor legislation therefore often excluded or treated differently occupations concentrated among Black workers and women.

The FLSA's original architecture reflected those compromises.

It is tempting to describe exclusion as an unfortunate gap around an otherwise universal reform. More accurately, exclusion was part of what made passage politically possible.

That distinction matters morally and causally.

A law can raise standards for millions while preserving a low-wage labor order for others. Those groups then begin the next round of political struggle from a lower statutory baseline.

Later amendments expanded coverage. The minimum wage rose. Equal-pay provisions, anti-discrimination laws, occupational safety rules, family leave, and other labor protections accumulated through separate statutes. Some domestic and agricultural workers eventually gained more protections, though gaps remain.

The labor floor became a layered floor.

This layering makes the original 1938 event hard to see. Modern employees encounter a bundle of rules—minimum wage, overtime, payroll taxes, safety requirements, anti-discrimination protections, unemployment insurance, workers' compensation under state law, benefit rules, leave policies, and local standards. The bundle can feel like one system.

Historically, it was assembled piece by piece.

The FLSA created one of the foundational layers.

Its economic effects have been argued over ever since. A minimum wage raises earnings for workers whose wages are pushed upward if employment and hours do not fall enough to offset the increase. Employers may respond through prices, productivity, reduced hiring, automation, benefit changes, scheduling, profit margins, or business closure. Effects vary with the level of the wage floor, market conditions, employer concentration, worker mobility, and other factors.

The law's existence did not settle the economic debate.

It moved the debate from whether there should be any federal floor to where the floor should be set and who should be covered.

That is a major shift in political baseline.

Even many politicians who argue fiercely over the federal minimum wage do not propose restoring the pre-1938 legal environment wholesale. Overtime rules and child-labor restrictions are deeply embedded in employment practice. The argument occurs inside the institution the statute created.

Durable reforms often win by becoming the terrain on which opponents fight.

The law also changed firm administration.

To comply with wage and hour rules, employers needed records. Timekeeping, payroll classification, exemption status, and compensation systems became legally consequential. The distinction between an employee and an independent contractor, between exempt and nonexempt work, and between ordinary and overtime hours can determine substantial liability.

A labor standard creates an information standard.

Workers' time must become legible enough to regulate.

That administrative demand has grown more complex with modern work. Salaried professionals, gig workers, remote employees, app-based contractors, tipped workers, home-care workers, and multi-state employers do not fit neatly into a 1938 factory model. Courts, agencies, Congress, states, and businesses continue arguing over definitions.

Old laws live longest at their boundaries.

The FLSA's survival does not mean its categories are timeless. It means the underlying question remains: what minimum conditions should attach to employment even when an individual worker agrees to something less?

That is a philosophical challenge to pure freedom of contract.

If two adults voluntarily agree to a very low wage for very long hours, why should government intervene? Reformers answered that labor markets do not always contain equal bargaining power and that competition can push conditions below socially acceptable minimums. Opponents warned that mandatory standards could destroy jobs, interfere with liberty, and substitute political judgment for market arrangements.

Both arguments have modern descendants.

The law institutionalized one side without eliminating the other.

The Great Depression gave the question unusual urgency because unemployment made individual bargaining weakness visible. A desperate worker's consent may be genuine and still occur under conditions where refusing the job is economically impossible.

Freedom can be formally equal and materially unequal.

The FLSA was one federal attempt to define how much inequality of bargaining power the law should tolerate.

Its passage also fit Roosevelt's broader effort to restore purchasing power and stabilize mass demand. Higher wages and shorter hours were not viewed only as worker welfare. New Dealers often linked labor standards to macroeconomic recovery, arguing that workers were consumers whose income supported demand.

This economic theory was contested and cannot be credited as the single rationale for the act.

It did make labor policy part of economic policy.

The law therefore helped normalize another twentieth-century idea: government can shape the terms of employment not only to correct abuse but to influence how prosperity is distributed.

That idea would become central to the politics of the postwar middle class.

Yet the postwar labor settlement rested on far more than federal wage law. Union density, industrial productivity, corporate structure, education, veterans' benefits, housing policy, tax policy, racial exclusion, suburbanization, and global economic conditions all mattered. The FLSA should not be turned into the secret source of mid-century prosperity.

It set a floor, not the ceiling.

The counterfactual demonstrates why. Without the federal act, strong unions and prosperous industries could still have achieved high wages and shorter hours for many workers. States could have raised standards. Large employers might have adopted forty-hour schedules for efficiency and worker retention. Child labor was already declining.

But low-wage workers in weakly organized sectors would have had far less leverage against employers in states unwilling to regulate.

National floors matter most where local bargaining is weakest.

The statute also created a federal enforcement problem that is easy to underestimate after compliance becomes routine.

A right to minimum pay is only as real as the ability to establish what happened during the workday. The government therefore needed investigators, records, definitions, complaint procedures, and remedies. Employers needed to know which workers were covered and what counted as compensable time. Workers needed a way to show that a violation had occurred.

This transformed payroll from bookkeeping into evidence.

A clock-in record could become proof of unpaid overtime. A job title could matter less than actual duties. A manager's instruction to finish a task after clocking out could become a legal event. Whether travel time, waiting time, training, preparation, or on-call periods counted as work could determine wages owed.

The law put legal pressure on the margins of the workday.

That pressure mattered because exploitation often hides in small units.

Ten unpaid minutes before a shift does not look like a national economic crisis. Multiply it across five days, fifty weeks, thousands of workers, and years of employment and the amount becomes meaningful. Wage theft rarely needs a spectacular act. It can operate through rounding, off-the-clock work, misclassification, illegal deductions, or a worker's belief that complaining will cost the job.

A national floor without enforcement would invite precisely that kind of quiet erosion.

The FLSA therefore helped create a recurring American pattern: statutory rights followed by an administrative struggle over whether the rights reach the people least able to enforce them privately.

That struggle is especially visible in the occupations originally left outside the bargain.

Agricultural and domestic work were not random omissions. They were labor systems embedded in race, region, gender, and household power. Excluding them allowed a federal labor floor to coexist with economic arrangements inherited from slavery, segregation, and the undervaluation of women's work.

Later expansion of coverage reduced some of the gaps without erasing the legacy.

Farm labor still operates under different overtime and child-labor rules in federal law in important respects. Domestic work has moved in and out of regulatory attention through amendments, agency rules, litigation, and state law. Tipped work created another distinctive wage structure in which employers can, under specified conditions, count part of tips toward minimum-wage obligations.

A floor can contain trapdoors.

That is not an argument that the floor is meaningless.

It is an argument that the politics of coverage are part of the policy, not a technical afterthought.

Every exemption answers a distributive question: whose work is normal enough to deserve the baseline, and whose work will be treated as special?

The answer changes bargaining power long after the congressional debate is forgotten.

The forty-hour norm also escaped the statute's exact legal boundaries and became cultural infrastructure. Employers outside a particular overtime rule still organize schedules around familiar ideas of full-time work. Benefits eligibility, office hours, school schedules, commuting patterns, and household expectations grew around a society in which forty hours became a reference point.

The reference point can be violated constantly and still remain powerful.

Many salaried employees work longer. Multiple-job holders work more. Part-time workers may want more hours. Gig workers may have no fixed week at all. Professionals check messages at night. Remote work blurs the boundary between presence and availability.

Yet “forty hours” remains the unit against which excess is perceived.

That is how law can influence culture without dictating every outcome.

It gives society a number around which expectations organize.

The 1930s offer almost impossible competition for an under-appreciated selection. The Social Security Act, Wagner Act, banking reforms, SEC, rural electrification, FHA, and many other New Deal institutions transformed the country. The Depression itself dwarfs any one statute.

The choice of FLSA is not a claim that it outranks Social Security.

It is a claim about intimacy.

Millions of Americans encounter the legacy of 1938 every time a payroll system distinguishes regular from overtime hours, every time a teenager's work is subject to age restrictions, every time a wage floor determines whether an offer is legal, every time a dispute turns on whether someone is a covered employee, and every time a few invisible minutes at the edge of a shift acquire a dollar value.

The law lives inside the week.

That is easy to under-appreciate because weeks repeat.

Before repetition, there was a fight over whether the federal government could put a number on them at all.
