# Citizens Without a State

On March 2, 1917, Congress made most people born in Puerto Rico citizens of the United States without making Puerto Rico a state.

That combination has shaped American citizenship ever since.

The Jones-Shafroth Act reorganized Puerto Rico's government, created an elected bicameral legislature, included a bill of rights, and granted statutory U.S. citizenship to Puerto Ricans. It also preserved decisive forms of federal control. The governor was appointed by the president. Congress retained authority over the territory. Puerto Rico did not gain voting representation in Congress or a vote for president.

Citizenship expanded.

Political equality did not expand with it.

The gap is the reason the 1917 law deserves more attention than it receives. Americans tend to imagine citizenship as a package. A citizen belongs to the nation, votes for national representatives, participates in presidential elections, receives constitutional protections, and stands in the same relation to the federal government as other citizens.

Puerto Rico exposes how historically contingent that package is.

The island had come under U.S. control in 1898 after the Spanish-American War. Spain ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines under the Treaty of Paris. Congress then had to decide what American rule meant for people living in acquired territories.

The old continental expectation—territory eventually becomes state—no longer fit comfortably.

The Northwest Ordinance and nineteenth-century expansion had created a familiar, if never universal, sequence in which organized territories could move toward statehood as populations grew and political conditions were met. Overseas possessions raised another possibility: the United States could exercise sovereignty without promising eventual equality within the federal union.

The Supreme Court helped create a doctrine for that possibility in the Insular Cases beginning in 1901.

Those decisions are complicated and remain controversial, but their broad effect was to distinguish between incorporated territories destined for full constitutional integration and unincorporated territories where not every constitutional provision automatically applied in the same way. Puerto Rico became the leading example of a place “belonging to” the United States without being fully incorporated into it.

That phrase contains an empire in grammar.

The 1917 citizenship grant did not dissolve the ambiguity.

It institutionalized a new form of belonging inside it.

Puerto Ricans became U.S. citizens by statute. They could move to the mainland without being treated as foreign immigrants. Those who resided in states could participate in state and federal elections under the same residency rules as other citizens. Migration between the island and mainland would become a major feature of twentieth-century Puerto Rican life, especially with growing communities in New York and other cities.

But a citizen living in San Juan remained outside presidential voting and voting congressional representation.

The difference was geographic, not ancestral.

Move from Puerto Rico to New York and national electoral power changes. Move back and it changes again, even though citizenship does not.

That is an unusual constitutional arrangement.

The timing of the Jones Act gave the citizenship question another immediate consequence. One month after the law passed, the United States entered World War I. Congress enacted the Selective Service Act in May. Puerto Rican men, now U.S. citizens, were subject to military service. Tens of thousands registered, and many served.

This juxtaposition has encouraged a cynical claim that citizenship was granted simply to draft Puerto Ricans into the war. The chronology makes the suspicion understandable, but the legislative history is more complex. Citizenship proposals had been debated for years before 1917, and the law contained a broader reorganization of island government. It would be too simple to reduce the act to manpower policy.

The war nevertheless made the meaning of citizenship immediate.

A government could require military service from citizens who could not vote for the president serving as commander in chief or elect voting members of the Congress declaring war and funding the military.

Obligation and representation were separated.

That separation reaches into the present.

Puerto Ricans have served in every major American war since. The island has produced generals, soldiers, sailors, airmen, federal officials, scientists, artists, athletes, and millions of citizens whose lives cross between island and mainland. Yet the territory's political status remains unresolved more than a century after citizenship was granted.

Statehood, independence, free association, and variations on territorial commonwealth status have all had advocates. Puerto Rico has held multiple status plebiscites with results shaped by turnout, ballot design, party strategy, and the available options. Congress, which has ultimate constitutional authority over territorial status, has not enacted a final change.

A supposedly transitional condition became durable.

That durability is one reason the Jones-Shafroth Act is under-appreciated. The law is often taught, when it is taught at all, as a milestone in Puerto Rican history rather than as a major event in the history of American citizenship.

It belongs to both.

The United States has repeatedly defined citizenship through conflict: the Fourteenth Amendment after slavery and Civil War, naturalization law, racial exclusions, Native citizenship, immigration, territorial acquisition, and court decisions. The Puerto Rican case shows that citizenship itself does not answer the question of how much political power a citizen possesses.

Residence and jurisdiction can matter just as much.

The act's name also creates confusion because Americans encounter several “Jones Acts.” The Jones-Shafroth Act of 1917 concerns Puerto Rico. The Merchant Marine Act of 1920, also commonly called the Jones Act, regulates domestic maritime shipping. The Philippine Autonomy Act of 1916 is another Jones Act. The overlapping names make the Puerto Rican statute easier to lose in general memory.

The consequences are less easy to lose.

The law created an elected Puerto Rican Senate and House of Representatives, replacing the earlier structure under the Foraker Act. That expansion of local self-government mattered. Puerto Rican politicians gained institutions through which parties, public policy, and island governance developed.

Yet the governor remained presidentially appointed until 1948, when Puerto Ricans first elected their own governor.

Even local democracy arrived in layers.

The federal relationship also shaped economic policy. Puerto Rico's currency, trade relations, tariffs, federal tax treatment, shipping rules, and access to U.S. programs became functions of territorial status. American firms invested on the island. Migration responded to labor markets and policy. Federal law could transform Puerto Rican economic options without Puerto Rico possessing voting power equal to a state in making that law.

Territorial status is not merely a question of flags and elections.

It is an economic constitution.

The island's relationship to federal taxes and benefits has varied by program and statute. Residents generally do not pay federal income tax on income sourced within Puerto Rico under ordinary circumstances, though they pay payroll taxes and other federal taxes, and Puerto Rican workers participate in programs such as Social Security and Medicare under federal rules. Other benefits have historically been capped or structured differently from those in states.

These arrangements are complicated enough to become politically invisible on the mainland.

Complexity protects neglected systems from public attention.

A citizen in a state can easily assume citizenship works everywhere the way it works locally. The Jones-Shafroth Act is a reminder that Congress has built multiple versions of American membership.

The law also had a cultural consequence through mobility.

Statutory citizenship and the absence of immigration barriers made movement to the mainland legally straightforward, though affordability and transport still mattered. Air travel later made movement much easier. Puerto Rican migration to New York accelerated in the mid-twentieth century, reshaping neighborhoods, music, politics, labor, literature, and urban culture.

The diaspora did not sever island ties. Families developed lives across jurisdictions.

A political status created a social geography.

Again, the law did not cause all migration. Economic conditions on the island, industrialization policy, agricultural change, labor demand in mainland cities, military service, transportation costs, and family networks all influenced movement. The Great Migration of African Americans, Caribbean migration, European immigration restrictions, and broader urban change also shaped New York and other cities.

The 1917 act provided a legal channel through which those other forces could operate without immigration quotas.

That matters especially when compared with the 1924 immigration law in the next chapter. The United States was simultaneously building exclusionary national-origin barriers against many foreign immigrants and maintaining unrestricted internal movement for Puerto Rican citizens.

The distinction between foreigner and territorial citizen became demographically consequential.

The act also complicates the narrative of American imperial retreat.

The United States eventually recognized Philippine independence after a long and violent colonial relationship. Cuba became formally independent under conditions of substantial U.S. influence. Puerto Rico did not move toward sovereignty in the same way. Citizenship created deeper legal attachment to the United States while leaving the island outside statehood.

Citizenship can integrate without incorporating.

That is not a contradiction in the narrow legal sense. Congress can define statutory citizenship and territorial governance separately. It is a contradiction in the democratic imagination because Americans are taught to associate citizenship with consent to national government.

Puerto Rico asks consent through incomplete institutions.

Residents vote for local officials and a resident commissioner who serves in Congress without a final vote on the House floor. They participate in party presidential primaries under party rules but do not receive Electoral College votes in the general election. They can move to a state and acquire those electoral rights through residency.

The island's citizens are inside the political nation and outside part of its electoral machinery at the same time.

No chapter can resolve the normative argument over Puerto Rico's status, and this one should not pretend to. Statehood supporters emphasize equality of citizenship, representation, and durable union. Independence supporters emphasize sovereignty, national identity, and the colonial nature of congressional plenary power. Advocates of free association or modified commonwealth arrangements seek other balances, though constitutional feasibility and congressional willingness are disputed.

The important historical point is that 1917 did not settle the choice.

It made one settlement durable enough to last.

The word statutory matters. The Fourteenth Amendment grants citizenship to people born or naturalized “in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof.” The application of constitutional birthright citizenship to unincorporated territories has been contested. Puerto Rican citizenship has long rested on federal statute, and people born in Puerto Rico today are U.S. citizens under federal law.

That legal history further distinguishes territorial citizenship from the simplified civics-book model.

Congress created the status and therefore demonstrated the national legislature's enormous power over membership.

This was not the only citizenship expansion of the early twentieth century. Congress would grant U.S. citizenship broadly to Native Americans in 1924 through the Indian Citizenship Act, though voting rights remained subject to state barriers for years. Women would gain constitutional protection for suffrage in 1920, but citizenship had never guaranteed women the vote before then. Black citizenship had been constitutionalized in 1868, yet Jim Crow states systematically denied Black citizens effective voting rights.

The Puerto Rican case belongs to a broader American truth: citizenship and political power have often traveled on separate schedules.

The 1917 event makes the separation unusually geographic and durable.

There is another reason it matters in the history of federalism. States enter the Union on an “equal footing” in constitutional doctrine. Territories are not states and sit under congressional authority far broader than ordinary federal power within states. By keeping Puerto Rico territorial, Congress preserved a category of governance that sits outside the standard federal-state symmetry.

This category later shaped debates over disaster relief, fiscal crisis, bankruptcy-like restructuring, federal benefits, and public debt.

When Puerto Rico confronted a severe debt crisis in the 2010s, Congress enacted the PROMESA law creating a federal oversight board with significant authority over fiscal policy. The episode made territorial power visible again. A state facing fiscal crisis would stand in a different constitutional relationship to Congress.

The century-old status still had teeth.

Hurricanes likewise revealed how political status affects public attention and federal response. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, questions about recovery, infrastructure, mortality, and federal aid became national controversies. Mainland Americans were periodically reminded that the island's residents were U.S. citizens.

The need for repeated reminders is itself evidence of under-appreciation.

If national citizenship were understood as a lived territorial system rather than a passport category, Puerto Rico would occupy a larger place in American history.

The Jones-Shafroth Act made that system possible in its modern form.

The law should not be treated simply as a gift of citizenship. Many Puerto Rican political leaders had complex views. Some welcomed citizenship; some opposed it or preferred independence; some accepted citizenship while seeking greater self-government. The statute was passed by a Congress in which Puerto Ricans had no voting representation.

A status conferred without equal legislative power can be both valuable and paternalistic.

Millions of Puerto Ricans have embraced U.S. citizenship as part of identity and rights. That lived reality is no less true because the original arrangement was colonial. Historical origin does not dictate present preference.

Again, two truths survive together.

The arrangement also changes the meaning of federal accountability. In a state, a citizen dissatisfied with a federal policy can at least participate in choosing senators, a voting House member, and presidential electors. Those mechanisms are imperfect, and a voter may lose every contest, but the constitutional chain is legible: representation is part of the bargain. Puerto Rico lives under federal statutes without that full chain.

That does not mean the island is politically voiceless. Puerto Rican officials lobby Congress and federal agencies. The resident commissioner can introduce legislation, serve on committees, build coalitions, and vote in committee under House rules. Island parties cultivate relationships with national parties. Governors can bargain with administrations. Diaspora voters in states can make Puerto Rican issues matter in elections. Power finds channels even where the formal architecture is incomplete.

But those channels are not substitutes for equal voting authority. Lobbying depends on persuasion. Committee participation stops short of a final House vote. Diaspora influence is exercised by citizens after they establish residence in states. The island itself cannot send two senators or electoral votes to Washington.

This distinction matters because territorial status can make national policy feel both intimate and remote. Federal decisions reach households, hospitals, ports, utilities, schools, veterans, businesses, and municipal budgets. Yet the officials making those decisions do not face the island's electorate in the same way a senator from Florida or Ohio does. The consequence is not that federal policy toward Puerto Rico is always hostile or inattentive. It is that one ordinary democratic feedback mechanism is structurally weaker.

The weakness becomes easiest to see when policy turns technical. A dramatic status vote can attract national attention. A change in reimbursement formula, shipping rule, tax treatment, disaster appropriation, debt authority, or program eligibility is harder to explain and easier for mainland politics to ignore. Territorial governance is often exercised through precisely those details. The architecture of belonging is hidden in tables, definitions, waivers, and statutory exceptions.

That is one reason the 1917 settlement endured. Large constitutional questions do not need to be answered every morning if thousands of smaller administrative decisions can keep the existing relationship functioning. Institutions become durable partly because people learn how to live inside them. Businesses adapt contracts. Families make migration choices. Agencies develop territorial rules. Courts accumulate doctrine. Political parties build strategies around the available options. Each adaptation can make the status quo easier to operate without making the underlying question more settled.

Durability, in other words, should not be confused with consent.

Nor should it be confused with stasis. Puerto Rico's relationship with the federal government has changed repeatedly since 1917. The island began electing its governor, adopted its own constitution in 1952 under congressionally authorized procedures, developed the modern Commonwealth structure, and repeatedly debated alternatives. Congress changed tax and program rules. Courts continued to define territorial power. The political argument moved even when the basic fact of nonstate U.S. territory remained.

That combination—change inside continuity—is historically important. It shows how constitutional systems can absorb pressure without resolving the dispute producing it. A reform can widen local democracy, another can alter economic authority, another can change federal benefits, and yet the core question of equal national representation can remain open.

The Jones-Shafroth Act therefore matters not only because of what it granted. It matters because of the institutional form it helped stabilize: citizenship without statehood, mobility without equal territorial representation, national obligation without a complete national vote. Those pairings are easy to describe as anomalies. After more than a century, anomaly is too weak a word.

And the arrangement complicates the usual map of federalism. American civics is often drawn as a contest between Washington and fifty states, with municipalities nested below them. Territories sit outside that diagram even though Congress governs millions of citizens through territorial clauses, program statutes, courts, agencies, and appropriations. Puerto Rico is therefore not a peripheral exception to federalism. It is evidence that the federal system has always contained categories that the standard two-level picture leaves out. Once that is visible, debates over representation, fiscal authority, disaster response, and citizenship look less like isolated Puerto Rican controversies and more like questions about the boundaries of the Union itself.

They are part of the American system.

The 1910s offer other enormous candidates for an under-appreciated event: the Federal Reserve Act, federal income tax, direct election of senators, the Great Migration, American entry into World War I, the first Red Scare, women's wartime labor expansion. Choosing the Jones Act over the Federal Reserve especially invites argument.

The Federal Reserve is certainly more central to national economic power.

The case for Jones-Shafroth rests instead on the book's purpose: to find a consequence that Americans live inside while rarely placing it near the center of the national story.

More than three million U.S. citizens can reside under a territorial status that denies them voting representation in the national institutions governing them. Millions more Puerto Ricans live in the states, part of a diaspora whose mobility was shaped by that citizenship.

That arrangement began in a durable form in 1917.

A country that prides itself on government by citizens created citizens who belonged to the country without their territory belonging to the constitutional structure of a state.

The law expanded the nation.

It left the question of the Union open.
