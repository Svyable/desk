# The Islands That Changed the Map

On July 7, 1898, President William McKinley signed the Newlands Resolution, and the United States claimed Hawai‘i through a joint resolution of Congress.

The method is part of the story.

An annexation treaty had been submitted to the Senate. A treaty required a two-thirds vote for ratification. The votes were not there. Native Hawaiian opposition was organized and visible. In 1897, delegates associated with Hawaiian anti-annexation groups carried petitions containing more than twenty thousand signatures to Washington. The petitions helped demonstrate that annexation could not honestly be described as the uncomplicated desire of the islands' people.

Then war changed the calculation.

The United States went to war with Spain in April 1898. American forces moved across the Pacific toward the Philippines. Hawai‘i's location, already important to American commercial and naval thinking, became more immediately strategic. Pearl Harbor rights had earlier been secured through an agreement with the Hawaiian Kingdom. Now the islands' usefulness as a coaling, supply, communications, and military point between North America and Asia became harder for expansionists to treat as hypothetical.

Congress passed a joint resolution instead of a treaty.

Hawai‘i became American territory in fact before becoming the organized Territory of Hawaii in 1900 and, much later, the fiftieth state in 1959.

The 1898 annexation is not obscure. Many Americans know Hawai‘i was annexed and know Pearl Harbor. What is under-appreciated is how much that act changed the geographic and constitutional imagination of the United States.

A republic that had expanded across contiguous North America now held a strategically vital archipelago deep in the Pacific. The map of American power no longer ended at the continental shore.

The annexation cannot be understood as one day's expansion. Its immediate history runs through the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy in 1893.

Queen Liliʻuokalani had sought a new constitution that would restore royal authority and political rights eroded under the 1887 constitution commonly known as the Bayonet Constitution. A group of businessmen and residents, many with American ties, organized against her. U.S. Minister John L. Stevens supported the provisional government, and U.S. Marines landed from the USS Boston during the crisis. The queen yielded under protest, seeking to avoid bloodshed and expecting the United States to correct what had occurred.

President Grover Cleveland, who took office shortly afterward, opposed annexation and commissioned an investigation. The Blount Report criticized American involvement and described the overthrow as occurring with improper U.S. support. A later Senate investigation, the Morgan Report, reached different conclusions and assigned less blame to U.S. officials. The competing reports became part of the historical dispute.

What followed was not restoration.

The provisional government became the Republic of Hawaii under Sanford Dole. Annexation advocates waited. McKinley's election in 1896 changed the political environment in Washington. By 1897 a new annexation treaty was before the Senate.

Native Hawaiian opponents responded with mass petitioning.

The petitions matter because annexation stories often center governments: the monarchy, provisional regime, republic, Senate, and president. Signatures restore the people governed by those decisions. More than 21,000 Native Hawaiians signed one major anti-annexation petition preserved by the National Archives, at a time when the Native Hawaiian population was far smaller than it had been before devastating nineteenth-century disease.

Opposition was not a footnote.

The treaty failed to obtain the necessary Senate supermajority. Had the constitutional process stopped there, annexation would have stalled again.

War supplied urgency and the joint resolution supplied a different vote threshold.

That legal route remains historically significant. Congress had used joint resolution in the annexation of Texas, but the power to acquire overseas foreign territory by ordinary legislation rather than treaty has been debated by scholars. The political actors in 1898 did not wait for the debate to be resolved in theory. They acted, and later governance built on the fact of American possession.

Power often settles constitutional ambiguity before courts do.

The strategic consequences appeared quickly.

The Spanish-American War transformed the United States' overseas position. Spain lost control of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. Cuba came under American occupation before formal independence under conditions of continued U.S. influence. Hawai‘i, though not taken from Spain, became part of the same 1898 turn toward overseas power.

The United States was no longer simply a continental nation with foreign commerce. It possessed a chain of interests and territories across the Caribbean and Pacific.

Hawai‘i was central to the Pacific side of that transformation.

Its location supported naval operations, communications, and later aviation. Pearl Harbor became a major American naval base. American strategy toward Asia increasingly depended on Pacific logistics. The islands sat on routes connecting the U.S. West Coast with the Philippines, China, Japan, and the wider Pacific.

Geography became strategy because annexation changed whose geography it was.

That sentence is precisely where triumphalist language becomes dangerous. Hawai‘i was not an empty platform waiting to host American power. It was a sovereign kingdom with an internationally recognized government before the overthrow. Native Hawaiians had political institutions, land relationships, language, religion, commerce, and diplomacy. American strategic usefulness was achieved through the subordination of Hawaiian sovereignty.

The strategic gain and the political loss were the same transaction viewed from different sides.

Economic forces had prepared the ground long before 1898. American missionaries arrived in the early nineteenth century, and their descendants became influential in commerce and politics. Sugar plantations tied the islands increasingly to the U.S. market. Reciprocity arrangements affected tariffs and trade. Plantation owners sought stable access to American consumers. Imported labor from Asia and elsewhere transformed the islands' demography.

By annexation, Hawai‘i was already deeply enmeshed in international capitalism.

That does not make political incorporation inevitable.

Economic integration can create pressure for annexation without determining it. Canadian and American economies became deeply integrated without Canada becoming a state. Cuba and the United States developed strong economic ties without formal annexation. Hawai‘i's path depended on specific political actors, constitutional struggles, strategic calculations, racial ideology, military involvement, and the unusual conditions of the 1890s.

Contingency matters most where later maps make borders look natural.

After 1898, territorial government changed political status but did not instantly settle questions of citizenship, rights, land, culture, or self-determination. The Organic Act of 1900 organized territorial government. Residents gained forms of U.S. political participation but lacked the full representation of a state. Hawai‘i sent a nonvoting delegate to Congress. The territorial relationship would last nearly six decades.

Statehood in 1959 is often treated as the resolution.

For many Native Hawaiian activists, it is not. The history of overthrow, annexation, land, and sovereignty remains politically alive. In 1993, on the centennial of the overthrow, Congress passed and President Bill Clinton signed a joint resolution acknowledging and apologizing for the role of U.S. agents and citizens in the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom.

That later acknowledgment did not reverse annexation. It did confirm that the older celebratory story was inadequate even by the federal government's own account.

The 1898 event also altered American racial and immigration politics.

Hawai‘i's population included Native Hawaiians, people of American and European descent, Japanese laborers, Chinese laborers, Portuguese immigrants, and others. Annexation brought a multiethnic Pacific society under American territorial rule at the same moment Congress and federal policy were constructing racial restrictions elsewhere.

The Chinese Exclusion regime had already established a federal system of racially targeted immigration restriction. The annexation of Hawai‘i raised questions about migration between the islands and the mainland and about how existing exclusion rules would apply. Plantation labor systems depended heavily on migrants who were not treated as racial equals in American law or society.

Empire complicated the boundary between inside and outside.

That complication became even larger with Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines after the Spanish-American War. The Supreme Court's Insular Cases in the early twentieth century developed a doctrine under which full constitutional protections did not automatically extend in the same way to all acquired territories. The category of “unincorporated territory” allowed the United States to hold sovereignty without promising an immediate path to statehood.

Hawai‘i ultimately followed a different path to incorporation and statehood, but the imperial turn of the 1890s forced constitutional law to confront possessions the continental expansion model did not neatly explain.

The republic had acquired territories without resolving what kind of political membership their people would receive.

That problem will return in the chapter on Puerto Rico.

Hawai‘i also became vital to twentieth-century war.

Pearl Harbor's name is globally synonymous with the Japanese attack of December 7, 1941. The attack propelled the United States directly into World War II. Popular memory begins the Hawaiian strategic story there, with battleships burning in an American harbor.

But Pearl Harbor was an American naval hub because of a longer nineteenth-century sequence of commercial treaties, strategic interest, overthrow, annexation, territorial governance, and base construction.

The famous event depends on the less remembered one.

Had Hawai‘i remained an independent kingdom or republic, American Pacific strategy would have developed differently. The United States might still have negotiated extensive basing rights. Economic dependence might have produced a quasi-protectorate. Another power could have sought influence. Hawai‘i's leaders might have balanced relationships among states. The Pacific war itself would have altered strategic incentives beyond recognition.

No counterfactual can confidently reconstruct 1941.

What we can say is that annexation removed uncertainty over political control of one of the Pacific's most strategically located island groups.

That certainty became an American asset.

It also became a Hawaiian loss of national sovereignty.

The event's under-appreciation therefore comes partly from statehood. Once Hawai‘i became the fiftieth state, annexation could be retroactively folded into a story of inevitable American union: territory first, state eventually. That narrative treats the end point as evidence that the path was consensual.

The petitions say otherwise.

Statehood itself received strong support in the 1959 referendum under the choices then presented, but that vote occurred in a society already transformed by sixty years of U.S. rule and did not ask whether the Hawaiian Kingdom should be restored. A later democratic decision cannot be used as a time machine to supply consent to 1898.

Historical legitimacy must be evaluated at the moment power was exercised.

This is one reason the anti-annexation petitions are among the most important objects in the chapter. They prevent the people opposing annexation from becoming an abstraction. The names are evidence that many Native Hawaiians understood exactly what was at stake and tried to use American political procedure to stop it.

They lost.

A history of American institutions must make room for losing petitions because power often looks more inevitable after it has won.

The annexation also contributed to the growing importance of the U.S. West Coast. San Francisco and later Los Angeles, Seattle, San Diego, and other Pacific ports developed within an American system increasingly oriented toward Asia. Hawai‘i served as waypoint, market, military position, and cultural bridge. Aviation later made the islands central to transpacific travel.

Tourism would create a new economic layer, selling Hawai‘i's climate, beaches, and culture to mainland consumers even as Native Hawaiian language and land rights suffered under earlier Americanization policies.

A place can be strategically absorbed and then aesthetically marketed.

The process changes what outsiders think the place is for.

Hawaiian history repeatedly resists that reduction. The islands were not made meaningful by American use. They had meaning, society, and sovereignty before American annexation. The United States changed their political context and then incorporated the resulting geography into its own national identity.

This book's subtitle says the selected events “made America.” Annexation is one of the chapters that forces the verb to work in both directions.

America made itself larger by making another polity smaller.

That expansion shaped military reach, commerce, constitutional doctrine, migration, and Pacific strategy. It also created obligations and conflicts that remain unresolved.

The 1890s contain many plausible selections: the Sherman Antitrust Act, the Panic of 1893, Pullman Strike, Plessy v. Ferguson, the closing of the western frontier, the Spanish-American War itself. Choosing Hawai‘i over Plessy especially demands humility because legalized segregation and racial terror defined American life for generations.

The case for annexation is not that it mattered more than Jim Crow.

It is that it changed the scale and location of the American project in a way national memory often compresses into a prelude to statehood.

After 1898, the country had a strategic center thousands of miles from the mainland. It had demonstrated that overseas territory could be acquired through congressional resolution after a treaty lacked votes. It had ignored organized Native opposition. It had strengthened a Pacific military posture that would define later wars.

The American map looked almost the same on a classroom wall if the inset box for Hawai‘i was small enough.

Strategically, the map had become an ocean larger.
