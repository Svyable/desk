# The Other 250

Twenty-Six Overlooked Events That Made America

Sven Hardy Benson

A country remembers itself in scenes.

A declaration is signed. A fort is fired upon. A president speaks from a platform. A market crashes. A sailor kisses a nurse in Times Square. A rocket rises. Two towers fall.

There is nothing wrong with this kind of memory. History needs landmarks. A person cannot carry two and a half centuries of legislation, migration, construction, argument, bankruptcy, invention, weather, violence, compromise, and administrative detail in his head without compressing it. We keep a few pictures and call them eras.

The trouble begins when the pictures become the explanation.

The United States did not become the United States only at the moments when everyone knew history was happening. Much of the country was made in rooms that would not survive in paintings: a congressional chamber refusing to receive petitions; a state government financing a canal that many people thought reckless; an Army office deciding to collect weather reports over telegraph wire; a hospital board matching a federal construction grant; a pension plan failing in Indiana; a navigation disaster on the far side of the world changing the civilian future of a military satellite system.

These were not necessarily secret events. Several were front-page news. Some produced enormous political fights. A few are taught in school, if usually as supporting details to a larger story. Under-appreciated is different from unknown. It describes a mismatch between the amount of attention an event receives in our common memory and the amount of consequence that flowed through it.

That mismatch is the subject of this book.

The premise is simple enough to be dangerous: choose one event from every calendar decade of America's first 250 years and make the strongest honest case that it deserves far more attention than it gets. Not the biggest event. Not the best event. Not the event that morally redeems or condemns the decade. The event whose afterlife is strangely larger than its place in the story we usually tell.

A ranking would be absurd. How would one measure whether the Erie Canal is more under-appreciated than the founding of a federal weather service, or whether the 1965 immigration law is more consequentially forgotten than the law that made Puerto Ricans U.S. citizens in 1917? There is no unit for historical neglect. The selections are arguments. Another historian could make twenty-six different choices and produce a serious book.

That is a feature rather than a defect. The point is to make causation visible again.

The familiar history remains in the room. The French alliance of 1778 means little without the American Revolution. The Morrill Act of 1862 cannot be separated from the Civil War, federal control of Congress, Indigenous dispossession, or the longer struggle over who public institutions were for. The Fair Labor Standards Act cannot be understood without the Depression and the New Deal. The National Defense Education Act makes no sense without Sputnik and the Cold War. The CHIPS and Science Act belongs to a twenty-first-century argument about globalization, China, supply chains, security, and the proper role of government in markets.

But familiar history often behaves like gravity. It pulls every smaller event into its orbit until the smaller event becomes merely evidence that the big event was big. This book tries the opposite. It holds the supposedly secondary event still long enough to see what passed through it.

That changes the shape of the story.

Take 1790. The Constitution was young, the Treasury was an experiment, and Revolutionary War debts were distributed across a federal government and states that had borrowed differently and paid differently. Alexander Hamilton's proposal that the federal government assume state debts sounds, at first glance, like the kind of subject designed to clear a dinner table. Yet the argument was about what the Union was. Would the new federal government become the financial center of the country? Would creditors come to depend on it? Would states that had already paid down their debts subsidize those that had not? Could public debt become a source of national power rather than simply a burden left by war?

The answer helped build a fiscal state. The debate also deepened the political divisions that would organize the new republic. A line on a balance sheet became part of the architecture of federalism.

Or take 1870. Congress authorized what became the national weather service inside the Army's Signal Service. The mechanism was almost comically plain by modern standards: observers, instruments, telegraph messages, maps, warnings. Yet the idea contained something larger. Weather information gathered across many places had value no single farmer, ship captain, railroad, merchant, or town could easily create alone. The federal government would collect it, synthesize it, and return it as a public service. Today a forecast arrives on a phone with so little friction that it can feel like a property of the atmosphere itself. It is not. It is an institution.

The most interesting infrastructure is often the infrastructure we stop noticing.

That observation recurs throughout these pages. The Erie Canal becomes geography. The telegraph becomes simultaneity. Petroleum becomes an energy system. Land-grant colleges become part of the educational landscape. Civil-service rules become the assumption that a change in president does not require replacing every federal employee. Food regulation becomes the expectation that a bottle's label should bear some relationship to what is inside it. Hospital grants become buildings that later generations mistake for inevitable features of their towns. GPS becomes a blue dot.

Forgetting success is easy. Once a system works, the political choice that created it can disappear inside the service it provides.

Forgetting harm can happen by the same mechanism.

The Morrill Act widened access to practical higher education while distributing value from millions of acres taken from Native communities. Hill-Burton helped finance an extraordinary expansion of hospital capacity while its original rules tolerated segregated facilities. The 1924 immigration law did not merely regulate a border; it encoded a hierarchy of desirable ancestry into national policy. The annexation of Hawai‘i cannot be reduced to a strategic stepping stone across the Pacific without also confronting the organized opposition of Native Hawaiians and the unusual constitutional route by which annexation occurred. The law that made Puerto Ricans citizens did not make Puerto Rico a state or give its residents voting representation in Congress.

There is a temptation in writing an under-appreciated history to turn every selection into a secret hero. That would be another form of distortion. Some events in this book expanded capacity by excluding people. Some solved one problem while creating another. Some were compromises whose contradictions became permanent. Some were accidents that governments later converted into policy. Several are still contested.

The word “made” in the subtitle therefore does not mean improved.

It means made possible, made durable, made normal, made harder to reverse, made available, made unequal, made connected, made governable, made profitable, made thinkable.

A second temptation is hindsight. An event looks beautifully causal when the writer starts with the outcome and walks backward until he finds a convenient beginning. The people in these chapters did not have that luxury. The officers at Newburgh in 1783 did not know whether the constitutional order Americans now regard as normal would exist four years later. New Yorkers digging a canal toward Lake Erie did not know precisely how settlement, commodity flows, and city growth would respond. The sponsors of the 1965 immigration act did not possess a demographic spreadsheet from the twenty-first century. NASA's commercial cargo experiment did not arrive with a guarantee that a private launch industry would become reliable, competitive, or geopolitically important. Semiconductor subsidies enacted in 2022 still have construction schedules, market cycles, technological shifts, and political reversals ahead of them.

The chapters try to preserve that uncertainty. A later consequence can be documented without pretending it was obvious in advance.

The decade structure creates another useful discomfort. History does not respect zeroes. Nothing reset in 1800 or 1900 or 2000. The calendar is an artificial frame imposed on continuous life. But artificial frames can reveal things. One selection per decade forces comparison across kinds of consequence. A war has to share a book with a canal. A constitutional fight with a weather bureau. A federal statute with a drilled hole in Pennsylvania. It prevents the story from becoming only presidential, only military, only technological, only economic, or only social.

It also exposes how often one decade builds the invisible floor of the next.

The 1844 telegraph line did not create modern communications by itself. It demonstrated a new relationship between information and distance. The 1859 oil well did not invent petroleum, but it helped prove a commercial method that drew capital and imitation. The 1906 food and drug law was weak by later regulatory standards, but it established a federal foothold that later law would expand. The 1958 education act was born from Cold War anxiety, yet federal loans and support for science, languages, and graduate education outlived the particular panic that helped enact them. A shock opens a door. An institution walks through. Later generations remember the shock and live inside the institution.

That may be the most American pattern in the book.

The country is often described through its ideals and its crises. It is also a machine for turning crises into arrangements. Some arrangements are wise. Some are cruel. Some are temporary and then become permanent. Some are announced as permanent and vanish. The interesting question is not whether government or markets made America. Both did, along with families, enslaved people, migrants, soldiers, engineers, workers, corporations, tribes, states, cities, universities, courts, churches, inventors, organizers, and accidents of weather and geography. The more useful question is how one arrangement changed the options available to the people who came after it.

That is what a hinge does. It does not decide where the door must go. It changes how the door can move.

The first chapter begins two years after independence was declared, when the United States had a name, an army, an argument for legitimacy, and no guarantee of survival. The last begins in a decade we are still living through, when the United States is again debating whether a government should deliberately shape which things are manufactured inside its borders. Between them are twenty-four other hinges.

They are not the whole story of America.

They are the part that disappears when the whole story gets too neat.
