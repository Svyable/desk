# The Government Starts Watching the Sky

On February 9, 1870, Congress authorized the federal government to collect weather observations and warn the country about approaching storms.

It sounds almost comically modest beside the events surrounding it. Reconstruction was remaking and then failing to remake political power in the South. The Fifteenth Amendment was being ratified. Railroads were knitting the continent together. Corporations were expanding. Western settlement and federal campaigns against Native nations were transforming enormous territories. The country was still learning what the Civil War had changed.

Then Congress told the Army's Signal Service to watch the weather.

The assignment created what became the national weather service.

The original institutional name was ungainly: a Division of Telegrams and Reports for the Benefit of Commerce. The phrase is revealing. Weather observation was not imagined primarily as a scientific luxury or a daily lifestyle feature. It was information infrastructure for people whose lives and property depended on storms, temperature, wind, and changing conditions.

Commerce needed forecasts because weather creates costs before anyone can negotiate with it.

Ships leave or remain in port. Farmers harvest or wait. Railroads manage snow and heat. Great Lakes traffic faces storms. Merchants care about crops and transport. Communities prepare for dangerous conditions. A warning has economic value because it changes a decision before damage occurs.

The difficulty is that useful weather information is collective.

A farmer can observe his own sky. A ship captain can read local conditions. A town can keep a barometer. But a storm system moving across a continent can only be understood well if observations from many places are combined quickly enough to reveal pattern and direction.

The telegraph made that possible.

The weather service was therefore one of the institutional children of the 1844 communications revolution. Observers in different locations could send measurements to a central office. Reports could be assembled into maps and summaries. Warnings could then travel back outward over the same network.

Information moved in two directions: local conditions became national knowledge, and national knowledge became local warning.

That feedback loop is the event.

The government was building an early data network.

Modern language can make the system sound more sophisticated than it was. Instruments varied. Observation practices needed standardization. Forecasting science was young. Telegraph lines failed. Geographic coverage was incomplete. Weather dynamics were poorly understood compared with modern meteorology. False alarms and missed storms could erode trust.

But the underlying architecture was astonishingly modern.

Distributed sensors. Standardized measurements. Communications links. Central aggregation. Analysis. Public distribution.

The country had discovered that some information becomes more valuable when no single user owns the whole system.

This is why the 1870 weather service deserves selection as an under-appreciated event. It represents a category of government activity Americans now use constantly while barely noticing that government is present: create a trusted information commons that lowers uncertainty for everyone.

Weather is only the obvious example.

The federal government would later collect agricultural statistics, labor data, public-health information, census data, geologic surveys, economic indicators, navigation charts, maps, scientific measurements, and countless other datasets. Private companies often turn public data into commercial products. Researchers use it. Local governments plan around it. Investors trade on it. Individuals make daily decisions from it.

The state becomes useful not only when it commands or pays but when it measures.

Measurement sounds passive. It is not.

To measure nationally, an institution has to decide units, times, instruments, definitions, reporting rules, locations, and quality standards. It has to employ people, train them, transmit information, preserve records, and correct errors. A national dataset is a political and administrative achievement before it becomes a scientific one.

Weather made the value intuitive.

The Army's Signal Service was a natural institutional home in the beginning because it already understood communications and maintained telegraph expertise. Military organization also gave the young service a structure for disciplined observation. Weather and signaling shared a technological backbone.

That arrangement did not last forever. In 1891, weather responsibilities moved from the Army to the Department of Agriculture, becoming the civilian Weather Bureau. In 1940, the Weather Bureau moved to the Department of Commerce. In 1970 it became the National Weather Service within the newly created National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

The institutional address changed. The public function persisted.

Persistence is one of the best tests for whether a policy solved a real problem.

Governments keep many programs because constituencies form around them, not because the original rationale remains sound. The weather service also developed constituencies, budgets, and bureaucracy. But the core information problem did not vanish. If anything, the value of national weather observation increased as aviation, electrification, industrial agriculture, shipping, highways, energy systems, and dense cities became more exposed to weather-related disruption.

Economic development made forecasting more valuable, not less.

A nineteenth-century mariner cared about a storm because it could sink a ship. A twentieth-century utility cared because heat or cold could move electricity demand. An airline cared because wind, icing, thunderstorms, and visibility affected flight. A modern logistics network cares because a snowstorm can close airports, highways, warehouses, and distribution centers across regions. Commodity traders care about rainfall. Insurers care about hurricanes. Emergency managers care about everything.

The same forecast can serve thousands of different decisions.

That is the public-good logic hiding inside the weather map.

If every farmer, airline, shipper, broadcaster, and household had to build an independent continental observation network, the duplication would be absurd. Central collection and open distribution create enormous spillover value. Private firms can add specialized forecasting, interfaces, analytics, and industry-specific services on top of public observations.

Government information can therefore support private markets rather than replace them.

The weather service became a platform.

This complicates the usual American argument between government and enterprise. A shipping company using a public forecast is not participating in a command economy. A private weather app displaying National Weather Service data is not evidence that government can forecast consumer preferences. The institutional question is narrower: which layers of information infrastructure have enough shared value that public provision or coordination lowers costs for everyone?

The 1870 answer was weather observation.

The service's early scientific limitations make the institutional decision more interesting. Congress was funding capability before capability was perfect.

Forecasting always contains uncertainty. A warning must be issued before an event is fully known. If forecasters wait until certainty, the warning may become useless. If they warn too often, false alarms create complacency and unnecessary cost. Meteorology therefore became not only a science of atmospheric prediction but a public practice of communicating uncertain risk.

That problem has never been solved completely.

Hurricane cones, severe-weather watches, tornado warnings, snowfall probabilities, heat advisories, and flood outlooks all translate uncertain models into decisions by people with different risk tolerances. A school superintendent deciding whether to cancel class does not need the same information as a cargo ship offshore, but both rely on a shared observation-and-forecast system.

The service made uncertainty administrable.

This is another form of state capacity: not deciding what everyone must do, but improving the information from which decentralized actors decide.

The distinction matters because weather forecasting can look like a free feature of nature once phones display it automatically. Most users do not know where an observation originated, who maintained the radar, who launched the weather balloon, who operated a satellite, who ran the numerical model, who staffed the forecast office, or who issued the warning.

Successful infrastructure disappears behind the interface.

The nineteenth-century system was more visible because the telegraph was visible. Reports arrived as telegrams. Maps were drawn manually. Newspapers reprinted forecasts. Signal flags could warn ports. People could see the apparatus of information collection.

Modernity hides the apparatus by making it seamless.

The service also helped professionalize meteorology. Regular observations create records. Records allow comparison. Comparison allows models and theories to be tested. An institution that collects data for practical forecasting simultaneously creates a scientific archive.

Long records become more valuable with age.

A temperature series collected for tomorrow's forecast can become evidence for climate research a century later. Historical storm tracks help study risk. Precipitation records support water planning. The original observers could not foresee every future use of the data they preserved.

Data infrastructure has option value.

This is one reason public measurement can produce returns impossible to justify from a narrow first-year cost-benefit analysis. The later users do not exist yet. A dataset gathered continuously can answer future questions no original appropriation specified.

The census has the same property. So do geological surveys, satellite imagery, public-health records, and economic time series.

The weather service is a particularly clean demonstration because the atmosphere never stops generating new observations.

There were failures and controversies. Early forecasts sometimes performed poorly. Bureaucratic rivalries mattered. Instrumentation and communications needed improvement. The service could be criticized for costs or methods. Weather forecasting remained limited enough that jokes about forecasters became a durable cultural genre.

Mockery can coexist with dependence.

People laugh at forecasts because they use them.

The agency's migration from military to civilian administration also reveals changing ideas about where scientific information belongs. The Signal Service had made sense when telegraphy and disciplined observation were central. The Department of Agriculture made sense as farming and commerce became major clients. Commerce later absorbed the Weather Bureau as aviation, shipping, industry, and broad economic uses expanded.

Institutional location follows the perceived purpose of information.

The modern National Weather Service now sits inside NOAA, alongside oceanic and atmospheric science. Satellites, radar, supercomputers, remote sensors, numerical models, international data exchange, and local forecast offices have transformed capability beyond recognition.

But the public logic of 1870 remains legible.

Observe widely. Share quickly. Warn usefully.

The event also has an international dimension. Weather systems cross borders without visas. Useful forecasting therefore benefits from international exchange. The United States' national network became part of a broader global system of meteorological cooperation. Satellites observe other countries. Foreign observations improve American forecasts. Ocean buoys and aircraft reports cross jurisdictions.

Like the French alliance and Tambora, weather reminds the country that sovereignty does not make physical systems national.

The atmosphere is a commons Americans can monitor but not contain.

This creates a curious contrast with many nineteenth-century government projects. The federal state spent much of the century acquiring, distributing, defending, surveying, and settling territory. Weather observation was a different relationship to space. It did not seek to own the sky. It sought to know it.

Knowledge could then be distributed back to citizens.

That is a gentler form of power but still power. A government capable of observing widely gains military, commercial, and administrative advantages. Weather intelligence supports armies and navies. Agricultural forecasts influence markets. Aviation forecasting supports strategic mobility. Scientific capacity can be dual-use.

Information infrastructure is rarely politically innocent.

Nor were the expanding observational networks separate from continental expansion. Stations, telegraph lines, military posts, and settlements spread across lands from which Native nations were being displaced. Federal presence in the West often combined scientific, military, commercial, and territorial functions. Mapping and observing a region can support governing it.

The weather service's public benefit does not erase the broader context of the state that built it.

Still, the choice to provide forecasting widely is significant. The service was not designed only as secret military intelligence. Congress's language emphasized commerce. Public reports became a civic utility.

That utility eventually produced private innovation around it.

Radio and television broadcasters made weather central to programming. Private meteorology firms sold specialized forecasts. Airlines built meteorological operations. Agricultural services used government observations. Smartphone applications turned public data into personalized interfaces.

A public platform can generate a private ecosystem while remaining essential beneath it.

The same pattern appears in GPS, another chapter later in the book. A military system becomes broadly available, private firms build thousands of products on top of it, and users come to experience the service as a feature of their device rather than a public infrastructure layer.

Weather came first.

The 1870 event also changes the way we think about federal administration after the Civil War. The era is often framed through corruption, patronage, railroad grants, Reconstruction conflict, and the limits of governance. All are central. Yet the same state was accumulating specialized capacities that would later make modern administration possible.

Bureaucracy is not one thing.

It can be patronage and expertise, coercion and service, waste and measurement, all in the same decade. The useful question is which administrative forms generate reliable capability and which merely consume resources or distribute favors.

Weather observation generated a capability difficult to replace through isolated local action.

The counterfactual is modest but real. Private telegraph companies, newspapers, scientific societies, universities, ports, and states could have continued collecting weather information. Some already did. A national private network might have emerged. International science would have pushed meteorology forward regardless.

What the federal service changed was scale, standardization, continuity, and public obligation.

A private forecast exists for customers who pay. A public warning system exists because an institution has accepted responsibility for producing the warning whether or not the people in danger have subscribed.

That difference is easy to notice during a hurricane and easy to forget on an ordinary Tuesday.

The most under-appreciated institutions often live in ordinary Tuesdays.

The 1870 law did not stop storms. It did not make every forecast accurate. It did not create modern climate science overnight. It did not build today's satellites or radar.

It made a national promise to watch.

From that promise grew an information system Americans now consult before planting, flying, shipping, commuting, playing baseball, launching rockets, fighting fires, closing schools, pricing energy, and deciding whether to carry an umbrella.

The sky did not change in 1870.

The country's ability to know it did.
