# America Chooses Its Ancestry

In 1924, the United States converted a theory of desirable ancestry into arithmetic.

The Immigration Act of 1924, commonly associated with Representative Albert Johnson and Senator David Reed, established a national-origins quota system that sharply restricted immigration and favored people from northern and western Europe over those from southern and eastern Europe. The law calculated quotas using an earlier American population as its reference and built exclusion into the machinery of admission. It also reinforced the exclusion of immigrants from Asia.

Immigration restriction was not new. The Chinese Exclusion Act dated to 1882. Other laws barred or restricted categories of migrants on grounds involving race, nationality, health, poverty, political belief, or morality. World War I and the first Red Scare intensified nativism. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 had already imposed numerical limits based on nationality.

What 1924 did was make hierarchy durable and systematic.

The law deserves attention because immigration rules do more than decide who crosses a border in the year they are enacted. They decide which families form, which communities grow, which languages become common, which labor markets receive workers, which refugees find safety, and which demographic futures become possible.

A quota is a population policy operating one visa at a time.

The national-origins system emerged from an era saturated with racial classification. Eugenics had acquired scientific prestige in universities, foundations, legislatures, and public discourse despite the false premises and prejudices beneath it. Immigration restrictionists argued that the country's existing racial and ethnic composition could be protected through law. The 1890 census was initially used as the quota reference under the 1924 framework precisely because it preceded the largest waves of southern and eastern European immigration.

The choice of baseline was political memory disguised as statistics.

A census seems descriptive. A quota formula built from a census becomes prescriptive.

The United States was deciding which version of its past should set the proportions of its future.

That is why the chapter title says America chose its ancestry. No government can choose the ancestry of people already alive. It can, however, influence which future Americans are likely to arrive, marry, have children, open businesses, create institutions, and become neighbors.

The effect compounds across generations.

Italian, Polish, Jewish, Greek, Slavic, and other immigrants from southern and eastern Europe faced much tighter restrictions than they had during the great migration waves of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Japanese immigration was effectively barred under the new law, worsening relations with Japan. The law's Asian exclusion provisions built on a longer structure of racial ineligibility for citizenship and exclusion.

The categories were not only xenophobic. They were geopolitical.

Japan had emerged as a major power and had been sensitive to American discrimination against Japanese migrants, especially on the West Coast. The 1907–1908 Gentlemen's Agreement had limited Japanese labor immigration through diplomacy rather than explicit statutory exclusion. The 1924 law's exclusionary treatment was viewed in Japan as an insult and damaged bilateral relations.

Immigration law can be foreign policy because migrants have countries of origin that notice how they are treated.

This does not mean the act caused the later Pacific War. Japan's imperial expansion, militarism, conflict in China, resource pressures, domestic politics, American embargoes, and strategic calculations were vastly more immediate causes. Treating a discriminatory immigration statute as a hidden cause of Pearl Harbor would be precisely the kind of overreach this book is designed to avoid.

The narrower point is enough: Congress made domestic racial policy in a world where racial status carried diplomatic meaning.

The law also affected people trying to flee danger.

The national-origins quotas remained in place as Nazi persecution escalated in Europe during the 1930s. Even where quotas were not fully used, restrictive administrative practices, documentary requirements, public opposition, and political fear narrowed refugee options. The tragedy of Jewish refugees turned away or unable to secure admission cannot be attributed solely to the 1924 act, but the quota structure formed part of the legal environment in which escape routes were limited.

A peacetime demographic policy became a wartime life-and-death constraint.

That is a recurring danger in durable law. Legislators optimize for the problem they imagine and create rules that later operate under conditions they did not foresee.

The designers of the quota system wanted stability in national composition. They got rigidity when flexibility mattered.

The act also changed American labor markets.

Industrialization had relied heavily on European immigrant labor. Restriction tightened that source just as other demographic movements reshaped the workforce. The Great Migration of Black Americans from the South to northern and midwestern cities had already accelerated during World War I when European immigration fell and industrial labor demand remained strong. Restriction after the war helped preserve conditions under which employers continued recruiting domestic workers, including Black southerners, though discrimination in hiring, housing, unions, and public life remained severe.

The relationship is not simple enough to say immigration restriction caused the Great Migration. The movement began earlier and was driven by Jim Crow violence, disenfranchisement, economic opportunity, agricultural change, family networks, newspapers, and wartime labor demand.

But labor supply is comparative.

Closing one channel can increase demand through another.

The 1924 law also helped shape the development of the U.S.-Mexico border system. Numerical quotas initially applied differently across the Western Hemisphere, and Mexican labor remained important to American agriculture, mining, railroads, and industry. Restrictionists pushed for tighter controls, while employers sought access to workers. Border enforcement expanded institutionally in the 1920s, including the creation of the U.S. Border Patrol in 1924.

A law designed around national origins therefore interacted with a labor system that treated different migration channels differently.

American immigration policy has often wanted workers and not wanted immigrants at the same time.

That contradiction produces temporary programs, irregular migration, enforcement cycles, employer dependence, and political anger. The roots run deeper than 1924, but the decade built institutional forms that still echo.

The Border Patrol's creation in the same year is not a coincidence of chronology. Numerical restrictions and exclusion rules require enforcement capacity. A legal boundary matters differently once the government builds an organization dedicated to policing it.

Policy creates bureaucracy; bureaucracy makes policy more real.

The national-origins system also influenced American identity by changing who was absent.

Demography is difficult to perceive counterfactually. A city contains the people who arrived, not the people who might have arrived under another law. A family that never entered leaves no neighborhood, restaurant, union local, congregation, company, or child whose absence can be photographed.

Restriction produces invisible history.

This is why immigration laws are routinely under-appreciated relative to elections and wars. Their effects unfold through missing people.

Imagine two countries identical in 1924 but governed by different admission rules for forty years. By 1965, their populations would differ not only by the first generation of entrants but by marriages, births, business networks, political organizations, and cultural institutions. The difference would then continue compounding.

An annual quota is a slow demographic engine.

The law's supporters did not hide their desire to shape national composition. They spoke in terms of preserving American character and managing assimilation. Restriction had broad public support, including among people who did not embrace the most explicit eugenic arguments. Economic concerns, postwar unemployment, labor competition, radicalism, urban change, and cultural anxiety all contributed.

Reducing the act to a handful of racist ideologues would let the wider political culture escape responsibility.

Restriction was mainstream.

That is precisely why it could last so long.

The quota regime remained central until the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 dismantled the national-origins system. Four decades is enough time for a temporary-looking policy to become part of how a country imagines normal migration.

By the time the system changed, Americans had forgotten how recent mass immigration from Europe had been.

The people who had once been targets of nativism could become part of a broader category of accepted whiteness. Irish, Italian, Jewish, Polish, and other European-descended Americans experienced different histories and never became socially identical, but the boundaries of racial and ethnic belonging shifted over generations.

Restriction helped freeze immigration while assimilation changed the status of populations already inside.

This produced a political irony. A law designed to preserve an older ethnic composition operated during decades in which the meaning of that composition changed.

The country was not culturally static even when immigration rules tried to make it more so.

The 1924 act also shows why immigration debates are rarely only about numbers.

A numerical limit sounds neutral until one asks how slots are allocated. By nationality? Family relationship? Skills? Lottery? Humanitarian need? Employment? Country caps? First come, first served? Every allocation system contains a theory of what immigration is for.

Johnson-Reed's theory privileged inherited national composition.

The 1965 law would privilege family relationships and certain skills. Later programs would add refugees, diversity visas, employment categories, temporary workers, and other mechanisms. The arguments changed because the country was choosing among different principles of membership.

The border decides more than quantity. It decides criteria.

The act's constitutional environment also matters. Immigration authority had become heavily federal by the late nineteenth century through Supreme Court doctrine and congressional practice. Earlier in American history, states had played larger roles in regulating arriving migrants. By the 1920s, national government controlled the core admissions regime.

This centralized the power to shape demography.

A state wanting more Italian workers could not simply ignore federal quotas. A city seeking refugees could not admit them independently. Employers wanting labor had to work through federal law or press for exceptions. Immigration became a national lever over local population change.

That centralization would make later reform equally national.

The event sits uncomfortably beside the era's ideal of the United States as a refuge. The Statue of Liberty had stood in New York Harbor since 1886. Emma Lazarus's poem would later become deeply associated with it. Yet national policy in the 1920s was moving toward restriction, not open welcome.

National symbols and national statutes can tell different stories at the same time.

The law also had an exclusionary relationship with naturalization. American citizenship law had long contained racial eligibility rules. Asian immigrants often faced barriers not only to entry but to naturalization and land ownership under federal and state regimes. Supreme Court cases in the early 1920s considered who qualified as “white” under naturalization statutes, rejecting claims by Japanese and South Asian petitioners.

The immigration act emerged from that racial jurisprudence, not outside it.

America was defining national membership at the border and in the naturalization court together.

The later repudiation of national-origins quotas can make the 1924 system seem like an embarrassing detour from a fundamentally inclusive trajectory. That framing is too forgiving.

Restriction was not a short accident. It was a stable national policy created by elected institutions and sustained across administrations of both parties.

Its durability shaped who became American.

The counterfactual is impossible to quantify cleanly. If the prewar immigration regime had continued, World War I, the Great Depression, European political instability, and later World War II would still have altered migration. Economic conditions alone reduced flows in some periods. Other laws might have imposed different limits. Host-country capacity and migrant preferences change over time.

No honest historian can produce the exact 1965 population of an America without Johnson-Reed.

We do not need one.

The law was explicitly designed to reduce and redirect immigration, and it did. The people excluded would otherwise have had some probability of entry greater than the law allowed. Their absence accumulated.

That is enough to make the statute a demographic hinge.

There was another transformation inside the 1924 system that made the restriction more durable: the border became paperwork before it became a physical encounter.

The modern image of immigration enforcement centers on the line itself—an airport booth, a port, a fence, a desert crossing, a checkpoint. But a numerical immigration regime cannot function only at the geographic boundary. It needs an administrative boundary that begins earlier.

Who is the applicant?

Which quota applies?

Is a visa number available?

What documents establish identity, nationality, family relationship, occupation, or eligibility?

Which consular officer is authorized to approve the journey?

A quota therefore creates a bureaucracy of classification.

The exclusion can occur before the migrant ever buys a ticket.

That shift matters because it changes what it means to be legally admissible. In the earlier era of mass European migration, many passengers arrived at American ports and were inspected there, with some excluded. Under the increasingly restrictive system of the 1920s, permission to embark became more tightly tied to visas issued abroad. The country was pushing part of its border outward into consulates and documents.

That is a form of state capacity.

The government did not merely announce that fewer people should come. It built a machinery capable of sorting people before arrival according to categories Congress had specified.

The machinery also produced a new kind of political identity: the person whose presence violated a numerical or documentary rule rather than a traditional criminal prohibition.

Unauthorized migration existed before national quotas, but numerical restriction expanded the circumstances in which ordinary labor migration could become illegal by operation of paperwork. A worker could be economically wanted, socially embedded, and legally barred at the same time.

That contradiction became especially important along the Mexican border. Employers in agriculture, railroads, mining, and other sectors continued to demand labor. Mexican migration remained outside the European national-origins quota structure in the same way, but documentation, inspection, deportation, and border enforcement became increasingly consequential. The government could pursue restriction while employers pursued recruitment.

The result was not a coherent closed system.

It was a system in which legality and economic demand could point in opposite directions.

That pattern would repeat for the rest of the century.

The Depression made the contradiction harsher. As unemployment rose, federal, state, and local officials participated in repatriation and deportation campaigns that drove or pressured large numbers of people of Mexican origin out of the United States, including some U.S. citizens. The episode was not a direct statutory command of Johnson-Reed, and it should not be collapsed into the 1924 act. It does show how an enforcement apparatus and a political culture of restriction could be redirected when the economy turned.

Institutions built for one definition of unwanted migration can be used under another.

The quota system also changed political memory by creating a pause in mass European immigration long enough for earlier immigrant groups to be recast as the traditional American population. The children and grandchildren of people once described as alien, unassimilable, radical, Catholic, Jewish, or racially suspect became voters, veterans, homeowners, officials, and members of an expanding middle class.

The line between “old stock” and “new immigrant” moved.

Then, when the 1965 law reopened immigration on different principles, descendants of earlier immigrants could remember Ellis Island as a family origin story rather than as a national emergency.

That change in memory matters because societies often romanticize yesterday's migration while fearing today's.

The person whose grandparent arrived under suspicion can become the citizen who believes the old migration was fundamentally different from the new one.

Law helped create the pause in which that transformation of memory occurred.

This is the deeper reason the chapter belongs beside the 1965 immigration chapter later in the book. The two statutes are not merely opposites, restriction followed by liberalization. They demonstrate how immigration systems create path dependence.

Johnson-Reed narrowed the set of people who could establish family and community networks in the United States for four decades. The 1965 law then gave family relationships a new importance in admissions. The families available to use that preference system were, in part, the product of the earlier restriction.

One generation of law determines the starting conditions for the next.

The 1920s contain other under-appreciated candidates with enormous claims: commercial radio, electrification, installment credit, zoning, aviation, the rise of chain retail, and the Federal Highway Act of 1921. The decade is also remembered for Prohibition, the Harlem Renaissance, the Scopes trial, and stock-market speculation.

Johnson-Reed belongs near the center because it decided something more durable than the style of the decade.

It turned ancestry into allocation, allocation into bureaucracy, and bureaucracy into missing people.

Forty-one years later, another Congress would choose differently.

By then, the country being changed by the new law had already been shaped by the old one.

There is a second reason the 1924 law matters beyond the number of admissions it denied. It trained the federal government to treat population composition as an object that could be engineered through categories.

That sounds abstract until one notices how much administrative power rests inside a category. A statute can say that people from one place belong in one quota and people from another place belong in another. A census can supply the denominator. A consulate can decide which documents establish nationality. A clerk can translate a complicated family history into a box on a form. Each step looks procedural. Together they decide which identities have legal consequences.

The machinery does not need to hate anyone in order to reproduce the hierarchy written into its rules.

That is one of the durable lessons of Johnson-Reed. Prejudice became more powerful when it stopped depending on a prejudiced encounter and became part of an ordinary workflow. The decision could be distributed across statutes, tables, visa numbers, certificates, consular offices, inspectors, and waiting lists. No single official had to reargue the theory of national origins each morning. The classification system carried the theory forward.

This is how controversial political choices become normal administration.

Once that happens, repeal becomes harder than changing opinion. An institution has forms, staffing, databases, precedents, offices, professional habits, constituencies, and expectations built around the existing rule. People learn how to navigate it. Employers adapt to it. Families plan around it. Politicians discover which parts of it are popular. The system acquires defenders who may care less about the original ideology than about the stability of the arrangement that ideology produced.

A durable policy therefore has two lives. There is the argument that creates it, and there is the administrative world that grows up after the argument has faded.

The national-origins system survived long enough to enter that second life.

This helps explain why demographic policy can outlast the coalition that first enacted it. The people who wrote a restriction may disappear from office. The language used to justify it may become embarrassing. Yet the quotas can remain because the burden of action has shifted. Keeping the system requires no new national decision. Replacing it does.

Inertia favors the law already translated into procedure.

The same feature makes baselines unusually important. A baseline is often presented as a technical starting point, but the choice of starting point can carry an entire political judgment. Which year counts as representative? Which population is treated as the reference America? Which distinctions are preserved in the table? Which are ignored?

After the table exists, later administrators can say they are merely applying it.

That is the quiet power of arithmetic in public policy. Numbers can clarify a choice, but they can also hide where the choice was made.

The quota formula did not discover an objective American ethnic composition that immigration policy was required to preserve. Congress selected a historical population and gave that selection legal force. Once encoded, the result could look less like an argument about belonging and more like a calculation.

The calculation was political all the way down.

That matters for the book's larger claim about overlooked hinges. Wars and elections announce that history is happening. Administrative categories rarely do. They enter through schedules, definitions, filing rules, eligibility tests, and institutional routines. Yet those routines can govern millions of individual decisions for decades.

The effects accumulate quietly because the unit of action is small.

One denied visa does not visibly change a nation. Neither does one approved visa. But a rule applied year after year changes who can build a life inside the country and who must build it somewhere else. It changes which relatives can later sponsor relatives, which neighborhoods acquire enough population to sustain institutions, which employers develop recruiting networks, and which political constituencies eventually exist.

This is demographic compounding through administration.

It also complicates the language of assimilation. Restrictionists often argued that fewer arrivals would give the country time to absorb people already present. But assimilation is not a neutral process occurring after immigration policy has finished its work. The policy changes the conditions under which assimilation happens.

A community that continues receiving newcomers can preserve language, newspapers, religious institutions, businesses, political organizations, and transnational ties differently from one whose migration channel has been sharply narrowed. Children grow up inside different social densities. Marriages occur inside different pools. Institutions either replenish themselves or slowly become heritage organizations.

Restriction therefore changes not only how many people enter but how the people already inside become American.

That is another reason the later acceptance of once-disfavored European groups should not be read as proof that the earlier fears simply dissolved on their own. Time mattered. Schooling mattered. Military service, labor, suburbanization, intermarriage, politics, economic mobility, and changing racial boundaries mattered. So did the legal interruption of the migration patterns that had made those groups seem perpetually new.

The country changed the flow and then watched the stock assimilate.

By the time lawmakers dismantled the national-origins quotas, the social world that made repeal possible had been partly produced by the restriction itself. That does not make repeal inevitable or restriction self-correcting. It means institutions alter the electorate and social landscape that later judge them.

Policy creates some of the conditions of its own revision.

That is a more unsettling form of path dependence than simple bureaucratic inertia. The law does not merely persist because offices are slow to change. It helps shape the population, interests, memories, and identities that will decide whether change is desirable.

The immigration system was therefore never only a gate at the edge of the country.

It was one of the mechanisms by which the country decided who would be present to argue about the next gate.
