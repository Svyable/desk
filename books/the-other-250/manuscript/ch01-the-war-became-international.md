# The War Became International

On February 6, 1778, the United States acquired something it had not possessed when it declared independence: an ally powerful enough to make Britain fight a different war.

The date is easy to pass over because the American Revolution has a more famous calendar. Lexington and Concord. July 4. Washington crossing the Delaware. Saratoga. Yorktown. The sequence is so familiar that the story can begin to feel self-contained, as though thirteen colonies discovered a reservoir of courage, declared themselves a country, and then defeated an empire through persistence.

Courage mattered. Persistence mattered. So did France.

The distinction is more than a correction to national vanity. The French alliance changed the strategic problem facing Britain. Before it, Britain was suppressing a rebellion inside its own empire. After it, Britain was at war with a European great power whose navy, money, arms, troops, and overseas interests could stretch British attention from North America to the Caribbean, the Atlantic, the Mediterranean, Africa, and India. Spain entered the war against Britain in 1779, though not as a formal American ally, and the Netherlands followed in 1780. A colonial rebellion had become part of a global conflict.

That change did not happen merely because France admired American liberty.

French policy had its own memory. Britain had defeated France in the Seven Years' War, ending in 1763 with major losses of French territory and prestige. Weakening Britain was therefore a strategic objective before the Continental Congress ever sent Benjamin Franklin across the Atlantic. France secretly supplied the Americans before a formal alliance existed. Arms, ammunition, uniforms, money, and other matériel flowed through arrangements designed to give the rebellion a better chance without immediately producing open war between France and Britain.

The Americans, for their part, understood that independence was an international claim. A declaration could announce sovereignty, but other countries would decide whether to recognize it, trade with it, lend to it, and risk war on its behalf. The Continental Congress created a diplomatic effort because military resistance alone could not turn a rebellion into an accepted state.

Franklin became the most famous face of that effort, and deservedly so, but the mythology that later gathered around his fur cap can obscure the harder problem. France had to decide whether the Americans were a useful bet. Supporting them too openly before they looked capable of survival could invite a costly war with Britain on behalf of a movement that might collapse. The Americans therefore needed not only sympathy but evidence.

Saratoga supplied it.

In October 1777, a British army under General John Burgoyne surrendered to American forces after the Saratoga campaign. The victory did not end the war. It did something more immediately useful in Paris: it demonstrated that the American rebellion could defeat a major British field army. The possibility of American independence became less theoretical.

The diplomatic result arrived in February. France and the United States signed two treaties, one of amity and commerce and one of alliance. France formally recognized the United States. The new commercial relationship mattered. The military alliance mattered more.

It is tempting to narrate the moment as if the alliance solved the American problem. It did not. The war continued for years. The Continental Army still suffered from supply shortages, weak finances, expiring enlistments, political friction, and the basic difficulty of holding together a confederation whose central government had little capacity to tax. French and American objectives could diverge. Coordination between allies was imperfect. European states acted from interest, not as supporting characters in an American morality play.

But the scale of the change is hard to overstate.

Britain now had to protect more than the thirteen colonies. French naval power threatened valuable British possessions in the Caribbean. The English Channel itself required attention. British forces and ships became part of a wider allocation problem. Every regiment and vessel committed somewhere else was a resource unavailable for use against the Americans.

That is one reason the alliance is under-appreciated. Its most important effects include events that did not happen in the United States at all. A ship sent to defend a sugar island can matter to a campaign in Virginia because it is not available in Chesapeake Bay. A war that expands geographically changes local odds without leaving a local monument.

The alliance also altered American access to the material means of war. The romantic image of a revolution tends to privilege motivation. Men decide they will be free, take up arms, and somehow convert conviction into victory. Armies require less romantic things. Muskets. Powder. Clothing. Food. Ships. Credit. Transport. Training. Cash. A government that struggled to collect revenue could not simply conjure these resources from patriotic feeling.

Foreign support entered that gap.

French assistance before and after the formal alliance provided weapons and supplies when American procurement was fragile. French loans and subsidies helped keep a government with chronically weak finances functioning. French officers served in American forces, though the quality and motives of foreign volunteers varied. Eventually a French expeditionary army under the comte de Rochambeau operated alongside Washington's forces.

Then came the sea.

The Yorktown campaign is often remembered as Washington's decisive triumph, which it was in an important sense. Yet the surrender of Lord Cornwallis in October 1781 depended on a strategic arrangement the Continental Army could not have produced by itself. French troops marched with the Americans. French naval forces under the comte de Grasse gained control of the Chesapeake approaches at the crucial moment. A British relief fleet failed to break that control. Cornwallis's army, pressed on land and unable to rely on rescue by sea, was trapped.

The siege was American and French. The strategic seal around it was international.

Remove French participation and the word “inevitable” disappears from the Revolution very quickly.

That does not mean independence would certainly have failed without France. Counterfactual history becomes dishonest when uncertainty is dressed as proof. Britain faced a difficult political and military challenge before the alliance. Distance was enormous. The colonies were geographically broad. American resistance had local support sufficient to sustain a long war in many places. British domestic opinion was divided. The costs of occupation and pacification were real. It is possible that the Americans could have survived long enough to obtain independence under a different sequence of events.

Possible is not the same as likely, and it is certainly not the same as demonstrated.

The stronger claim is narrower: French intervention substantially improved the American position and forced Britain to allocate military attention across a global war. The actual road to Yorktown ran through French diplomacy, finance, troops, and naval power. That road was not incidental.

Why, then, does the alliance tend to sit below the Declaration, Valley Forge, or Yorktown in popular memory?

Partly because nations prefer founding stories with a single protagonist. The United States is especially attached to self-reliance as a national virtue. A revolution won with decisive external assistance is harder to compress into a clean origin myth than a revolution won by citizen-soldiers refusing to surrender.

Partly because diplomacy is structurally bad at monuments. A treaty signed in Paris lacks the visual clarity of a battlefield. Financial support is difficult to paint. Naval deployments across several theaters resist simple narration. The work of making Britain face too many obligations at once looks less dramatic than a charge across a field even when it may matter more.

And partly because Yorktown absorbs the alliance into an American climax. The French are present in many tellings, but often as help arriving inside a story whose causal center remains Washington. Change the camera angle and the event looks different. Yorktown was not merely an American victory with French assistance. It was the North American culmination of a war whose strategic geometry had changed when France openly entered it.

The consequences outlasted the surrender.

Recognition matters in state formation. The United States' existence became a fact other powers had acted upon before Britain formally accepted independence in the 1783 Treaty of Paris. Commerce and diplomacy gave the new republic relationships external to the British imperial system. French support also left debts and expectations. Alliances create obligations as well as advantages, and American leaders would spend the 1790s arguing bitterly over the meaning of the French relationship as revolutionary France went to war with European powers.

The alliance that helped secure independence later became a test of how independent American foreign policy actually was.

That complication is useful. The event does not offer a simple lesson that alliances are always good, or that foreign aid determines wars. France's intervention was expensive. The French monarchy's financial problems had many causes and cannot be reduced to American support, but participation in the war added to fiscal strain in the years before the French Revolution. American gratitude did not produce permanent policy alignment. George Washington's administration eventually pursued neutrality in the European war that began after the French Revolution, while arguments over treaty obligations fed the emerging American party system.

The 1778 alliance mattered precisely because alliances are not acts of charity. They are arrangements between governments whose interests overlap for a time.

For the Americans, the overlap was extraordinary. France wanted Britain weakened. The United States wanted to survive. A European rivalry that predated American independence became one of the conditions that made independence more attainable.

Seen from 1776, this was hardly guaranteed. The Declaration announced a country into a world of monarchies and empires. Its last paragraph claimed that the colonies were “Free and Independent States” with the power to wage war, make peace, contract alliances, and establish commerce. Those were not decorative verbs. They described the external powers of sovereignty the Americans now had to make real.

The treaty with France was an early proof that they could.

It also exposes a recurring feature of American history: domestic capability often rests on international systems that disappear from the national story. The Revolution required transatlantic finance and weapons. Nineteenth-century growth would depend on migration, foreign capital, imported knowledge, commodity markets, and territorial struggles involving other powers. Twentieth-century power would be built through alliances, trade routes, supply chains, overseas bases, scientific exchange, and institutions whose benefits were easiest to notice when they failed.

Self-government and self-sufficiency are not the same thing.

There is another reason to dwell on February 1778 rather than treat it as diplomatic scenery. It restores contingency to the founding. The United States did not emerge from a closed experiment conducted on the eastern edge of North America. It emerged within an Atlantic world of empires, slave economies, commercial rivalries, dynastic competition, maritime power, and war. American leaders had ideals, but they also had to navigate that system. Their enemy had enemies. Their cause needed credit. Their army needed powder. Their independence needed recognition.

The achievement becomes more interesting when the dependencies are visible.

A mature national story should be able to hold both ideas at once. The Americans created a political movement capable of sustaining rebellion against Britain. They also benefited decisively from the choices of another state pursuing its own strategic interests. Neither fact cancels the other.

The alliance also changes the meaning of Saratoga. In a battlefield-centered history, Saratoga is important because an American army defeated Burgoyne. In a systems history, its second-order effect may be even more consequential: it changed France's estimate of the rebellion. An event in upstate New York altered a calculation in Versailles, which altered naval and military allocations across an empire, which helped produce conditions in Virginia four years later.

That is what a hinge looks like when the hinge crosses an ocean.

The wider war deserves one more look because it explains what “international” really meant.

France's entry did not merely add French units to an American order of battle. It forced Britain to rank interests that had previously been handled inside a more concentrated war. The British Empire was a commercial and territorial system. Its Caribbean sugar colonies were enormously valuable. Sea lanes mattered. Gibraltar mattered. India mattered. The home islands mattered. Once France, Spain, and eventually the Dutch Republic were in conflict with Britain, North America became one theater among several.

That altered British decision-making even when no French soldier was present beside an American one.

Spain's role illustrates the point. Spain did not sign the American alliance and had its own reasons to fear republican rebellion inside empires. It entered the war against Britain in 1779 because the European balance and its own territorial objectives made the conflict useful. Spanish forces under Bernardo de Gálvez attacked British positions along the Gulf Coast and lower Mississippi and later captured Pensacola. Those campaigns were not sentimental assistance to American liberty. They nevertheless complicated Britain's southern and western position.

The Dutch entry did something similar at a different scale. Britain went to war with the Dutch Republic in 1780 amid disputes involving trade, neutrality, and support flowing toward Britain's enemies. Again, the result was not a tidy coalition assembled around the Declaration of Independence. It was a widening European struggle in which the American rebellion benefited from Britain's multiplying obligations.

This is why the phrase “foreign assistance” can be misleadingly small.

Assistance sounds as though an outside actor chose to help an American project while remaining outside the project. What happened after 1778 was larger. The strategic environment itself changed. Britain had to ask not merely how to defeat Washington but how to allocate naval and military power across an empire while facing other great powers.

The Americans' advantage was partly the benefit of becoming one front in somebody else's war.

That fact should not be embarrassing. It should make the founding more realistic.

Small and newly declared states often survive by finding larger states whose interests temporarily align with theirs. Diplomacy is one of the technologies of survival. The founders understood this well enough to put the power to contract alliances into the Declaration's final claim of sovereignty.

The later United States would repeatedly relearn the same lesson from the opposite position.

In the twentieth century, American leaders would build alliances in Europe and Asia not because allies shared every interest or political habit but because coalitions changed the strategic arithmetic facing rivals. Bases, intelligence, finance, logistics, interoperability, markets, and diplomatic recognition can matter as much as battlefield valor.

The founding alliance is an early American case of that logic.

It also complicates the peace.

By 1782, after Yorktown had transformed British politics and the prospects for continued war, American diplomats had to negotiate an independence settlement inside an alliance whose members did not have identical objectives. France had commitments to Spain. Spain had territorial goals, including interests along the Mississippi and a desire to recover Gibraltar. American claims to western land and navigation could conflict with other powers' preferences.

The American commissioners ultimately negotiated preliminary peace terms directly with Britain rather than perfectly following the consultation expectations of the French alliance. France did not become an enemy as a result, but the episode revealed what had always been true: the partnership was based on overlapping interests, not merged sovereignty.

That is the full life cycle of the hinge.

First the United States needed another power badly enough to make an alliance.

Then it needed enough independence to disagree with that ally about peace.

The diplomatic achievement was not merely obtaining help. It was using help without becoming a client state permanently bound to another government's goals.

That balance was difficult because the United States began the relationship from weakness. It needed money it could not easily raise, military supplies it could not reliably manufacture, naval capacity it did not possess, and recognition it could not award itself.

Dependence is most politically uncomfortable when it is also strategically necessary.

The later memory of self-reliance smooths over that discomfort. But the dependence is analytically useful because it reminds us that sovereignty is not the same as autonomy in every domain. A state can be politically independent and materially dependent on foreign credit, weapons, shipping, technology, markets, or allies.

The newborn United States was sovereign in aspiration before it was self-sufficient in capability.

France helped close part of that gap.

The Revolution's best-known sentence begins with truths held to be self-evident. The survival of the polity that wrote it was not self-evident at all.

In 1778, the war acquired a new balance of risk. Britain could no longer decide how much force to devote to America without asking what France might do elsewhere. The United States acquired recognition, commerce, material support, and eventually the naval partner it lacked. Spain and the Dutch Republic widened the conflict further for their own reasons. The rebellion became one theater in a global contest, and that expansion made British victory harder.

A country that would later tell itself a story about standing alone survived its founding war in part because it did not.

The alliance also changed the Americans' relationship to time.

Before formal French entry, a bad American campaign season could threaten the rebellion directly. A lost army, an exhausted treasury, or another winter of shortages might bring the experiment closer to collapse before the political argument had time to mature. After 1778, time worked differently because Britain could no longer concentrate on ending the rebellion alone. The war had acquired other clocks: convoy seasons, naval deployments, Caribbean campaigns, European diplomacy, Spanish operations, Dutch trade disputes, and the constant possibility that a crisis far from the thirteen states would redirect ships or money.

That did not make American survival automatic. It made survival more plausible as a strategy.

A weaker belligerent rarely needs to defeat a stronger one everywhere. It needs to keep the stronger power from converting superiority into a decisive political result. The wider war helped the United States do that. Every additional theater complicated Britain's ability to impose a clean ending in North America. Delay became less a sign that the rebellion was merely failing slowly and more a way to let costs, elections, diplomatic pressures, and competing imperial priorities accumulate.

This matters because wars are political competitions over endurance as much as military contests over territory. An empire can win battles and still lose the argument over how much more effort the objective deserves. A rebellion can lose cities and still remain alive if its institutions, armies, credit, and foreign relationships keep enough of the project functioning.

French entry changed the price Britain paid for additional time.

It also changed what the Americans could promise one another. Alliances operate abroad, but their effects reach inward. A government that can point to recognition by a major power has a different claim on its own citizens, creditors, soldiers, and skeptical local governments than one asking them to endure indefinitely on faith. Recognition does not settle domestic legitimacy, yet it supplies evidence that the outside world has begun treating the political experiment as something more durable than an insurrection awaiting suppression.

That evidence can affect behavior before it affects constitutional theory.

A merchant deciding whether to extend credit, a soldier deciding whether another enlistment is worth the risk, or a state government deciding how much scarce material to commit does not need to believe victory is certain. Each needs some basis for believing that sacrifice is not being poured into a project whose strategic position is steadily narrowing. The French alliance could not solve every procurement or morale problem, but it changed the information available to people making those decisions.

The rebellion had acquired a powerful witness with money, ships, and reasons of its own to keep Britain occupied.

There is a useful asymmetry here. France did not need the United States to become strong enough to replace Britain as a global power. It needed the rebellion to survive and impose costs. The United States, meanwhile, needed France to do things it could not yet do for itself. The two governments therefore entered the alliance with very different capacities and somewhat different measures of success.

That is often true in alliances between unequal partners.

The weaker party gains capabilities it cannot generate quickly. The stronger party gains leverage, geography, intelligence, distraction, legitimacy for a wider strategy, or a cheaper way to burden a rival. Those interests can align without becoming identical. They can also diverge the moment the weaker partner becomes more secure or the stronger partner's priorities change.

Seeing 1778 this way makes the alliance less sentimental and more impressive.

The Americans did not persuade France to adopt the Revolution as its own moral project. They persuaded a monarchy with its own strategic ambitions that American survival was worth backing openly. That required battlefield evidence, diplomacy, and enough institutional persistence for the new state to appear capable of remaining on the board.

The achievement was therefore partly military and partly reputational.

Saratoga did not merely destroy Burgoyne's army. It changed an assessment.

The treaties did not merely add signatures. They changed expectations.

Yorktown did not merely reward Washington's persistence. It revealed what could happen once those altered expectations had been converted into ships, troops, credit, coordination, and pressure across several theaters.

The chain is easy to flatten when history is told backward from independence. Seen forward, each link was contingent. France could have delayed. American armies could have collapsed. British leaders could have found a more effective combination of force and political accommodation. Allied coordination could have failed at a critical moment.

None of those possibilities is visible if independence is treated as the natural destination of 1776.

The alliance is valuable precisely because it prevents that mistake.

It reminds us that declarations create obligations faster than capabilities. The United States claimed the powers of a sovereign state before it possessed many of the instruments needed to exercise them reliably. Diplomacy became one way to borrow capability while domestic institutions were still being built.

That is not a footnote to independence.

For a fragile state, borrowing capability can be one of the ways independence survives long enough to become real.
