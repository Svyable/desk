# The Day Medicine Went Digital

On February 17, 2009, the United States put electronic medical records inside an economic rescue bill.

That is not how anyone remembers the financial crisis.

The winter of 2009 is remembered through collapsing banks, emptying retirement accounts, foreclosed homes, emergency lending, stimulus checks, arguments over deficits, and a new president warning that the economy was in a crisis unlike anything Americans had seen in generations. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act was a vast piece of legislation built for that emergency. Roads, schools, energy, unemployment insurance, tax relief and state budgets all competed for attention.

Buried inside was a decision about the most intimate archive most people will ever have: the record of what has happened to their bodies.

The Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act, better known as HITECH, did not invent the electronic health record. Hospitals and doctors had been buying computer systems for years. Large medical centers had laboratories, billing systems, imaging networks and digital records long before 2009. The federal government had already created an Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology. Technology companies had been promising the paperless clinic since computers first became small enough to imagine one.

But the promise and the practice were different things.

In 2008, only about nine percent of non-federal acute-care hospitals had the set of computerized capabilities that researchers described as a basic electronic health record. Many institutions used computers for some functions while the legal and clinical center of the visit remained a paper chart. A patient could have an MRI produced by a machine capable of reconstructing the inside of a skull in exquisite detail and still watch a nurse pull a folder from a wall. A physician could receive laboratory results electronically and then write a medication order by hand. A hospital could spend millions on technology while information remained trapped in departments that did not speak the same digital language.

This was not simply technological backwardness. Medicine is unusually difficult to computerize.

A clinical record is not a bank balance. It is a running argument about a human being. It includes objective measurements, uncertain diagnoses, copied history, provisional judgments, images, medications, allergies, billing codes, nursing observations, pathology, insurance requirements, patient statements, specialist opinions and the residue of earlier errors. The information changes meaning as the patient changes. A sentence that is useful in an emergency department can be misleading ten years later. A drug that was stopped for one reason may look, without context, as though it was stopped for another. A diagnosis can be both necessary for reimbursement and more definite than the doctor actually feels.

Paper handled this mess badly in obvious ways. It could be illegible, unavailable, duplicated, damaged or locked in a filing room across town. Yet paper had one enormous advantage: it fit the habits of medicine because medicine had grown around it.

Turning the record into software meant turning habits into fields, menus, permissions, alerts, codes and workflows. Every choice about the interface became a choice about how medical work would be performed.

HITECH pushed the country across that threshold.

The law used a mechanism that was more ambitious than simply buying computers for hospitals. It tied Medicare and Medicaid incentive payments to the adoption and use of certified electronic health-record technology. The phrase that mattered was “meaningful use.” The government was not supposed to reward a hospital merely for owning a server or purchasing software. Providers had to demonstrate that they were using certified systems in prescribed ways. The accompanying federal rules specified objectives, certification standards and capabilities. Incentive payments began in 2011.

The distinction sounds bureaucratic. It changed the market.

Before HITECH, the decision to computerize remained, in many organizations, one capital project among many. A hospital could postpone it. A physician nearing retirement could decide the learning curve was not worth the trouble. A small rural practice could look at the price of a system and the disruption of converting years of paper charts and sensibly put the project off another year.

After HITECH, delay had a different price.

Federal money lowered the cost of adoption. Certification reduced some uncertainty about what systems needed to do. Technical-assistance programs helped smaller practices. Medicare and Medicaid became levers for changing behavior across a fragmented industry. The government did not nationalize medical records or build a single federal chart. It changed the economics of private adoption so sharply that the default moved.

The numbers moved with it.

Hospital electronic-record adoption had been rising before 2009, and it would be too neat to attribute every installation afterward to one statute. Computers were getting cheaper. Networks were improving. Health systems were consolidating. Physicians were already demanding faster access to laboratory and imaging information. Insurers and administrators wanted structured data. Patients increasingly expected digital services in the rest of their lives.

HITECH did not create those pressures. It concentrated them.

Within a few years, electronic records moved from an uneven advantage of large and technically sophisticated institutions toward a near-universal operating system for American medicine. By the end of the 2010s, federal measurements of basic and certified hospital electronic-record adoption had effectively converged. More than ninety-five percent of hospitals possessed an electronic health record. By the middle of the 2020s, the federal government no longer needed to tell the story primarily as one of adoption. The record had become infrastructure.

That success is why the event is under-appreciated.

Americans usually notice infrastructure when it is missing. Nobody gives a speech because the electric outlet worked. A road becomes a political subject when the bridge closes. A payment network becomes visible when the card fails. The electronic health record was most politically salient while it was being installed, when doctors complained about clicking through screens and hospitals worried about cost. Once the system became normal, the argument moved to everything wrong with the system.

There is plenty wrong with it.

The first generation of nationwide digitization often made physicians feel less, not more, technologically liberated. A computer that could theoretically make the entire chart available at once could also require dozens of clicks to perform a simple task. Templates produced notes that were longer while sometimes being less informative. Copy-and-paste made it easy for yesterday's assumptions to become today's apparent facts. Billing requirements colonized the clinical record. Alert systems designed to prevent mistakes could generate so many warnings that users learned to dismiss them. The promise that a patient’s record would follow the patient ran into incompatible systems, business incentives, privacy rules, local custom and vendors with little reason to make switching easy.

Doctors acquired a new physical posture: body turned partly away from the patient, eyes on a screen.

This was not incidental. Any claim that HITECH simply modernized medicine has to survive the experience of the people who had to use what modernization produced.

The important distinction is between digitization and good digitization.

HITECH achieved the first much faster than the second.

That matters because the first was a prerequisite for almost every health-technology argument that came after it.

Consider what a paper-based medical system can and cannot do at national scale. It can provide excellent care inside a room. A great physician with a paper chart may understand a patient better than a distracted physician with the finest software. No national statistic should erase that. But paper cannot be searched across millions of encounters in seconds. It cannot support an application programming interface. It cannot produce a medication list remotely without someone finding and transmitting the chart. It cannot automatically check a laboratory value against a prescribing rule. It cannot let a patient download a longitudinal record from a portal at midnight. It cannot feed a modern health-information exchange without first being converted into data.

Once the record becomes digital, an entirely different set of possibilities appears.

Some are mundane. A prescription can be sent electronically. A patient can receive a test result without waiting for a letter. A physician can see the radiology report before the patient returns. A nurse can reconcile medications against a structured list. An emergency department may be able to retrieve information from another institution.

Some are managerial. Health systems can measure performance with a granularity impossible in a paper world. Insurers can demand structured documentation. Researchers can identify cohorts from clinical databases. Quality programs can turn chart elements into metrics. Administrators can know more about what clinicians do, how long it takes and what gets billed.

Some are commercial. Electronic health-record vendors became enormously consequential intermediaries in American medicine. Their databases, interfaces and standards helped determine how information moved. A hospital changing EHR systems was no longer replacing software in the ordinary sense. It was undertaking something closer to a nervous-system transplant.

And some consequences were not visible in 2009 at all.

The explosion of artificial intelligence in medicine during the 2020s depends on a world in which health information already exists as machine-readable material. Large language models can summarize notes only because notes are digital. Algorithms can search for patterns in imaging, laboratory or clinical records only because those records can be made computationally accessible. Ambient transcription can promise to free physicians from typing because the destination for the transcript is an electronic chart. Patient-facing agents can retrieve histories, explain results or coordinate appointments because there is a digital layer to connect to.

The quality, ownership and interoperability of that layer now matter enormously.

The odd thing about technological revolutions is that the dramatic invention is often less important than the boring conversion that makes it usable.

Artificial intelligence looks like an invention. A national electronic-record infrastructure looks like paperwork.

Without the second, much of the first has nowhere to go.

This is one reason the 2009 event deserves more weight than it receives. HITECH did not merely encourage hospitals to replace folders with screens. It helped turn the clinical encounter into a source of structured, persistent, networked data.

That change redistributed power.

In a paper office, the physician and the local institution possess much of the practical information advantage. The chart is where the chart is. A patient can request copies, but friction is enormous. In a digital system, the information can theoretically move. That does not mean it always does, or that patients automatically control it. It means the political question changes. Instead of asking whether the record can be copied at all, policymakers can ask who must provide access, under what technical standards, at what speed, through which interfaces and with what restrictions.

The later battles over interoperability, information blocking, patient APIs and data exchange belong to the world HITECH accelerated into existence.

So do the later battles over surveillance and privacy.

A filing cabinet is a terrible database and a fairly good privacy device. Access requires physical proximity or deliberate copying. A digital chart is vastly more useful and vastly easier to reproduce. The same architecture that allows a specialist to retrieve critical history can expose thousands of patients when credentials are stolen or a network is compromised. Health care became a major target for ransomware in part because digital dependence made interruption so costly. A hospital can keep treating some patients during a computer outage, but a modern institution quickly discovers how many ordinary functions assume the system is alive.

The digital record therefore did something paradoxical. It made information more durable while making access to that information newly fragile.

A paper chart can burn. A digital health system can stop an entire organization at once.

This is the pattern throughout this book. Under-appreciated events are rarely clean victories. They are usually choices that create a new capability and therefore create a new category of failure.

The Erie Canal made transport cheaper and accelerated ecological and social disruption. The telegraph made communication faster and concentrated power in networks. Federal weather observation made forecasting possible and made society increasingly dependent on centralized information. The Pure Food and Drugs Act increased trust in national markets and expanded the federal role in defining acceptable products.

HITECH belongs in that lineage.

It standardized expectations without standardizing every system. It encouraged private organizations to build public-facing infrastructure. It made the medical record more useful by making it more legible to machines, and in doing so made medicine more legible to institutions that wanted to measure it.

That last consequence deserves attention because the record is not simply a neutral container.

Once information is structured, what gets a field can become more visible than what does not. A blood pressure has a number. A diagnosis has a code. A medication has an identifier. A physician’s uncertainty is harder to encode. A patient’s social circumstances may be reduced to a checkbox or not recorded at all. The system can create an illusion that what is easily counted is what matters most.

This is not a reason to prefer paper. It is a reason to understand what digitization actually does.

A digital record converts part of the mess of illness into an administrative language. That language can travel farther than the original encounter. It can be copied, searched, billed, audited, studied, exchanged and analyzed. Every one of those verbs can help a patient. Every one can also serve an institution whose interests are not identical to the patient’s.

HITECH’s under-appreciated importance lies precisely here: it changed the scale at which those verbs became possible.

The legislation also offers a useful lesson in how the American state often changes technology. The United States did not usually compel every physician to use a single government-designed system. It used reimbursement, certification, grants, technical assistance and standards to alter the private market. The resulting ecosystem was decentralized, commercially diverse, administratively complex and often frustrating. It was also enormous.

This is a recurring American form of industrial policy, even when the country avoids the phrase.

Government sets a condition on money. Private firms build to meet it. Standards harden. Adoption follows. A fragmented landscape gradually becomes a system.

The same pattern can be seen in mortgage finance, highways, university research, defense procurement, retirement benefits and, later, semiconductor manufacturing. The boundary between “public” and “private” becomes less useful than the flow of incentives between them.

HITECH was particularly powerful because Medicare and Medicaid sit near the center of American health economics. When the country’s largest public health programs change the conditions under which money moves, private institutions notice. That leverage turned a policy objective into a purchasing signal felt by vendors, hospitals and physicians across the country.

The result was not a single national health record. America built something more characteristically American: thousands of institutions using a smaller number of dominant platforms, governed by federal standards, reimbursement rules, privacy law, local practice and contracts.

It is tempting to judge the 2009 decision by asking whether electronic records made care cheaper or doctors happier.

Those are reasonable questions, but neither is sufficient.

Technologies often arrive with benefits that are different from the benefits used to justify them. The interstate highway system was a mobility project that also transformed land use. GPS was a military navigation system that became part of daily civilian life. The internet was not built so people could summon a car with a phone. A platform creates options; society chooses among them later.

HITECH created options.

Some were realized quickly. Electronic prescribing, patient portals and computerized records became normal. Some remain unfinished. Seamless exchange among institutions is still less seamless than the word suggests. Patient access improved dramatically without becoming universal comprehension or control. The United States digitized medical information faster than it solved the problem of making that information useful across organizational boundaries.

And some options are only now becoming visible.

By 2026, electronic-record adoption is so complete in hospitals that the more interesting questions concern what sits on top of the record: automated documentation, clinical decision support, patient access, interoperability, research networks and artificial intelligence. These systems inherit every strength and every defect of the data beneath them. If the chart contains copied errors, the model can summarize copied errors beautifully. If the data are fragmented, intelligence operating on the data inherits fragmentation. If the record was designed primarily for billing, a more sophisticated interface does not magically transform the incentives embedded in it.

Infrastructure is destiny only in the weakest sense. It does not determine what happens next. It determines what is easy next.

HITECH made digital medicine easy enough to become mandatory in practice even where it was not mandatory in name.

There are other candidates for the most under-appreciated American event of the 2000s. The financial crisis produced reforms that still shape banking. The creation and expansion of the Department of Homeland Security remade security bureaucracy after September 11. The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq changed foreign policy, military families, public trust and the federal budget. The smartphone began remaking social life before the decade ended.

Choosing a provision of a stimulus law over those events is intentionally provocative.

The case is not that HITECH was bigger than the financial crisis or September 11. It plainly was not. The case is that its importance is more under-valued relative to how much of the present it quietly supports.

A person born in 2009 can reach adulthood in a country where the concept of a paper medical record already feels antique. That person’s vaccination history, prescriptions, diagnoses, laboratory results, imaging reports and messages may exist across digital systems for an entire lifetime. Their health may be managed partly through software that would have been impossible to deploy nationally in the paper world. Their privacy risks will also be different. Their physician may spend less time looking at a clipboard and more time negotiating what software asks to be documented. Their care may one day involve algorithms trained on records produced by millions of patients who never imagined their charts as computational material.

The decisive turn did not arrive with a robot surgeon or a miraculous drug.

It arrived as reimbursement policy.

That is why February 17, 2009 belongs in this book.

The American medical system did not wake up the next morning transformed. Hospitals still had paper. Doctors still dictated notes. Vendors still sold incompatible systems. Patients still carried folders between specialists.

But the economic direction had changed.

A decade later, the record was digital almost everywhere that mattered. Another decade later, machines were beginning to read it.

The quiet event was not the invention of electronic medicine.

It was the moment the United States decided that electronic medicine would no longer be optional infrastructure.
