# The Return of the Factory State

On August 9, 2022, President Joe Biden signed the CHIPS and Science Act into law.

The photographs looked familiar: politicians around a desk, pens held up, flags behind them, a bill becoming a law in the practiced theater of Washington.

The underlying decision was stranger.

The United States was going to pay companies to build semiconductor manufacturing capacity on American soil.

Not merely fund basic science. Not merely buy chips for the military. Not merely cut corporate taxes and hope factories followed. The federal government would make direct incentives available for semiconductor fabrication and related facilities, support research and development, attach conditions to awards, and use public money to influence where some of the most advanced industrial capacity in the world would be built.

For a country whose political vocabulary had spent decades treating “industrial policy” as something other countries did, the shift was easy to underestimate.

The CHIPS Act did not arrive from nowhere.

The federal government has shaped American industry throughout the nation’s history. Alexander Hamilton’s generation argued over tariffs, debt and manufacturing. The government granted land and support to railroads. Federal purchasing helped create industries from aircraft to computers. Defense contracts helped sustain semiconductor research and early demand. The interstate highway system transformed logistics. Agricultural policy reshaped farming. The space program created markets for advanced engineering. Tax policy has always favored some investments over others, even when politicians preferred to describe those effects as neutral.

America never stopped having industrial policy.

It often stopped calling it that.

By the late twentieth century, the dominant public story was that markets should decide where production occurred. Government could fund research, protect property rights, enforce contracts, train workers, maintain infrastructure and perhaps respond to national-security exceptions. But deliberate efforts to build particular industries at home were frequently treated as protectionist, inefficient or nostalgic.

There were good reasons for the skepticism.

Governments can pick losers. Subsidies can become political trophies. Firms can take public money for investments they would have made anyway. Local officials can compete away the value of a project through tax breaks. A factory can be announced with great ceremony and still arrive late, over budget or not at all. Political goals can conflict with commercial logic. Once an industry learns that strategic importance attracts money, every industry can discover a strategic argument.

The market critique of industrial policy is not a straw man. It is a warning backed by experience.

The remarkable fact of the early 2020s is that the warning did not disappear. It was overruled by a different fear.

Dependence had become visible.

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed supply chains that had been optimized for efficiency and then tested by disruption. Shortages appeared in places consumers could see: masks, medicines, appliances, cars. Automakers slowed or stopped production because they could not get enough semiconductors. A component most drivers never thought about suddenly determined whether a vehicle could leave a factory.

At the same time, the strategic importance of Taiwan became harder to separate from the strategic importance of chips. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company had become the world’s dominant manufacturer of the most advanced logic semiconductors, while American firms remained leaders in design, equipment, software and other parts of the ecosystem. The geography of fabrication had become a national-security concern.

The problem was not that the United States had forgotten how to invent semiconductors.

The problem was that invention and production had separated.

That distinction matters because modern manufacturing knowledge lives partly in factories.

A semiconductor fab is not a warehouse containing machines that can be purchased, installed and switched on like office printers. Leading-edge production requires extraordinary equipment, clean rooms, chemicals, materials, process control, suppliers, trained workers and accumulated tacit knowledge. Yield—the share of chips that emerge usable—depends on learning that happens through production. A country can lead in design while becoming dependent on manufacturing capacity located elsewhere.

Efficiency made that arrangement economically attractive.

Geopolitics made it look brittle.

The CHIPS and Science Act was an answer to that brittleness.

It authorized a large federal intervention aimed at semiconductor manufacturing, research and the surrounding innovation system. The manufacturing incentives became the most visible component. Companies could negotiate awards with the Department of Commerce for projects that promised to build or expand American capacity. The law also created restrictions and guardrails intended to keep subsidized firms from using public support to expand certain advanced semiconductor activities in countries of concern.

The government was no longer acting only as customer or regulator.

It was trying to shape the map.

This is why the law is my choice for the most under-appreciated event of the 2020s so far.

The decade is unfinished. That has to be stated plainly.

A book written for the country’s 250th anniversary is choosing the 2020s before the 2020s are over. COVID-19 itself may prove to be the defining event, and by any measure of death, social disruption, education, labor markets, public trust and government policy it is far larger than a semiconductor statute. The January 6 attack on the Capitol, the Dobbs decision, inflation, wars abroad, the rise of generative artificial intelligence, changes in immigration, shifts in energy and the political upheavals of the decade may all matter more.

The claim here is not that CHIPS is the biggest event.

It is that the law may mark a deeper change in American statecraft than the public memory has yet absorbed.

The United States began treating productive capacity itself as a strategic asset.

That is a different way of seeing the economy.

For decades, policy arguments often assumed that if an American company could buy a component more cheaply from abroad, the lower price was a gain unless a narrow security exception applied. Consumers benefited. Firms became more efficient. Capital moved toward higher-value activity. Manufacturing location was important to the workers and communities involved, but in the aggregate model it could look like a detail.

The pandemic and the semiconductor shortage challenged the word “detail.”

A supply chain can be efficient and fragile at the same time.

A factory can be commercially replaceable in theory and strategically irreplaceable during a crisis.

A country can save money for years by concentrating production and then discover that resilience had never appeared on the invoice.

CHIPS made resilience something the government was willing to purchase.

That does not mean it knew the correct price.

The scale of the program created immediate questions. How much subsidy is necessary to make an American fab competitive with facilities in East Asia? Which costs reflect wages, permitting, construction, energy or the maturity of local supplier ecosystems? Would companies have built in the United States anyway because geopolitical risk was already changing corporate strategy? Would the government end up paying firms to follow decisions they had privately reached?

Those questions are central, not peripheral.

Industrial policy is easiest to defend when its costs are invisible. CHIPS made them visible enough to argue about.

The first wave of projects was enormous. TSMC expanded its Arizona plans. Intel pursued major projects in Ohio and elsewhere. Samsung committed to Texas. Micron announced long-term investments in memory manufacturing in New York and Idaho. Suppliers and packaging firms became part of the program as policymakers recognized that a fab alone does not constitute a domestic semiconductor ecosystem.

By early 2025, the Department of Commerce said it had awarded more than thirty-three billion dollars of the manufacturing incentives then allocated across projects in more than twenty states. The public commitments were designed to unlock far larger amounts of private capital.

The exact totals are less important than the mechanism.

Public dollars were being used as anchors for private factories.

That is an old American mechanism wearing twenty-first-century clothes.

The Morrill Act used federal land to capitalize colleges. Railroad policy used land, loans and contracts to accelerate a transportation network. Defense procurement bought early technologies at prices ordinary consumers would not have paid. HITECH used Medicare and Medicaid incentives to move medical records onto digital systems. NASA used milestone payments and contracts to help create a commercial orbital-transportation market.

CHIPS belongs to the same family.

The government identifies a capability it wants to exist at greater scale. It changes the private calculation. If the program works, the resulting capacity eventually looks less like a government project and more like ordinary economic geography.

That last step is where historical memory becomes deceptive.

A university founded with federal land becomes simply a university. A commercial launch company supported by NASA becomes simply a company. A semiconductor fab subsidized into existence may eventually become simply a factory employing thousands of people and buying from local suppliers.

Public scaffolding disappears behind private normality.

The CHIPS Act also exposed how much manufacturing had become a workforce problem.

Building a fab is not only a question of pouring concrete and ordering lithography equipment. Technicians, engineers, construction workers and specialized suppliers must exist in sufficient numbers. Community colleges, universities and workforce programs become part of semiconductor strategy. Immigration policy becomes semiconductor strategy. Housing near project sites becomes semiconductor strategy. Power generation and grid reliability become semiconductor strategy. Water becomes semiconductor strategy.

Industrial policy expands as soon as you try to do it.

That is both its strength and its danger.

A narrow subsidy can reveal the complementary systems the market had previously assembled elsewhere. Policymakers then face a choice: stop at the factory gate or begin solving adjacent bottlenecks.

The same problem appears in every attempt to rebuild physical capacity.

A battery plant needs minerals and processing. A shipyard needs skilled trades and suppliers. A data center needs power and transmission. A pharmaceutical plant needs ingredients, quality systems and trained chemists. A chip fab needs equipment whose own supply chain crosses borders.

The fantasy of total national self-sufficiency collapses quickly under modern industrial complexity.

CHIPS was not a plan for autarky.

It was a plan to reduce dangerous concentration while keeping the United States embedded in allied supply chains.

That distinction is important because “reshoring” can become a slogan that obscures tradeoffs. No advanced economy can efficiently manufacture every component domestically. Resilience may come from diversification across trusted countries rather than reproducing every stage at home. American semiconductor strategy therefore became inseparable from relationships with Japan, South Korea, the Netherlands, Taiwan and other partners.

The factory state is not necessarily a solitary state.

It is a state that cares where factories are.

The durability of that change became clearer after the 2024 election.

A new administration took office in 2025 with different political language, different priorities and criticism of parts of the earlier approach. It did not simply return the United States to the pre-CHIPS assumption that semiconductor capacity was none of Washington’s business. The Department of Commerce renegotiated and restructured arrangements, pressed companies for larger commitments, and in the case of Intel converted part of federal support into an equity stake. By 2026, Commerce was describing semiconductor deals in openly strategic terms and taking credit for increased domestic investment.

The policy changed form across administrations.

The premise survived.

That may be the most historically important evidence in the chapter.

A policy can be partisan when enacted and still reveal a bipartisan shift in the range of acceptable government action. The CHIPS and Science Act itself passed with support from lawmakers in both parties. Later arguments focused on implementation, conditions, cost, tariffs, equity and how aggressively the government should bargain—not on whether semiconductor manufacturing had any legitimate relationship to national power.

The argument had moved.

Once an issue is reframed as state capacity, the old baseline becomes difficult to restore.

Semiconductors made the shift unusually visible because chips sit underneath almost everything else.

Cars use them. Phones use them. weapons use them. Data centers use them. Medical devices use them. Power systems use them. Industrial machines use them. Artificial-intelligence systems depend on vast quantities of advanced processors and memory. A shortage can therefore propagate through sectors that look unrelated.

The AI boom intensified the strategic logic after the law had already passed.

In 2022, generative AI had not yet become the consumer phenomenon it would become after the release of ChatGPT later that year. By the middle of the decade, demand for advanced accelerators, high-bandwidth memory, packaging and data-center infrastructure had turned semiconductor supply into an even more obvious element of economic power.

The timing made CHIPS look prescient to supporters.

It also created a risk of overbuilding.

Capital-intensive industries are vulnerable to cycles. Scarcity produces high prices and political attention. High returns attract investment. Governments subsidize capacity. Companies all expand at once. By the time new factories open, demand may have changed or too much supply may arrive together. Semiconductor history is full of cycles in memory, logic and equipment.

A strategic asset can still be a bad investment.

That sentence should sit near the center of any serious industrial-policy argument.

The government may rationally choose to pay for excess or higher-cost capacity because resilience has value. But if that is the rationale, policymakers should admit it. A factory built partly as insurance does not have to maximize private financial return to have public value. Confusing the two invites dishonest accounting.

The same applies to jobs.

New factories can create well-paid employment and local tax revenue. They can also impose large infrastructure costs, compete for workers, raise housing demand and automate more tasks than ribbon-cutting speeches imply. The number of permanent jobs can be far smaller than the number of construction jobs. Communities that expect a twentieth-century mass-employment factory may discover that twenty-first-century fabs are extraordinarily productive with relatively specialized workforces.

Industrial policy is not a time machine.

It cannot recreate the employment structure of 1965 by building a 2026 factory.

The deeper consequence may be technological rather than nostalgic.

Manufacturing location affects innovation because engineers learn from production. When designers, process engineers, equipment suppliers and manufacturers work near one another, problems can be solved faster. New processes create tacit knowledge. Suppliers cluster. Workers move among firms. A country that loses production can preserve intellectual leadership for a long time and then discover that some forms of invention have followed the factories.

This is the strongest case for CHIPS beyond crisis resilience.

It is not about preserving every old manufacturing job.

It is about preserving the ability to make the frontier physical.

The United States has been extraordinarily good at creating companies whose value resides in software, design, intellectual property and networks. The 2020s raised a question that had been easier to ignore during the long expansion of globalization: what happens when the most important technologies once again require enormous quantities of matter?

Artificial intelligence requires data centers, chips, transformers, power plants and transmission. The energy transition requires mines, refineries, batteries, factories and grids. Biomanufacturing requires facilities. Defense production requires machine tools, explosives, shipyards and skilled labor. Climate adaptation requires concrete, steel and construction capacity.

The digital economy turned out to be physical underneath.

CHIPS was the law in which the federal government openly acted on that realization.

It also changed corporate strategy by changing what executives expected future governments to care about.

A company deciding where to build a fab makes a bet over decades. Construction takes years. Equipment costs billions. A policy that lasts only until the next election is not enough. The durability of public support, trade rules, export controls, tax treatment and national-security policy all influence the calculation.

When multiple administrations treat domestic semiconductor capacity as strategic, the signal becomes stronger than any single subsidy.

This is why the chapter should not end with a list of factories.

Some announced projects will be delayed. Some may shrink. Some may never deliver the promised economic benefits. Technology will change. The frontier node of 2026 will eventually become legacy capacity. Governments will make mistakes. Companies will lobby. Costs will rise. There will be scandals somewhere in a program this large.

The historical question is larger than whether every grant was wise.

Did the United States permanently change how it thinks about the relationship between markets and national capability?

The evidence so far suggests yes.

The change can be seen beyond semiconductors. The same decade brought major public support for batteries, clean energy, infrastructure, critical minerals and regional technology hubs. Tariffs returned as a central instrument of economic policy. Export controls became tools of technological strategy. Supply chains entered ordinary political speech.

Republicans and Democrats disagreed intensely about the instruments, the industries, the climate rationale, labor conditions, China policy, tariffs and the size of government.

They increasingly argued inside a shared world in which production location mattered.

That is the return of the factory state.

Not a return to government-owned steel mills.

Not a command economy.

Not the end of globalization.

A return to the idea that the state has a legitimate interest in the productive structure beneath the market.

America has been here before. Hamilton would recognize the question even if he would not recognize a semiconductor. So would the lawmakers who financed canals, land-grant colleges, railroads, arsenals, aviation, electrification and the space program.

What changed in the late twentieth century was partly memory. Public investment became background. The market appeared natural because earlier government choices had become infrastructure.

CHIPS made the scaffolding visible again.

In that sense, the act is a fitting event for the decade that contains the country’s 250th anniversary.

The first 250 years of the United States are not a story of government or markets winning.

They are a story of the boundary moving.

The country nationalized Revolutionary debt and created private credit. It gave away public land and built universities. It created public weather data used by private commerce. It regulated food to make national markets more trustworthy. It used public reimbursement to make private health systems digital. It used NASA contracts to help build commercial launch.

Then, in 2022, it used public capital to ask private firms to put some of the world’s most sophisticated factories back on American ground.

Whether those factories justify their cost will take years to know.

Whether the policy altered American political economy is easier to see.

A semiconductor fab is too expensive, too strategic and too slow to build for the country to pretend any longer that where things are made is someone else’s problem.

The under-appreciated event of the 2020s may turn out to be the moment America remembered that power has a floor plan.
