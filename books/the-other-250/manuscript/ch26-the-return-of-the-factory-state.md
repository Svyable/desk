# The Return of the Factory State

On August 9, 2022, the United States made an unusually explicit admission.

Markets alone were not going to decide where some of the country’s most important factories would be built.

President Joe Biden signed the CHIPS and Science Act into law that day. The legislation appropriated tens of billions of dollars for semiconductor manufacturing incentives, research, development, and related programs. The Department of Commerce would administer the core CHIPS for America effort, including manufacturing incentives and a large research-and-development portfolio.

The headline was semiconductors.

The deeper event was the return of industrial policy to respectable American language.

The United States had never truly stopped practicing industrial policy.

It had stopped liking the phrase.

For generations, federal purchasing, tax rules, defense contracts, trade policy, agricultural subsidies, infrastructure, research funding, export controls, procurement standards, and regional development programs shaped what Americans made and where they made it. The interstate highway system reorganized logistics. Defense spending sustained aircraft and electronics industries. Federal research helped create technologies later commercialized by private firms. Energy policy repeatedly picked fuels, technologies, and geographies through subsidies and regulation.

Yet by the late twentieth century, the dominant vocabulary in national economic policy often described markets as the allocator and government as the referee.

Factories moved anyway.

Supply chains stretched across oceans.

Companies optimized inventory, labor cost, tax exposure, supplier specialization, and access to fast-growing markets. American consumers gained enormous quantities of inexpensive manufactured goods. American firms became extraordinarily profitable through globally distributed production. Regions that retained specialized manufacturing could thrive; others lost plants, tax bases, supplier networks, and skilled labor.

Semiconductors made the tradeoff difficult to ignore.

A chip is tiny enough to disappear inside almost every modern object and important enough to stop an economy when unavailable.

Cars use them.

Phones use them.

Medical equipment uses them.

Power systems use them.

Data centers use them.

Weapons use them.

Factories use them to make other things.

The modern economy is not merely computerized.

It is physically dependent on a layered semiconductor supply chain involving design software, intellectual property, specialized manufacturing equipment, chemicals, gases, wafers, packaging, testing, fabrication plants, and highly trained workers distributed across several countries.

That distribution had been treated largely as a triumph of specialization.

Then it began to look like a strategic vulnerability.

The COVID-19 pandemic did not create semiconductor dependence. It made dependence visible to people far outside the industry.

Demand shifted abruptly as offices, schools, factories, and households reorganized. Automakers cut orders and then struggled when vehicle demand returned. Consumer electronics surged. Shipping became erratic. Factory shutdowns and public-health restrictions disrupted production. Shortages cascaded into auto plants and other industries.

A missing component costing a few dollars could delay a product worth tens of thousands.

That inversion changed the politics.

Supply chains had been optimized for efficiency.

The new policy word was resilience.

Resilience is expensive because redundancy is expensive.

A second supplier may cost more than the cheapest supplier. Extra inventory ties up capital. Domestic production can face higher construction, labor, permitting, and operating costs. A strategically placed factory may not be the one a company would build if it were choosing solely by expected private return.

If a government wants redundancy that markets do not reward, government has to pay for some of it or change the incentives around it.

The CHIPS Act accepted that logic.

That is the hinge.

The legislation did not nationalize semiconductor companies.

It did not command one federal corporation to build all American chips.

It used grants, loans, tax incentives, research funding, guardrails, and negotiated commitments to alter private investment decisions.

This was neither laissez-faire nor state ownership.

It was government attempting to purchase strategic geography.

The geography mattered because semiconductor production had become concentrated in East Asia, including leading-edge fabrication in Taiwan and major capacity in South Korea, Japan, and elsewhere. The United States remained powerful in chip design, equipment, software, research, and several manufacturing segments, but its share of global fabrication had fallen sharply from earlier decades.

The problem was not simply national pride.

Taiwan sits within one of the most dangerous geopolitical fault lines in the world. Advanced chips made there are essential to global technology companies and military systems. A conflict, blockade, earthquake, infrastructure failure, or other interruption could create consequences far larger than the pandemic shortage.

Economic efficiency had created a security problem.

Security policy began rewriting economic policy.

The CHIPS Act belongs beside export controls imposed on advanced semiconductor technology, government scrutiny of supply chains, allied coordination, and later restrictions aimed at preserving technological advantages over China. Manufacturing policy and national-security policy began merging in a way Americans had not seen so clearly since the Cold War.

That merger is one reason the act matters even if individual subsidies later disappoint.

Industrial policy is not important only when every subsidized factory succeeds.

Its deeper importance appears when both political parties begin arguing over how government should shape production rather than whether government should shape it at all.

By the early 2020s, the debate had moved.

Republicans who had long criticized government attempts to pick winners supported semiconductor incentives on national-security grounds. Democrats who had long supported public investment framed domestic manufacturing as a labor, resilience, innovation, and regional-development priority. Business executives who usually preferred fewer conditions on investment competed for federal support.

The argument shifted from principle to design.

How much should government pay?

Which companies should qualify?

What obligations should recipients accept?

Should subsidies require childcare plans, labor standards, domestic sourcing, workforce training, limits on stock buybacks, or restrictions on expansion in China?

Should government receive equity, warrants, repayments, or other upside when public money helps create private value?

How should awards change when market conditions change?

Those are the questions of a factory state.

Not a state that owns the factory.

A state that negotiates over whether the factory exists, where it exists, and what public purposes it serves.

The distinction is important because industrial policy carries real risks.

Companies are better informed than governments about their own costs and technologies. Firms can exaggerate the public value of projects they already planned to build. States and cities can bid against one another with subsidies that transfer public money without changing national investment. Political leaders can favor visible plants over less photogenic investments in research, training, infrastructure, or supplier networks.

A factory ribbon-cutting is easy to photograph.

A healthy industrial ecosystem is harder.

Semiconductor fabs make the problem extreme.

They are among the most complex manufacturing facilities humans build. A leading-edge fab costs billions of dollars, requires enormous quantities of specialized equipment, precise utilities, reliable power and water, highly trained engineers and technicians, and a network of suppliers whose capabilities take years to develop.

Writing a check does not create the ecosystem.

It can help anchor one.

That difference separates industrial policy from industrial fantasy.

The law therefore has to be judged on more than announced investment totals.

A promised factory is not a functioning factory.

A construction site is not sustained yield.

A fab producing wafers is not necessarily a globally competitive cluster.

The public return depends on whether plants operate, workers are trained, suppliers locate nearby, research produces usable advances, private capital continues after subsidies decline, and the resulting capacity can survive the next technological generation.

Semiconductor manufacturing is particularly unforgiving because leadership moves.

A plant can be technologically advanced when announced and less advanced when completed. Process nodes change. Packaging becomes more important. Artificial-intelligence workloads alter demand. Memory and logic markets cycle. Equipment bottlenecks emerge. Geopolitical rules change the available customer base.

Industrial policy therefore has to learn while it spends.

That is not the kind of government Americans are most comfortable imagining.

The classic bureaucratic model writes a rule and enforces it consistently.

A strategic-investment program has to negotiate with firms whose information changes faster than statutes do.

It must preserve fairness without pretending every project is identical.

It must protect taxpayers without making agreements so rigid that companies decline to invest.

It must move quickly enough to matter without converting urgency into favoritism.

The CHIPS program became a test of whether the American administrative state could behave as a sophisticated investor without ceasing to be a public institution.

That question reached beyond semiconductors.

The Inflation Reduction Act used tax incentives and public spending to reshape clean-energy manufacturing. Infrastructure legislation supported broadband, transportation, and energy systems. Defense policy increasingly emphasized domestic or allied supply chains for critical minerals, batteries, munitions, and electronics.

The pandemic also changed corporate behavior.

Executives who had once described lean global supply chains as unquestionably rational began discussing resilience, dual sourcing, reshoring, nearshoring, and geopolitical exposure. Governments in Europe and Asia expanded their own industrial policies. The United States was not acting in isolation. It was entering a subsidy competition among states that all feared strategic dependence on one another.

That creates another risk.

If every country subsidizes the same industries, global overcapacity can follow. Political alliances can become trade conflicts. Domestic-content rules can undermine partners whose cooperation is necessary for resilience. A policy meant to reduce dependence on one rival can create unnecessary dependence on one’s own expensive capacity.

Resilience is not autarky.

No advanced country can efficiently reproduce the entire semiconductor supply chain within its borders.

The relevant question is which dependencies are tolerable.

That is a political question disguised as an economic one.

Before the 2020s, policymakers could often discuss supply chains as though geography were an optimization variable.

After the pandemic, the Russian invasion of Ukraine, intensifying U.S.-China competition, and repeated disruptions in shipping and energy, geography became strategy.

Factories acquired foreign-policy meaning.

The CHIPS Act made that meaning statutory.

Its downstream effect appeared quickly in announcements of new or expanded semiconductor facilities in Arizona, Ohio, New York, Texas, Idaho, and other states. Major companies including TSMC, Intel, Samsung, Micron, and others announced large U.S. investments, some tied to federal incentives and broader market trends.

The causal claim should be careful.

Not every dollar of announced investment was created by the act.

Companies had commercial reasons to expand. Pandemic shortages had already changed corporate planning. Customers wanted geographic diversification. State governments offered their own incentives. Geopolitical risk had become more expensive even before Congress acted.

Industrial policy works inside those forces.

Its effect is often marginal but decisive: altering the timing, scale, location, or technical ambition of a project that might otherwise happen differently.

That is harder to narrate than a government building a factory itself.

It is also closer to how modern states shape markets.

The title of this chapter—the return of the factory state—is deliberately provocative because the United States never stopped caring about production.

What returned was candor.

Policymakers again spoke openly about manufacturing capacity as a public objective.

For decades, the prevailing assumption in many elite economic debates had been that it mattered less where a product was made than whether Americans could buy it cheaply and firms could obtain it reliably. Comparative advantage would distribute production; trade would allocate resources; innovation and services would replace lower-value manufacturing where appropriate.

That framework captured real gains.

It also underestimated what disappears when production leaves.

Factories carry tacit knowledge.

Workers learn by doing.

Suppliers learn by being nearby.

Engineers solve problems on production lines that cannot be solved in design software alone.

Research and manufacturing can reinforce each other.

A region with no fabrication capability may gradually lose the skilled workforce required to rebuild it quickly.

Industrial ecosystems have memory.

Once forgotten, they are expensive to relearn.

The semiconductor debate turned that insight into policy.

The strongest rival for the 2020s chapter might be Operation Warp Speed and the government-backed acceleration of COVID-19 vaccine development and purchasing. It demonstrated another form of market shaping: federal funding, advance purchase commitments, regulatory coordination, and massive guaranteed demand helped compress timelines for a life-saving technology.

Another rival is the Inflation Reduction Act, whose clean-energy incentives may ultimately produce larger investment and emissions consequences than CHIPS.

A third is the pandemic-era expansion of fiscal support itself, which altered expectations about how aggressively the federal government would respond to economic emergency.

CHIPS wins because it marks the clearest return of production geography as national policy.

A vaccine program can be framed as emergency medicine.

A semiconductor program is harder to explain away as temporary.

Its logic is structural: the United States believes some industrial capacity is too important to leave entirely to the global allocation of private capital.

Once accepted, that principle travels.

It travels to batteries.

Critical minerals.

Grid equipment.

Defense manufacturing.

Pharmaceuticals.

Artificial-intelligence infrastructure.

Shipbuilding.

Energy technology.

The industries will change.

The policy question remains.

Which capacities are public goods even when privately owned?

That question brings the book back to its beginning.

The United States was built through institutions that often hid their economic importance behind other names: an alliance that internationalized the Revolution, a debt arrangement that created national credit, a canal that reorganized geography, a land-grant law that built colleges, a weather service, a civil-service reform, a hospital-construction program, an education law, a pension guarantee, a navigation decision, a communications statute, a digital-health incentive.

None looks like the whole country.

Each altered what the country could do next.

The CHIPS Act belongs in that lineage.

It is not important because Congress discovered manufacturing in 2022.

It is important because a political system that had spent decades obscuring its industrial choices decided to make one loudly.

The law said that semiconductor capacity was not merely a corporate balance-sheet question.

It was a national capability.

That assertion may prove expensive.

Some projects will underperform.

Some subsidies will look excessive in hindsight.

Some conditions will be revised by later administrations.

Technologies will change faster than appropriations.

Companies will bargain hard because they know governments are competing for their plants.

None of that makes the policy historically trivial.

Failure would still teach the state what it cannot purchase cheaply.

Success would teach firms that strategic geography can become part of expected return.

Either way, the market has already heard the signal.

The United States is again willing to pay for factories not only because they produce things, but because their existence creates options the country does not want to lose.

That is the factory state.

Not a command economy.

Not nostalgia for smokestacks.

A government treating production capacity as infrastructure.

On August 9, 2022, the United States did not return to an earlier economy.

It returned to an older truth.

Where things are made can be a form of power.
