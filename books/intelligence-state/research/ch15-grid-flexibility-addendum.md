# Chapter 15 — grid flexibility research addendum

_Checked: 2026-09-02._

## Claim under review

Chapter 15 argues that some data-center workloads can shift in time or location, coordinate with utilities, reduce demand during stressed periods, use storage, and otherwise behave as flexible grid participants rather than as permanently inflexible peak load.

## Primary and authoritative evidence

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, **DOE Data Center Load Flexibility Workshop Summary** (January 2025), summarizes a November 1, 2024 U.S. Department of Energy workshop with 49 participants from industry, utilities, regulators, national laboratories, and other organizations. The workshop identified workload shifting, on-site energy resources, storage, and operational coordination as potential sources of data-center demand flexibility, while also emphasizing barriers including reliability requirements, regulatory complexity, economics, and the need for meaningful incentives and standardized approaches.

Source: https://datacenters.lbl.gov/publications/doe-data-center-load-flexibility

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, **Speed to Power: Solutions for Accelerating Large Load Connections** (June 2026), identifies more than 40 approaches to large-load connection bottlenecks across forecasting, interconnection, resource planning and procurement, markets and operations, and cost allocation and ratemaking. The report treats load flexibility as one tool among many rather than a substitute for grid investment.

Source: https://datacenters.lbl.gov/publications/speed-power-solutions-accelerating

Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, **FERC Launches Aggressive Targeted Action to Speed Large Load Integration** (June 18, 2026), directed all six FERC-jurisdictional regional grid operators to justify or reform tariffs governing data centers and other large loads. FERC framed the action around both faster integration and consumer safeguards, reinforcing the chapter's point that large-load growth raises questions of reliability, connection rules, and who bears system costs.

Source: https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/ferc-launches-aggressive-targeted-action-speed-large-load-integration

## Strongest counterargument / limiting evidence

Data-center flexibility should not be treated as an inherent property of all computing demand. Latency-sensitive services, reliability commitments, hardware utilization economics, contractual obligations, and operational risk can sharply constrain curtailment or temporal and geographic shifting. Even where flexibility is technically possible, market rules and compensation may not make it economical. LBNL's 2025 workshop summary explicitly notes these barriers, and its 2026 large-load work treats flexibility as one option within a much larger portfolio that also includes new generation, transmission, interconnection reform, planning, and rate design.

The manuscript should therefore preserve its existing qualifier that not every workload can move and avoid implying that flexible computing can eliminate the need for physical grid expansion. The defensible claim is narrower: **some** data-center load can become more grid-responsive, and designing incentives and operating rules around that capability can reduce strain or improve utilization in particular systems and hours.
