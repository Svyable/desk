# The Eighty Percent Problem

The most dangerous number in an AI strategy is the number of people who have tried AI.

It is dangerous because it feels like progress.

An employee opens a chatbot. A manager approves a pilot. A company buys licenses. A ministry announces an assistant. A bank adds a model to a workflow. A factory experiments with computer vision. A lawyer drafts a memo faster. A student asks for help with an assignment.

The usage curve rises.

Then the productivity statistics barely move.

This is the eighty percent problem.

In Latin America and the Caribbean, survey evidence published in 2026 captured the contradiction neatly: around four in five firms reported using AI tools, but fewer than one in four reported economic benefits, and only a small minority reported significant impact. The exact percentages will age. The structural lesson will not.

Adoption is not transformation.

The difference between the two is organizational design.

## A tool arrives before the workflow

Most institutions absorb new technology by placing it inside old routines.

Email initially reproduced the memo. Websites reproduced brochures. Enterprise software reproduced paper forms. Mobile apps reproduced websites. AI is beginning the same way.

A worker has a process with twelve steps. Management adds an assistant to step six. The worker saves seven minutes. The other eleven steps remain unchanged.

This is useful.

It is not a productivity revolution.

Large gains usually appear when organizations stop asking how AI can accelerate an existing task and start asking whether the task sequence itself should still exist.

If software can read every incoming document, why is a clerk manually sorting them before review?

If an agent can retrieve policy, prior cases, and relevant records, why does a citizen have to restate the same information to three departments?

If a model can draft a first-pass inspection report, why is an engineer waiting until Friday to compile notes from Monday?

If customer questions can be resolved automatically, why is the human team organized around answering routine questions instead of exceptions?

The productivity unit is not the prompt.

It is the workflow.

## The invisible cost of redesign

This sounds obvious until someone has to do it.

Redesign is expensive in ways technology budgets often ignore.

People need authority to change procedures. Data have to be cleaned. Systems need permissions. Legal teams need to determine whether a step is required by statute or merely inherited habit. Managers must decide who owns an automated process when it fails. Procurement contracts may restrict integration. Cybersecurity teams may block tools that cannot meet requirements. Workers need training. Metrics need to change because old output counts may no longer measure useful work.

A pilot can avoid these problems.

Production cannot.

This explains why demos spread faster than productivity.

The demo asks whether the model can do something impressive.

Transformation asks whether an institution can reorganize around the fact that it can.

The second problem is harder.

## Diffusion infrastructure

Frontier technology does not diffuse automatically.

The countries that benefit most will build institutions whose job is to make useful knowledge travel.

Call this **diffusion infrastructure**.

It includes technical schools that update curricula quickly, sector associations that publish implementation playbooks, public extension programs that help smaller firms adopt proven systems, test beds where companies can experiment without betting the company, shared procurement frameworks, cloud credits, model-evaluation resources, financing for software and process redesign, and consultants who understand both technology and the industry being transformed.

Economic history is full of technologies whose social value depended on boring institutions that taught ordinary organizations how to use them.

Electricity did not transform factories merely because motors existed. Production layouts changed.

Computers did not transform offices merely because processors became cheap. Databases, networks, standards, training, and management practices had to accumulate.

AI will be no different.

The countries that build the best diffusion machinery may capture more value than countries that simply have the highest chatbot usage.

## The small-firm penalty

The productivity challenge is especially sharp for small and medium-sized firms.

A multinational can hire a transformation team. A fifty-person manufacturer cannot.

A global bank can evaluate five model providers. A neighborhood lender may buy whatever its software vendor offers.

A large retailer can build proprietary data pipelines. A family business may still manage inventory through spreadsheets and memory.

The smaller firm therefore faces a paradox.

It may have more to gain from automation because administrative overhead consumes a larger share of its capacity.

It also has less ability to implement automation safely.

This creates a market for intermediaries.

Trusted accountants, software vendors, trade associations, local universities, chambers of commerce, technical institutes, and regional development agencies can become AI multipliers if they move beyond generic seminars and into workflow-level assistance.

A two-hour course titled *Introduction to Artificial Intelligence* is awareness.

Helping a metalworking company cut quoting time from two days to two hours is diffusion.

A serious productivity strategy measures the second.

## The infrastructure bottleneck inside the laptop

Some jobs could benefit from generative AI but cannot because the worker lacks reliable connectivity, adequate hardware, enterprise software, or digital records.

This is the hidden infrastructure gap.

World Bank and ILO research on Latin America and the Caribbean has estimated that a substantial share of jobs are exposed to generative AI, while millions of jobs with augmentation potential may fail to capture it because digital access is insufficient.

The important phrase is **augmentation potential**.

A theoretical productivity gain is not an economic gain.

A worker cannot use a cloud model through a connection that drops.

A clinic cannot automate records it never digitized.

A municipality cannot train systems on data scattered across paper archives.

A small firm cannot integrate an API into software it does not use.

AI policy therefore loops backward into old development policy: broadband, electricity, digital identity, payment systems, record quality, accounting, management, and education.

The future keeps rediscovering infrastructure.

## Management is a technology

Firms with similar tools can produce wildly different outcomes.

The difference is often management.

Good managers identify bottlenecks, define metrics, assign responsibility, create feedback loops, reward learning, stop failing projects, and scale successful ones.

Bad managers buy technology as theater.

AI magnifies this distinction because its uses are unusually open-ended. A forklift has a relatively obvious job. A language model does not. Someone must decide where it belongs.

The management question is not: *Do we have AI?*

It is: *What constraint are we trying to remove?*

Then: what would success look like? What data are required? Who checks the output? What happens when the system is uncertain? Which human decision becomes easier? Which step disappears? Which new risk is introduced? Who owns the result?

These questions are not glamorous. They are where productivity hides.

## The complementarity test

A useful way to evaluate AI investments is to ask what complementary asset becomes more valuable if the AI works.

If coding becomes faster, product judgment becomes more valuable.

If document review becomes cheaper, high-quality records become more valuable.

If translation becomes abundant, distribution into new markets becomes more valuable.

If customer support becomes automated, exception handling and product quality become more valuable.

If medical documentation becomes easier, clinician time becomes more valuable.

If analysis becomes cheaper, decision rights become more valuable.

Technology changes prices inside organizations.

The organization that notices these new relative prices can redesign around them.

The organization that does not simply adds another subscription.

## The measurement trap

AI creates its own accounting problem.

Time saved is not automatically value created.

Suppose an employee saves five hours each week drafting routine documents. What happens to the five hours?

If they become additional useful output, productivity rises.

If they become faster completion of the same work and the worker is given more meaningful tasks, productivity rises.

If they disappear into meetings, inboxes, and organizational slack, the measured gain may be small.

If management eliminates the worker without redesigning the rest of the process, costs may fall temporarily while service quality deteriorates.

This is why AI adoption surveys can overstate transformation. They count tools. Economies need to count outcomes: revenue per worker, cycle time, defect rates, customer resolution, hospital throughput without lower quality, permit duration without weaker review, learning outcomes, energy efficiency, wage growth.

The metric should sit close to the problem the technology is supposed to solve.

## Public productivity

The eighty percent problem exists in government too.

A civil servant can use an assistant and become individually faster while the citizen notices nothing because the actual bottleneck is a legal handoff three agencies downstream.

Public productivity is difficult because government output is not always sold at a market price. Faster is not always better. A court that disposes of cases instantly by ignoring due process is not productive.

The relevant concept is **valuable throughput under constraint**.

How many legitimate claims can be processed accurately? How quickly can a permit be decided while preserving required review? How many tax anomalies can be examined without burdening compliant taxpayers? How much time can teachers redirect from paperwork to students? How much administrative friction can be removed without erasing appeal rights?

AI can improve public productivity only when governments know what public value they are optimizing.

This is harder than selling software.

It is also more important.

## The productivity bargain

Productivity is politically durable when people can see where the gain went.

If AI lets a company produce more but wages stagnate, customers pay the same, and employment becomes less secure, workers may rationally conclude that productivity is someone else's word for extraction.

If AI reduces prices, raises wages, improves services, shortens work, creates new occupations, or funds public goods, the political meaning changes.

The productivity bargain is therefore distributive from the beginning.

Who pays for the transition? Who receives the savings? Who owns the improved process? Who learns the new skills? Who is protected when a workflow disappears?

The next chapter turns directly to that question.

Because the great political test of AI will not be whether economists can eventually detect a productivity boom.

It will be whether ordinary people experience that boom as a raise.
