# The Country That Thinks

Drive far enough through the industrial edges of a modern American city and the cloud eventually becomes a building.

It has walls. It has fences. It has substations. It has diesel generators waiting for the moment the grid fails. It has cooling systems, fiber routes, transformers, security gates, and a parking lot that can look strangely empty compared with the amount of money humming inside. Somewhere beyond the concrete and steel, rows of processors turn electricity into inference.

This is an important correction to the popular picture of artificial intelligence. We tend to encounter AI as language: a box on a screen, a voice in an earbud, an answer arriving from nowhere. The interface makes intelligence feel weightless.

The infrastructure is anything but.

By 2023, data centers were already consuming about 4.4 percent of electricity in the United States. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory analysis prepared for the Department of Energy estimated that the share could rise to somewhere between 6.7 and 12 percent by 2028. The International Energy Agency expects the United States to account for the largest part of the global increase in data-center electricity demand through 2030.

Those are energy statistics, but they are also political statistics.

A country that wants more machine intelligence does not merely need better algorithms. It needs generation. Transmission. Transformers. Chips. Financing. Construction crews. Water in some designs and climates. Permits. Fiber. Cybersecurity. Engineers. Operators. Lawyers. Procurement officers. Regulators. People who know what should be automated and people empowered to say what must not be.

The machine is not just a machine. It is a stack of institutions.

That is why the most consequential question of the AI age may not be which country produces the single smartest model.

It may be which countries become capable of thinking at scale.

## The invisible difference

Imagine two people, each opening the same AI service on the same morning.

One is in a wealthy country with several cloud regions, abundant capital, deep research universities, multiple model providers, strong broadband, large domestic customers, sophisticated procurement teams, and a government capable of buying computing capacity by the megawatt.

The other is in a country where the service arrives through infrastructure owned abroad, paid for in a foreign currency, governed partly by contracts written elsewhere, optimized primarily for languages and markets larger than their own, and dependent on international links the country does not control.

On the screen, the experience can look almost identical.

Strategically, it is not.

The first user lives inside an ecosystem with options. If one supplier becomes too expensive, too restrictive, or too unreliable, other suppliers may exist. If a local industry needs a specialized model, the capital and expertise required to build or adapt one may be available. If the government decides that certain records must remain under domestic legal control, it may have somewhere to put them. If an emergency interrupts one part of the system, there may be alternatives.

The second user may have access but little leverage.

This distinction is easy to miss because digital products compress geography. A search engine in Bogotá can look like a search engine in Boston. A cloud dashboard in Barbados can look like a cloud dashboard in California. A generative model can produce fluent Spanish in seconds without revealing where the chips sit, which company sets the terms, which legal system governs the data, or whether the service would remain available during a diplomatic or financial dispute.

The interface says abundance.

The dependency graph may say something else.

This is the difference between **AI consumption** and **AI capacity**.

Consumption is the ability to use intelligence produced somewhere in the network.

Capacity is the ability to decide how intelligence is produced, purchased, powered, adapted, governed, and deployed—and to preserve meaningful options when conditions change.

Every country in the Americas will consume AI. Most already do.

The harder question is what they will be able to do with it.

## From counting people to augmenting institutions

States have always been information machines.

Long before computers, governments built systems for seeing territory and population: censuses, cadastral maps, tax rolls, birth certificates, customs manifests, court records, crop surveys, weather stations, postal routes, disease reports, military maps. The quality of these systems often determined what the state could actually accomplish.

A government that cannot reliably identify a citizen struggles to deliver a benefit. A government that cannot map land struggles to tax or protect property. A government that cannot count imports struggles to collect customs revenue. A government that learns about an epidemic weeks late governs a different epidemic than one that sees it in near real time.

State capacity has always been partly cognitive capacity.

The great administrative technologies of the modern state expanded that cognition. Paper forms standardized memory. Telegraphs accelerated communication. Statistics compressed societies into quantities that could be compared. Computers turned ledgers into databases. Networks joined agencies that had once been informational islands.

Artificial intelligence changes the character of the next layer.

Traditional software is extremely powerful, but much of it waits for humans to specify the path. A database stores a case. A rules engine checks a threshold. A spreadsheet calculates a result. A search system retrieves a record.

AI systems increasingly operate on messier terrain. They summarize, classify, draft, translate, compare, predict, search, recommend, interpret, converse, generate software, and coordinate sequences of actions. Their reliability varies. Their authority should be bounded. But the category of work they can enter is much wider than the narrow computational lanes institutions built during the previous software era.

This matters because much of government and economic life is made from language and judgment.

A customs officer reads descriptions. A nurse reviews notes. A public defender examines documents. A teacher explains an idea three different ways. A regulator compares a filing with a rule. A procurement officer evaluates bids. A small manufacturer tries to understand why a machine keeps failing. A farmer watches weather, prices, pests, and soil. A minister reads briefing papers assembled from information that moved through a bureaucracy at human speed.

When machines become competent at pieces of that work, the unit of automation changes.

The state does not merely get faster databases.

It gets cognitive machinery.

## A new kind of national advantage

The United States is the easiest place to see the scale of the emerging advantage.

In 2025, according to Stanford's AI Index, private AI investment in the United States reached roughly $286 billion. That figure alone does not measure national intelligence. Money can be wasted, bubbles can form, and investment statistics do not capture public expenditure cleanly. But the magnitude reveals something essential: the American AI system is not just a collection of laboratories. It is a capital-allocation machine able to finance enormous uncertainty.

It also sits on top of cloud platforms, semiconductor design, research universities, defense demand, software companies, venture capital, deep public markets, vast corporate customers, and a continental economy capable of turning experimental tools into products at enormous scale.

None of those assets guarantees wise deployment.

The same country capable of training frontier systems can still operate public institutions with brittle software. A hospital can possess extraordinary medical technology while forcing clinicians through terrible interfaces. A government can procure advanced military systems while leaving a benefits office dependent on forms that should have disappeared twenty years ago.

AI leadership and institutional competence are not the same thing.

That distinction matters for every country in this book.

A nation does not become an Intelligence State merely by hosting a famous laboratory. Nor does it fail to become one because it does not train a frontier model.

The question is whether intelligence diffuses into the systems that determine ordinary capability.

Can a small business use it to compete with a larger one?

Can a teacher use it without surrendering the purpose of teaching?

Can a government shorten the distance between a citizen's problem and a competent response?

Can an electrical utility use better forecasting while keeping the grid secure?

Can a hospital make expertise more available without turning opaque recommendations into unappealable authority?

Can a country bargain with model and cloud suppliers rather than merely accept their defaults?

These questions are less glamorous than benchmark scores. They are also closer to power.

## The hemisphere is already using the future

The Americas make the distinction between consumption and capacity unusually vivid.

Latin America and the Caribbean are not waiting passively for AI to arrive. ECLAC's regional AI work has found high levels of public engagement with AI services; the region accounts for a disproportionately large share of visits to AI web tools relative to its share of global internet users. By 2026, an Inter-American Development Bank analysis reported that around 80 percent of surveyed firms in Latin America and the Caribbean were already using AI tools.

But only 23 percent reported economic benefits, and just 6 percent reported significant impact.

That gap may be one of the defining economic facts of the next decade.

Using AI is easy to start.

Reorganizing a company around what AI makes possible is hard.

A subscription can be purchased in minutes. Data integration can take years. A worker can ask a chatbot to draft an email this afternoon. Redesigning an insurance claims operation, a port, a hospital intake system, or a ministry requires authority, budgets, training, reliable records, process changes, incentives, cybersecurity, and political permission.

The productivity revolution therefore does not arrive as a software update.

It arrives as an institutional test.

Countries with competent organizations may extract large gains from models they did not invent. Countries with weak organizations may buy excellent technology and achieve surprisingly little.

This is good news and bad news for much of the hemisphere.

The bad news is that access to the same model does not equal access to the same outcome.

The good news is that model leadership is not the only game worth playing.

## The Intelligence State

The phrase **Intelligence State** does not mean a surveillance state, though surveillance is one possible and dangerous use of machine intelligence.

It means something broader.

An Intelligence State is a political economy in which access to machine cognition becomes a meaningful determinant of national capability, and in which institutions learn to convert that cognition into useful action.

The intelligence can live in government, private firms, universities, hospitals, farms, factories, or households. It can be domestically produced or purchased from allies. What matters is whether the society can use it without surrendering all meaningful control over the conditions of use.

This definition deliberately rejects a fantasy of technological autarky.

No serious modern economy is sovereign because it manufactures every transistor, writes every line of software, generates every kilowatt, and trains every model inside its borders. Modern power comes from networks of interdependence.

But interdependence is not the same as helplessness.

A country with several suppliers has more room to maneuver than a country with one. A country with abundant electricity can bargain differently from one with chronic shortages. A country with excellent public datasets can build services that a country with fragmented paper records cannot. A country that trains engineers but loses nearly all of them may subsidize another country's intelligence economy. A country whose citizens do not trust automated institutions may discover that technical capacity without legitimacy is politically unusable.

Sovereignty in the AI age is therefore not self-sufficiency.

It is **optionality**.

Can you choose?

Can you switch?

Can you refuse?

Can you keep essential systems operating?

Can you adapt intelligence to your own language, laws, industries, and public purposes?

Can you capture enough of the value created by the system to become richer rather than merely more dependent?

Those questions turn AI from a technology story into a state-capacity story.

## Intelligence has a balance sheet

Once intelligence becomes infrastructure, countries begin to discover that it has assets and liabilities.

The assets are visible: better science, faster administration, cheaper expertise, more productive firms, improved logistics, new businesses, accelerated engineering, expanded access to education and health knowledge.

The liabilities are less comfortable.

A country can become dependent on a foreign cloud. A ministry can automate a bad rule and make injustice faster. A labor market can generate productivity without distributing income. A city can welcome data-center investment and discover that grid capacity, water, land, or tax concessions were more valuable than expected. A local language can become second-class inside the conversational interface through which more economic life is conducted. A bureaucracy can gain the ability to process every violation and lose the mercy once provided by its own limited attention.

Machine intelligence does not eliminate politics.

It gives politics new machinery.

That is why the distribution of intelligence matters as much as its level.

Who gains agency?

Who becomes dependent?

These two questions will follow us from the United States to Canada, Mexico, the Caribbean, the Andes, Brazil, and the Amazon. They will follow us into factories, electrical grids, public agencies, classrooms, courtrooms, and data centers.

And they require a map.

The old geopolitical map emphasizes borders, coastlines, capitals, shipping lanes, pipelines, and military bases.

The new map does not erase any of those things. It adds another layer beneath and above them: chip supply, cloud regions, fiber paths, power markets, data centers, research networks, language coverage, public datasets, model providers, and the legal jurisdictions through which intelligence moves.

To read that map, we need to understand the currencies with which machine sovereignty is purchased.

There are five.

Compute. Energy. Data. Talent. Trust.

A country does not need to dominate all five.

But it cannot ignore any of them.
