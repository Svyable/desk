# The Hemisphere Bargain

The Americas do not need an AI union.

They do need a bargain.

The hemisphere contains the world's largest technology economy, major energy systems, critical mineral producers, manufacturing platforms, sophisticated middle powers, globally important biodiversity, financial centers, agricultural exporters, advanced digital governments, and small states whose constraints make them unusually inventive.

It also contains profound asymmetries.

The United States can finance frontier systems at a scale no other country in the hemisphere can match.

Canada can pay a sovereignty premium that many smaller states cannot.

Mexico can bargain with geography.

Brazil can bargain with scale, language, energy, and market size.

Chile can bargain with minerals and institutional credibility.

Caribbean states may need collective purchasing simply to face giant technology vendors on less unequal terms.

A serious hemispheric strategy cannot pretend these countries want the same things.

It can identify the things they should not have to build alone.

## Cooperation without dependency

Regional integration becomes politically fragile when cooperation feels like subordination.

The strongest country says standardization.

The smaller country hears lock-in.

The strongest company says efficiency.

The local firm hears acquisition.

The cloud provider says scale.

The government hears dependency.

The answer is not autarky.

It is cooperation designed around optionality.

A good regional institution makes members more capable of choosing, not less.

That is the test for a hemispheric AI bargain.

## A Hemisphere Intelligence Compact

Imagine a compact built around practical infrastructure rather than grand declarations.

Its purpose would be to increase the bargaining power, resilience, and productive capacity of participating states while preserving national authority.

Seven areas stand out.

### 1. Shared safety and performance evaluations

Countries should not each rebuild the same model-testing capacity from zero.

A hemispheric network could publish multilingual evaluations for public-sector use, cybersecurity, health, education, financial services, and other consequential domains.

The tests would not produce one approved model.

They would create comparable evidence.

Smaller states would gain technical leverage. Larger states would gain broader testing across languages, institutions, and real-world contexts.

### 2. Emergency compute access

Natural disasters, cyber incidents, health emergencies, or sudden research needs can create temporary demand for computing capacity.

A compact could establish emergency-access arrangements among public supercomputing centers, universities, national laboratories, and participating commercial providers.

The objective is not a permanent regional cloud.

It is a mutual-aid layer for computation.

Fire departments already understand mutual aid.

Compute can too.

### 3. Talent mobility

The hemisphere trains talented people and then makes their movement unnecessarily difficult.

Researchers, engineers, electricians, grid specialists, cybersecurity professionals, health technologists, and technical founders could benefit from faster temporary mobility, credential recognition, research visas, and cross-border project arrangements.

The point is not to accelerate brain drain.

It is to create circulation.

A researcher should be able to spend a year in a regional laboratory without severing ties to home.

A technical expert should be able to deploy after a disaster.

A company should be able to assemble a hemispheric engineering team without converting immigration law into its main technical obstacle.

### 4. Trusted data spaces

Some datasets become more valuable when combined across borders: weather, climate, disease surveillance, trade, logistics, biodiversity, agriculture, energy, and scientific research.

But sharing cannot mean indiscriminate extraction.

Trusted data spaces could define purpose, access, governance, provenance, security, benefit sharing, and exit rules for specific collaborations.

The Amazon chapters of this book make the boundary clear: openness without origin and reciprocity can become extraction.

Regional data cooperation should increase scientific capability without dissolving rights.

### 5. Infrastructure coordination

Compute follows fiber and electricity.

The hemisphere already coordinates trade corridors, energy flows, telecommunications, and standards imperfectly.

AI adds another reason to improve the physical map.

Cross-border transmission where economic. Resilient submarine cables. Redundant network routes. Shared cybersecurity protocols. Data-center planning that reflects grid constraints. Development finance for generation and transmission that leaves public capacity behind.

The point is not to place every server in the same jurisdiction.

It is to make the region harder to disconnect.

### 6. Procurement standards

Public buyers across the Americas should converge on a small set of non-negotiable contractual principles for consequential AI systems.

Data portability.

Auditability.

Security reporting.

Transparent subcontracting.

Model and vendor replaceability.

Clear retention rules.

Human contestability.

Exit assistance.

Multilingual performance disclosure.

A tiny government negotiating alone may struggle to obtain these terms.

A market of hundreds of millions of citizens can make them normal.

### 7. Capability-building finance

Development finance should stop treating technology as a box to purchase.

An AI project should be evaluated partly by what capability remains after the vendor leaves.

Did local staff learn?

Did the grid improve?

Did public data become cleaner?

Did domestic suppliers gain competence?

Can the system be maintained locally?

Are researchers stronger?

Can another vendor compete later?

Finance should reward **capability residue**.

The best project leaves behind more agency than it found.

## The United States has to learn to have partners

The United States sits at the center of any hemispheric AI arrangement because of its technological scale.

That creates responsibility.

American policy often alternates between neglecting the hemisphere and treating it primarily through security, migration, or competition with outside powers.

AI offers a more constructive frame.

A prosperous, technically capable hemisphere is not charity.

It increases supply-chain resilience, creates markets, expands energy and research partnerships, improves cybersecurity, and produces allies capable of contributing rather than merely requesting access.

The strategic mistake would be to equate American leadership with American exclusivity.

Leadership that weakens partner capability eventually produces brittle dependency.

The stronger system creates capable partners with meaningful options who still choose deep cooperation because the cooperation is useful.

That is harder.

It is also more durable.

## Latin America has to learn to bargain as a market

Latin American countries, meanwhile, often negotiate with giant technology firms individually despite sharing many requirements.

There are reasons: different laws, politics, procurement systems, languages, markets, and strategic priorities.

But fragmentation has a price.

Shared evaluations, model incident reporting, cybersecurity intelligence, procurement clauses, language benchmarks, and infrastructure standards can increase bargaining power without requiring political union.

The region does not need one AI policy.

It needs more reusable capacity.

Every country should not have to rediscover the same vendor risk independently.

## Canada and the middle-power bridge

Canada can play a distinctive role because it understands both sides of the sovereignty problem.

It is deeply integrated with the American technology economy while actively investing in domestic compute and capability.

That gives it credibility as a bridge between continental scale and middle-power optionality.

It can help normalize a principle the hemisphere needs badly:

You can be a close ally and still want your own keys.

That is not disloyalty.

It is mature interdependence.

## Small states need federation, not imitation

For Caribbean and smaller Central American states, the compact should make sovereign capability cheaper.

Shared procurement. Regional cybersecurity teams. Common evaluation tools. Disaster-compute mutual aid. Language resources. Training programs. Digital identity interoperability where appropriate.

The objective is not to make every small country look like a miniature United States.

It is to let small states pool scale while preserving local authority.

Sovereignty can be federated at the layer where scale matters.

## The bargain over extraction

Minerals, energy, biodiversity, agricultural data, labor, and markets all create the risk of a familiar hemispheric pattern: value enters through capital and leaves through ownership.

AI does not abolish this pattern.

It can digitize it.

The new bargain should therefore ask what stays.

Skills.

Infrastructure.

Research capacity.

Local suppliers.

Tax revenue.

Data rights.

Language capability.

Grid improvements.

Institutional knowledge.

A country can welcome foreign investment and still insist that development produce residue.

This is not about forcing every layer of the value chain to remain local.

It is about refusing a future in which the only local layer is the externality.

## The hemisphere as portfolio

The Americas do not need every country to possess the same strengths.

They are stronger as a portfolio.

American capital and frontier research.

Canadian compute strategy and institutional finance.

Mexican manufacturing depth.

Brazilian scale, energy, agriculture, and Portuguese-language capability.

Chilean minerals and technical institutions.

Uruguayan digital-government competence.

Caribbean resilience and small-state experimentation.

Andean geology.

Amazonian ecological knowledge.

Regional universities, companies, grids, ports, cables, diasporas, and markets.

A portfolio is valuable because the components differ.

The policy task is to make difference interoperable.

## A bargain worth choosing

The first AI race asked who could build the best model.

The second asked which organizations could redesign work around models.

The third race is political economy.

Who can make intelligence abundant without making sovereignty scarce?

The winning hemisphere will not be the one that eliminates dependency.

It will be the one that turns interdependence into a system of negotiated strength.

Countries will still compete.

Companies will still concentrate power.

Governments will still disagree.

Trade conflicts will occur. Elections will change priorities. Technologies will surprise planners.

The compact does not need to prevent politics.

It needs to make politics less catastrophic by preserving options.

That is the final bargain of the Intelligence State:

cooperate deeply enough to become stronger together,

retain enough capacity to remain free to choose,

and make sure the intelligence flowing across the hemisphere leaves more capability behind than it consumes.
