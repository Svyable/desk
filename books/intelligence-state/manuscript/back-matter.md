# Back Matter

## A Note on Method

*Intelligence State* is a work of narrative nonfiction and institutional analysis. It uses current policy documents, public statistics, government strategies, multilateral research, technical standards, and reported institutional practice to build a framework for understanding AI as state capacity across the Americas.

The book distinguishes among four kinds of claims wherever the distinction matters:

1. **Measured history** — observed data and documented events.
2. **Announced policy** — plans, budgets, strategies, and programs that governments or institutions have publicly committed to but may not yet have fully implemented.
3. **Modeled projection** — scenarios about future energy demand, labor exposure, technology diffusion, and other uncertain outcomes.
4. **Analytical synthesis** — the book's own concepts and arguments derived from the evidence.

Forecasts are not treated as destiny. Policies are not treated as implementation merely because they have been announced. A country's possession of an asset—energy, minerals, data, talent, or compute—is not treated as proof that the country will capture durable value from it.

The research ledger in `research/source-notes.md` records the principal factual spine used during drafting and should be rechecked whenever a later edition is prepared.

## Core Concepts

### Intelligence State
A political economy in which access to machine cognition becomes a meaningful determinant of national capability, and institutions learn to convert that cognition into useful action.

### Five Currencies of Sovereignty
**Compute, energy, data, talent, and trust.** A country does not need to dominate all five, but weakness in one can strand strength in the others.

### Meaningful Choice Under Pressure
The book's operational definition of sovereignty: the ability to switch, refuse, continue operating, adapt systems to local needs, and bargain over dependencies when conditions worsen.

### Sovereignty Premium
The rational cost a country pays above the cheapest immediate market solution in order to preserve future options, resilience, domestic competence, or strategic independence in selected layers.

### Minimum Viable Sovereignty
The smallest set of capabilities a state must retain to remain meaningfully in control of essential functions even when much of its technology stack is imported or shared.

### Maximum Learning per Installation
A development principle: foreign technology adoption creates durable value when local workers, firms, universities, and institutions accumulate capability from each deployment rather than remaining passive users.

### Competence Premium
The outsized return smaller or less capital-rich states can earn by making ordinary institutional layers—identity, procurement, interoperability, records, appeals, security, public data—work unusually well.

### Abundance Doctrine
The proposition that AI strategy should expand the physical and institutional capacity that machine intelligence depends on rather than treating compute, energy, skills, and infrastructure as a fixed pool to ration.

### Resource Curse 2.0
A digital version of extractive development in which a country supplies electricity, minerals, data, labor, or ecological knowledge while the highest-value intellectual property, platforms, customer relationships, and strategic control remain elsewhere.

### The Curse of Indispensability
The mistake of assuming that being essential to another actor's supply chain automatically produces domestic power. Strategic value depends on the ability to bargain, substitute, learn, and capture capability.

### Maximum Learning per Tonne
The mineral-economy counterpart to maximum learning per installation: extraction becomes development when it accumulates engineering, supplier, research, environmental, recycling, and institutional expertise.

### Diffusion Infrastructure
The institutions that turn frontier capability into ordinary productivity: technical schools, extension services, standards, test beds, financing, shared procurement, integration expertise, and sector-specific implementation support.

### Machine Speed with Human Contestability
A design principle for public-sector AI: automation can increase speed and capacity, but consequential decisions must remain traceable, explainable enough to challenge, and subject to meaningful human appeal.

### High-Quality Friction
Deliberate pauses or checks—appeal, review, provenance, public notice, independent audit, verification—placed where machine speed would otherwise outrun accountability.

### Capability Residue
The skills, infrastructure, data quality, supplier depth, institutional knowledge, and bargaining power that remain locally after an investment or technology project is complete.

## Acknowledgment of Uncertainty

AI is moving faster than book production cycles. Model capability, investment totals, electricity projections, public policy, company structure, and international rules will change after publication. The durable purpose of this book is therefore not to freeze a snapshot of 2026. It is to offer a framework that remains useful as the numbers change.

The central test is always the same:

**Does a new layer of machine intelligence leave a society with more meaningful choices, more productive capacity, and more legitimate institutions than it had before?**

If yes, intelligence is becoming state capacity.

If no, the technology may still be impressive.

The state is not yet stronger.
