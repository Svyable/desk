# The Amazon Has a Data Policy

A leaf can become a dataset before it becomes a medicine.

Someone photographs it. Someone records where it grew. Someone identifies the species. Someone sequences genetic material. Someone writes down what a community has long known about the plant. Someone stores the observation in a database. Someone else combines that database with thousands of others. Years later, a model notices a pattern no single researcher could have seen.

Value has moved.

Not the leaf. Not the forest. Information about the forest.

This is the part of the AI economy that makes the phrase *data is the new oil* collapse under its own laziness.

Oil does not know who extracted it.

Oil does not contain a people's language.

Oil does not reproduce when copied.

Oil does not reveal the location of an endangered species, encode the accumulated knowledge of generations, or become more valuable when combined with information collected somewhere else.

The Amazon is not a reservoir of raw data waiting for better machines.

It is a living territory whose information has owners, custodians, histories, risks, obligations, and consequences.

That means the Amazon already has a data policy, whether governments call it that or not.

Every rule about who may enter a territory, collect a sample, publish a coordinate, sequence a genome, record a language, train a model, commercialize a compound, share a database, or distribute benefits is part of the information architecture of the forest.

AI makes that architecture harder to ignore.

## The second extraction

The first extraction is familiar.

Timber leaves the forest.

Ore leaves the ground.

Cattle occupy cleared land.

Plants become commodities.

The second extraction can happen without moving much physical matter at all.

A sample becomes a sequence. A sequence becomes a database entry. A database becomes training material. Training material becomes a model. The model becomes a product.

At each step, the original relationship between knowledge and place becomes easier to lose.

This is not automatically exploitation. Scientific exchange has produced extraordinary public benefits. Open biodiversity databases allow researchers to compare ecosystems across continents. Genetic information can accelerate medicine, agriculture, conservation, and basic science. Shared environmental data can help governments detect deforestation, disease, drought, fire, and illegal activity.

The problem is not circulation.

The problem is circulation without legible rights.

Who may use the information?

For what purpose?

Who must be consulted?

What must be shared back?

What happens when a use that did not exist at the time of collection becomes commercially valuable later?

Machine learning makes secondary use the rule rather than the exception.

The dataset collected to answer one question may become useful for a hundred questions its collectors never imagined.

This is why consent designed for a single transaction can become brittle in an age of reusable intelligence.

## The forest as model input

The Amazon can be represented in layers.

Satellite imagery.

River levels.

Rainfall.

Soil chemistry.

Species observations.

Forest inventories.

Acoustic recordings.

Genomic sequences.

Land tenure.

Fire alerts.

Health records.

Languages.

Trade flows.

Traditional ecological knowledge.

Individually, each layer tells a partial story. Combined, they can produce extraordinary analytical power.

A model might identify where a species is likely to occur, predict fire risk, detect illegal mining, estimate carbon, classify forest degradation, discover a biological relationship, or help a community document changes in its territory.

Combination is where AI becomes useful.

Combination is also where governance becomes difficult.

A dataset that seems harmless alone can become sensitive when joined with another. A public map of biodiversity can help conservationists and poachers. Language recordings can support revitalization and also become training data for a commercial model. Health information can reveal patterns about small communities even after names are removed. Ecological knowledge can guide scientific discovery while obscuring the people who maintained that knowledge.

The most important data-governance question is therefore not merely *is this dataset public?*

It is *what new power appears when this dataset is combined?*

AI is a machine for creating new power from combination.

## The knowledge that had a custodian

Western information systems have a habit of converting relationships into records.

A plant has a Latin name.

A place has coordinates.

A remedy has ingredients.

A story has text.

A language has tokens.

Once recorded, the information can look ownerless.

But traditional knowledge often exists inside relationships: between families, communities, species, seasons, ceremonies, territories, and obligations. The fact that something can be written down does not mean its meaning survives the conversion.

This matters because AI systems are hungry for decontextualized material.

Models learn from fragments detached from the social conditions that produced them. At internet scale, that detachment can feel abstract. In a small Indigenous community, it can be intimate.

A recording made for language preservation can become part of a voice system.

A plant use described in an ethnobotanical archive can guide commercial research.

A sacred story can become generated entertainment.

A map intended to defend territorial claims can expose sensitive locations.

The legal question is only one layer.

The deeper question is authority.

Who has the standing to say yes?

Who has the standing to say no?

Who decides whether a use is compatible with the purpose for which knowledge was shared?

The Intelligence State will need to understand that some data are governed not merely by privacy but by relationship.

## Brazil's legal memory

Brazil does not enter this problem with a blank sheet.

Its biodiversity regime already addresses access to genetic heritage, protection of associated traditional knowledge, and benefit sharing. That framework reflects decades of argument over biopiracy, scientific research, conservation, commercialization, and the rights of traditional peoples and communities.

AI does not make those debates obsolete.

It changes their technical surface.

The old image of access involved a researcher collecting a physical sample.

The new image may involve a company thousands of kilometers away using digital sequence information that has circulated through databases for years.

Nothing crosses a customs desk.

Yet economic value can still be created from biological information.

This is why the global debate over digital sequence information matters so much to the Amazon.

At COP16, governments operationalized a multilateral mechanism for sharing benefits from commercial use of digital sequence information on genetic resources. The resulting Cali Fund explicitly reaches sectors that can include artificial intelligence, and half of its resources are intended for the self-identified needs of Indigenous peoples and local communities.

That mechanism will evolve. Its implementation will be imperfect. It will not settle every dispute over ownership, consent, databases, or traditional knowledge.

But it establishes a crucial principle for the intelligence economy:

Digitization does not erase the politics of origin.

## Benefit sharing after the sample disappears

Benefit sharing sounds straightforward when a company takes a biological resource, develops a product, and earns revenue.

The chain is visible.

Digital systems blur it.

Imagine that genetic information from thousands of organisms is stored in public databases. A model learns statistical relationships across the entire corpus. It helps researchers identify a useful protein. Which record created the value?

Perhaps no single record did.

Perhaps the value came from the aggregate.

Traditional bilateral accounting becomes difficult when intelligence is produced from millions of distributed contributions.

This is a recurring problem in AI.

The model economy aggregates tiny pieces of information until provenance becomes technically and economically inconvenient.

Then it is tempted to declare provenance irrelevant.

The Cali Fund represents one answer: when exact attribution is impractical, create a multilateral mechanism that returns some value to biodiversity stewardship and to Indigenous peoples and local communities.

The idea is larger than genetic sequence information.

The AI age will need institutions capable of governing aggregate value.

Not every contribution can be priced individually.

Not every contributor can negotiate with a multinational company.

Sometimes justice requires architecture rather than invoices.

## Open data is not open season

Brazil has strong reasons to make environmental information available.

Open data can improve science, public oversight, conservation, entrepreneurship, and policy. In 2026, the country's environmental authorities described open bioeconomy data as strategic for sustainable development, sociobiodiversity, local economies, genetic heritage, benefit sharing, traditional communities, restoration, research, and conservation.

That is a powerful agenda.

But openness requires classification.

Some information should be widely accessible.

Some should be accessible under conditions.

Some should be aggregated.

Some should be delayed.

Some should remain under community control.

The mature data state does not confuse transparency with indiscriminate disclosure.

It asks what disclosure enables.

A map of illegal deforestation can strengthen enforcement.

A precise location for a rare species can create risk.

A public repository of government spending can improve accountability.

A public repository of culturally sensitive knowledge can create extraction.

The slogan *open by default* is useful for bureaucratic information.

It is dangerous as a universal philosophy of living knowledge.

## The API in the rainforest

The Amazon is already computational.

Research institutions maintain biodiversity repositories that can be queried through software interfaces. Satellites repeatedly image the canopy. Sensors measure rivers and weather. Scientists build inventories containing hundreds of thousands of observations. Brazil's National Forest Inventory has measured hundreds of thousands of trees and interviewed thousands of people across Amazonian states.

This is not a pristine forest meeting digital technology for the first time.

It is an increasingly instrumented territory.

The strategic question is what happens to the instrumentation layer.

If the data architecture is fragmented, underfunded, inaccessible to local institutions, or controlled through external platforms, the region can become information-rich and decision-poor.

That is one of the strangest failures available to a modern state: knowing more and governing less.

A useful Amazon data policy therefore requires more than collection.

It requires interoperable systems, long-term stewardship, metadata, security, local technical capacity, community governance, and institutions capable of turning evidence into action.

A sensor without maintenance is litter.

A dataset without stewardship is decay with a file extension.

A dashboard without authority is decoration.

## Indigenous data capacity

The phrase *data sovereignty* can sound abstract until one asks who owns the laptop.

Who controls the account?

Who knows the password?

Who can maintain the server?

Who can export the archive?

Who can train the next person?

Indigenous data governance cannot depend entirely on outsiders behaving well. Communities need practical capability to store, classify, authorize, document, and use their own information on terms they understand.

That may involve community archives, local language tools, territorial monitoring, controlled-access repositories, legal support, technical training, and protocols specifying which knowledge can circulate and which cannot.

Brazil's Ministry of Indigenous Peoples has itself set institutional goals around Indigenous languages, memory, archival repositories, cultural materials, and virtual heritage. Those efforts belong inside the AI story because language and memory are becoming machine-readable assets.

A community that digitizes its language should not have to choose between disappearance and surrender.

Preservation and control must be designed together.

Otherwise digitization can become a one-way door.

## The inference problem

Even perfect control over raw data does not solve the next problem.

Models infer.

A community may never publish a sensitive fact, yet a system may predict it from surrounding information.

A company may not possess an exact map of a resource, yet geological, ecological, satellite, and infrastructure data may narrow the search.

A health system may remove names, yet small population size can make re-identification easier.

A language model may generate culturally specific material without reproducing any single protected document.

Data governance therefore has to move beyond access governance.

The question is not only *what information did you take?*

It is *what did you infer, and what are you allowed to do with the inference?*

This is a difficult frontier because law has historically attached rights to records, works, samples, and transactions more easily than to statistical conclusions.

AI shifts value toward conclusions.

The Amazon makes the stakes visible because inference can affect land, biodiversity, commercial discovery, cultural knowledge, and political power.

The next generation of data policy will need rules for derived knowledge.

## Conservation's surveillance temptation

AI can make environmental enforcement more capable.

Satellite systems can detect clearing. Models can identify roads, dredging, fires, and changes in land cover. Automated systems can help prioritize scarce inspectors.

This is good.

It also creates a temptation to treat conservation as a surveillance problem.

The forest becomes pixels. People become risk scores. A distant agency sees an alert and assumes the map contains the whole truth.

But the Amazon is inhabited.

Communities farm, hunt, travel, build, burn, restore, and manage territory in ways that cannot always be interpreted correctly from orbit.

A model may identify change without understanding legitimacy.

The governance principle should be familiar by now:

Machine speed with human contestability.

Environmental intelligence should increase the state's ability to see without decreasing its obligation to listen.

The most legitimate systems may combine remote sensing with community monitoring rather than treating local people as objects of observation.

The people closest to the forest are not merely data points.

They are potential operators of the sensing system.

## The bioeconomy test

Brazil speaks increasingly of a bioeconomy: creating prosperity from living systems without destroying the ecological base that makes the prosperity possible.

AI could accelerate that project.

Better species identification. Supply-chain traceability. Forest management. Drug discovery. Crop science. Carbon measurement. Logistics. Market access. Language services. Ecological monitoring.

But the bioeconomy has a test.

Where does the value accumulate?

If biological information is collected in the Amazon, processed in a foreign cloud, transformed into intellectual property abroad, financed elsewhere, and sold back as an expensive service, the forest may be preserved while the old extractive structure survives in digital form.

That is not enough.

A successful bioeconomy needs local science, local firms, local institutions, local bargaining power, and benefits visible to the people whose territories make the knowledge possible.

Conservation without economic agency is politically fragile.

Extraction without conservation is ecologically suicidal.

The intelligence economy has to solve both constraints at once.

## The right to be illegible

Modern states usually want more data.

More records improve taxation, planning, health, infrastructure, enforcement, and services.

AI intensifies the appetite because models improve when fed more context.

But not every society should be maximally legible to every system.

There is a political value in selective illegibility.

A community may decide that some sacred knowledge should not be digitized.

A territory may keep precise ecological information restricted.

A language project may permit educational use but not unrestricted model training.

A researcher may publish findings without releasing raw community data.

These choices can frustrate the ideology of frictionless information.

That friction may be the point.

Sovereignty includes the ability not to optimize for someone else's model.

The right to participate in the intelligence economy must be paired with the right to withhold.

## A forest that can negotiate

The Amazon will generate more data every year.

More satellites will observe it. More genomes will be sequenced. More sensors will measure it. More archives will be digitized. More models will infer from the resulting material.

Trying to stop that process would be neither realistic nor desirable.

The objective is to make the forest negotiable rather than merely readable.

Readable means outsiders can extract information.

Negotiable means institutions and communities can set terms.

Readable means a model can identify value.

Negotiable means the people connected to that value have standing when it is used.

Readable means the state can see.

Negotiable means citizens can contest what the state concludes.

Readable means biodiversity becomes machine legible.

Negotiable means digitization does not dissolve custody.

The Amazon's data policy is therefore not a side issue in environmental governance.

It is a prototype for the politics of intelligence itself.

The future economy will create enormous value by converting reality into representations and representations into predictions.

The societies that retain agency will be those that govern the conversion.

The leaf becomes a dataset.

The dataset becomes a model.

The model becomes a decision.

At every step, someone should still be able to ask:

Who gave you permission?
