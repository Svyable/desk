# The Philippines and the Southern Arc

The southern approaches to Taiwan turn the Philippines into a strategic hinge.

Geography creates relevance before politics creates certainty.

Northern Philippine territory sits close to the Luzon Strait and to routes that would matter in any crisis around Taiwan. Access, logistics, surveillance, air and maritime operations, humanitarian support, evacuation, and the movement of supplies could all become consequential.

But the Philippines is not a chess square.

Its geography belongs to a sovereign government with its own alliance commitments, its own disputes with China, its own domestic politics, and its own calculations about risk.

Deterrence fails when planners confuse geographic usefulness with automatic political consent.

## The map invites overconfidence

Look at a map and the logic seems obvious.

Taiwan sits to the north.

The northern Philippines sits below it.

The Luzon Strait connects the Philippine Sea and the South China Sea.

Military planners immediately see routes, sensors, airfields, ports, islands, and logistics nodes.

Political geography is less tidy.

An airfield that is useful in a contingency is not the same thing as an airfield that has been authorized for every contingency. An alliance treaty creates commitments, but it does not answer every question about access, mission, timing, or escalation. A facility can support training, humanitarian assistance, maritime awareness, or regional readiness without every use being preapproved.

The gap between physical possibility and political permission is the central problem of the southern arc.

## The alliance is real

The U.S.-Philippines alliance is not hypothetical.

The 1951 Mutual Defense Treaty, the Visiting Forces Agreement, and the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement form a substantial legal and operational foundation for cooperation. The two countries have expanded exercises, maritime activity, planning, and investment in agreed locations. In 2025, their governments again publicly emphasized the treaty and the importance of EDCA to coordination and interoperability.

The newer EDCA locations make geography more consequential. Several are in northern Luzon, closer to the Luzon Strait than older arrangements. U.S. officials have consistently described EDCA as supporting a range of alliance and regional missions rather than as permanent U.S. basing.

That distinction matters.

The sites expand options.

They do not erase Philippine authority over those options.

## Access is a verb

Security discussions often use *access* as though it were a property.

We have access.

They have access.

In reality, access is a sequence of permissions and capabilities.

Who can enter?

For what mission?

With which equipment?

For how long?

Under whose command?

Can munitions be stored?

Can intelligence be collected?

Can a system operate from the site during a crisis that does not involve an armed attack on the Philippines?

Can an ally transit material intended for Taiwan?

Which activities require fresh approval?

These questions determine whether geographic potential becomes strategic effect.

The answers can change with the political context.

That is why access must be politically renewable.

## The gray-zone mismatch

The hardest scenarios may be below declared war.

A major attack creates legal and political clarity that a quarantine, inspection regime, cyber campaign, or sustained coercive exercise may not.

This creates a mismatch between the contingency planners imagine and the crisis politicians may actually face.

A Philippine government could strongly oppose coercion around Taiwan and still ask whether a specific U.S. military activity from Philippine territory would expose the country to retaliation before an armed conflict exists.

That question is not alliance weakness.

It is sovereign risk allocation.

The country being asked to host the activity is also the country that may absorb the consequences.

Good planning therefore needs a ladder rather than a binary switch.

What cooperation is routine in peacetime?

What expands during heightened tension?

What support becomes available after coercive interference with commercial traffic?

What changes after an armed attack?

What requires separate national authorization regardless of the stage?

The clearer this ladder is, the less room there is for an adversary to bet on political surprise.

## The South China Sea changes the calculation

The Philippines also has its own direct security problem with China in the South China Sea.

That makes the alliance relationship more than a Taiwan derivative.

Manila evaluates U.S. credibility partly through whether the alliance helps the Philippines defend its own maritime rights, public vessels, armed forces, and coast guard. Washington evaluates the relationship partly through regional posture and deterrence.

Those interests overlap substantially.

They are not identical.

A partnership that treats Philippine territory mainly as useful geography for somebody else’s contingency risks weakening the political basis of access.

A partnership that strengthens Philippine defense, maritime awareness, resilience, and disaster response can make broader cooperation more legitimate because the benefits are visibly reciprocal.

This is the political infrastructure beneath military infrastructure.

## Batanes and the symbolism of proximity

The Batanes Islands make the issue unusually concrete.

They sit in the Luzon Strait, geographically closer to Taiwan than to Manila. Military activity there is therefore scrutinized for its Taiwan implications even when official descriptions emphasize regional defense, training, or broader readiness.

In 2025, U.S. forces exercised a mobile anti-ship missile system in the Batanes during alliance training. The military value was obvious. The political meaning was more complex.

A deployment in a geographically sensitive place does several things at once.

It trains forces.

It demonstrates access.

It signals capability.

It affects Chinese threat perceptions.

It creates domestic debate in the Philippines.

It changes what future planners imagine is possible.

This is why geography can become signaling before a crisis begins.

The most useful peacetime activity is one that improves readiness without creating false assumptions about what Manila has already agreed to do later.

## Domestic legitimacy is operational readiness

Philippine security policy is shaped by elections, public opinion, elite debate, local communities, economic interests, and constitutional and legal constraints.

Those are sometimes treated as political noise around the military problem.

They are part of the military problem.

A facility whose local benefits are visible, whose mission is understood, and whose legal basis is credible is more politically durable than one perceived as an externally imposed risk.

Infrastructure spending, disaster-response capacity, local consultation, environmental management, and transparency about activities can therefore affect deterrence.

They help determine whether access survives the change from one administration to another or from peacetime to crisis.

Durability is a capability.

## Consultation before request

The worst moment to explain a contingency to an ally is when asking it to accept the risk.

Consultation has to begin earlier.

What scenarios worry each government?

Which activities would be politically difficult?

Which forms of support would be most useful below the threshold of war?

How would each side interpret a quarantine?

What if commercial shipping is impeded but no Philippine vessel is attacked?

What if cyber operations affect Philippine infrastructure during a Taiwan crisis?

What if Chinese military pressure around the South China Sea intensifies simultaneously?

These questions link separate theaters into one decision problem.

Pre-crisis consultation does not guarantee agreement.

It reduces surprise about disagreement.

That is strategically valuable.

## The humanitarian mission is not secondary

A Taiwan crisis could produce evacuation, disrupted shipping, displaced civilians, medical needs, and pressure on air and maritime transport before or alongside military conflict.

The Philippines could become important to humanitarian response because of proximity even if its government sought to limit direct military involvement.

This is another reason to avoid binary planning.

Humanitarian assistance, noncombatant evacuation, search and rescue, medical support, and logistics for civilian continuity can require access and coordination at sub-war thresholds.

Those missions can also become politically contested if they overlap with military infrastructure.

Planning should distinguish them clearly enough that humanitarian options remain usable even when governments disagree about combat roles.

## The coercer studies politics too

China does not need to assume the U.S.-Philippines alliance will collapse.

It only needs to identify uncertainty that can be exploited.

If Manila’s authorization process is unclear, pressure can be calibrated around that ambiguity. If domestic opposition to a particular mission is strong, information operations can amplify it. If the alliance has rehearsed only wartime scenarios, coercion can remain deliberately below the trigger everyone prepared for.

This is why deterrence depends on disciplined uncertainty.

Not eliminating all political choice.

Making the boundaries of likely choice clearer enough that an adversary cannot confidently expect paralysis.

## What should be pre-negotiated

The most useful planning is not a secret promise that every future request will be approved.

It is a set of prepared categories.

Standing access for specified activities.

Fast procedures for new requests.

Rules for storage and movement.

Data-sharing authorities.

Humanitarian contingencies.

Cyber and communications support.

Protection of Philippine infrastructure.

Consultation requirements if missions expand.

Clear distinction between routine cooperation and actions that require renewed national consent.

This makes the alliance faster without making it automatic.

## The Philippine veto is part of the alliance

Strategists sometimes speak as though a partner’s ability to withhold permission is a weakness in the network.

It is actually part of what makes the network an alliance rather than an empire.

The Philippines retains a veto over consequential uses of its territory because it remains sovereign.

The objective is not to remove that veto.

It is to make the alliance relationship valuable and predictable enough that consent is more likely when shared interests are genuinely at stake.

That requires reciprocity.

Consultation.

Defense of Philippine interests.

Visible investment in Philippine capacity.

Respect for national authority.

These political variables belong in deterrence models as surely as runway length and missile range.

## The southern arc

The Philippines adds deterrent value not because its geography erases uncertainty.

It adds value when geography, alliance commitments, infrastructure, and political consent have been connected through real planning.

The southern arc is therefore not a line of bases on a map.

It is a chain of permissions that must remain credible under pressure.

An adversary should not be able to assume automatic access.

Nor should it be able to assume automatic denial.

The uncertainty has to be disciplined enough that coercion cannot safely bet on coalition paralysis.

That is a harder form of deterrence than drawing a red line.

It is also the form that gray-zone competition requires.
