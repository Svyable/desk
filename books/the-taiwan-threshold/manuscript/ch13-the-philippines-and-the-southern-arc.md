# The Philippines and the Southern Arc

The southern approaches to Taiwan turn the Philippines into a strategic hinge.

Geography creates relevance before politics creates certainty.

Northern Philippine territory sits close to the Luzon Strait and to routes that would matter in any crisis around Taiwan.

Access, logistics, surveillance, air and maritime operations, humanitarian support, evacuation, and the movement of supplies could all become consequential.

But the Philippines is not a chess square.

Its geography belongs to a sovereign government with its own alliance commitments, its own disputes with China, its own domestic politics, and its own calculations about risk.

Deterrence fails when planners confuse geographic usefulness with automatic political consent.

## The map invites overconfidence

Look at a map and the logic seems obvious.

Taiwan sits to the north.

The northern Philippines sits below it.

The Luzon Strait connects the Philippine Sea and the South China Sea.

Military planners immediately see routes, sensors, airfields, ports, islands, and logistics nodes.

Political geography is less tidy.

An airfield that is useful in a contingency is not the same thing as an airfield that has been authorized for every contingency.

An alliance treaty creates commitments, but it does not answer every question about access, mission, timing, or escalation.

A facility can support training, humanitarian assistance, maritime awareness, or regional readiness without every use being preapproved.

The gap between physical possibility and political permission is the central problem of the southern arc.

## The alliance is real

The U.S.-Philippines alliance is not hypothetical.

The 1951 Mutual Defense Treaty, the Visiting Forces Agreement, and the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement form a substantial legal and operational foundation for cooperation.

The two countries have expanded exercises, maritime activity, planning, and investment in agreed locations.

In 2025, their governments again publicly emphasized the treaty and the importance of EDCA to coordination and interoperability.

The newer EDCA locations make geography more consequential.

Several are in northern Luzon, closer to the Luzon Strait than older arrangements.

U.S. officials have consistently described EDCA as supporting a range of alliance and regional missions rather than as permanent U.S. basing.

That distinction matters.

The sites expand options.

They do not erase Philippine authority over those options.

Nor does access under one category establish automatic consent for a Taiwan contingency.

That evidence boundary should remain explicit.

An agreement can create infrastructure.

An exercise can demonstrate a function.

Neither by itself proves that Manila has pre-authorized every future mission planners can imagine from the same geography.

## Access is a verb

Security discussions often use *access* as though it were a property.

We have access.

They have access.

In reality, access is a sequence of permissions and capabilities.

Who can enter?

For what mission?

With which equipment?

For how long?

Under whose command?

Can munitions be stored?

Can intelligence be collected?

Can a system operate from the site during a crisis that does not involve an armed attack on the Philippines?

Can an ally transit material intended for Taiwan?

Which activities require fresh approval?

These questions determine whether geographic potential becomes strategic effect.

The answers can change with the political context.

That is why access must be politically renewable.

## The permission graph

The southern arc should be planned as a permission graph rather than a base map.

The nodes are not only airfields and ports.

They are decisions.

Entry authority.

Storage authority.

Transit authority.

Overflight authority.

Intelligence authority.

Force-protection authority.

Weapons-employment authority.

Humanitarian authority.

Cyber-support authority.

Construction authority.

Local-government coordination.

Public-notification authority.

The edges show which permission depends on another.

A logistics mission may require access to a site, customs clearance, fuel contracting, local security, communications, and movement authority on Philippine roads.

A surveillance mission may require a different chain.

A combat-support mission may require a substantially different chain again.

The value of the graph is not that it predicts Manila’s future answer.

It makes clear which answer is actually being requested.

That alone can reduce strategic overconfidence.

## Permission latency is operational distance

A nearby airfield can be farther away than a distant one if authorization takes longer than the mission allows.

This creates a useful concept: permission latency.

How long from request to legally and politically usable access?

How many offices must act?

Which decisions can be made in advance?

Which require cabinet-level attention?

Which depend on the type of crisis?

Which depend on whether Philippine forces or territory have been attacked?

Which depend on whether the requested mission is defensive, humanitarian, logistical, intelligence-related, or combat-oriented?

The longest consequential permission becomes part of the theater geometry.

Planners should measure political distance alongside nautical miles.

The same runway can be close in peacetime, farther during gray-zone pressure, and close again after an unmistakable armed attack.

The geography has not changed.

The permission state has.

## Mission categories matter

A binary model of access produces bad planning because real political decisions are often modular.

The Philippines may be willing to approve one category and withhold another.

Humanitarian evacuation.

Search and rescue.

Medical support.

Fuel transit.

Intelligence sharing.

Defensive cyber support.

Maritime-domain awareness.

Force protection.

Repair and maintenance.

Prepositioning.

Combat sortie generation.

Offensive strike support.

Each category carries a different political and escalation burden.

The alliance should therefore maintain a mission matrix rather than one access flag.

For each category: standing authority, conditional authority, fresh-consent requirement, likely decision owner, legal constraints, local implementation needs, and expected political sensitivity.

This is not a secret promise about future Philippine decisions.

It is a way to stop one kind of cooperation from being misread as consent to another.

## The gray-zone mismatch

The hardest scenarios may be below declared war.

A major attack creates legal and political clarity that a quarantine, inspection regime, cyber campaign, or sustained coercive exercise may not.

This creates a mismatch between the contingency planners imagine and the crisis politicians may actually face.

A Philippine government could strongly oppose coercion around Taiwan and still ask whether a specific U.S. military activity from Philippine territory would expose the country to retaliation before an armed conflict exists.

That question is not alliance weakness.

It is sovereign risk allocation.

The country being asked to host the activity is also the country that may absorb the consequences.

Good planning therefore needs a ladder rather than a binary switch.

What cooperation is routine in peacetime?

What expands during heightened tension?

What support becomes available after coercive interference with commercial traffic?

What changes after an armed attack?

What requires separate national authorization regardless of the stage?

The clearer this ladder is, the less room there is for an adversary to bet on political surprise.

## A consent state machine

The ladder can be made more precise without pretending the answer is predetermined.

Routine cooperation.

Heightened consultation.

Protective preparation.

Limited contingency support.

Direct defense of Philippine interests.

Expanded allied operations after a qualifying attack or political decision.

The labels matter less than the discipline of defining transitions.

What evidence moves the system from one state to another?

Who decides?

Which permissions automatically become reviewable?

Which never change without a fresh political act?

Which authorities can be delegated to reduce mechanical delay?

Which must stay at the highest political level because the mission itself creates strategic commitment?

The state machine is not a legal interpretation of the treaty.

It is a planning tool for sovereign choice.

That distinction is essential.

## The South China Sea changes the calculation

The Philippines also has its own direct security problem with China in the South China Sea.

That makes the alliance relationship more than a Taiwan derivative.

Manila evaluates U.S. credibility partly through whether the alliance helps the Philippines defend its own maritime rights, public vessels, armed forces, and coast guard.

Washington evaluates the relationship partly through regional posture and deterrence.

Those interests overlap substantially.

They are not identical.

A partnership that treats Philippine territory mainly as useful geography for somebody else’s contingency risks weakening the political basis of access.

A partnership that strengthens Philippine defense, maritime awareness, resilience, and disaster response can make broader cooperation more legitimate because the benefits are visibly reciprocal.

This is the political infrastructure beneath military infrastructure.

## Reciprocity is an access variable

Alliance planners often model Philippine access as something Washington may need later.

Manila models alliance value through what the relationship provides now.

Maritime awareness.

Coast-guard support.

Defense modernization.

Infrastructure.

Training.

Disaster response.

Economic confidence.

Political backing in disputes that matter directly to Philippine sovereignty.

The exact list is political and changes over time.

The principle is stable.

Consent is easier to sustain when the relationship visibly increases Philippine capacity rather than merely increasing exposure.

This can be measured imperfectly through what might be called reciprocity depth.

How many alliance investments solve Philippine problems even outside a Taiwan scenario?

How much local infrastructure remains useful when U.S. forces are absent?

How much training increases independent Philippine capability?

How much shared sensing improves Manila’s own maritime decisions?

The stronger the answers, the more durable the political foundation beneath access.

## Batanes and the symbolism of proximity

The Batanes Islands make the issue unusually concrete.

They sit in the Luzon Strait, geographically closer to Taiwan than to Manila.

Military activity there is therefore scrutinized for its Taiwan implications even when official descriptions emphasize regional defense, training, or broader readiness.

In 2025, U.S. forces exercised a mobile anti-ship missile system in the Batanes during alliance training.

The military value was obvious.

The political meaning was more complex.

A deployment in a geographically sensitive place does several things at once.

It trains forces.

It demonstrates access.

It signals capability.

It affects Chinese threat perceptions.

It creates domestic debate in the Philippines.

It changes what future planners imagine is possible.

This is why geography can become signaling before a crisis begins.

The most useful peacetime activity is one that improves readiness without creating false assumptions about what Manila has already agreed to do later.

## Demonstrated function is not standing consent

An exercise can demonstrate that a system can be moved, connected, supplied, and operated from a place.

That is valuable evidence.

It is evidence of demonstrated function.

It is not necessarily evidence of standing political authorization for the same function in a different crisis.

That distinction should be recorded, not left to institutional memory.

For each exercised activity, planners should identify:

What exactly was authorized?

Under what legal and political context?

Which approvals were exceptional?

Which support procedures became reusable?

Which local relationships were built?

Which mission categories were not tested?

Which permissions would have to be renewed in a real contingency?

This exercise-to-consent ledger prevents operational familiarity from quietly turning into a political assumption.

## Domestic legitimacy is operational readiness

Philippine security policy is shaped by elections, public opinion, elite debate, local communities, economic interests, and constitutional and legal constraints.

Those are sometimes treated as political noise around the military problem.

They are part of the military problem.

A facility whose local benefits are visible, whose mission is understood, and whose legal basis is credible is more politically durable than one perceived as an externally imposed risk.

Infrastructure spending, disaster-response capacity, local consultation, environmental management, and transparency about activities can therefore affect deterrence.

They help determine whether access survives the change from one administration to another or from peacetime to crisis.

Durability is a capability.

## Local implementation is a strategic seam

National permission can exist while local implementation fails.

A road cannot carry the vehicle.

A port lacks a handling procedure.

A local official has not been briefed.

A fuel contract cannot expand quickly.

Customs rules are unclear.

A community opposes a construction change.

Environmental approvals become contested.

A communications link works at the facility but not across the surrounding civilian infrastructure.

These are not trivial details.

They are the point at which national strategy becomes physical throughput.

A permission graph should therefore include implementation nodes below the national level.

The alliance needs to know the time from political approval to usable effect.

That interval is consent-to-throughput latency.

Reducing it does not require reducing Philippine sovereignty.

It requires exercising the administrative path sovereignty uses.

## Consultation before request

The worst moment to explain a contingency to an ally is when asking it to accept the risk.

Consultation has to begin earlier.

What scenarios worry each government?

Which activities would be politically difficult?

Which forms of support would be most useful below the threshold of war?

How would each side interpret a quarantine?

What if commercial shipping is impeded but no Philippine vessel is attacked?

What if cyber operations affect Philippine infrastructure during a Taiwan crisis?

What if Chinese military pressure around the South China Sea intensifies simultaneously?

These questions link separate theaters into one decision problem.

Pre-crisis consultation does not guarantee agreement.

It reduces surprise about disagreement.

That is strategically valuable.

## Consultation latency matters

Consultation can be formal and still be too slow.

A crisis may move through commercial, cyber, coast-guard, and military phases faster than ordinary diplomatic machinery.

The two governments should therefore understand how quickly different questions can be elevated.

Who calls whom first?

Which channel carries operational facts?

Which channel carries political requests?

Can an acknowledgement be sent before a substantive answer?

Can a request be narrowed to one mission category instead of bundling several together?

Can Manila ask for clarification about the consequence of saying yes or no?

Can Washington revise a request without making the revision look like political pressure?

The objective is not instant consent.

It is high-quality consent under compressed time.

A rushed yes can be as brittle as a delayed no.

## The humanitarian mission is not secondary

A Taiwan crisis could produce evacuation, disrupted shipping, displaced civilians, medical needs, and pressure on air and maritime transport before or alongside military conflict.

The Philippines could become important to humanitarian response because of proximity even if its government sought to limit direct military involvement.

This is another reason to avoid binary planning.

Humanitarian assistance, noncombatant evacuation, search and rescue, medical support, and logistics for civilian continuity can require access and coordination at sub-war thresholds.

Those missions can also become politically contested if they overlap with military infrastructure.

Planning should distinguish them clearly enough that humanitarian options remain usable even when governments disagree about combat roles.

## Mission separation protects option value

If every humanitarian route is designed around the same infrastructure, authorities, and force package as combat support, the humanitarian option may become politically unusable at the very moment it is needed.

Mission separation therefore has strategic value.

Different authorization package.

Different public description.

Different command relationship where appropriate.

Different logistics markings.

Different data rules.

Different thresholds for expansion.

The separation will never be perfect because infrastructure overlaps.

The goal is to preserve a credible lower-intensity option rather than make every use of the southern arc look like one bundled war plan.

## The coercer studies politics too

China does not need to assume the U.S.-Philippines alliance will collapse.

It only needs to identify uncertainty that can be exploited.

If Manila’s authorization process is unclear, pressure can be calibrated around that ambiguity.

If domestic opposition to a particular mission is strong, information operations can amplify it.

If the alliance has rehearsed only wartime scenarios, coercion can remain deliberately below the trigger everyone prepared for.

This is why deterrence depends on disciplined uncertainty.

Not eliminating all political choice.

Making the boundaries of likely choice clearer enough that an adversary cannot confidently expect paralysis.

## Permission denial should be survivable

A mature alliance plan should assume that some requests will be denied.

Not because the alliance is weak.

Because sovereignty is real.

The question is whether one denial collapses the entire regional plan.

If combat sorties are not authorized from one site, can logistics continue?

If one mission category is withheld, can maritime awareness continue?

If a local facility becomes politically unavailable, can another route absorb some throughput?

If Manila chooses a humanitarian role but not a combat role, can the coalition use that contribution without treating it as insufficient loyalty?

Designing for partial consent makes the network more resilient and more respectful of the partner.

It also reduces adversary leverage over any single permission point.

## What should be pre-negotiated

The most useful planning is not a secret promise that every future request will be approved.

It is a set of prepared categories.

Standing access for specified activities.

Fast procedures for new requests.

Rules for storage and movement.

Data-sharing authorities.

Humanitarian contingencies.

Cyber and communications support.

Protection of Philippine infrastructure.

Consultation requirements if missions expand.

Clear distinction between routine cooperation and actions that require renewed national consent.

Fallback plans when consent is narrower than requested.

Processes for restoring ordinary access after a crisis.

This makes the alliance faster without making it automatic.

## Consent renewal after the crisis

Access politics does not end when the immediate crisis ends.

Emergency permissions can create political residue.

Facilities may have hosted activities beyond their normal profile.

Local communities may have experienced disruption.

New infrastructure may remain.

Temporary authorities may need to expire.

A partner may want reassurance that exceptional use has not quietly become permanent precedent.

The alliance therefore needs a reset procedure.

Which permissions expire automatically?

Which infrastructure remains under which authority?

Which data collected during the crisis can continue to be used?

Which temporary forces depart?

Which after-action findings are shared with Philippine institutions and communities?

How are compensation, repair, or environmental issues handled?

Consent that has an exit path is easier to grant than consent that appears irreversible.

Reset design is part of deterrence because it affects whether political leaders believe today’s yes will still be governable tomorrow.

## The Philippine veto is part of the alliance

Strategists sometimes speak as though a partner’s ability to withhold permission is a weakness in the network.

It is actually part of what makes the network an alliance rather than an empire.

The Philippines retains a veto over consequential uses of its territory because it remains sovereign.

The objective is not to remove that veto.

It is to make the alliance relationship valuable and predictable enough that consent is more likely when shared interests are genuinely at stake.

That requires reciprocity.

Consultation.

Defense of Philippine interests.

Visible investment in Philippine capacity.

Respect for national authority.

Prepared fallback options when interests do not align completely.

These political variables belong in deterrence models as surely as runway length and missile range.

## The southern-arc ledger

A serious planning system should be able to answer a small set of questions without pretending they determine future sovereign choices.

Which mission categories have standing authority?

Which require renewed consent?

What is the permission latency for each?

Who owns the decision?

What was actually demonstrated in the last relevant exercise?

Which permissions were exceptional to that exercise?

How long from national approval to usable local throughput?

What alliance investments create independent Philippine value?

Which mission categories can remain available if combat access is withheld?

Which routes or sites can substitute for a denied node?

How are emergency permissions retired after the crisis?

What evidence would show that planners have begun confusing geographic proximity with political availability?

The ledger does not answer whether the Philippines will say yes.

It makes the cost of pretending the answer is already known visible.

## The southern arc

The Philippines adds deterrent value not because its geography erases uncertainty.

It adds value when geography, alliance commitments, infrastructure, and political consent have been connected through real planning.

The southern arc is therefore not a line of bases on a map.

It is a chain of permissions that must remain credible under pressure.

An adversary should not be able to assume automatic access.

Nor should it be able to assume automatic denial.

The uncertainty has to be disciplined enough that coercion cannot safely bet on coalition paralysis.

The strongest version of the alliance is not one in which Philippine choice disappears.

It is one in which Philippine choice can be made quickly, with clear categories, visible consequences, reciprocal value, and credible alternatives.

That is a harder form of deterrence than drawing a red line.

It is also the form that gray-zone competition requires.
