# What Counts as War?

A blockade is an old instrument with an unusually modern problem: everyone knows what the word means until a government tries to avoid using it.

In the conventional picture, a blockade is military. Ships and aircraft prevent traffic from entering or leaving an adversary’s territory. The action is coercive, visible, and difficult to separate from armed conflict. It has a beginning that can be named.

A quarantine is murkier.

In a 2024 study of possible Chinese coercion against Taiwan, the China Power Project at the Center for Strategic and International Studies drew the distinction carefully. A quarantine, in its formulation, could be led by law-enforcement forces such as the China Coast Guard rather than the People’s Liberation Army. Its aim would not necessarily be to seal Taiwan completely. It could instead impose inspection rules, selectively control traffic, or demonstrate Beijing’s claimed authority over access to the island.

The difference sounds technical until one asks how to stop it.

A military blockade invites military planning. A law-enforcement operation invites a jurisdictional argument. If the United States sends a destroyer to challenge an inspection regime enforced by coast-guard cutters, Beijing can portray Washington as the actor that militarized the confrontation. If Washington does not act, commercial carriers may begin complying because they are paid to deliver cargo, not adjudicate sovereignty.

The gray zone is powerful because the responder pays an escalation premium.

This premium appears whenever one side uses an instrument that looks civilian, administrative, temporary, reversible, or legally framed while the obvious countermeasure looks military, permanent, or dramatic.

Taiwan is especially exposed to this logic because so much of its economic life crosses water.

The island is not isolated in the ordinary sense. It is deeply connected to global markets. That connection is precisely what creates the vulnerability. Oil, liquefied natural gas, coal, food, industrial inputs, components, and exports move through maritime routes. Semiconductor production sits inside supply chains that extend far beyond the island. International communications depend heavily on undersea cables. The system works because ordinary traffic is assumed to remain ordinary.

Coercion tries to change the assumption before it changes every ship.

Suppose Beijing announces that vessels bound for Taiwan must file documentation with Chinese authorities. It may justify the measure as customs enforcement, safety, environmental regulation, anti-smuggling policy, or another exercise of sovereignty. Chinese maritime law-enforcement ships are positioned to conduct inspections. The PLA remains visible nearby but officially in support or on exercise.

Some carriers ignore the instruction.

Others comply.

The first strategic contest is over that ratio.

If most carriers comply voluntarily, China does not need to intercept most carriers. Commercial risk management performs part of the coercion. A threat to inspect can be more efficient than the inspection itself.

That feature makes the opening phase of a quarantine fundamentally different from an invasion. Beijing’s initial target is not necessarily Taiwan’s military capacity. It is other actors’ expectations.

Will a shipping company treat Chinese instructions as enforceable?

Will an insurer raise rates?

Will a port of departure delay a vessel?

Will a flag state advise caution?

Will a multinational company reroute a shipment because its legal department cannot guarantee that a cargo will arrive?

Will governments publicly tell their companies to ignore the regime and privately advise them to avoid risk?

Every answer teaches Beijing something.

The same logic applies to exercises.

Military exercises around Taiwan have become more frequent, complex, and politically salient. Exercises are useful signals because they reveal capability, impose operational costs, and demonstrate presence. They are also reversible. Aircraft return to bases. Ships disperse. Exercise areas close.

The strategic question changes if they do not.

An exercise can become a cordon through persistence rather than announcement.

This possibility creates what might be called the last-normal-day problem. When should Taiwan, the United States, Japan, or a commercial actor decide that an activity previously categorized as exercise has become preparation for coercion or war?

Too early, and the response itself can appear provocative.

Too late, and the operating environment may already have changed.

Intelligence services spend enormous effort trying to reduce that uncertainty. They watch mobilization, logistics, missile units, air activity, naval movements, reserve patterns, political messaging, cyber behavior, economic preparations, and many other indicators. Artificial intelligence can improve parts of this work by processing imagery, detecting anomalies, translating large volumes of material, correlating sensor streams, and generating hypotheses.

More observation does not remove the threshold problem.

It can make it more crowded.

An analyst may be able to say with high confidence that additional coast-guard vessels have sailed, that a rocket force unit has changed posture, that cyber activity has increased, or that commercial satellite imagery shows unusual loading patterns. None of those observations answers the political question: what does Beijing intend to do next, and what action should follow from the possibility that the answer is coercion?

This is where the Taiwan problem meets the AI problem.

Machine-assisted analysis can lower the cost of generating warnings. It can also increase the number of warnings available to senior officials. A system that flags every anomaly may make leaders more informed and more anxious at the same time. The scarce resource becomes not detection but discrimination.

What deserves escalation?

What deserves a phone call?

What deserves a public statement?

What deserves an escort?

What deserves a sanction?

What deserves force?

A threshold is a rule for converting evidence into action.

States often prefer to keep such rules ambiguous because ambiguity can deter. The United States’ long-running approach to Taiwan has relied in part on uncertainty about exactly how Washington would respond to a Chinese attack and exactly how it would respond to decisions by Taipei that Washington opposed. The intention is to discourage unilateral changes to the status quo from either direction.

Gray-zone coercion puts pressure on that architecture because it asks what counts as the kind of event the ambiguity was meant to cover.

A full invasion is easy to classify.

A limited quarantine is not.

The answer matters not only in Washington. Japan faces its own legal, geographic, and military decisions. The Philippines may face questions about access, bases, airspace, or the safety of its territory. Australia may be asked for intelligence, logistics, forces, or political support. European governments may be asked to impose sanctions or coordinate export restrictions. Commercial actors need to know whether governments will protect traffic or merely condemn interference.

Coalitions move at the speed of shared classification.

That may be the single most important sentence in this book.

A coalition does not need to agree on every long-term objective to act together. It does need enough agreement about what has happened to authorize compatible responses. Gray-zone strategy attacks that agreement.

Consider undersea cables.

Cable faults are common enough that a break is not proof of sabotage. Anchors damage cables. Earthquakes damage cables. Fishing activity, equipment failure, and seabed conditions can damage cables. Taiwan’s Ministry of Digital Affairs has treated repeated incidents as a resilience problem, expanding backup routes, satellite capacity, microwave links, legal protections, monitoring, and repair planning.

This is exactly the right engineering response to uncertainty: build a system that does not require perfect attribution before it can continue operating.

But governments still have to decide how to describe suspicious incidents.

Attribution has strategic consequences. Label a cable break an attack without strong evidence and credibility can be damaged. Treat a deliberate act as an accident and the coercer may learn that ambiguity is protection.

The same tension appears in cyber operations. A network intrusion can prepare the battlespace without causing an immediate destructive effect. Malware discovered inside infrastructure may be espionage, prepositioning, coercive signaling, or preparation for attack. Removing it is a technical task. Deciding what it means is strategic.

Taiwan therefore needs two kinds of resilience at once.

The first is physical and institutional: systems continue operating after disruption.

The second is political: leaders retain enough decision space to respond proportionately even when attribution and intent remain incomplete.

These two forms of resilience support each other.

A government under immediate pressure to restore electricity, communications, or fuel has less room to deliberate. A government with reserves, redundancy, alternative links, and practiced procedures can afford to wait for better evidence. Engineering can buy judgment time.

That is a form of deterrence rarely captured by missile counts.

If coercion is designed to exploit panic, systems that refuse to panic reduce its value.

The concept extends to shipping. The more routes, inventories, contractual alternatives, escort procedures, and pre-agreed government responses exist before a crisis, the less leverage a sudden inspection regime can extract. Resilience raises the number of actions Beijing would need to take before pressure produces the intended political effect.

This is denial rather than punishment.

Punishment threatens costs after coercion. Denial makes coercion less likely to work.

Taiwan needs both, but gray-zone scenarios make denial unusually important because punishment can be difficult to calibrate when allies disagree about whether the triggering action crosses a threshold.

A selective quarantine reveals the problem sharply.

Imagine China announces inspections only for certain classes of cargo. Food and medicine continue. Civilian passenger travel continues. Some energy shipments continue. The stated target is weapons or goods Beijing classifies as illegal. The operation is framed as limited and humanitarian.

Taiwan sees an assertion of Chinese sovereignty and an attempt to establish a precedent.

The United States sees coercion but must decide whether protecting a shipment of weapons warrants risking an exchange of fire.

A European government may condemn the action while arguing that military intervention would be disproportionate.

A shipping company sees a contract and a vessel.

China needs those perspectives to remain separate.

Taiwan and its partners need them to converge enough to resist the precedent.

That is why advance planning matters more in the gray zone than grand declarations. Governments can pre-negotiate response ladders, insurance support, sanctions packages, escort rules, intelligence-sharing procedures, legal interpretations, public messaging, and thresholds for moving from civilian to military instruments. The objective is not to eliminate political choice. It is to avoid discovering every disagreement for the first time while ships are waiting.

Exercises can do the same work if they test decision systems rather than only forces.

A useful Taiwan exercise should ask an awkward question before it asks where destroyers will sail: what happens when half the coalition calls the event a quarantine and half calls it an enhanced customs regime?

Who speaks first?

Which ministry owns the response?

What can be done before attribution is complete?

What support can be offered to commercial traffic without using force?

How are synthetic videos, false orders, hacked accounts, and contradictory intelligence handled when public pressure is rising?

Which decisions require a president or prime minister, and which can be pre-delegated?

The era of AI makes these questions more acute because the information environment will not be quiet while governments deliberate.

Synthetic media can manufacture apparent orders, speeches, military incidents, or public reactions. Automated systems can produce persuasive claims faster than officials can authenticate them. Open-source analysts can publish satellite imagery and confident interpretations before classified channels finish assessment. Markets will move. Prediction markets may quote probabilities. Social platforms will fill gaps in official information with whatever travels fastest.

The first battle over the threshold will therefore be informational.

Not because propaganda is new.

Because the volume and speed of plausible evidence can make uncertainty feel like certainty in several incompatible directions at once.

A resilient decision system must be able to say three things simultaneously:

we see the pressure;

we are not yet certain what comes next;

we already know what we can do now.

That combination is harder than it sounds. Bureaucracies often prefer either certainty or delay. Politicians face incentives to sound decisive. Militaries plan around categories. Markets punish ambiguity. Journalists ask whether an action is war because the public wants a name.

Beijing can exploit the demand for a name.

The answer for Taiwan and its partners is not to invent a definition broad enough to call every hostile act war. That would erase the ladder and make proportional response harder.

Nor is the answer to reserve serious action for the moment missiles fly. By then the coercive campaign may have already achieved important objectives.

The task is to build a richer ladder between normal and war.

Each rung needs observable indicators, available responses, coalition partners, legal authorities, and a theory of what the response is meant to change. Some rungs will be diplomatic. Some economic. Some technical. Some maritime. Some military. Some private-sector.

The ladder should be visible enough to shape expectations and flexible enough not to become a script for an adversary.

That is deterrence in the gray zone.

It does not eliminate the threshold.

It stops treating the threshold as a line that someone else gets to draw.
