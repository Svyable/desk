## Note on sources and method

The manuscript separates four categories wherever possible: observed behavior, attributable assessment, analytic inference, and scenario. That distinction matters because the book studies ambiguity itself. A Taiwan exercise built around quarantine or inspection does not prove Beijing has chosen that course. A capability assessment does not prove intent. A plausible scenario is not a prediction.

Legal and political labels are treated as consequential but contested. Blockade, quarantine, inspection regime, customs enforcement, exclusion zone, armed attack, and law-enforcement action are not used as interchangeable synonyms. Where the manuscript discusses how governments might classify an ambiguous act, it is analyzing a decision problem rather than asserting a settled legal answer.

## Source notes by part

**Opening framework and exercises.** Taiwan presidential and whole-of-society resilience materials, observed exercise patterns, and policy research support the discussion of coercion below invasion. Exercise behavior is not treated as a transparent window into intent.

**Maritime coercion and inspection.** Official and analytical sources support the quarantine and inspection chapters. The manuscript distinguishes military blockade from coast-guard, customs, inspection, and administrative mechanisms whose strategic effects may accumulate before governments agree on a war label.

**Infrastructure, cyber, finance, and semiconductors.** Public material on cable resilience, cyber risk, financial pressure, and semiconductor concentration supports these chapters. Attribution is kept separate from vulnerability, and current capacity figures should be revalidated near publication.

**Partner thresholds and crisis design.** U.S.–Japan and U.S.–Philippines treaty, access, and policy material inform the partner chapters. Treaty text does not mechanically determine a future political response; current policy and circumstances still matter.
