# AI and Misclassification

AI promises to reduce ambiguity by seeing more.

More satellite imagery.

More maritime tracks.

More communications.

More economic signals.

More historical comparison.

In a Taiwan crisis, this could improve warning dramatically.

It can also produce a dangerous illusion: that ambiguity disappears when enough data is fused.

The central problem is intent.

A model may accurately identify unusual logistics, force dispersal, cyber activity, shipping notices, and political rhetoric. Those facts can still support several explanations.

Preparation for attack.

Preparation to deter an attack.

Coercive signaling.

Exercise activity.

A hedge against uncertainty.

Machine classification can become political classification if the output is presented too cleanly.

A dashboard that labels activity “invasion preparation” may compress a chain of assumptions into one category.

This changes the burden of proof.

Officials who disagree are no longer offering an alternative hypothesis. They appear to be overruling the machine.

The safer design is explicit hypothesis competition.

Systems should display multiple plausible interpretations, identify which evidence distinguishes them, and show sensitivity to missing or compromised inputs.

They should expose disagreement among models.

A model that says “uncertain” can be more strategically useful than one that produces a confident label.

AI also changes adversary behavior.

If Beijing knows allied systems look for certain signatures, it can conceal, imitate, or manipulate them. The warning environment becomes adversarial in a machine-learning sense and a political sense at the same time.

This is why model performance in normal data cannot settle crisis reliability.

The system must be tested against deception, distribution shift, and deliberate ambiguity.

The goal is not perfect classification.

It is to avoid making bad classification look scientific.
