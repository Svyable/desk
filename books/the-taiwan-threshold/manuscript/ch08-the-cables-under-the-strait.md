# The Cables Under the Strait

Modern islands are connected by things nobody sees.

Undersea cables carry data. Power systems depend on imported components and fuels. Ports connect supply chains. Satellites and terrestrial networks provide redundancy, but not unlimited redundancy.

This makes infrastructure a gray-zone target even when nobody claims to target it.

A cable can fail through accident, negligence, natural causes, commercial activity, or deliberate interference.

Attribution may be slow.

The effect can be immediate.

That combination is strategically attractive.

An adversary does not always need to produce a total communications blackout. Repeated disruptions can impose repair costs, create public anxiety, force rerouting, and test how much redundancy exists.

The political problem is attribution under pressure.

If every cable break is treated as sabotage, the defender risks overreaction.

If every break is treated as accident, a coercive pattern may remain politically invisible.

The correct response is resilience before attribution.

Repair capacity, spare cable, diversified routes, satellite backup, protected landing stations, clear reporting, and coordination with commercial operators reduce the leverage of ambiguity.

A resilient system does not need to know who caused the first failure before it begins restoring service.

This changes deterrence.

The less disruption changes daily life, the less political value the coercer receives.

Resilience denies coercive return.

Partners matter because undersea infrastructure is international by nature. Cables connect jurisdictions, companies, repair ships, and landing points across borders.

A cable strategy is therefore also an alliance strategy.

The threshold under the strait may not be a military line.

It may be a line of glass on the seafloor whose strategic importance becomes visible only when it breaks.
