# The Cables Under the Strait

Most people experience the internet as a wireless abstraction.

Taiwan experiences it, in part, as a set of fragile physical routes under salt water.

Submarine telecommunications cables carry the bulk of international digital traffic. They connect financial markets, logistics systems, government communications, cloud services, and ordinary social life to networks beyond the island. Their landing stations, repair ships, spare capacity, and alternative pathways are therefore strategic infrastructure whether or not a crisis has been declared.

Cables fail in ordinary life. Anchors drag. Earthquakes occur. Equipment ages. Fishing gear snags. Repairs take time even when no adversary is involved.

That ordinary vulnerability is exactly what makes cables attractive in a gray-zone campaign.

A missile announces itself.

A cable cut can begin as a maintenance ticket.

## Ambiguity as the first effect

The strategic value of interference with undersea cables is not only outage.

It is ambiguous outage.

If several cables are damaged in a short period, what happened? Accident? Negligence? Coercion? Preparation for larger isolation? A mix?

Attribution may take days or weeks. Legal proof may take longer. Public communication must begin before proof is complete. Companies need guidance while investigators are still comparing AIS tracks, seabed imagery, and repair timelines.

This is a classification crisis in physical form.

Taiwanese authorities have treated cable security as a national resilience problem rather than a narrow telecom issue. Official and analytical accounts in 2025 and 2026 describe a pattern of nearshore human interference risks, legislative efforts to raise penalties and improve vessel monitoring, coast-guard patrols near critical routes, watch lists of suspicious vessels, and automatic warning systems that alert when ships behave anomalously near cable corridors.

Foreign Minister Lin Chia-lung’s RISK initiative, launched in October 2025 at a Taiwan-Europe undersea cable security forum, framed the problem as international: risk mitigation, information sharing, systemic reform, and knowledge building. The initiative’s premise is that cable security cannot be solved by one island alone because repair capacity, flag-state enforcement, insurance, and legal standards are transnational.

That premise is strategically correct even if every diplomatic detail remains contested.

## The shadow-fleet problem

Reporting and policy analysis have increasingly focused on vessels linked to Chinese ownership or crews operating under flags of convenience near cable routes. Taiwanese coast-guard practice has included monitoring lists of dozens of China-linked vessels and around-the-clock patrols near high-risk segments. Legislative changes entering force in early 2026 strengthened AIS management and raised the cost of intentional damage, including through vessel confiscation authorities described by Taiwan’s Ministry of Digital Affairs.

These measures matter.

They also illustrate the difficulty.

A vessel can disable or spoof AIS. A flag of convenience can slow accountability. A crew can claim accident. A corporate chain can be opaque. Even when Taiwan secures convictions in specific cases, as official statements have claimed in some 2025 incidents, the broader pattern can continue through other hulls.

The gray zone loves interchangeable instruments.

Cable interference is especially suited to that logic because the physical act can look like ordinary maritime clumsiness.

## What isolation means without occupation

A society can be partially isolated without being invaded.

If international connectivity degrades, financial transactions slow, logistics coordination suffers, cloud-dependent services fail, foreign media access changes, and public confidence can wobble. Governments with satellite backups, microwave links, and hardened priority communications can preserve core functions. Ordinary commercial and civic life may still feel the hit.

This is why cable pressure fits a coercion-below-war strategy.

It can impose political and economic cost while remaining deniable enough to complicate collective response. It can also serve as rehearsal for wartime isolation by revealing how quickly Taiwan and partners restore capacity.

The U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission and other analytical bodies have described undersea cable activity as part of a broader gray-zone toolkit. Think-tank and legal analyses have argued that Beijing has incentives to develop cable-cutting options for crisis and wartime use. Those claims should be treated as analytical assessments, not as courtroom findings about any single incident.

The prudent strategic posture does not require proving a master plan behind every outage.

It requires reducing the value of outages.

## Repair ships and the tyranny of capacity

Resilience is constrained by repair capacity.

Cable repair ships are specialized and limited in number. Weather, permitting, seabed conditions, and security environments affect how quickly a fault can be fixed. In a crisis, repair assets themselves could become scarce, delayed, or unsafe to operate in contested waters.

A coercer does not need to cut every cable if it can overwhelm repair timelines.

Multiple near-simultaneous faults can create a backlog. Insurance and operating risk can delay mobilization. Diplomatic friction over access can consume days. Meanwhile, the political effect of degraded connectivity accumulates.

This is why transnational coordination on repair, spare capacity, and emergency permitting belongs in peacetime diplomacy. Europe’s experience with undersea infrastructure protection after sabotage concerns in other theaters has been cited by analysts as a relevant, if imperfect, comparison. Taiwan’s RISK framing explicitly reaches for that kind of cooperation.

No amount of domestic coast-guard excellence substitutes for scarce global repair capacity.

## Landing stations and the shore problem

Cables are not only wet.

Landing stations, power, physical security, and the terrestrial networks behind them are concentrated points of failure. Sabotage, cyber intrusion, insider threats, and simple accident can produce effects similar to a seabed cut. A strategy that hardens only the undersea segment while leaving shore infrastructure soft has misunderstood the system.

Taiwanese designation of certain domestic submarine cables as critical infrastructure, higher penalties for damage, and cross-ministerial coordination are attempts to treat the system as a system. The quality of implementation matters more than the announcement. Critical-infrastructure labels without drills, spare parts, and guarded redundancy are stationery.

## Satellites are complements, not magic

When cables fail, public conversation often jumps to satellites.

Satellite communications can provide vital backup for government, military, and priority users. Consumer and enterprise satellite services can blunt the worst isolation effects. Diversified pathways are unambiguously good.

They are not perfect substitutes for high-capacity submarine cables.

Bandwidth, latency, cost, terminal availability, spectrum, ground stations, and vulnerability to jamming or cyber attack all constrain satellite failover. A society that assumes “the satellites will handle it” may discover that priority traffic survives while ordinary commercial life degrades sharply.

Resilience planning should therefore treat satellites as part of a portfolio: cables, microwave where relevant, diversified landing points, spare capacity, prioritized restoration lists, and clear public communication about what will work for whom during an outage.

The goal is graceful degradation, not cinematic invulnerability.

## Attribution without paralysis

Governments facing ambiguous cable damage confront a familiar dilemma.

Speak too early and risk error.

Speak too late and lose the narrative.

Remain permanently uncertain and teach the coercer that deniable infrastructure attacks are cheap.

The workable approach is layered attribution.

Immediate public facts can be limited to what is solid: which cables, what capacity loss, what mitigation is underway, what vessel behavior is under investigation. Parallel classified assessment can run hotter. Partners can share track data and repair intelligence. Legal cases can proceed on evidence standards appropriate to courts rather than press conferences.

This layered approach denies the coercer a simple win. It also protects institutional credibility. A government that overclaims on day one and walks back on day ten teaches the public to discount future warnings.

Cable crises are as much about trust as about terabits.

## International law and the thinness of the regime

UNCLOS and related practice provide some scaffolding for submarine cable protection, but analysts and legal committees have long noted gaps when hybrid threats and flags of convenience enter the picture. Domestic laws can raise penalties inside a state’s jurisdiction. Enforcement beyond that is harder. Port-state measures, denial of entry, insurance pressure, and coordinated watch lists can extend reach without waiting for a new global treaty.

Taiwan’s attempt to internationalize cable security through RISK is partly a recognition that legal thinness is a strategic vulnerability. If every incident remains a bilateral argument with imperfect evidence, coercion stays cheap. If a wider set of states treats suspicious cable interference as a shared infrastructure problem, the political temperature of the next incident rises.

Whether RISK and similar initiatives mature into operational institutions or remain conference language will matter more than the elegance of the acronym.

## Coupling cables to the wider campaign

Cable pressure rarely travels alone in a serious coercion campaign.

It can accompany air and maritime activity, cyber operations against terrestrial networks, disinformation about the causes of outage, financial volatility, and commercial uncertainty. The combined effect can make an island feel smaller without a single amphibious ship loading tanks.

This coupling is why cable security belongs inside the same crisis architecture as quarantine and inspection scenarios. A government that treats telecom outages as a utilities problem and boardings as a coast-guard problem may miss the joint campaign.

The join is the strategy.

## Holding connectivity

The threshold lesson is straightforward and demanding.

Undersea cables are part of Taiwan’s political geography. Their vulnerability creates a pathway for coercion below war. Their protection requires domestic law, coast-guard capacity, warning systems, shore security, satellite complements, repair diplomacy, partner information sharing, and public trust.

None of that prevents every cut.

It can prevent a cut from becoming isolation, and isolation from becoming panic, and panic from becoming political concession.

In gray-zone competition, that chain matters as much as the seabed fault itself.

The cables under the strait are not merely engineering.

They are one of the places where the modern state discovers whether it can remain connected while someone tries to teach it how alone it can be made to feel.


## Matsu, Penghu, and the domestic cable map

Taiwan’s cable problem is not only international.

Links between the main island and outlying areas such as Matsu and Penghu have their own vulnerability. Damage to those links can separate communities, disrupt local services, and create vivid domestic images of isolation that travel internationally. Official Taiwanese reporting has discussed incidents involving Taiwan-Matsu and Taiwan-Penghu cables and the legal follow-through against responsible vessels in some cases.

Domestic cable resilience therefore belongs in the same strategic conversation as transoceanic routes. A society that can be cut into pieces electronically while remaining physically uninvaded has been shown a preview of coercive geography.

Redundancy between outlying islands and the main island, priority restoration lists, and clear local communication plans are part of whole-of-society defense for a reason. The outages are local. The political lesson is national.

## AIS, dark vessels, and the evidence fight

Automatic Identification System data has become central to public investigation of cable incidents. It is also imperfect. Vessels can go dark, spoof, or behave ambiguously. Legislative pushes to strengthen AIS compliance and to investigate abnormal signals are attempts to thicken the evidence environment.

Even perfect AIS would not end the argument. A track near a cable at the wrong time is suggestive, not always dispositive. Seabed inspection, cable-fault signatures, witness statements, corporate records, and repair-ship findings all matter. The evidence fight is slow. The political fight is fast.

This mismatch is structural. It cannot be wished away. It can be managed by preparing public communication templates that separate known facts, under-investigation items, and legal conclusions. It can also be managed by sharing more raw maritime data with trusted partners so that attribution is not a solitary national burden.

## The insurer as strategic actor

Cable owners, content companies, and governments all meet in insurance and finance.

If repeated interference raises premiums or narrows coverage, investment patterns change. If underwriters demand better physical protection, monitoring, or routing, commercial incentives can reinforce public policy. If markets conclude that risk is unpriceable during a Taiwan crisis, capacity can vanish exactly when it is most needed.

Public-private contingency planning should therefore include insurers and major capacity buyers, not only ministries and coast guards. A quiet rehearsal of who pays for emergency repair under different attribution scenarios is more valuable than another conference panel about the importance of cables.

## Escalation ladders for infrastructure attacks

What should a state do after a suspected intentional cable cut?

Diplomatic protest is the floor.

Public attribution, when evidence supports it, is a next step.

Port-state measures and watch-list coordination can follow.

Sanctions against specific corporate networks may be available in some jurisdictions.

Coast-guard and naval protection of repair operations may become necessary.

Cyber and economic countermeasures will be debated.

Military force in response to a cable cut alone would be a dramatic escalation that many governments would reject, which is exactly why the tactic is attractive to a gray-zone actor.

The ladder must be built in advance because the first instinct in a democracy after an outage is often either technical quiet or rhetorical overreach. Neither is a strategy. A pre-agreed menu tied to evidence thresholds is a strategy.

## Coupling to quarantine and cyber

Imagine a week in which coast-guard inspections slow selected shipping, a cable outage degrades international connectivity, and cyber incidents disrupt port or customs systems. No missile has flown. No beach has been stormed. Yet the island’s sense of secure access to the world has been damaged from three directions.

That coupling is the campaign form this book keeps returning to.

Cable defense that ignores quarantine scenarios, or cyber defense that ignores seabed infrastructure, recreates bureaucratic seams for the adversary to play. Joint resilience exercises should therefore include simultaneous infrastructure and maritime stressors. The point is not theatrical realism for its own sake. It is teaching institutions to share a clock and a picture.

## What holding the cables means

Holding the threshold beneath the water means accepting that perfect prevention is unavailable and perfect attribution is often delayed.

It means investing in redundancy, warning, patrols, law, repair diplomacy, satellite complements, and partner watch lists anyway.

It means treating connectivity as political endurance.

It means refusing to let an ambiguous outage become a story that only the coercer is prepared to tell.

The seabed will remain a place where accidents and strategy can look alike.

The state’s job is to ensure that resemblance does not become a permanent license for coercion.


## Public confidence during a digital brownout

Technical restoration is only half the cable problem. The other half is public confidence during a digital brownout.

If payment systems stutter, if messaging apps degrade, if overseas family calls fail, if markets show odd behavior, rumor fills the channel. Disinformation can claim that the government is hiding a larger collapse, that foreign partners have abandoned the island, or that the outage proves invasion is imminent. Even without foreign amplification, domestic anxiety can create political pressure for visible action that may not match the evidence.

Whole-of-society resilience therefore includes pre-drafted public explanations, priority service lists people can understand, and local government capacity to answer practical questions. A population that knows what to expect from degraded connectivity is harder to panic.

This is soft infrastructure for hard cables.

## Industrial customers and the quiet reroute

Large industrial and financial users often feel cable risk before the general public does because their traffic engineering and contractual service levels make degradation visible. Some will quietly shift workloads, delay transfers, or activate contingency sites. Those quiet moves can be stabilizing. They can also become leading indicators that something is wrong before ministries complete their first meeting.

A mature resilience system cultivates trusted channels with major network users so that early commercial adaptation informs government awareness rather than blindsiding it. The point is not to commandeer private networks. It is to avoid discovering the outage’s economic meaning from equity-market headlines alone.

## Closing

Cables make Taiwan’s connection to the world look ethereal and make it in fact terrestrial, fragile, and political.

A gray-zone actor that understands that contradiction will keep returning to the seabed.

A defender that understands it will keep returning to redundancy, law, patrols, repair, partners, and public trust.

The threshold under the strait is held not by denying vulnerability, but by denying vulnerability its political harvest.


## Scenario discipline

It is easy, and irresponsible, to slide from “cables are vulnerable” to “Beijing will cut them on Tuesday.”

This book’s method forbids that slide. Capability and opportunity are not intent. Accidents remain common in crowded seas. Some vessels near cables are doing ordinary work. Over-attribution can waste diplomatic capital and frighten markets without improving security.

Scenario discipline means holding several possibilities at once: accident, negligence, opportunistic coercion, coordinated campaign, and wartime preparation. Evidence moves probability across that set. Doctrine and resilience should be robust across several members of the set rather than optimized for the most cinematic one alone.

That discipline is harder than alarm and more useful than complacency.

It is also the only way to keep cable security aligned with the book’s larger thesis: the danger is sequential coercion below the war threshold, and the defense is clearer classification, higher friction for gray moves, and enough resilience that ambiguous harm does not automatically become political surrender.


A final practical note concerns spare capacity and inventory that never appear in dramatic narratives: spare cable sections, jointing kits, trained technicians, fuel for repair ships, and pre-cleared customs processes for emergency equipment. These unglamorous stocks determine whether a fault becomes a day, a week, or a political season. Governments that fund only sensors and statements while neglecting repair logistics have bought the appearance of seriousness. The seabed will test the difference.


Partners can help by treating repair access and cable-protection cooperation as ordinary security assistance rather than as crisis improvisation. Shared vessel watch lists, joint tabletop exercises on simultaneous maritime and cable incidents, and quiet agreements on emergency satellite capacity for priority government traffic all raise the cost of isolation strategies. None of these steps requires a treaty revolution. They require administrative seriousness in peacetime, which is often scarcer than rhetoric after an outage.


That peacetime seriousness is the true undersea deterrent: not a promise that no cable will ever be cut, but a demonstrated capacity to restore connection fast enough that coercion cannot cash the outage as political victory.
