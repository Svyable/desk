# Air and Maritime Pressure

Pressure can be continuous without being decisive.

Aircraft fly near Taiwan. Ships operate around it. Patrols, drills, coast-guard activity, and military movements consume attention even when no shot is fired.

This creates a resource asymmetry.

A larger power can impose activity that a smaller defender feels obligated to monitor, intercept, shadow, or publicly explain.

The defender pays for vigilance.

The coercer chooses the tempo.

The obvious response is to match every action.

That can be a trap.

Deterrence is not measured by how many sorties are mirrored. It is measured by whether pressure changes political behavior, erodes readiness, creates operational surprise, or normalizes a new status quo.

Taiwan therefore needs selective responsiveness.

Some incursions or approaches require immediate tactical action. Others can be monitored through sensors and handled at lower cost. The objective is to preserve readiness for the activity that actually matters.

This makes automation valuable.

Machine systems can help classify tracks, prioritize anomalies, predict maintenance needs, and reduce the human burden of persistent monitoring.

But they must be designed to avoid routine pressure becoming routine alarm.

The same principle applies politically.

Every movement should not generate maximal rhetoric. Constant alarm can weaken credibility and exhaust foreign attention.

Instead, public communication should distinguish trends from incidents.

A single flight may be less important than a change in pattern.

A single ship may be less important than a new operating area becoming persistent.

The strategic contest is over normalization.

Who decides what becomes ordinary?

The answer should not be the actor capable of producing the most daily activity.
