# The Semiconductor Problem

Taiwan’s semiconductor industry creates a strategic paradox.

Its importance to the global economy can deter disruption because so many actors depend on continued production.

The same importance can make the island a focal point of strategic anxiety.

This is sometimes simplified into a silicon shield: the idea that semiconductor dependence protects Taiwan.

A shield is too simple.

Economic value influences incentives.

It does not control them.

The more useful image is a network under stress.

The strategic question is not merely whether factories survive.

It is how much of the semiconductor system can keep converting electricity, water, equipment, materials, designs, labor, logistics, and trust into qualified output while coercion changes the environment around it.

## The factory is not the system

The most common mental image is a fabrication plant.

That image is useful because the fab is where extraordinary manufacturing precision becomes visible. But a leading-edge semiconductor factory is not an island of self-contained capacity.

It is the center of a network.

Electricity has to remain stable. Water has to arrive at the required quality and volume. Specialty chemicals and gases have to be delivered. Equipment has to be maintained. Spare parts have to move. Engineers and operators have to reach the site. Software and design information have to flow. Customers have to be able to send designs and receive finished wafers. Packaging and testing have to continue. Ports and airports have to function. Financial settlement and insurance have to remain credible enough that suppliers keep participating.

TSMC’s own 2025 reporting makes this systems character explicit. The company identifies power interruptions, water availability, cyberattacks, supply-chain disruption, geopolitical tension, sabotage, and failures of critical facilities among the risks that can interrupt operations. Its annual reporting also notes the enormous electricity and water requirements of semiconductor manufacturing.

This is strategically important because a coercive campaign does not need to crater a fabrication facility to create semiconductor shock.

The ecosystem can fail around it.

A quarantine can delay chemicals. A cyberattack can disrupt logistics. Air or maritime pressure can affect insurance and shipping schedules. A power problem can halt production. Worker safety concerns can reduce staffing. Export controls can interrupt equipment support. A communications disruption can interfere with the design and operational systems that connect customers to manufacturing.

The semiconductor problem is therefore not a question of whether the fabs survive.

It is whether the industrial metabolism around them keeps moving.

## Map the production graph

A semiconductor supply chain should be treated as a production graph rather than a list of companies.

For every strategically important chip family, map the nodes required to move from design to qualified product.

Design tools.

Intellectual property blocks.

Mask production.

Wafer fabrication.

Specialty chemicals.

Industrial gases.

Equipment maintenance.

Replacement parts.

Process-control software.

Metrology.

Advanced packaging.

Testing.

Substrates.

Power.

Water.

Telecommunications.

Transport.

Finance.

Customer qualification.

A second fab does not create a second supply chain if it shares the same critical upstream or downstream node.

This is the semiconductor version of common-mode failure.

The graph should therefore mark each dependency by concentration and replacement time.

Which nodes can be bypassed in days?

Which require months?

Which require years?

Which depend on a small number of specialized engineers?

Which depend on software licenses or remote support?

Which are geographically concentrated outside Taiwan even when fabrication is on the island?

The strategic bottleneck may not be the fab.

It may be the node with the longest restoration clock.

## Qualified output is the real unit

Nominal capacity is not the same as usable semiconductor output.

A facility can be physically capable of producing wafers and still be strategically unavailable because the relevant process is not qualified for the customer’s product.

Yields matter.

Process stability matters.

Customer validation matters.

Packaging matters.

Volume matters.

This suggests a better metric than announced wafer capacity.

**Qualified substitute output.**

How much relevant product can a substitute site deliver at acceptable yield, quality, packaging, and volume within the required time?

This makes diversification more demanding.

A second site that can eventually make a similar chip may still provide little short-term resilience.

A mature-node facility may be highly valuable for one industrial sector and irrelevant for another.

A package bottleneck can nullify extra wafer capacity.

A customer may need months to validate a new source.

Strategic resilience therefore needs product-specific substitution maps rather than national capacity totals.

## The restart clock matters as much as the outage

Semiconductor disruption is not binary.

A facility can stop and restart.

The strategic question is how long recovery takes and what conditions must be restored first.

This creates a **fab restart clock**.

Power restoration.

Water quality verification.

Equipment inspection.

Clean-room stabilization.

Process recalibration.

Material replenishment.

Workforce return.

Cyber assurance.

Wafer-flow normalization.

Yield recovery.

Packaging and testing restart.

Customer acceptance.

A short technical outage can create a longer production outage if recovery requires sequential validation.

The same is true after cyber compromise.

Restoring network connectivity is not the same as restoring confidence in process-control data.

The restart clock should therefore be exercised and measured.

How long to safe restart?

How long to useful output?

How long to pre-disruption yield?

How long until customers trust the output again?

Those are four different clocks.

## Yield is strategic resilience

Semiconductor manufacturing is unusually sensitive to small process deviations.

A facility may remain open while yield falls.

That means the strategic effect of pressure can appear before complete shutdown.

Power-quality problems.

Material variability.

Delayed maintenance.

Staff fatigue.

Software disruption.

Environmental instability.

All can reduce effective output without creating a cinematic failure.

This suggests another metric.

**Resilient yield.**

What fraction of normal qualified output can be maintained under specified degraded conditions?

Not every resilience investment needs to preserve one hundred percent.

Preserving sixty percent of a critical product may have enormous political value if the alternative is zero.

The objective is graceful degradation of industrial output.

That is easier to plan when the system knows which dependencies reduce yield first.

## A shield made of incentives

The strongest version of the silicon-shield argument is not irrational.

Taiwan occupies a critical position in global semiconductor manufacturing, especially in advanced foundry production. Major technology firms, industrial companies, governments, and militaries depend on chips that pass through Taiwanese production.

A severe disruption would impose costs far beyond the Taiwan Strait.

That creates constituencies for stability.

Customers want continuity. Supplier countries want continuity. Financial markets want continuity. Governments worried about inflation, industrial output, and military supply want continuity.

In this sense, semiconductor importance can widen the coalition of actors that care about Taiwan’s security.

But economic interdependence is a deterrent only through political interpretation.

It works if leaders believe the economic consequences are large, relevant to their objectives, and difficult to absorb.

Those conditions can change.

A state may accept enormous economic cost for a political objective it considers fundamental. Leaders may believe disruption will be temporary. They may expect the other side to suffer more. They may miscalculate how fragile the supply chain is. They may assume coercion can remain below the threshold that causes catastrophic industrial interruption.

A shield made of incentives is therefore probabilistic.

It does not physically stop an action.

It changes the expected price.

The important question is how that price is distributed.

Who bears it first?

Who can absorb it?

Who can substitute?

Who panics?

Who pressures whom politically?

Economic pain can strengthen deterrence if it creates resistance to coercion.

It can weaken deterrence if it creates pressure on Taiwan to concede quickly.

The same dependency can push politics in opposite directions.

## Build an incentive matrix

The silicon-shield debate should therefore be analyzed through an **incentive matrix** rather than one slogan.

For each major partner or industry:

How dependent is it on Taiwanese output?

How quickly can it substitute?

How much inventory exists?

Which sectors experience shortage first?

How politically salient are those sectors?

Does the actor blame the coercer, Taiwan, or both?

What policy options become easier under resilience?

What policy options become harder under panic?

The matrix makes an uncomfortable point visible.

Economic importance is not automatically political support.

The mechanism matters.

The strategic goal should therefore be to convert semiconductor importance into durable coalition interest while reducing the panic channels that can make the same importance coercive.

## The wrong lesson from diversification

This is where semiconductor diversification becomes strategically confusing.

Governments outside Taiwan have invested heavily in domestic semiconductor capacity. The United States, Japan, Europe, and others have pursued subsidies, industrial policy, and partnerships intended to reduce dangerous concentration. TSMC itself has expanded manufacturing outside Taiwan, including in Arizona and Japan, while retaining enormous capacity on the island.

A January 2026 U.S.-Taiwan trade and investment agreement went further, with the U.S. Commerce Department announcing large Taiwanese investment and credit commitments intended to expand semiconductor and related technology capacity in the United States.

One interpretation is that diversification erodes Taiwan’s silicon shield.

If the rest of the world becomes less dependent on Taiwan, perhaps outsiders become less motivated to protect it.

That conclusion treats dependence as if maximum vulnerability were strategically desirable.

It is not.

Extreme dependence can create panic.

And panic can weaken deterrence.

If policymakers believe any resistance to coercion risks an immediate global depression, they may pressure the victim to concede rather than pressure the coercer to stop. The more catastrophic the expected economic damage, the greater the temptation to buy short-term stability at the target’s expense.

Resilience can change that calculation.

If partners have inventories, alternative capacity, repair plans, distributed packaging, diversified equipment support, resilient energy systems, and credible continuity plans, they may be better able to sustain a political response.

Diversification can therefore reduce vulnerability without reducing commitment.

The distinction is essential.

A coalition that depends on terror of supply collapse is fragile.

A coalition that can absorb disruption has more room to act.

## Diversification is slower than announcements

The opposite mistake is believing announced fabs equal replicated ecosystems.

Semiconductor production is not portable in the way a simple assembly line is portable.

Facilities require years to build, equip, qualify, and ramp. Leading production depends on dense supplier relationships, skilled labor, process knowledge, design ecosystems, equipment vendors, packaging, and accumulated operational experience.

TSMC reported that its 2025 manufacturing network still included four 12-inch gigafabs in Taiwan along with additional Taiwanese facilities, even as overseas capacity expanded.

That should temper both triumphal and alarmist interpretations.

The world is diversifying.

It is not instantly duplicating Taiwan.

Strategic resilience should therefore be measured in capabilities rather than ribbon cuttings.

How much qualified capacity exists at the relevant process nodes?

Can substitute fabs make the same products at sufficient yield?

Is advanced packaging available outside the primary geography?

Are equipment maintenance, chemicals, masks, substrates, and design workflows also diversified?

How quickly can customers shift production?

Can the receiving facility handle the volume?

A second factory is not a second supply chain if the same hidden dependencies remain underneath both.

This suggests a **diversification clock**.

Announced.

Under construction.

Equipment installed.

Process qualified.

Customer qualified.

Volume ramped.

Supply-chain diversified.

Each stage has different strategic meaning.

Political rhetoric often treats them as one event.

Resilience planning should not.

## Customer switching time is part of national security

A customer cannot always redirect a design instantly.

Different foundries may have different process design kits, libraries, packaging options, yield characteristics, and qualification requirements.

The technical and commercial switching process therefore matters.

This creates **time to qualified switch**.

How long from loss of the primary source to acceptable production at an alternate source?

Include redesign if necessary.

Verification.

Security review.

Yield ramp.

Packaging.

Testing.

Volume allocation.

The switching clock can be longer than inventory buffers.

If so, the system has a gap.

That gap is a strategic vulnerability even when an alternate fab exists.

Governments and companies can reduce it through prequalification, dual sourcing where practical, common design practices, and contingency agreements.

Not every product can justify that cost.

Critical products may.

## Inventory is time, not independence

Chip inventories can buy time.

They do not create production.

A customer with several months of stock can absorb a short disruption while suppliers restart or switch.

A customer with minimal inventory can transmit the shock almost immediately.

This makes inventory policy part of resilience.

But inventories vary by product and shelf constraints.

The important measure is **buffer-to-switch ratio**.

How long can the existing stock support critical demand relative to the time required to restore or switch supply?

If the buffer exceeds the switching clock, the system has room.

If the buffer is shorter, political pressure begins before technical substitution is ready.

That is where resilience becomes crisis diplomacy.

## Packaging is part of the chokepoint

The strategic conversation often stops at wafer fabrication.

It should not.

Advanced packaging and testing can become separate bottlenecks.

A wafer is not a finished product.

The chain has to continue.

Distributed fabrication with concentrated packaging can leave the overall system fragile.

The same applies to substrates and testing capacity.

This is another reason the production graph must extend beyond the fab.

A resilient semiconductor network should know which products fail because wafers cannot be made and which fail because completed wafers cannot be packaged, tested, or moved.

Different bottlenecks require different investment.

## Utilities are strategic dependencies

Power and water deserve special treatment because they are both ordinary infrastructure and semiconductor inputs.

The fab may have backup systems.

The broader industrial ecosystem may not.

Suppliers need power.

Packaging needs power.

Transport systems need power.

Communications need power.

Water treatment and pumping need power.

A resilience plan that protects one facility while the surrounding infrastructure degrades can still fail.

The relevant metric is **utility continuity depth**.

How long can critical semiconductor production sustain specified degraded grid or water conditions?

Which functions shut down first?

Which can be prioritized?

What local backup exists?

How quickly can damaged infrastructure be repaired?

This turns whole-of-society resilience into industrial arithmetic.

## The workforce is part of the machine

Semiconductor expertise is embodied in people.

Engineers.

Technicians.

Operators.

Maintenance specialists.

Process experts.

Managers who know how to recover from abnormal conditions.

A crisis can interrupt labor without damaging equipment.

Transport may fail.

Families may evacuate.

Schools may close.

Communications may become unreliable.

Fatigue may rise.

This creates a **critical workforce continuity** problem.

Which roles must be physically present?

Which can work remotely?

Which skills have only one or two qualified people?

What transport and shelter arrangements exist?

Can shifts be sustained?

Can foreign specialists reach the facility if required?

The workforce should be mapped with the same seriousness as equipment spares.

Human single points of failure exist too.

## Cyber recovery is a trust problem

A cyberattack can halt operations directly.

It can also create uncertainty about whether process data, recipes, scheduling systems, or maintenance records can be trusted.

This creates a recovery problem different from ordinary IT restoration.

The network may come back before confidence does.

A fab or supplier needs to know whether the restored system is safe enough to resume precision production.

That requires clean backups, independent logs, configuration baselines, segmented networks, and known recovery procedures.

The critical metric is not only **time to reconnect**.

It is **time to trusted production**.

A system that restarts quickly but cannot prove the integrity of its process state may create defective output or deeper disruption.

Cyber resilience therefore belongs inside the fab restart clock.

## The coercion ladder

Semiconductors also change the gray-zone ladder because industrial disruption can be calibrated.

A coercer does not face only two choices: leave the fabs alone or destroy them.

There are intermediate pressures.

Shipping can be delayed without being stopped permanently. Customs or inspection regimes can create uncertainty. Cyber operations can target business systems rather than fabrication equipment. Energy infrastructure can be pressured. Specific companies can face sanctions or licensing barriers. Air and maritime exercises can raise insurance costs and complicate schedules. Information operations can target worker confidence or investor expectations.

Each action can be described as limited.

The cumulative effect can be severe.

This is why semiconductor resilience belongs in a book about thresholds rather than only in a book about industrial policy.

The relevant question is how much economic disruption can be imposed before governments classify the activity as something requiring a stronger response.

That threshold may differ by actor.

A shipping company may react before a government does. An insurer may change prices before a navy changes posture. A technology firm may reroute orders before diplomats agree on the legal characterization of the crisis.

Commercial behavior can therefore become part of escalation dynamics.

The strategic indicator is **industrial degradation without physical destruction**.

How much qualified output has fallen?

How much restart time has increased?

How much insurance or logistics friction has accumulated?

How much customer switching has begun?

Those effects can classify the severity of coercion before intent is fully known.

## The hostage theory

There is a darker version of the silicon-shield argument: the idea that semiconductor capacity is valuable partly because no actor can seize it intact and use it easily.

That may be true in a narrow operational sense. Modern fabs depend on global equipment, software, materials, expertise, and continuous technical support. Control of the building would not automatically mean control of the capability.

But treating an industry as a strategic hostage is dangerous.

It encourages fatalism.

It can turn economic concentration into a security policy rather than a condition to be managed.

Taiwan’s safety should not depend on convincing everyone that a war would destroy something they cannot live without.

The better objective is to make coercion less rewarding while preserving Taiwan’s central role in advanced technology.

That means resilience on the island and resilience among partners.

## Resilience on the island

Taiwanese semiconductor resilience is inseparable from whole-of-society resilience.

Power generation and transmission matter. Water storage and reuse matter. Cyber defense matters. Ports and airports matter. Telecommunications matter. Civil defense matters. Repair crews matter. The ability to restore services after disruption matters.

Industrial continuity cannot be protected only at the factory gate.

This creates a useful policy test.

If a security investment protects a fab but not the grid that feeds it, the protection is incomplete.

If a continuity plan assumes employees can commute normally during sustained coercion, it is incomplete.

If spare parts exist but cannot cross a pressured port, it is incomplete.

If alternative power exists but communications needed to coordinate production do not, it is incomplete.

Resilience is the ability to keep the network functioning in degraded conditions, not the ability to preserve every component unchanged.

A useful exercise is to define **minimum viable semiconductor function**.

Which product categories must continue?

At what percentage of normal output?

Which customer or national-security needs receive priority?

Which utilities receive restoration priority?

Which materials must be prepositioned?

Which transport lanes matter most?

Which parts of the ecosystem can pause without creating irreversible damage?

The objective is not central planning of every commercial wafer.

It is to know what preserving industrial continuity actually requires.

## Resilience outside Taiwan

Partners face the mirror-image task.

They should reduce the political leverage created by their own fragility.

That means more than building fabs.

It includes inventories, supplier mapping, packaging capacity, equipment maintenance, workforce development, design portability, alternative logistics, and clear crisis priorities for scarce chips.

Governments should also understand which semiconductor shortages are strategically intolerable and which are economically painful but manageable.

Without that distinction, every disruption looks equally catastrophic.

Panic thrives on undifferentiated dependency.

Planning turns dependency into categories.

Partners should therefore maintain a **critical chip register**.

Not every product in the economy.

The components whose loss would rapidly affect essential infrastructure, defense production, communications, transport, energy, health, or other nationally important systems.

For each category:

Inventory buffer.

Qualified suppliers.

Switching time.

Packaging dependencies.

Repair alternatives.

Demand-reduction options.

The register makes crisis allocation less improvised.

It also avoids treating every commercial shortage as the same strategic emergency.

## Scarcity allocation will become political

If disruption is severe, scarce semiconductors will have competing users.

Consumer electronics.

Vehicles.

Industrial machinery.

Data centers.

Telecommunications.

Defense production.

Critical infrastructure.

Governments may face pressure to influence allocation.

That creates difficult questions.

Who decides priority?

How much should markets determine?

Which firms have existing contracts?

How are allied needs weighed against domestic needs?

How does a government avoid turning temporary allocation into permanent industrial favoritism?

These questions should be discussed before scarcity becomes acute.

The purpose is not to prescribe one allocation system.

It is to recognize that semiconductor resilience ends in political choice when supply cannot satisfy all demand.

## The coalition effect

The deepest strategic consequence of diversification may therefore be political rather than industrial.

A more resilient partner network can make deterrence commitments more credible because leaders know they have options after disruption begins.

This reverses the usual intuition.

Reducing dependence on Taiwan does not necessarily reduce Taiwan’s security value.

It can reduce the coercive leverage created by everybody else’s fear.

The objective is not to make Taiwan economically ordinary.

Its semiconductor ecosystem will remain extraordinarily important for a long time.

The objective is to ensure that importance strengthens a durable coalition rather than creating a panic button an adversary can press.

This can be measured through **coalition shock absorption**.

How long can major partners sustain critical functions after disruption before economic pressure begins to dominate political choice?

Days?

Weeks?

Months?

What fraction of demand can be shifted?

Which sectors become politically acute first?

Which support measures extend the decision window?

The longer the coalition can absorb shock, the less valuable semiconductor disruption becomes as a tool for forcing rapid political concession.

## AI can map the system without replacing industrial judgment

AI can help identify hidden supplier dependencies, forecast inventory depletion, model customer switching, prioritize repair, detect anomalous production patterns, and compare resilience scenarios.

It can help maintain the production graph.

It can estimate which nodes create the largest restoration delay.

It can model how a shipping disruption propagates into qualified output.

But the model should not be allowed to convert sparse operational data into false certainty.

Yield recovery is context-specific.

Customer qualification is partly organizational.

Political allocation is not an optimization problem alone.

Worker behavior under crisis is not a fixed variable.

The machine can make dependencies legible.

It cannot make the system physically redundant by describing it better.

## Exercise industrial recovery

Semiconductor resilience should be rehearsed at the system level.

Simulate loss of one utility.

Loss of one port route.

Loss of one equipment supplier.

Cyber compromise of scheduling or process data.

Delayed packaging.

A sudden customer shift to alternate capacity.

A workforce disruption.

Then measure:

Time to safe restart.

Time to trusted production.

Time to useful yield.

Time to qualified customer output.

Percentage of critical product preserved.

Inventory buffer consumed.

Number of single-source dependencies exposed.

Number of substitutes that existed only on paper.

The exercise does not need to shut down a fab physically.

Tabletops, technical drills, supplier exercises, and continuity tests can reveal the dependency graph.

The important distinction is between stated resilience and demonstrated recovery pathways.

## The falsifiable test

The thesis can be tested.

If announced off-island fabrication capacity reliably substitutes for disrupted Taiwanese production without major delay in qualification, yield, packaging, logistics, or customer switching, then the ecosystem arguments here are overstated.

If fab continuity proves largely independent of utilities, workforce, cyber trust, materials, packaging, and transport, then the network model is too broad.

If extreme partner dependency consistently strengthens political support without generating panic pressure for concession, then the concern about vulnerability-driven coalition fragility is weaker than argued.

The opposite pattern would strengthen the thesis.

If disruption propagates through non-fab nodes, if restart takes much longer than technical repair, if customer switching exceeds inventory buffers, if off-island capacity shares hidden chokepoints, or if partner resilience increases the time governments can sustain a political response, then semiconductor security is fundamentally a systems problem.

## The strategic objective

For Taiwan, the strategic goal is to remain valuable without relying on value as the sole deterrent.

For partners, the goal is to become resilient without confusing resilience with abandonment.

For industry, the goal is to preserve enough redundancy and recovery capacity that a disruption does not become an irreversible production collapse.

For governments, the goal is to keep economic fear from owning the decision clock.

The important unit is the system around the fab.

A shield that requires the world to remain terrified is not a stable defense.

A network that can take a hit and keep choosing is.

The semiconductor problem is therefore not solved when another factory opens.

It is solved only in degrees, when the network can show that power, water, people, equipment, materials, software, packaging, logistics, finance, and political cooperation can continue producing qualified output after the assumptions of normal commerce stop being true.
