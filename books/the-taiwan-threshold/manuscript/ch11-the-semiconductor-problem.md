# The Semiconductor Problem

Taiwan’s semiconductor industry creates a strategic paradox.

Its importance to the global economy can deter disruption because so many actors depend on continued production.

The same importance can make the island a focal point of strategic anxiety.

This is sometimes simplified into a silicon shield: the idea that semiconductor dependence protects Taiwan.

A shield is too simple.

Economic value influences incentives.

It does not control them.

## The factory is not the system

The most common mental image is a fabrication plant.

That image is useful because the fab is where extraordinary manufacturing precision becomes visible. But a leading-edge semiconductor factory is not an island of self-contained capacity.

It is the center of a network.

Electricity has to remain stable. Water has to arrive at the required quality and volume. Specialty chemicals and gases have to be delivered. Equipment has to be maintained. Spare parts have to move. Engineers and operators have to reach the site. Software and design information have to flow. Customers have to be able to send designs and receive finished wafers. Packaging and testing have to continue. Ports and airports have to function. Financial settlement and insurance have to remain credible enough that suppliers keep participating.

TSMC’s own 2025 reporting makes this systems character explicit. The company identifies power interruptions, water availability, cyberattacks, supply-chain disruption, geopolitical tension, sabotage, and failures of critical facilities among the risks that can interrupt operations. Its annual reporting also notes the enormous electricity and water requirements of semiconductor manufacturing.

This is strategically important because a coercive campaign does not need to crater a fabrication facility to create semiconductor shock.

The ecosystem can fail around it.

A quarantine can delay chemicals. A cyberattack can disrupt logistics. Air or maritime pressure can affect insurance and shipping schedules. A power problem can halt production. Worker safety concerns can reduce staffing. Export controls can interrupt equipment support. A communications disruption can interfere with the design and operational systems that connect customers to manufacturing.

The semiconductor problem is therefore not a question of whether the fabs survive.

It is whether the industrial metabolism around them keeps moving.

## A shield made of incentives

The strongest version of the silicon-shield argument is not irrational.

Taiwan occupies a critical position in global semiconductor manufacturing, especially in advanced foundry production. Major technology firms, industrial companies, governments, and militaries depend on chips that pass through Taiwanese production.

A severe disruption would impose costs far beyond the Taiwan Strait.

That creates constituencies for stability.

Customers want continuity. Supplier countries want continuity. Financial markets want continuity. Governments worried about inflation, industrial output, and military supply want continuity.

In this sense, semiconductor importance can widen the coalition of actors that care about Taiwan’s security.

But economic interdependence is a deterrent only through political interpretation.

It works if leaders believe the economic consequences are large, relevant to their objectives, and difficult to absorb.

Those conditions can change.

A state may accept enormous economic cost for a political objective it considers fundamental. Leaders may believe disruption will be temporary. They may expect the other side to suffer more. They may miscalculate how fragile the supply chain is. They may assume coercion can remain below the threshold that causes catastrophic industrial interruption.

A shield made of incentives is therefore probabilistic.

It does not physically stop an action.

It changes the expected price.

## The wrong lesson from diversification

This is where semiconductor diversification becomes strategically confusing.

Governments outside Taiwan have invested heavily in domestic semiconductor capacity. The United States, Japan, Europe, and others have pursued subsidies, industrial policy, and partnerships intended to reduce dangerous concentration. TSMC itself has expanded manufacturing outside Taiwan, including in Arizona and Japan, while retaining enormous capacity on the island.

A January 2026 U.S.-Taiwan trade and investment agreement went further, with the U.S. Commerce Department announcing large Taiwanese investment and credit commitments intended to expand semiconductor and related technology capacity in the United States.

One interpretation is that diversification erodes Taiwan’s silicon shield.

If the rest of the world becomes less dependent on Taiwan, perhaps outsiders become less motivated to protect it.

That conclusion treats dependence as if maximum vulnerability were strategically desirable.

It is not.

Extreme dependence can create panic.

And panic can weaken deterrence.

If policymakers believe any resistance to coercion risks an immediate global depression, they may pressure the victim to concede rather than pressure the coercer to stop. The more catastrophic the expected economic damage, the greater the temptation to buy short-term stability at the target’s expense.

Resilience can change that calculation.

If partners have inventories, alternative capacity, repair plans, distributed packaging, diversified equipment support, resilient energy systems, and credible continuity plans, they may be better able to sustain a political response.

Diversification can therefore reduce vulnerability without reducing commitment.

The distinction is essential.

A coalition that depends on terror of supply collapse is fragile.

A coalition that can absorb disruption has more room to act.

## Diversification is slower than announcements

The opposite mistake is believing announced fabs equal replicated ecosystems.

Semiconductor production is not portable in the way a simple assembly line is portable.

Facilities require years to build, equip, qualify, and ramp. Leading production depends on dense supplier relationships, skilled labor, process knowledge, design ecosystems, equipment vendors, packaging, and accumulated operational experience.

TSMC reported that its 2025 manufacturing network still included four 12-inch gigafabs in Taiwan along with additional Taiwanese facilities, even as overseas capacity expanded.

That should temper both triumphal and alarmist interpretations.

The world is diversifying.

It is not instantly duplicating Taiwan.

Strategic resilience should therefore be measured in capabilities rather than ribbon cuttings.

How much qualified capacity exists at the relevant process nodes?

Can substitute fabs make the same products at sufficient yield?

Is advanced packaging available outside the primary geography?

Are equipment maintenance, chemicals, masks, substrates, and design workflows also diversified?

How quickly can customers shift production?

Can the receiving facility handle the volume?

A second factory is not a second supply chain if the same hidden dependencies remain underneath both.

## The coercion ladder

Semiconductors also change the gray-zone ladder because industrial disruption can be calibrated.

A coercer does not face only two choices: leave the fabs alone or destroy them.

There are intermediate pressures.

Shipping can be delayed without being stopped permanently. Customs or inspection regimes can create uncertainty. Cyber operations can target business systems rather than fabrication equipment. Energy infrastructure can be pressured. Specific companies can face sanctions or licensing barriers. Air and maritime exercises can raise insurance costs and complicate schedules. Information operations can target worker confidence or investor expectations.

Each action can be described as limited.

The cumulative effect can be severe.

This is why semiconductor resilience belongs in a book about thresholds rather than only in a book about industrial policy.

The relevant question is how much economic disruption can be imposed before governments classify the activity as something requiring a stronger response.

That threshold may differ by actor.

A shipping company may react before a government does. An insurer may change prices before a navy changes posture. A technology firm may reroute orders before diplomats agree on the legal characterization of the crisis.

Commercial behavior can therefore become part of escalation dynamics.

## The hostage theory

There is a darker version of the silicon-shield argument: the idea that semiconductor capacity is valuable partly because no actor can seize it intact and use it easily.

That may be true in a narrow operational sense. Modern fabs depend on global equipment, software, materials, expertise, and continuous technical support. Control of the building would not automatically mean control of the capability.

But treating an industry as a strategic hostage is dangerous.

It encourages fatalism.

It can turn economic concentration into a security policy rather than a condition to be managed.

Taiwan’s safety should not depend on convincing everyone that a war would destroy something they cannot live without.

The better objective is to make coercion less rewarding while preserving Taiwan’s central role in advanced technology.

That means resilience on the island and resilience among partners.

## Resilience on the island

Taiwanese semiconductor resilience is inseparable from whole-of-society resilience.

Power generation and transmission matter. Water storage and reuse matter. Cyber defense matters. Ports and airports matter. Telecommunications matter. Civil defense matters. Repair crews matter. The ability to restore services after disruption matters.

Industrial continuity cannot be protected only at the factory gate.

This creates a useful policy test.

If a security investment protects a fab but not the grid that feeds it, the protection is incomplete.

If a continuity plan assumes employees can commute normally during sustained coercion, it is incomplete.

If spare parts exist but cannot cross a pressured port, it is incomplete.

If alternative power exists but communications needed to coordinate production do not, it is incomplete.

Resilience is the ability to keep the network functioning in degraded conditions, not the ability to preserve every component unchanged.

## Resilience outside Taiwan

Partners face the mirror-image task.

They should reduce the political leverage created by their own fragility.

That means more than building fabs.

It includes inventories, supplier mapping, packaging capacity, equipment maintenance, workforce development, design portability, alternative logistics, and clear crisis priorities for scarce chips.

Governments should also understand which semiconductor shortages are strategically intolerable and which are economically painful but manageable.

Without that distinction, every disruption looks equally catastrophic.

Panic thrives on undifferentiated dependency.

Planning turns dependency into categories.

## The coalition effect

The deepest strategic consequence of diversification may therefore be political rather than industrial.

A more resilient partner network can make deterrence commitments more credible because leaders know they have options after disruption begins.

This reverses the usual intuition.

Reducing dependence on Taiwan does not necessarily reduce Taiwan’s security value.

It can reduce the coercive leverage created by everybody else’s fear.

The objective is not to make Taiwan economically ordinary.

Its semiconductor ecosystem will remain extraordinarily important for a long time.

The objective is to ensure that importance strengthens a durable coalition rather than creating a panic button an adversary can press.

For Taiwan, the strategic goal is to remain valuable without relying on value as the sole deterrent.

For partners, the goal is to become resilient without confusing resilience with abandonment.

For everyone, the important unit is the system around the fab.

A shield that requires the world to remain terrified is not a stable defense.

A network that can take a hit and keep choosing is.
