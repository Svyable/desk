# The Crisis Table

A crisis table is where abstract strategy becomes a list of names: president, defense and foreign ministers, military commanders, intelligence officials, economic agencies, infrastructure operators, coast-guard leadership, central bankers, cybersecurity officials, allied representatives, and private companies that control ports, cables, communications, insurance, shipping, or cloud infrastructure. The quality of deterrence depends partly on whether these people and institutions have rehearsed the same problem before they meet under pressure.

Strategy is usually written in nouns: deterrence, resilience, interoperability, escalation control, whole-of-society defense. At the table every noun becomes a question about action. Who decides, calls, classifies, signs, tells the public or an ally? Who has authority if the first person is unavailable? Who can act before the legal label is settled? These are not administrative details. They are how deterrence operates.

Taiwan’s whole-of-society resilience exercises point toward the problem because gray-zone coercion does not fit neatly inside one ministry. A quarantine reaches maritime authorities, ports, customs, trade, insurance, defense, diplomacy, law enforcement, financial regulators, and public communication. A cable incident reaches telecom operators, repair companies, coast guards, intelligence services, prosecutors, shipping firms, and foreign partners. Cyber operations can reach nearly everyone, while disinformation can change public behavior before officials agree on whether the underlying incident was deliberate. A military exercise can alter commercial insurance costs without a single order from Taipei.

The crisis table therefore needs cross-domain muscle memory, by which I mean something less grand than doctrine and more useful than a binder. People need to know which number to call, which authority applies, what information can be shared, which channel an ally will recognize as authoritative, and whether a company can expect government support for a risky repair operation. The sequence will change under pressure. Having one at all keeps the first hour from becoming pure institutional improvisation.

That matters most below the threshold of obvious war. A missile strike clarifies many authorities quickly. A contested inspection regime, suspicious cable break, coordinated cyber disruption, or cluster of maritime safety notices and commercial pressure may not fit any familiar category. The coercer benefits if the defender requires a clean label before its institutions can coordinate.

The table should be able to function while the label remains provisional. If a cable is down, restore connectivity. If commercial shipping avoids a route, find out why and create alternatives. If banks face a liquidity shock, stabilize the system. If false emergency messages circulate, authenticate the official channel. Attribution, legal classification, and political responsibility still matter, but resilience cannot wait for perfect forensic certainty. The table has to keep two questions separate: what do we know about who caused this, and what must we do regardless?

Exercises are where that distinction becomes operational. A useful tabletop creates friction rather than proving readiness. The coast guard may regard an event as law enforcement while the military sees operational preparation. The finance ministry may fear market panic while the foreign ministry wants allied language before public attribution. A telecom company may say repair access is unsafe. An ally may request information that domestic classification rules make difficult to release. A minister may want to speak before the intelligence assessment is finished. The exercise is the disagreement.

Its value appears in what changes afterward: perhaps a phone tree, a clarified delegation of authority, a data-sharing agreement, a pre-negotiated commercial contract, a backup communications channel, or an allied procedure tested before the next exercise. If nothing changes, the exercise may have produced confidence rather than readiness.

Time also needs to be separated from category. A cyber intrusion into a critical control system may require isolation in seconds. A suspicious maritime pattern may deserve hours of observation before political classification. A sanctions decision may permit longer consultation. Military dispersal may be urgent but reversible; public attribution may be slower precisely because it is harder to reverse. The fastest problem should not force every other problem onto its clock.

AI can help maintain this moving picture: which actions are pending, who owns them, what information they depend on, which decisions become harder if delayed, what authorities a classification activates, and which commercial systems are already reacting without government instruction. That is coordination support, not a machine chairing the crisis.

The political table still needs hierarchy, not because one ministry should dominate every domain but because responsibility must become clear when disagreement persists. A process in which everyone has a veto and nobody owns the consequence of delay can fail through paralysis. The opposite failure is a highly centralized table that moves quickly by suppressing specialized knowledge. Legal uncertainty disappears from the briefing, economic effects become secondary, allied concerns become footnotes, and the leader receives one fused recommendation that is cleaner than the world it describes.

Structured dissent is the harder design. Someone must own the current operational picture while someone else maintains competing interpretations. Lawyers should explain authorities without being asked to settle strategic ambiguity that law cannot settle. Economic agencies should model commercial reaction rather than assume firms will wait for government direction. Infrastructure operators need a direct route into the table when their systems become strategic terrain, and allied liaisons need to surface political constraints early enough to shape options. Someone should be responsible for saying that the current story may be wrong.

That role becomes more important as machine systems produce increasingly coherent situational pictures. A dashboard can fuse shipping data, air tracks, cyber alerts, market moves, social-media narratives, satellite imagery, intelligence reporting, and diplomatic statements into one sequence. Chronology can make the sequence feel causal when it is not. The table needs to preserve the distinction between what was observed and what was inferred. Source lineage, correlated evidence, alternative hypotheses, and fragile assumptions should survive presentation rather than disappearing inside a confident synthesis.

Simulation can expose the same problem from another direction. What happens if an inspection regime expands but no shots are fired, commercial shipping stops voluntarily, a cable failure coincides with a cyberattack, or an ally supports economic measures while rejecting military signaling? A machine can generate hundreds of variants; quantity is not the point. The useful branch is the one that reveals a dependency planners did not know they had.

Traditional table exercises often have a script, and participants gradually learn its assumptions as the event advances toward a designed teaching point. An adaptive simulation can respond to choices instead: close one port and another becomes congested; issue a public statement and markets react; move a military unit and an ally asks for clarification. But the simulation should not become theater or a contest against the scenario generator. Its job is to expose institutional seams.

Commercial power belongs at the table because governments do not control every consequential response to gray-zone coercion. Insurers can raise premiums, shipping companies reroute, banks de-risk, technology firms suspend services, repair companies judge an operating area unsafe, airlines cancel routes, and investors move money. Those decisions can intensify coercion without any new military act. Relationships with firms therefore have to exist before the crisis: what information would change an insurer’s risk judgment, what protection a repair vessel needs, what assurance keeps a payment channel open, and what redundancy a cloud provider actually has.

Public communication presents a related problem because gray-zone crises are partly contests over classification. Accident, exercise, law enforcement, inspection, blockade, sabotage, cyber incident, economic coercion, war: the label affects expectations, legal authorities, allied politics, and commercial behavior. Governments should not improvise every category under maximum pressure. They can distinguish confirmed fact from assessed pattern, unverified claim from attribution under investigation, and operational response from political judgment. Credibility is part of the response. Calling every ambiguous incident aggression eventually weakens the word; waiting for perfect attribution can allow a coercive pattern to normalize before the public understands it.

Allied coordination has the same requirement for preparation. Consultation is not itself the problem. Discovering incompatible definitions, intelligence classifications, legal authorities, sanctions procedures, public messages, and thresholds for support after the crisis begins is the problem. Known disagreement can be planned around. Surprise disagreement consumes the time an adversary is trying to exploit.

That is why liaison matters. The first allied conversation should not begin with introductions. The people should know one another, the channels should have been exercised, the terminology compared, and authentication tested. Succession deserves the same treatment. Crisis plans often assume the named leader is available, yet communications fail, travel becomes impossible, cyberattacks isolate offices, and ministries become overloaded. Authority has to move without becoming ambiguous. The second person should know they are second, and allies should be able to authenticate that authority under degraded conditions.

The final value of the crisis table is political. Deterrence depends not only on capability but on an adversary’s estimate of whether the defender can convert capability into coordinated action. Delay, jurisdictional gaps, public confusion, commercial panic, and alliance friction all create openings. Gray-zone strategy works partly in the space between institutions.

Exercises narrow that space by building decision capacity. A government that can classify provisionally, act on effects, consult allies, stabilize civilian systems, preserve uncertainty, communicate publicly, and escalate proportionally is harder to manipulate with ambiguity. The gray zone remains. It becomes less profitable.

That is what the table is for: not instant certainty or universal agreement, but enough practiced coordination to notice when the threshold moves and know which institutions have to move with it.
