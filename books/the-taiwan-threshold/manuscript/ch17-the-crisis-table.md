# The Crisis Table

A crisis table is where abstract strategy becomes a list of names.

President.

Defense minister.

Foreign minister.

Military commanders.

Intelligence officials.

Economic agencies.

Infrastructure operators.

Allied representatives.

The quality of deterrence depends partly on whether these people and institutions have rehearsed the same problem before they meet under pressure.

Taiwan’s whole-of-society resilience exercises point toward this reality. Gray-zone coercion does not fit neatly inside one ministry.

A quarantine affects maritime authorities, ports, customs, trade, insurance, defense, diplomacy, and public communication.

A cable incident affects telecom operators, repair companies, coast guards, intelligence services, and foreign partners.

A cyber campaign affects nearly everyone.

The crisis table therefore needs cross-domain muscle memory.

Tabletop exercises are valuable when they create friction rather than when they prove readiness.

The best exercise produces uncomfortable questions.

Who has authority?

Who speaks publicly?

Which partner must be consulted?

What information can be shared?

What happens if the first assumption is wrong?

Where is the civilian continuity plan?

What if commercial actors behave differently from the government’s expectation?

AI can make these exercises richer by generating adaptive scenarios, information noise, conflicting reports, and branching consequences.

It should not make them theatrical.

The objective is not to defeat the simulation.

It is to discover the seams before reality finds them.

The crisis table is a deterrent asset because an adversary calculates not only capability but decision speed.

A government that knows how to classify, consult, communicate, and act proportionally is harder to manipulate with ambiguity.

Prepared judgment narrows the gray zone.
