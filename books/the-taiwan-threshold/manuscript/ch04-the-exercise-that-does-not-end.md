# The Exercise That Does Not End

Military exercises have a built-in ambiguity.

They are real military activity conducted under a label that says they are not war.

Troops move. Aircraft fly. Ships deploy. Command systems activate. Logistics are tested. Units learn routes and timing.

The same activities can prepare a force for actual operations.

That does not mean every exercise is cover for attack.

Treating every drill as imminent war would make warning impossible because the system would remain permanently alarmed.

The harder problem is transition.

When does an exercise stop being an exercise?

A coercive strategy can exploit this uncertainty by extending, repeating, or varying drills until elevated military presence becomes normal. Commercial actors adapt. Taiwan’s forces spend resources monitoring and responding. Foreign governments become accustomed to alerts.

Normalization can reduce the political shock of the next escalation.

It can also create warning fatigue.

If every large exercise triggers predictions of invasion that do not materialize, decision-makers may become less responsive when a later exercise contains genuinely unusual indicators.

The solution is not better rhetoric.

It is better discrimination.

Analysts need indicators that distinguish routine exercise behavior from operational transition: logistics patterns, reserve activation, fuel and ammunition movement, command changes, medical preparation, air and maritime exclusion patterns, civilian shipping measures, communications behavior, and other observable differences.

Even then, intent remains uncertain.

A force can prepare for several options at once.

This is where AI can help and harm.

Machine systems can compare current activity with large historical baselines and identify anomalies faster than human teams. They can fuse maritime, satellite, communications, and open-source data.

But anomaly is not intention.

The model may become excellent at saying “this is unusual” while leaving the political system tempted to translate unusual into attack.

The safest warning architecture separates three questions.

What is happening?

How unusual is it?

What might it mean?

Machines can contribute strongly to the first two.

The third remains a contest among hypotheses.

The exercise that does not end is therefore a test of institutional patience.

The defender must remain alert without becoming predictable, exhausted, or easy to manipulate.

Deterrence depends not only on seeing the signal.

It depends on refusing to let repeated signals destroy the ability to judge.
