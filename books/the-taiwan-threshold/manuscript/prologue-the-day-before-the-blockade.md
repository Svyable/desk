# The Day Before the Blockade

The first sign may not be a missile.

It may be an email from an insurer.

A shipping company learns that a vessel scheduled to call at Kaohsiung now carries a premium its customer did not budget for. Another carrier receives a notice about an exercise area. A captain is told that Chinese maritime authorities have announced new inspection requirements. A port operator sees ships begin to slow or alter course before any government has told them to stop.

Nothing has been sunk.

No declaration of war has been issued.

The cargo still exists. The port still exists. The route still exists on the chart.

But commerce is made not only of ships and water. It is made of assumptions about what will happen if the ship keeps going.

That is where a Taiwan crisis can begin.

The image that dominates public discussion is an amphibious invasion: missiles, aircraft, ships, beaches, urban fighting. The scenario deserves attention because its consequences would be enormous. It is also unusually clear. A large-scale invasion presents governments with terrible choices, but not much semantic uncertainty about what is happening.

Coercion below that threshold is different.

China can place coast-guard vessels around Taiwan, announce inspections, conduct exercises, increase military patrols, use cyber operations, pressure companies, manipulate information, interfere with communications, or selectively challenge traffic while insisting that it is enforcing laws in territory it claims. Each action can be made to look smaller than the response required to stop it.

That asymmetry is the strategic instrument.

A blockade announces military conflict. A quarantine can be described as regulation. A missile strike gives an alliance a visible event around which to organize. A customs rule forces lawyers, insurers, port operators, military staffs, and cabinets to decide whether compliance has already conceded the principle at stake.

The distinction is not academic.

If a commercial vessel receives an instruction from the China Coast Guard, does it ignore the instruction because Taipei rejects Beijing’s jurisdiction? Does its insurer agree? Does the flag state send an escort? If an American or Japanese warship accompanies the vessel, has a law-enforcement dispute been transformed into a military confrontation? If no escort appears and the vessel complies, has China demonstrated practical control without firing?

The threshold becomes a sequence of private decisions before it becomes a public one.

This is why the most important actors in the opening hours of a crisis may include people rarely shown in war games: shipping executives, insurers, telecommunications engineers, cable-repair coordinators, energy traders, cloud operators, customs officials, and lawyers. Their actions can determine whether pressure remains symbolic or begins to change the physical behavior of the island’s economy.

Taiwan has spent years thinking about resilience because geography gives it little choice. It depends heavily on maritime trade and imported energy. Its digital links run through undersea cables that can fail for ordinary reasons and can also become objects of suspicion when vessels behave strangely around them. The practical response is redundancy: more cables, backup routes, satellite links, microwave capacity, inventories, repair capability, dispersed infrastructure, and institutions that can continue operating when one layer fails.

Resilience is deterrence when the coercer’s strategy depends on fragility.

But resilience cannot answer the political question by itself.

A society can absorb an outage and still face a sovereignty test. A port can remain open while carriers stay away. A cable can be repaired while uncertainty about sabotage grows. A military can identify every ship around the island and still not know which action should trigger force.

The day before the blockade is therefore a strange period.

It can last for years.

Exercises normalize presence. Patrols establish patterns. legal claims are repeated until foreign companies build them into compliance procedures. Infrastructure is probed. Political statements are tested for reaction. Each episode can be explained as temporary.

Then one temporary measure does not end.

The book begins there because deterrence is easiest to describe at the extremes. If one side launches a clearly identifiable attack, the other side can threaten a clearly identifiable response. The gray zone exists because both sides have reasons to avoid the extreme while still changing facts on the ground and water.

The central danger is not that leaders fail to see pressure.

It is that they see it, disagree about what category it belongs to, and lose time deciding what the category permits them to do.

A threshold can deter when everyone knows where it is.

It can also become terrain to be fought over.
