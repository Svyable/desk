# What Do We Call This?

On June 25, 2026, Taiwan’s Presidential Office ran a tabletop exercise built around a problem that sounds administrative until it becomes strategic.

Chinese law-enforcement authorities begin boarding and inspecting non-Chinese vessels. Commercial shipping is disrupted. Supplies still exist. Ports still exist. No amphibious force has landed on Taiwan. No declaration says a blockade has begun.

What is happening?

The exercise was part of the eighth meeting of Taiwan’s Whole-of-Society Defense Resilience Committee. The official description emphasized gray-zone aggression and high-intensity maritime coercion. The scenarios included supply delivery, the boarding and inspection of foreign vessels, and disruptions to logistics. President Lai Ching-te later ordered stronger coordination around shipping, maritime response, and reconnaissance.

The language matters because response begins with classification.

If the act is called a customs inspection, one set of authorities becomes relevant. If it is called harassment, another. If it is a quarantine, governments must decide what legal and operational meaning that word carries. If it is a blockade, the term reaches toward the law of armed conflict and toward military responses that can themselves turn a coercive episode into open war.

The ships do not change when the noun changes.

The political world around them does.

This is the threshold problem.

Taiwan is often discussed through the most dramatic scenario: a large-scale Chinese invasion across the Taiwan Strait. That scenario deserves attention because its consequences would be enormous and because preparing for it can deter it. But the invasion frame can also make everything short of invasion look secondary.

In practice, the space below war is crowded.

Military aircraft and ships can operate around the island. Exercises can close areas or simulate encirclement. Coast guard vessels can assert jurisdiction. Cyber operations can interrupt services. Cables can be damaged in ways that are difficult to attribute quickly. Financial pressure can be imposed selectively. Disinformation can make a real incident harder to interpret. Commercial shipping and aviation can be made to carry political risk long before a missile is fired.

Each action asks a different question of the target.

Must Taiwan respond now, or can it absorb the pressure? Must the United States act, or would acting elevate a limited coercive move into a military confrontation? Does Japan treat the event as a direct security threat? Do shipping companies reroute before governments decide what the situation is? Do insurers change prices? Do semiconductor customers build inventories? Does a temporary disruption become a new normal simply because reversing it would require more escalation than creating it did?

That last asymmetry is central.

A gray-zone strategy succeeds when the cost of undoing a change is higher than the cost of making it.

A ship inspection can be temporary. A pattern of inspections can become an expectation. An exercise can end. Repeated exercises can normalize a military presence that once would have been extraordinary. A cable incident can be repaired. Repeated incidents can force a society to rebuild communications architecture around the assumption of persistent pressure.

None of this means that every pressure campaign works. Coercion can strengthen Taiwanese identity, increase defense spending, deepen external partnerships, and encourage resilience. Aggression can reveal intentions and produce balancing coalitions.

The threshold is therefore not a one-way staircase designed by Beijing.

It is a contested political boundary.

Taipei can harden society without declaring an emergency. Washington can change military posture without issuing an ultimatum. Tokyo can revise plans, laws, logistics, and public expectations. Manila can deepen access arrangements. Companies can diversify routes and inventories. Allies can coordinate sanctions in advance. All of these steps change the meaning of the next coercive act.

Artificial intelligence enters this system in an awkward place.

AI can improve sensing, fuse data, help classify vessel behavior, analyze satellite imagery, translate public and private messaging, model logistics, identify cyber anomalies, and generate response options. It can also accelerate deception, automate influence campaigns, magnify false confidence, and encourage decision-makers to treat classification as a technical output rather than a political judgment.

A model can tell a government that a pattern resembles a blockade.

The government still has to decide what calling it a blockade would do.

The purpose of this book is to stay inside that decision.

Not: When does the war start?

But: What changes before war, who is expected to absorb it, and when does refusing to respond become more dangerous than responding?

Taiwan’s June tabletop exercise did not answer those questions. That was not its purpose. It made them visible.

A crisis around Taiwan may arrive with explosions. It may also arrive with paperwork, maritime notices, inspections, insurance calls, corrupted networks, rerouted ships, and statements insisting that nothing unprecedented has occurred.

Before leaders decide what to do, they will have to decide what to call it.
