# The Reactor That Had to Fly

*Project Pluto and the Difference Between Feasibility and a Weapon*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a particular kind of technological story Americans like because it is clean. Somebody proposes an impossible machine. Engineers work in the desert. The machine works. History changes.

Project Pluto ruins the shape of that story.

At Jackass Flats, Nevada, engineers built a nuclear reactor meant to fit inside a supersonic ramjet missile. The reactor would not sit behind yards of shielding and turn steam turbines. Air from the sky itself would pass through a very hot reactor core and leave as thrust. The engine had to be small enough to fly, powerful enough to push a large missile at supersonic speed near the ground, resistant to vibration and thermal shock, and willing to live in a radiation environment hostile to almost everything placed around it.

They made it work.

That is the fact worth holding onto, because it makes what followed more interesting. Tory II-A ran in 1961. Tory II-C, a much more mature engine prototype, reached full-design-power testing in May 1964. The laboratory later described the latter as a 500-megawatt-class machine. The Nevada National Security Site still treats Pluto as one of the technically remarkable programs conducted there. Declassified design reports are hundreds of pages long. They are full of the physical detail that turns a fever dream into hardware: beryllium oxide, uranium oxide, boron, tie rods, support structures, pressure drops, thermal stresses, control systems, air flow.

And then the program stopped.

It is tempting to make the cancellation morally theatrical. The internet version says the missile was so monstrous that even the Pentagon recoiled. A different version says the nuclear ramjet was a brilliant system killed by timid bureaucrats just as it proved itself. A third says intercontinental ballistic missiles simply made Pluto obsolete, end of story.

All three versions remove the part I care about.

A machine can work while the system around it fails.

The reactor was never the entire weapon. A weapon also had to be launched, tested, commanded, flown, based, maintained, kept from killing the wrong people before war, integrated into strategy, defended in budgets, and made preferable to other ways of doing the same terrible job. Every one of those requirements had physics hiding inside it. They were not public-relations footnotes attached to the real engineering. They were engineering.

That is the deeper subject of this book.

Project Pluto came out of a moment when nuclear energy appeared capable of dissolving old constraints. If chemical fuel limited range, remove the need for chemical fuel. If a bomber could be intercepted, fly a missile faster and lower. If one warhead was not enough, make the vehicle keep going. The logic had a frightening elegance. It also pushed one part of a weapon—the power plant—so far beyond normal assumptions that the boundaries between propulsion, contamination, testing, safety, and strategy began to collapse.

I am not going to pretend the documentary record answers every question cleanly. Some of the best-known Pluto claims come from later journalistic retellings and participant recollections rather than design documents. Claims about radioactive exhaust are often repeated without specifying whether we are talking about neutron activation of air, fission-product release from fuel erosion, direct radiation near the operating reactor, an accident, or the terminal destruction of the missile. Claims about the vehicle's shock wave sometimes migrate from “significant low-altitude overpressure” to “a planned mass-killing weapon” without the evidentiary bridge. The cancellation decision itself had multiple constituencies and occurred in a strategic environment changing faster than the reactor program.

So the book uses four evidence classes throughout. Declassified and official records get the most weight. Credible reporting and attributable participant testimony are useful, but they remain reporting and testimony. Disputed allegations stay disputed. My own synthesis is labeled as synthesis.

The reward for this fussiness is that Pluto becomes stranger, not less strange.

The familiar story is about a doomsday machine. The better story is about a team that solved an extraordinary technical problem and, by solving it, exposed how many other problems had been hidden behind the word feasible.
