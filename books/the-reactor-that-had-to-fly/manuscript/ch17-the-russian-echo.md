# The Russian Echo

In March 2018, Vladimir Putin stood before Russia's Federal Assembly and announced a collection of strategic weapons intended to demonstrate that American missile defenses had not made Russian deterrence obsolete. One of them was a nuclear-powered cruise missile.

The resemblance to Pluto was immediate enough that headlines did the historical work for everyone. The Flying Crowbar was back.

That comparison is useful for about five minutes.

Both programs begin with the same provocation: what becomes possible if a cruise missile is no longer tightly constrained by chemical fuel? Long range. Indirect routes. Continued powered flight. More freedom to approach defensive systems from unexpected directions. Both also put a compact nuclear reactor inside an airborne weapon, which means they inherit questions about testing, accidents, radiation, materials and strategic necessity that do not disappear just because sixty years passed.

The similarity is real.

The identity is not.

Russia calls the modern system 9M730 Burevestnik. NATO uses SSC-X-9 Skyfall. Public technical information remains incomplete, and some of the most important details come from Russian official claims, Western intelligence assessments, satellite imagery and expert inference rather than an open engineering record like the declassified Tory reports now available for Pluto. The responsible comparison therefore begins with an asymmetry: we know much more about the inside of a canceled American reactor from 1964 than we do about a Russian weapon program active in the 2020s.

That is not a license to fill the gap with Pluto.

A modern compact reactor may use different fuel, coolant paths, structural materials, control mechanisms, startup logic and flight profile. Modern guidance is incomparably better. Electronics can be smaller and more radiation tolerant. Computational fluid dynamics, materials modeling and manufacturing have changed what can be designed before hardware is built. A system launched in the twenty-first century enters a satellite-navigation, digital-control and high-resolution surveillance environment that Pluto's engineers would barely recognize.

Yet some old problems are stubborn because they come from architecture rather than era.

A nuclear-powered cruise missile still has to reach the condition in which its reactor propulsion works. It still has to be tested. If its reactor operates during a failed test, the failed article contains an irradiated nuclear system. If the propulsion path exposes reactor material to the working fluid, release mechanisms matter. If the weapon's advantage is essentially unlimited or extraordinarily long range, planners still have to explain why that endurance adds something worth the development and safety burden over ballistic missiles, submarines, conventional cruise missiles and other penetration systems.

Technology can improve the answers without changing the questions.

The public history of Burevestnik has already made the test problem impossible to ignore. Western governments and independent analysts have associated a series of Russian test activities with the program, and a 2019 accident near Nyonoksa killed Russian personnel during work the United States publicly linked to a nuclear-powered cruise-missile effort. The precise hardware involved and sequence of that accident have never been fully established in a public technical record. That boundary matters. A politically charged attribution repeated often enough can harden into an engineering fact it has not earned.

What is safe to say is narrower: developing a compact nuclear propulsion system for a missile has again produced a test and recovery ecology significant enough to attract international radiation monitoring, satellite analysis and intelligence attention.

The railroad has become the surveillance image.

In October 2025, Russia announced what it described as a major Burevestnik test. General Valery Gerasimov told Putin that the weapon had flown about 14,000 kilometers for roughly fifteen hours on October 21 and had operated on nuclear power throughout the flight. Putin said crucial testing was complete and ordered work toward deployment infrastructure. Reuters reported the claims while noting that Western experts continued to question the system's strategic value and radiological implications.

Those are Russian state claims about a Russian strategic program. The distance, duration and continuous nuclear operation should not be treated as independently instrumented public facts merely because they are specific. States advertise weapons for deterrence as well as describe them.

But the announcement changes the comparison with Pluto in one important way.

The modern echo is no longer hypothetical at the level of claimed flight endurance. Russia is asserting that it has crossed the boundary the United States declined to cross in 1964: prolonged flight of a nuclear-powered cruise missile.

If the claim is substantially accurate, it does not prove Pluto was canceled too early.

It proves that a state in a different strategic environment, with different technology and different tolerance for development risk, has decided the category is worth pursuing.

That distinction is essential.

There is a seductive historical argument that works backward from Burevestnik: if Russia can build one now, the United States abandoned a winning idea in the 1960s. This is like saying the existence of modern electric cars proves every 1910 electric-car company should have survived. Later feasibility cannot retroactively supply earlier strategic value.

Pluto competed against the strategic forces of 1964. Burevestnik competes against the defenses and deterrent architectures of the 2020s.

Russia's public rationale emphasizes missile defense. Putin has repeatedly framed novel strategic systems as responses to American withdrawal from the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty and subsequent U.S. missile-defense development. A weapon with long endurance and an unpredictable route can be presented as a way to approach around fixed intercept geometry rather than through it.

The word presented is doing work.

Missile defense is not one wall that a missile either penetrates or does not. Warning satellites, over-the-horizon radar, airborne sensors, fighters, surface-to-air systems, command networks and point defenses form different layers. A low-flying long-range cruise missile may avoid some ballistic-defense assumptions while exposing itself to other detection and interception mechanisms. Its long flight time may increase route flexibility and also increase the time available to detect it.

Endurance is not stealth.

This is the same conceptual trap Pluto invites.

The phrase unlimited range sounds like the end of geometry. In reality it makes geometry negotiable. The vehicle can choose more routes. It does not become absent from space.

Burevestnik also revives the question of strategic marginal value. Russia already possesses a large ballistic-missile force, ballistic-missile submarines, air-launched cruise missiles and other nuclear delivery systems. Any new nuclear-powered cruise missile must earn its place inside that portfolio. It may earn it by complicating defenses, by providing a retaliatory option, by signaling technological prowess or by forcing adversaries to spend money on new surveillance. Those values are not identical.

A weapon can be useful as an operational system and as a message at the same time.

The publicity around the October 2025 test makes the signaling function unusually visible. Putin described the missile as unique. Gerasimov emphasized distance and duration. The announcement occurred amid intense confrontation with the West over Ukraine and alongside other demonstrations of Russian nuclear forces. Even if every technical claim is accurate, the choice to announce those claims is political behavior.

Project Pluto's secrecy worked differently. The United States developed Tory largely out of public view, then canceled it before deployment. Burevestnik has been introduced to the world as a strategic symbol before outsiders can inspect its reactor design.

The evidentiary direction is reversed.

With Pluto we can inspect the engine and argue about the weapon.

With Burevestnik we can inspect the claims about the weapon and infer the engine.

That asymmetry should make us humble.

It should also make us notice how much Pluto's declassified fossil record contributes to present analysis. Because we know what a real nuclear ramjet program demanded—ceramic development, enormous test infrastructure, remote handling, specialized manufacturing, a progression from core experiments toward flight—we have a better checklist for evaluating modern nuclear-propulsion claims. Not a template. A checklist.

Where are the test ranges?

What kinds of accidents would the architecture create?

What materials and reactor states are plausible?

Which observed signatures distinguish nuclear propulsion from conventional launch or recovery equipment?

What does a successful long-duration flight actually demonstrate about deployment readiness?

How much of the program's public narrative is deterrence signaling rather than technical disclosure?

The most useful lesson from Pluto is not that Burevestnik must fail.

It is that a reactor can work and leave the strategic question untouched.

Russia may eventually deploy the system. If it does, the engineering achievement will be real. The system will still have to justify itself through readiness, survivability, command, maintenance, accident doctrine and the opportunity cost of keeping an exotic nuclear weapon in service.

Deployment is not the point where systems questions end.

It is where they stop being hypothetical.
