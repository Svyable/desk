## Fresh empirical core

The book’s empirical spine is:

- direct-air nuclear ramjet reactor physics;
- high-temperature beryllia/uranium ceramic fuel manufacturing;
- reactor power density and air-flow constraints;
- Jackass Flats static-test infrastructure and remote handling;
- the epistemic difference between component tests and integrated flight tests;
- range safety for a moving irradiated reactor;
- the historical transition from reactor R&D to strategic-system procurement;
- competition with Atlas/Titan/Minuteman/Polaris;
- cancellation causation under changing strategic need;
- program residue/declassification as evidence of physical scale;
- a controlled modern comparison with Russia’s Burevestnik program.

## Distinct thesis

The book is not principally “a history of a bizarre weapon.” Its distinct claim is that **Pluto is a rare clean case where the subsystem most people would have predicted to be impossible was demonstrated, yet the larger technology still failed to become a viable weapon system.** It uses that mismatch to interrogate the word *feasible* without converting technical success into deployment inevitability or bureaucratic cancellation into proof the engineering was misguided.
