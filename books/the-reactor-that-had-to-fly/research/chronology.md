# Chronology

Dates are kept conservative where public sources differ on exact run sequence or terminology. Test-level chronology needs primary report extraction in the publication pass.

- **Mid-1950s** — U.S. laboratories and military sponsors examine nuclear propulsion for aircraft, rockets and air-breathing systems amid uncertainty about future strategic delivery systems.
- **1957-01-01** — Official Nevada history dates the start of Project Pluto as a joint U.S. Air Force–Atomic Energy Commission effort centered at Lawrence Radiation Laboratory.
- **1957–1960** — Reactor physics, high-temperature materials, ceramic fuel-element manufacturing and Site 401/Jackass Flats test infrastructure develop in parallel.
- **1960–1961** — Tory II-A assembly and ground-test preparations mature; the Nevada complex supplies the compressed heated air needed to simulate ramjet conditions without flight.
- **1961-05-14** — Tory II-A conducts its landmark nuclear-ramjet test at Jackass Flats. The test establishes enough core feasibility to justify development of a more flight-relevant reactor.
- **1961–1963** — Fuel-element process development and industrialization continue; Coors Porcelain participates in production for Tory II-C. Tory II-C core/support/control design matures.
- **1962** — Polaris strategic submarine deployment and rapidly improving land-based ICBMs increasingly change the comparison set against which a new strategic cruise-missile system must justify itself.
- **1963** — Minuteman deployment and the Limited Test Ban Treaty belong to a strategic/political environment materially different from the one in which Pluto began. Neither fact alone is treated as a cancellation cause.
- **Spring 1964** — Tory II-C test series demonstrates a substantially more realistic nuclear-ramjet reactor. LLNL later summarizes six successful tests without failure; exact run-by-run conditions remain to be reconstructed from primary test/data reports.
- **1964-05** — Official reactor inventories record Tory II-C as successfully tested at full design power. Institutional histories commonly describe the machine as roughly 500-MW-class, while design/test numbers vary by source and condition.
- **1964-07-01** — Project Pluto is canceled. Contemporaneous Air Force reporting makes clear that strategic-system commitment and funding were already contested rather than arising suddenly from the last test.
- **1964-11-01** — UCRL-7679, *Engineering Design of the Tory II-C Nuclear Ramjet Reactor*, documents the mature reactor after program termination and calls it the culmination of Livermore’s 1957–1964 effort.
- **1965** — Fuel-fabrication reporting documents the industrial process developed for Tory ceramic fuel elements.
- **1970s** — Major Pluto technical records begin entering declassified/public archival channels, allowing later reconstruction of the actual reactor rather than reliance on recollection alone.
- **1990** — Gregg Herken’s “The Flying Crowbar” helps bring Pluto to a broad public audience and becomes an important ancestor for many later retellings. Its participant recollections remain secondary evidence.
- **2018-03** — Russian President Vladimir Putin publicly unveils 9M730 Burevestnik, a nuclear-powered, nuclear-capable cruise-missile program, framing it as a response to U.S. missile defense and broader strategic competition.
- **2019-08** — A fatal accident near Nyonoksa becomes publicly associated by U.S. officials and analysts with a Russian nuclear-powered cruise-missile effort. The precise hardware/event chain remains incompletely documented in public technical sources.
- **2025-10-21** — According to Russian General Staff claims later reported by Reuters, Burevestnik flies roughly 14,000 km for about 15 hours and operates on nuclear power throughout. These are attributable state claims, not independently released telemetry.
- **2025-10-26** — Putin publicly announces the claimed Burevestnik milestone and directs work toward classification/deployment infrastructure. Reuters reports both the Russian claims and continuing expert questions about strategic value and radiological implications.
- **2026-09-14** — This manuscript freezes its present evidence posture: Pluto’s reactor success is well documented; system-level SLAM history and radiological pathway quantification require deeper primary reconstruction; Burevestnik remains a modern comparison rather than proof that the programs are technically identical.
