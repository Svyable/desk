# The Ring That Should Not Be There

The tree did not look struck.

That is worth keeping in mind because almost every popular image of an extreme event begins with damage. A telegraph key sparking during the Carrington storm. A transformer failing. An aurora over a latitude where auroras do not belong. Some visible disturbance that announces the sky has misbehaved. The trees that gave us the first Miyake event offered no such courtesy. Their rings were ordinary to the eye. The anomaly was chemical, a change in the ratio of radiocarbon that had been fixed from the atmosphere while the tree grew.

Fusa Miyake and colleagues reported the result in Nature in 2012 after measuring annual rings from Japanese cedars. Between the rings assigned to 774 and 775 CE, atmospheric carbon-14 jumped sharply. The paper, “A signature of cosmic-ray increase in AD 774–775 from tree rings in Japan,” is still the proper place to begin because its surprise has been blurred by what followed. Today we can say “Miyake event” as though nature had supplied a named category. In 2012 there was first a measurement that did not fit the familiar scale of year-to-year solar modulation. The jump was about twenty times the ordinary annual variation the researchers expected. Something had happened to atmospheric isotope production quickly enough to appear inside annual wood.

The word “event” came later. At first the question was uglier and better: what could make this number?

Radiocarbon is not sprinkled onto trees from space. Energetic particles interact with Earth's atmosphere, producing secondary particles including neutrons. Those reactions can convert atmospheric nitrogen into carbon-14. Carbon dioxide carrying that isotope joins the carbon cycle. Plants take up atmospheric carbon during photosynthesis, and a portion becomes cellulose in annual rings. The tree therefore does not record the incoming particle directly. It records the carbon cycle's handling of a changed atmospheric inventory. That indirection matters because the shape of the signal depends on production, atmospheric transport, seasonal growth, species physiology, and the structure of the carbon cycle.

It also makes the original observation more interesting. A cedar is not a particle detector designed by a physicist. It is a biological system that happens to integrate atmospheric carbon while laying down a chronological structure. Dendrochronology supplies the year. Accelerator mass spectrometry supplies the isotope ratio. Atmospheric physics supplies the pathway. Solar physics supplies a candidate cause. None of those disciplines alone owns the result.

The first temptation was to explain the spike with something correspondingly spectacular. A nearby supernova? A short gamma-ray burst? A giant solar event? The 2012 paper treated the cause as unresolved. That caution has aged well. Later work added independent tree-ring records from other continents, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 evidence from ice, particle-production modeling, and comparisons with known solar spectra. The case for a solar energetic particle origin became much stronger. But the order matters. Measurement first. Mechanism second.

That order is the quiet hero of this book.

There is a bad habit in anomalous research, and it is not limited to UFOs, psi, cold fusion, or anything else routinely filed under controversial. An observation arrives, then the proposed explanation becomes so vivid that people begin to remember the explanation as the observation. Soon the claim “a radiocarbon spike exists” becomes “the largest solar flare in history happened in 775,” which is more specific, more cinematic, and less directly measured. The better way is almost bureaucratic. Keep the layers separate.

Class one: the annual radiocarbon excursion exists and has been reproduced in wood from widely separated regions. Class one also includes corresponding cosmogenic isotope evidence in polar ice for several of the major events. Class two: the combined isotope ratios and production modeling strongly favor an extreme solar particle event for 774/775 and 993/994. Class three: particular medieval texts describe the same storm, or a single flare caused the entire excursion on a known day, or the event had a precisely specified modern technological effect. Class four: we can use this family of events as a designed planetary timestamp system.

Once you write the bins down, something unexpected happens. The book becomes less paranormal and more strange.

A tree in Japan and a tree in California do not need to know about each other to carry the same atmospheric event. Ice in Greenland does not need to agree with either tree. The agreement is valuable precisely because the archives are physically different. Carbon-14 moves through the carbon cycle before entering wood. Beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 follow different production and deposition pathways before entering ice. Their clocks have different errors. Their environmental noise is different. When multiple archives carry a compatible excursion, the case that something happened in the atmosphere becomes harder to dismiss as laboratory error, local contamination, tree physiology, or a mistaken ring count.

In 2014, A. J. Timothy Jull and colleagues reported the 774–775 excursion in tree rings from Russia and the United States, adding to earlier records from Japan, Germany and New Zealand. The Geophysical Research Letters paper emphasized the global character of the signal. You can read it here: https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2014GL059874. The important word is not global because global sounds dramatic. It is independent.

Independent evidence streams are difficult to arrange on purpose. Science spends real money trying to obtain them. The Miyake events came with some for free.

This is where the story turns from astrophysics to chronology. If a radiocarbon spike is global and can be assigned to a calendar year in a securely dated tree-ring sequence, then the spike is not merely evidence about the Sun. It is a marker. Find it in an undated timber, and you have found a known year inside that timber. If the wood preserves later rings, count outward. If it preserves the bark edge, the felling year can become exact.

Radiocarbon dating is usually introduced to the public as a machine that tells you how old something is. That is not quite right. Conventional radiocarbon measurement gives a radiocarbon age that must be calibrated because atmospheric carbon-14 has varied over time. Calibration curves turn isotope measurements into probability distributions over calendar time. The result can be broad, multimodal, or stubbornly insensitive in parts of the curve. Archaeologists then combine it with stratigraphy, typology, dendrochronology, documentary history and Bayesian models.

A Miyake event changes the geometry of the problem. Instead of asking which part of a calibration curve best fits a sample, researchers can look across a sequence of individual annual rings for a distinctive rapid excursion already known from absolutely dated wood. The shape and year become a tie-point. Once the tie-point is found, the rest of the timber can be counted relative to it.

The distinction is subtle until it solves a historical argument.

L'Anse aux Meadows had long been securely identified as a Norse site in Newfoundland and broadly dated around the turn of the first millennium. The discovery did not depend on Miyake events. What the 993/994 radiocarbon excursion provided was a way to pin three pieces of worked wood to a felling year: 1021 CE. The researchers found the cosmic-ray marker in the wood, then counted twenty-eight annual rings to the bark edge. Nature published the result in 2022: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03972-8.

A thousand years earlier, nobody at L'Anse aux Meadows knew the Sun had left archaeologists a future timestamp in the trees. Nobody needed to. The signal entered the wood because photosynthesis was happening.

This accidental infrastructure is the real subject. We tend to imagine scientific instruments as deliberate objects: telescopes, satellites, magnetometers, dosimeters, spectrometers. But nature is full of systems that record without intending to record. Ice preserves trapped gases and deposited aerosols. Lake sediments preserve pollen and ash. Coral skeletons preserve geochemical conditions. Trees preserve growth, fire, drought, volcanic cooling, and, under the right measurement, cosmogenic isotope production.

The archive becomes an instrument only after someone learns how to read it.

That sentence can become sentimental if we leave it alone, so it needs a harder edge. Natural archives are not perfect witnesses. Trees can have missing rings. Wood can be reused. Bark can be lost. Carbon incorporated into a ring can reflect stored carbohydrates from an earlier season. Ice-core deposition can be delayed and mixed. Laboratory preparation can contaminate samples. Dendrochronological cross-dating can fail when sequences are short or local chronologies are weak. A spike can be claimed too eagerly because researchers are looking for it. The method earns precision only by carrying those failure modes with it.

This is especially important now that “Miyake dating” has acquired glamour. A distinctive spike is powerful because it is rare. Rarity is also what makes false matches dangerous. If you are allowed to slide a short sequence across a long calibration record until something looks spike-like, you can manufacture confidence. The disciplined version of the method therefore uses multiple adjacent rings, blind or predeclared matching where practical, replicated measurements, independent dendrochronology, and careful attention to species and seasonal carbon allocation.

The method is strongest when it does not act alone.

At Por-Bajin, a monumental eighth-century enclosure on an island in Tuva, researchers combined tree-ring structure and annual radiocarbon measurements to locate the 775 event and date construction to the summer of 777. The result mattered because the monument's short occupation and uncertain historical attribution had made chronology central to competing interpretations. The PNAS paper is here: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32513700/. Again, the cosmic event did not explain Por-Bajin. It disciplined one variable: when.

At Dispilio, a waterlogged Neolithic settlement in northern Greece, researchers used the 5259 BCE event to anchor a 303-year tree-ring chronology, placing its final ring at 5140 BCE and assigning construction phases to precise calendar years. Nature Communications published the work in 2024: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48402-1. A solar storm thousands of years before the Classical world now serves as a datum for European Neolithic archaeology.

The oddness here is not that the Sun influences Earth. That is ordinary astrophysics. The oddness is that an extreme event can be simultaneously a hazard, a geochemical perturbation, a solar-physics data point, an archaeological timestamp, and a synchronization tool for ice-core chronologies. One pulse crosses disciplinary boundaries because the atmosphere and biosphere did not care about our departments.

The same fact looks different from each side. A space-weather scientist sees the tail of the solar energetic particle distribution. A radiocarbon specialist sees a production excursion and carbon-cycle response. A dendrochronologist sees an annual marker embedded in cross-dated wood. An archaeologist sees a way out of a floating chronology. A power-grid engineer sees a reminder that the instrumental record is short. A historian sees a temptation to search chronicles for red skies and celestial portents.

The last observer needs restraint. Historical texts are not dosimeters. Medieval records can be copied, misdated, metaphorical, geographically ambiguous, or detached from the event modern readers want them to describe. A report of a red sky near 993 may be aurora; it may also be something else. The chronology can be useful without forcing every manuscript into the role of eyewitness.

The tree ring is more stubborn. It does not tell us what the sky looked like. It tells us that the atmospheric radiocarbon inventory changed.

That narrowness is a strength.

The first ring, then, should not be treated as a prophecy of technological collapse or a relic of a once-in-ten-thousand-years superflare. It is a measurement problem that became a historical tool. Miyake's original anomaly was valuable because it did not fit. The later science became valuable because researchers did not sand the anomaly down into a familiar story. They checked other trees. They checked other isotopes. They modeled production. They argued about source spectra. They searched older wood. They discovered more events. Archaeologists noticed that the same spikes could solve an entirely different class of problem.

A ring that should not have been there became a ruler.
