# How Often Does the Sun Do This?

The hardest number in this field is not an isotope ratio. It is a denominator.

How often does the Sun produce an event large enough to leave a Miyake-scale cosmogenic signature?

The question sounds like counting. Find the known events, divide by the number of years surveyed, and you have a recurrence interval. That would be reasonable if the survey were uniform. It is not.

The high-resolution tree-ring record is a patchwork. Some periods have been measured annually because researchers already knew they were interesting. Some were selected because lower-resolution radiocarbon data showed a suspicious bump. Some were targeted because archaeology needed an absolute anchor. Other centuries remain represented only by five-year or ten-year blocks, exactly the resolution that can smear a short event into the background.

The numerator is incomplete and the denominator is biased.

This is not a minor technicality. It is the difference between “we have found several events in a few thousand years of detailed observation” and “the Sun does this every few hundred years.” Those statements are not equivalent.

Brehm and colleagues were unusually explicit about this in their 2022 paper on the 7176 and 5259 BCE events. At the time, only about 2,030 years of the roughly 12,400-year absolutely dated tree-ring record had been measured at annual or biennial resolution suitable for finding large rapid excursions. Five strong events had appeared in that high-resolution coverage. A naive rate would be about one per four hundred years. But some of the measured intervals had been chosen because something already looked odd. If all strong Holocene events had somehow already been found—which is unlikely—the rate would be closer to one per 2,400 years.

Their bracket of roughly 400–2400 years was therefore not a prediction schedule. It was a warning label around sparse sampling.

That distinction has been lost in many summaries.

A recurrence interval is seductive because it turns uncertainty into a calendar. If someone says “once every thousand years,” the mind immediately asks whether we are due. That question is usually meaningless. Rare solar events are not clockwork. The underlying rate may vary with long-term solar behavior, and even a stationary Poisson process has no memory of how long it has been since the last event.

We are not “overdue” for a Miyake event in the way a library book is overdue.

The better question is probabilistic: what distribution of event sizes and waiting times is consistent with the observed archive, given the sampling process and detection threshold?

That is a harder problem and a more honest one.

The first ingredient is detection completeness. How large must an annual carbon-14 increase be to stand out in a given laboratory dataset? Does the event need to be one year long? How does carbon-cycle smoothing affect detectability? Could a hard-spectrum event produce a strong beryllium signal but a less obvious carbon spike? What happens when rings are measured in five-year blocks?

The second ingredient is search selection. Were annual measurements chosen randomly, systematically, or because a candidate had already been noticed? A targeted search is excellent for discovery and poor for prevalence estimation.

The third ingredient is event definition. The canonical 774 and 993 events are clear. The 660 BCE, 5259 BCE and 7176 BCE episodes have strong support. Additional candidate excursions at other dates vary in confirmation. If a catalog mixes confirmed and candidate events without weighting confidence, the recurrence curve becomes a reflection of editorial choice.

The fourth ingredient is physics. A carbon-14 production event of a given magnitude does not translate uniquely into a solar proton fluence. Spectrum matters. Geomagnetic field strength matters. Atmospheric modeling matters. Comparing ancient events therefore requires normalization.

The fifth ingredient is secular solar behavior. We do not know whether the probability of extreme particle events is constant across millennia. The Sun's dynamo varies. Grand minima and maxima occur. Active-region distributions change. The most extreme events may cluster in phases of otherwise high activity, or not.

A hazard curve built from five or six events should carry humility in its margins.

The instrumental record offers another constraint. Modern solar energetic particle events are numerous enough to characterize the common end of the distribution. The February 1956 event provides a hard-spectrum benchmark. August 1972 provides a large-fluence case with important astronaut implications. The October 1989 and 2003 episodes add more modern examples. None reaches the inferred particle fluence of 774.

This tells us the distribution extends beyond what instruments have seen. It does not tell us its exact tail shape.

Stellar observations add a third constraint. Kepler and other telescopes have observed superflares on Sun-like stars, some orders of magnitude more energetic in electromagnetic radiation than ordinary solar flares. These observations demonstrate that magnetic stars of roughly solar type can produce much larger events. But translating stellar flare energy into Earth-directed solar energetic proton fluence is uncertain.

A superflare catalog is not a Miyake catalog at a greater distance.

The relation between flare photons, coronal mass ejections and accelerated particles has scatter even on the Sun. Some flares are rich in particles; others are not. Magnetic geometry determines whether particles intersect Earth. A stellar light curve cannot tell us whether an imagined planet in that system receives a 774-like proton spectrum.

The archives therefore provide a rare local tail constraint that stellar statistics cannot replace.

There is a temptation to solve sparse data with theory. Solar magnetic free energy can place upper bounds on flare sizes. Active-region area and field strength constrain available energy. Particle acceleration models constrain spectra. These are valuable. But physical upper bounds are not recurrence distributions. A bridge can withstand a thousand tons without thousand-ton trucks arriving every year.

The field needs more observations.

Fortunately, the observations already exist in material form. The unsampled years are sitting in wood.

This creates an unusually tractable path forward. A systematic radiocarbon survey could select blocks of absolutely dated tree rings without looking at lower-resolution isotope anomalies first. Measure every annual ring. Publish the entire sequence, including nothing-happened intervals. Repeat across independent chronologies and hemispheres where possible. This would build a denominator that means something.

The cost is substantial but not astronomical. It is laboratory time, cellulose preparation, accelerator mass spectrometry and precious wood. Compared with launching a space observatory, it is almost comically terrestrial.

The survey design should include negative controls by construction.

If the literature consists mostly of papers announcing new spikes, the field will inherit publication bias. Clean centuries are scientifically valuable because they constrain event frequency. A predeclared survey makes those null intervals publishable data rather than failed discoveries.

This is another lesson frontier fields could borrow. The search program should be designed so that finding nothing still changes the model.

Miyake-event science is fortunate because the archive is long enough for this to be possible. A systematic one-thousand-year annual survey would dramatically improve recurrence estimates. Several independent blocks distributed through the Holocene would be better. Sampling across geomagnetic field states would help separate event statistics from detector sensitivity.

The archaeological value could be layered on top. Every new confirmed event becomes an anchor. Survey blocks could be selected partly to overlap major floating tree-ring chronologies, as long as the recurrence analysis keeps targeted and random components separate.

Two datasets can share a laboratory and keep different inferential rules.

The recurrence problem also changes once the late-glacial event around 12,350 BCE is included. A very large event outside the Holocene expands the observed maximum. But it should not simply be added to a Holocene rate count because archive quality, chronology and carbon-cycle conditions differ. The farther back we go, the less uniform the detector.

Extreme-value statistics in changing archives can become a machine for manufacturing confidence if the caveats are hidden.

A better presentation would show a family of curves under different assumptions: confirmed-only versus confirmed-plus-candidate events; annual-survey denominator versus full chronology denominator; alternative spectral reconstructions; alternative geomagnetic models. If the engineering conclusion changes wildly across reasonable assumptions, then the uncertainty is operationally relevant.

For many systems, it may not.

Satellite operators and deep-space mission designers already know that rare solar particle environments exceed routine conditions. The exact recurrence of a 774-class event matters for insurance, design margins and mission risk, but one does not need a precise thousand-year probability to justify radiation shelters for crews beyond Earth's protective magnetosphere.

Grid planning is more complicated because carbon-14 events do not directly measure geomagnetic storm severity. Here recurrence of particle events is only part of the hazard.

The public discussion often collapses these distinctions into a single sentence: “A Miyake event happens every thousand years and would destroy the grid.” Both halves are too confident.

The archive says something more interesting.

Earth has been hit repeatedly by solar energetic particle events far beyond the modern measured record. Their recurrence is rare on human timescales but not geological. The current catalog is too sparse and selectively sampled for a precise rate. Their modern effects would depend on multiple coupled solar and technological variables.

That is enough to take them seriously.

The most dangerous probability is not the one we cannot calculate to three decimal places. It is the one we round to zero because our instruments have not been running long enough.
