# The Older Storms

Once scientists learned what a Miyake event looked like, the past became searchable in a new way.

That sounds easier than it is.

The absolutely dated tree-ring record extends more than twelve thousand years, but only a fraction has been measured at annual or biennial radiocarbon resolution. Most calibration data combine multiple rings. A one-year production spike can be blurred into a modest bump. Ice-core beryllium records cover long intervals but often at lower temporal resolution and with deposition noise.

Finding old events is therefore a problem of where to spend measurements.

The 774 and 993 events were found in historical-era wood. The next major confirmed event emerged around 660 BCE, first from cosmogenic isotope evidence and later with tighter annual tree-ring dating. Then researchers pushed much farther. In 2022 Nicolas Brehm and a large international team reported strong events at 7176 and 5259 BCE in Nature Communications: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-28804-9.

Both were visible as rapid rises in carbon-14 measured across independent, absolutely dated tree-ring chronologies. The 5259 event appeared in trees from the Alps, Ireland, Russia and the United States. The 7176 event appeared in European chronologies and a floating bristlecone-pine sequence whose placement could then be improved.

The raw radiocarbon increases were roughly two percent—larger than the famous 775 rise—but raw amplitude is not event strength.

Earth's geomagnetic field was different.

A weaker magnetic field allows more charged particles to reach the atmosphere, increasing cosmogenic isotope production for the same incoming solar particle spectrum. Brehm and colleagues therefore normalized inferred event production to modern geomagnetic conditions. After that correction, the ancient events were comparable to, but not necessarily stronger than, 775.

This is an excellent example of why ancient proxies cannot be ranked by eyeballing their plots.

The detector changed.

So did the carbon cycle baseline, solar modulation and archive quality. Comparing events across millennia requires models that translate measured isotope concentration into production and then production into particle fluence.

Each translation carries uncertainty.

The reward is a solar record orders of magnitude longer than any instrument archive.

The 7176 and 5259 discoveries also changed recurrence estimates. Before them, two famous events in the first millennium CE could be dismissed as a peculiar cluster. Events spread across the Holocene suggest a persistent tail of extreme solar particle behavior.

But the number of events divided by twelve thousand years is not an honest rate estimate.

High-resolution coverage is sparse. Some intervals were selected because lower-resolution isotope records already looked suspicious. The 5259 interval, in particular, was connected with difficulties in archaeological radiocarbon modeling. Targeted search inflates discovery efficiency.

Brehm et al. discussed this directly. If one naively divides five strong events by roughly two thousand years of annual high-resolution coverage then available, the rate looks like one every few hundred years. If all strong events in the full twelve-thousand-year span had somehow already been found, the rate would be closer to one every few thousand years. Reality lies between, and the interval will tighten as systematic annual coverage grows.

The uncertainty is not merely statistical. Event definition matters.

How large must an annual carbon-14 increase be to count? Must it be confirmed in beryllium-10? What if the signal lasts two or three years? What if a hard-spectrum event produces a strong chlorine-36 signature with only a moderate carbon response? What about candidate excursions such as 1052 or 1279 that need further independent confirmation?

A catalog is a theory with rows.

The older events are especially vulnerable to overclassification because the supporting archives become thinner. A tree-ring spike may be found in one chronology before replication. Ice-core dating may be uncertain by several years. Geomagnetic reconstruction broadens. The temptation is to place every sharp feature into one named family because the alternative is an untidy table.

Untidy tables are often healthier.

For this book's purposes, each proposed event stays in one of four evidence classes. Confirmed annual carbon-14 excursions replicated across independent chronologies and supported by other cosmogenic nuclides belong in class one. Events with strong annual evidence but incomplete multinuclide confirmation sit near the boundary of classes one and two. Candidate peaks from lower-resolution records remain class two or three depending on support. Claims about source details or historical consequences are separate.

The goal is not to gatekeep the term. It is to prevent the count from outrunning the measurements.

The 660 BCE event shows how a candidate matures. High-resolution beryllium-10 from Greenland ice indicated an extreme solar proton event around 2610 years before present. Carbon-14 measurements supported the episode. In 2024 Panyushkina and colleagues used larch tree rings from Yamal and Altai to constrain the event to 664–663 BCE. The paper is at https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01618-x.

A broad cosmogenic signal became an annual anchor.

That maturation is exactly what future event catalogs should show. Rather than a binary list of “Miyake events,” each row could include archive types, independent replications, annual resolution, isotope species, chronology quality, production estimate, geomagnetic correction, source confidence and archaeological applications.

Some rows would be stronger than others.

That is useful.

The older event search also reveals a peculiar collaboration between archaeology and astrophysics. Archaeologists possess floating tree-ring chronologies that may contain excellent annual sequences but lack absolute dates. Solar physicists possess candidate cosmogenic events in lower-resolution isotope records. If a floating chronology's broad radiocarbon age overlaps a candidate event, high-resolution measurement can test both problems at once.

If the spike appears, the chronology gains an anchor and the event gains another tree.

This is what happened in part with the 7176 bristlecone sequence. The cosmic event helped place previously floating wood more securely against the absolute chronology.

The archive repairs itself with extraterrestrial punctuation.

There are practical limits. Accelerator mass spectrometry is destructive and expensive. Annual cellulose extraction across centuries consumes material and laboratory capacity. Ancient tree-ring collections are precious. Some sequences exist only as small archaeological specimens. Measurement strategies therefore need triage.

One promising approach uses lower-resolution records to identify suspicious intervals, then annual measurements to resolve them. Another uses statistical detection in IntCal calibration data. A third targets archaeological periods where an anchor would solve a major chronology. A fourth samples systematically regardless of anomalies to estimate event frequency without selection bias.

Those strategies answer different questions and should not be mixed casually.

The systematic survey is the most important for hazard science. Without it, recurrence estimates remain hostage to where researchers expected to find something interesting.

The targeted survey may be the most important for archaeology. A single new anchor year near a major floating chronology can generate more historical value than a randomly selected clean century.

Funding bodies tend to demand one objective. The material is refusing.

The older events also create a bridge to stellar astronomy. Kepler and TESS observations show superflares on Sun-like stars, including events far more energetic than familiar solar flares. Stellar statistics can estimate how often stars of similar type produce extreme radiative events. Cosmogenic archives constrain what our own Sun has done.

The two datasets should overlap but not be equated.

A stellar superflare observed in broadband light is not a direct measure of solar energetic proton fluence at an Earth-like planet. Magnetic geometry, CME occurrence and particle acceleration differ. The ancient isotope events therefore provide a rare local calibration for the high-energy particle tail.

They tell us our Sun is capable of more than our instruments have seen.

They do not tell us it behaves like every active solar analog.

The difference matters for exoplanet habitability too. Repeated extreme particle events can alter atmospheric chemistry and surface radiation, especially on planets with weak magnetic fields or thin atmospheres. Researchers model such effects, but the Miyake catalog provides only one star's sparse empirical tail.

The irony returns: the best record of the Sun's ancient particle violence is stored in biological tissue.

The oldest storms are not fossils of sunlight. They are fossils of nuclear reactions in air.

This language can make the field sound more certain than it is. A radiocarbon spike is not a fossil in the strict paleontological sense, and the particle spectrum is inferred. But the analogy captures a useful point. Natural archives preserve consequences after the source is gone.

The better the archive network becomes, the less we need to imagine the source from theory alone.

Every new event adds two things to the world: another data point for the solar tail and another potential calendar year for any wood spanning it.

That is an unusual dual use.

A stronger storm is a worse hazard and a better timestamp.
