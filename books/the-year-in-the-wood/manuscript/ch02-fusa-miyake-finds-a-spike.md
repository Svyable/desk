# Fusa Miyake Finds a Spike

The easiest way to misremember a discovery is to start with the field it created.

Once a phenomenon has a name, a literature, review papers, conference sessions and a Wikipedia page, the first observation can look inevitable. Someone was bound to find it. The instruments were improving. The calibration curve was sitting there. Solar physicists already knew energetic particles can create cosmogenic nuclides. Tree-ring laboratories already had annual wood. The ingredients seem to line themselves up after the fact.

They did not line themselves up.

Fusa Miyake was a doctoral student at Nagoya University when she and colleagues Kentaro Nagaya, Kimiaki Masuda and Toshio Nakamura published the 2012 Nature paper. Their samples came from two Japanese cedar trees. They measured annual rings around the eighth century and found the sharp increase between 774 and 775. The result was not simply a slightly larger wiggle in an established high-resolution record. Most radiocarbon calibration work had not been built to look for single-year excursions of this size. The resolution that makes a Miyake event obvious is expensive. Accelerator mass spectrometry time is not free, and dissecting annual rings is labor.

That practical point matters because our map of past extreme events is shaped by where scientists have looked at annual resolution. A low-resolution radiocarbon curve can smear a one-year production pulse across several years. An ice core sampled at five- or ten-year resolution can hint at an excursion without providing the sharp edge needed to classify it. A century of tree rings can contain a global signal nobody sees because the samples were combined into decadal blocks.

Discovery therefore depends on resolution before it depends on theory.

The 2012 Nature paper can still be read at https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11123. Its editorial summary described an increase in carbon-14 concentration in the annual rings of Japanese cedars far larger than ordinary solar-cycle variation. At publication, the cause remained uncertain. The researchers considered an energetic event in the space environment but found problems with simple versions of both the supernova and solar-flare explanations.

That uncertainty is one reason the episode deserves more attention than the usual “student discovers giant ancient solar storm” telling. It shows a young scientist doing the thing anomalous research claims to value but often fails to protect: reporting a result before the story is comfortable.

There was an obvious route to a weaker paper. Average more years. Treat the excursion as noise. Increase error bars. Explain it as local contamination and move on. Instead the team isolated the annual behavior.

The act sounds trivial until you have worked with any historical proxy. Proxies are attractive because they extend observation backward. They are dangerous because they transform what you care about into something else. We care about energetic particles. We measure carbon-14. Carbon-14 production is not particle fluence; it depends on particle energy, atmospheric nuclear reactions and geomagnetic shielding. The carbon then enters reservoirs and moves through the carbon cycle. The tree samples a portion of that carbon during growth. The laboratory measures an isotope ratio after chemical preparation. Every arrow can distort amplitude and timing.

The discovery was therefore not a photograph of a solar storm. It was a sharp feature in a proxy chain.

That distinction explains why later work mattered so much. Researchers found the same 774/775 excursion in German oak, Russian larch, California bristlecone pine, New Zealand kauri and other annual sequences. A local Japanese problem became improbable. The event also appeared in other cosmogenic radionuclides in polar ice. Those nuclides arise from related particle cascades but have different atmospheric chemistry and deposition. That made a global production event much harder to avoid.

Then the search began to run backward.

In 2013 Miyake, Masuda and Nakamura reported another rapid carbon-14 excursion around 993/994 in Nature Communications: https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms2783. The second event changed the ontology of the first. One spike can be a freak. Two establish at least the possibility of a class.

The naming followed. “Miyake event” became the convenient label for abrupt cosmogenic isotope production excursions of this kind. The name does not specify a single mechanism with perfect precision; in practice it often refers to rapid radiocarbon spikes that, when independently supported, are interpreted as extreme solar energetic particle events. That distinction has become more important as new candidate events have been reported. Not every abrupt carbon-14 feature deserves the same confidence. Cross-isotope confirmation and replication across independent tree-ring chronologies matter.

The terminology also hides a small injustice common to eponymous science. An event named for the person who recognized it can sound like a thing waiting fully formed for discovery. The actual contribution includes a choice about scale. Miyake's group took annual variation seriously enough to ask whether an abrupt change could be physical.

Science is full of effects that disappear when you average them.

Consider what annual resolution does to time. A conventional radiocarbon date might tell an archaeologist that a piece of wood belongs somewhere across a range of decades. Dendrochronology can sometimes do much better when a local master chronology exists and enough rings survive for pattern matching. But many regions and periods lack continuous absolute tree-ring chronologies. A Miyake event provides a globally generated marker that can be carried into those otherwise floating sequences.

That use was not the original headline. It emerged because researchers in another field recognized the shape of the data as a chronological resource.

Michael Dee and Benjamin Pope argued in 2016 that cosmic-ray events could provide “astro-chronological tie-points” for historical sequences. The phrase sounds grander than the operation. Find a known radiocarbon spike in an annually resolved sequence, then use ordinary ring counting around it. The cleverness lies in importing an absolute marker into wood whose local dendrochronological sequence is otherwise floating.

This is where the discovery acquires an almost archaeological personality. The signal is universal enough to travel and local enough to be counted.

The event year enters atmospheric carbon everywhere, but each tree stores it inside its own biography. A cedar in Japan and a pine in Arizona can share the same cosmogenic perturbation while differing in species, growing season, climate, ring width and stored-carbohydrate behavior. A building timber can then preserve that atmospheric year inside a human artifact. The farther you move from the physics, the more practical the timestamp becomes.

It is tempting to call this a natural barcode. I will resist because barcodes are designed. The lack of design is the point. A solar particle event did not intend to label Norse wood. The cedar did not intend to archive it. The archaeological timber did not intend to survive. The method works because several ordinary systems happened to preserve one extraordinary production pulse.

The discovery also offers a warning about databases.

Once the 774 and 993 events were known, researchers could scan existing lower-resolution records for suspicious intervals, then target annual measurements. This is efficient. It is also statistically treacherous. If you select periods because they already look unusual, then count the number of events found and divide by the amount of high-resolution coverage, you can badly overestimate frequency. Brehm and colleagues made this explicit in their 2022 Nature Communications paper reporting events at 7176 and 5259 BCE. Annual or biennial measurements then covered only a fraction of the full absolutely dated tree-ring record, and some intervals had been chosen precisely because lower-resolution data hinted at structure. Their suggested recurrence bracket of roughly one strong event per 400–2400 years was a constraint, not a metronome.

The paper is worth reading for the care around selection: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-28804-9.

This is the less cinematic half of discovery. Once you find something rare, the sampling process itself becomes part of the phenomenon. Where did you look? Why there? What counts as confirmation? Did the search start before or after the candidate interval was visible? Are negative high-resolution intervals preserved and published with the same enthusiasm as positive ones?

Miyake events sit at the intersection of two kinds of rarity. The solar events are rare. The measurements capable of detecting them are also sparse. Confusing those two sparsities is easy.

A similar problem appears in UAP studies, rare-disease surveillance, earthquake precursors, archaeological anomalies and almost every domain where attention follows surprises. The archive is not sampled uniformly. Researchers go where something already looks odd. The resulting event catalog can be real and still produce a biased estimate of how often the phenomenon occurs.

The correct response is not skepticism as posture. It is survey design.

One of the most promising futures for this field is therefore boring: predeclared annual sampling blocks across absolutely dated tree-ring chronologies, selected without reference to lower-resolution isotope anomalies. A systematic survey would produce both events and clean non-events. It would allow frequency estimates to depend less on opportunistic discovery. It would also identify new chronological anchor years for archaeology.

The interests line up unusually well. Space-weather researchers want a longer, less biased catalog of extreme solar particle events. Archaeologists want more globally available annual tie-points. Ice-core specialists want synchronization markers. Solar physicists want constraints on event spectra and recurrence. A single measurement campaign can produce value for all of them.

This is a recurring theme in frontier science: the most useful observatory may be built by people asking different questions of the same material.

The original Japanese cedars were not selected because archaeologists needed to date Vikings. The 993 event was not discovered because a Newfoundland timber was waiting for a timestamp. The later applications were possible because the measurement was published with enough specificity that another discipline could reuse it.

That makes reproducibility more than a policing mechanism. Replication can enlarge the meaning of a discovery.

When the 774 spike appeared in trees across continents, its value as a geophysical event increased. When the same family of signal appeared in ice-core radionuclides, its value for source attribution increased. When archaeologists found the 993 excursion inside worked wood at L'Anse aux Meadows, its value as a chronological instrument increased. When the 5259 BCE event anchored Dispilio, the method escaped the first millennium and entered deep prehistory.

The spike did not change. The network around it did.

That is why I am reluctant to describe Miyake's discovery as simply finding an ancient solar storm. She found a feature sharp enough to force several sciences to renegotiate their time resolution. The particle event is dramatic, but the methodological shock is broader. Annual tree rings turned a vague past into a sequence containing globally shared markers.

A good anomaly does not merely demand explanation. Sometimes it becomes equipment.
