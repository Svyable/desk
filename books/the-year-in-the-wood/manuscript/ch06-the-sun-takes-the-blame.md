# The Sun Takes the Blame

The first explanations for the 774 event reached for objects far away.

A nearby supernova would be dramatic enough. A short gamma-ray burst could dump high-energy photons into the atmosphere. A comet impact had the right flavor of suddenness. An extreme solar particle event had one apparent problem: nothing remotely comparable existed in the instrumental record.

That last objection weakened once researchers remembered what the instrumental record is.

It is short.

Modern direct observations of solar energetic particles cover decades. Ground-level enhancements measured by neutron monitors extend the useful high-energy record into the mid-twentieth century. The best-characterized extreme cases—especially February 1956—provide spectra and fluences for events we know the Sun can produce now. But a physical process with a recurrence time of centuries or millennia will almost certainly be undersampled by seventy years.

The 774 isotope excursion therefore did something conceptually important before its exact source was settled. It forced solar physics to accept a data point outside the instrument era.

The challenge was attribution.

A carbon-14 spike alone does not identify the source. High-energy gamma rays and charged particles can both generate atmospheric nuclear reactions. A nearby supernova might produce sustained cosmic-ray changes. A gamma-ray burst could create a sharp pulse. The absence of a bright historical supernova around 774 argued against some scenarios but was not decisive by itself. More useful were the relative cosmogenic isotope yields.

Different incident radiation spectra produce different proportions of carbon-14, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36. This turns the atmosphere into a particle-physics filter. If the measured ratios across tree rings and ice match solar proton production better than gamma-ray production, the source inference becomes quantitative.

Mekhaldi et al. 2015 supplied a major step. Their multiradionuclide analysis found that the 774/775 and 993/994 events were compatible with extreme solar proton events. Later modeling refined spectra, fluences and geomagnetic corrections. The question shifted from “was the Sun capable?” to “what sort of solar particle event can reproduce these archives?”

That shift is easy to miss because the public version of the story usually jumps straight from tree ring to superflare.

A superflare is not required.

Observations of Sun-like stars by Kepler revealed flares far larger in radiated energy than typical modern solar flares, raising the question of whether our Sun could produce similarly extreme events. But the relationship between flare electromagnetic energy, CME behavior and high-energy proton fluence is not one-to-one. A Miyake event could involve an unusual particle acceleration geometry without requiring the most luminous conceivable solar flare.

The term “superstorm” is therefore safer if treated as journalistic compression, not a physical category.

The source evidence is strongest for the particle component. We know the atmosphere experienced exceptional cosmogenic production. We can infer a high fluence of energetic protons. We do not know the visible appearance of the Sun, the exact flare class, or the magnetic structure of any accompanying CME.

This is an example of asymmetrical historical knowledge. We know something about invisible particles better than we know what people saw.

The irony is pleasing but should not be romanticized. We know the particles because nuclear physics leaves durable byproducts. Human visual records are culturally filtered. A monk can ignore an aurora. A nitrogen nucleus cannot.

The solar interpretation gained further support as additional events were found. The 993/994 excursion showed a similar multiradionuclide signature. The 660 BCE event appeared in ice and tree records. Events at 7176 and 5259 BCE extended the family deep into the Holocene. Each new case made it less necessary to invent a different exotic astrophysical transient for every spike.

The simplest family-level explanation became rare, extreme solar energetic particle events.

“Simplest” is not “finished.”

There are still arguments about the spectral hardness of individual events, the total proton fluence, the role of geomagnetic shielding, the event duration, and whether some radiocarbon excursions may have different causes. There are candidate spikes that lack independent beryllium or chlorine confirmation. There are proposed events based on lower-resolution data that may dissolve under annual measurement. There may be smaller events hidden below current thresholds.

The Sun takes the blame for the best-established events, but the indictment has counts.

This matters for recurrence risk. If all large radiocarbon spikes are casually treated as identical solar storms, the inferred frequency and severity distribution will be wrong. An event with a hard particle spectrum is disproportionately important for cosmogenic production and deep atmospheric penetration. A softer event can carry large total energy without leaving the same signature. Geomagnetic field strength changes the production yield for identical incoming spectra.

A catalog needs normalization before it becomes a hazard curve.

Brehm et al. explicitly normalized ancient event production to a modern geomagnetic field when comparing 7176, 5259, 775, 993 and 660 BCE. That is an unusual but essential detail. Earth's magnetic field is part of the detector, and the detector changed while the experiment was running.

This makes natural archives different from laboratory instruments in a way physicists find both annoying and useful. The detector cannot be recalibrated at the time of observation. You must reconstruct its state later.

Solar modulation complicates the baseline too. Galactic cosmic rays continuously produce cosmogenic isotopes, but the solar magnetic field modulates how many reach Earth. The familiar eleven-year solar cycle therefore appears in cosmogenic records at some level. Long-term changes in solar activity alter background production. A Miyake event is a spike on top of a moving baseline.

That moving baseline is another reason annual sampling matters. A rapid one-year increase is qualitatively different from a century-scale rise caused by weak solar activity or geomagnetic change.

The event shape is information.

In 2022 Qingyuan Zhang and colleagues modeled cosmic radiation events in tree-ring radiocarbon records and argued that some known excursions do not fit a single uniform phenomenology. Carbon-cycle response and event duration may differ. This kind of result is productive because it resists turning “Miyake event” into a natural kind before the data earns it.

A named category can become a trap.

The safest definition is observational: a rapid, unusually large cosmogenic radionuclide production excursion recognized at annual or near-annual resolution. The source then has to be established event by event. For the canonical 774 and 993 cases, solar energetic particles are strongly supported. For every newly proposed spike, the four-bin discipline should restart.

Class one: measured isotope excursion, replicated across samples.

Class two: source inference supported by multinuclide ratios and physical modeling.

Class three: historical reports or specific solar phenomenology proposed without direct physical linkage.

Class four: broader synthesis about solar extremes, technological risk and chronological use.

This may seem pedantic until someone asks whether the Sun can do it again.

The answer “yes” is much stronger than the answer “we know exactly what it would look like.” The archive demonstrates that Earth has experienced events of this kind multiple times. That alone expands the plausible solar-particle envelope beyond the modern instrumental record. But forecasting a modern event requires knowledge the archive only partly provides.

The Sun is a star with magnetic cycles, active regions and eruptive behavior. It has produced thousands of energetic particle events in the space age, mostly modest. It produced the 1956 ground-level enhancement, which remains a hard-spectrum benchmark. It produced the August 1972 event between Apollo missions, famous in radiation-risk discussions because astronauts on the lunar surface during such an event could have received a serious dose without adequate shelter. It produced the Halloween storms of 2003. These modern events give detailed physics.

The ancient events give tail amplitude.

A mature hazard model needs both.

There is a tendency in frontier science to choose between direct measurement and proxy evidence as though one invalidates the other. Direct observations are cleaner. Proxies are longer. The correct relationship is calibration. Use modern events to understand how particle spectra map into detector responses. Use natural archives to learn whether larger responses occurred. Use models to connect them. Keep the uncertainty visible.

This is exactly what makes the field intellectually satisfying. Nobody has to believe in an unobserved mechanism. The Sun's ordinary physics, extended into a sparsely sampled tail, is enough.

That should also make us cautious about the word “unprecedented.”

Every satellite-age solar event is unprecedented inside the satellite age until it happens. The tree-ring record says the Sun has already produced particle environments outside that sample. If a large event occurs next year, it may be unprecedented for our instruments and entirely precedented for Earth.

The trees do not predict the date. They correct the adjective.

The solar attribution finally gives the archaeological method a peculiar authority. A timestamp created by a particle storm is useful precisely because it is external to human chronology. Kings cannot revise it. Scribes cannot move it to flatter a dynasty. Archaeologists cannot create it by typological preference. If the annual ring is correctly identified and the global event year is secure, the marker enters a timber independently of the cultural system later built from that wood.

That does not make archaeological interpretation objective in some naive sense. Sampling, ring counting, old-wood effects and context still matter. But the tie-point comes from the sky.

The Sun takes the blame.

History gets the date.
