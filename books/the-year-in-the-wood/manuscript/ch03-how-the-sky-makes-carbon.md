# How the Sky Makes Carbon

Carbon-14 is a clock only because the sky keeps making it.

That sentence is obvious to anyone who has taught radiocarbon dating and still strange enough to deserve lingering over. The isotope is radioactive. With a half-life of about 5,730 years, any fixed reservoir of it would decay away. Yet living plants keep incorporating carbon-14 because cosmic radiation continually generates new atoms in the atmosphere. Radiocarbon dating works not because carbon-14 is rare, but because it is rare in a structured way.

The structure begins above the tree.

High-energy primary cosmic rays—mostly protons and atomic nuclei—hit Earth's atmosphere and initiate cascades of secondary particles. Neutrons produced in those cascades can interact with nitrogen-14 nuclei, converting them into carbon-14. The newly made radiocarbon oxidizes and joins atmospheric carbon dioxide. From there it enters the carbon cycle: atmosphere, vegetation, oceans, soils, organisms. A tree fixes some of that carbon during photosynthesis and turns it into wood.

The annual ring is therefore several transformations removed from the original particle.

This matters for every claim in this book. A spike in tree-ring carbon-14 is not a direct readout of solar proton count. To infer the source event, researchers need models of particle spectra, geomagnetic cutoff, atmospheric production yields and carbon-cycle mixing. The same additional radiocarbon production could in principle be generated by different particle spectra. Earth's magnetic field changes over millennia, altering how readily charged particles reach the atmosphere at different latitudes. The timing of carbon fixation differs among tree species and climates. Stored carbon from previous years can leak into new growth.

The proxy is powerful because the chain is understood well enough to model, not because the chain disappears.

There are three cosmogenic radionuclides that repeatedly appear in Miyake-event papers: carbon-14, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36. They do not behave identically. Carbon-14 becomes part of atmospheric carbon dioxide and is globally mixed through the carbon cycle. Beryllium-10 attaches to aerosols and is deposited onto Earth's surface, including polar ice. Chlorine-36 has its own production channels and atmospheric transport. Their responses to particle energy differ. Comparing them can therefore constrain the incoming spectrum better than any one isotope alone.

That is why the multiradionuclide evidence was decisive in moving the 774/775 and 993/994 events toward a solar energetic particle interpretation. Florian Mekhaldi and colleagues published a Nature Communications paper in 2015 comparing carbon-14, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 evidence: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms9611. The relative production was compatible with energetic solar particles and difficult to reconcile with some alternatives.

A gamma-ray burst, for instance, would produce a different relationship among cosmogenic nuclides. The isotope ratios do not merely tell us an energetic event happened. They act as a crude spectrometer stretched across natural archives.

“Crude” is not an insult. The detector covers millennia.

Modern particle instruments are precise but temporally shallow. The space age gives us direct observations across decades. Ground-based neutron monitors extend useful particle records into the mid-twentieth century. That is excellent for common events and inadequate for the far tail. If an event occurs once every several centuries or millennia, a seventy-year instrumental record is almost designed to miss it.

Natural archives exchange precision for duration. They are messy, but they were running before electronics.

This mismatch between instrumental precision and archival duration appears everywhere in hazard science. Seismometers record earthquakes exquisitely but only recently; paleoseismology reads older ruptures from sediments and displaced landforms. Tide gauges and satellites measure sea level precisely; corals and coastal deposits extend the story backward. Telescopes monitor stars now; meteorites preserve early solar-system chemistry. The tail of a distribution often lives in inferior instruments with superior patience.

Miyake events are the solar version of that bargain.

To understand the signal, it helps to separate solar energetic particles from geomagnetic storms. They are related aspects of solar eruptions but not interchangeable. A coronal mass ejection interacting with Earth's magnetosphere can drive geomagnetic currents that threaten power grids and produce auroras. Solar energetic particles, accelerated near flares or CME-driven shocks, can penetrate the atmosphere and alter radiation conditions. The carbon-14 spike is primarily evidence about energetic particle fluence, not a direct measurement of the geomagnetic field disturbance that would have occurred at the same time.

This is why using the Carrington event as a universal yardstick causes trouble. Carrington is famous because it was observed in 1859, produced spectacular auroras and disrupted telegraph systems. But it did not leave a Miyake-scale cosmogenic isotope spike. A solar event can be extreme in one dimension and less extreme in another. The Sun does not owe us a single scalar called “storm size.”

Particle spectrum matters.

A large number of relatively low-energy protons and a smaller number of high-energy protons can have different effects on isotope production, aviation dose, satellite electronics and ground-level neutron monitors. Researchers often express solar energetic particle events using fluence above energy thresholds, such as F30 for particles above 30 MeV. Reconstructing those quantities from cosmogenic nuclides requires assumptions about spectral shape.

The best papers are explicit about that uncertainty. Brehm and colleagues, when comparing events at 7176 and 5259 BCE with 775 CE, normalized inferred carbon-14 production for changes in geomagnetic field strength. They found that the raw isotope excursion is not identical to particle-event strength because Earth's magnetic shielding was different thousands of years ago. The ancient atmosphere and magnetosphere are part of the detector.

This is the kind of detail that saves an extraordinary result from extraordinary storytelling.

There is no need to call a 19-per-mille radiocarbon excursion “the biggest solar storm ever” when the more precise statement is available: a particular annual tree-ring sequence shows a rapid increase in atmospheric carbon-14; models infer additional global production; companion isotopes support a solar particle source; event strength relative to other episodes depends on geomagnetic normalization and assumed particle spectrum.

That sentence is less viral and more useful.

The carbon cycle adds another layer. Once carbon-14 is made, it does not stay where it was produced. Atmospheric exchange moves it between stratosphere and troposphere. Photosynthetic timing controls when plants fix it. Oceans absorb enormous amounts of carbon and smooth changes. A production pulse that may last weeks or months can appear as a one- or two-year rise in annual wood. Carbon-cycle models are used to invert the observed ring sequence back toward a production history.

This inversion is not unique. Different production profiles can sometimes generate similar annual observations. Subannual sampling can help.

Researchers have split annual rings into earlywood and latewood, exploiting the fact that these tissues form during different parts of the growing season. In Finnish Lapland, work on the 774 event suggested that the production pulse likely occurred during spring 774, though exact seasonal interpretation depends on carbon transport and tree physiology. Similar subannual studies in Arizona pines have tried to narrow timing. A one-year cosmic timestamp may eventually become a seasonal one in favorable species.

That prospect is appealing to archaeologists because felling season can matter. It is also dangerous because precision can outpace understanding. A tree can use stored carbohydrates. Earlywood in ring-porous species may incorporate carbon fixed in previous years. Evergreen and deciduous species differ. Growing seasons shift with latitude and climate. The same atmospheric pulse can enter tissues on different schedules.

More digits do not automatically mean more truth.

The discipline here is to ask which clock you are reading. There is the event clock: when energetic particles reached Earth. The production clock: when cosmogenic nuclides were made in the atmosphere. The mixing clock: when those nuclides reached the relevant reservoir. The biological clock: when the tree fixed the carbon. The ring clock: which annual band incorporated it. The archaeological clock: when the tree was felled or the wood used.

Miyake dating works because these clocks can be related. It fails when they are casually collapsed.

Ice introduces a different set of clocks. Beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 produced in the atmosphere attach to aerosols and are deposited with snow. Weather and atmospheric circulation create noise. Ice accumulation varies. Layer counting introduces uncertainty that grows with depth. A sharp event known absolutely from tree rings can therefore help synchronize ice cores, while the ice provides independent isotope evidence about the event source.

This reciprocity is beautiful in a way that scientific diagrams often hide. The tree helps date the ice. The ice helps explain the tree.

Neither archive is subordinate.

The deep-time events make this especially valuable. Brehm et al.'s 7176 BCE event was found in multiple absolutely dated tree-ring chronologies and also used to improve the placement of a floating bristlecone-pine sequence. The same event can be sought in ice-core beryllium and chlorine records to tighten cross-archive chronology. If a new event is discovered in wood, it becomes a candidate tie-point for polar records. If an ice core contains an unexplained narrow cosmogenic peak, it becomes a target interval for annual tree-ring measurement.

The archive network can bootstrap itself.

This is different from claiming a single master chronology. Natural archives have independent errors and should keep them. The value comes from matching sharp, physically global features across records without pretending the records are identical.

There is a useful analogy to distributed computing, but I promised not to turn another book into information theory. So keep it physical. A cedar takes carbon from air. Snow takes aerosols from air. Both happen after a burst of atmospheric particle production. Centuries later, laboratories measure different atoms in different materials and find compatible timing. That is enough.

The carbon-14 atom itself has no historical meaning. It becomes historical because it sits in a ring.

The ring has no astrophysical meaning. It becomes astrophysical because the isotope changed globally.

The event has no archaeological meaning. It becomes archaeological because someone cut the tree after it.

Those transformations are not metaphors. They are the actual chain of evidence.

And because the chain is physical, it can be audited. Measure another tree. Use another species. Sample another hemisphere. Measure beryllium-10. Measure chlorine-36. Rebuild the dendrochronology. Blind the ring assignment. Vary the carbon-cycle model. Test alternative particle spectra. Look for the event in an independently dated archive. The result can become stronger without requiring anyone to agree on a grand interpretation first.

This is one reason Miyake events are such useful anomalous science. They began as an anomaly and matured through independent constraint. The field did not need a charismatic witness. It needed more atoms.
