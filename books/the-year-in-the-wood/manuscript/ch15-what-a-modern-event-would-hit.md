# What a Modern Event Would Hit

The first machine to fail in a modern Miyake event might not be on Earth.

That is the simplest way to keep the hazard discussion from collapsing into another blackout story.

The isotope archives tell us that extreme solar energetic particle events have reached Earth repeatedly. High-energy particles are therefore the part of the modern risk we can connect most directly to the ancient evidence. The systems most exposed to particles are spacecraft, astronauts and high-altitude aviation.

Start in orbit.

Energetic protons can deposit charge in semiconductor devices, trigger single-event upsets, damage solar cells, degrade sensors and contribute to cumulative radiation dose. The severity depends on energy spectrum, spacecraft shielding, component design, orbit and operational mode. Modern spacecraft are tested against radiation environments, but design standards necessarily use a model of the tail.

Miyake events widen that tail.

A satellite hardened for the largest event measured since 1956 is not necessarily hardened for an event inferred from 774. That does not mean it would fail. Shielding, redundancy and component choice matter. It means the environment model should include proxy-derived extremes rather than stopping at the instrument record.

Low Earth orbit provides some geomagnetic shielding, especially away from polar regions, though particles can access high latitudes and the South Atlantic Anomaly complicates exposure. Medium Earth orbit navigation satellites and geosynchronous spacecraft face different radiation conditions. Interplanetary spacecraft and crews outside Earth's magnetosphere are more exposed.

The Moon is where the ancient trees become an engineering document.

A crew on the lunar surface has no global magnetic shield and little atmosphere. Habitats and vehicles need storm shelters or sufficient shielding to reduce solar particle dose. The August 1972 event is often used as a historical reference because it occurred between Apollo missions. A 774-like event, depending on spectrum, could be substantially more demanding.

The event would also arrive quickly.

Energetic particles can follow solar eruption signatures within tens of minutes to hours. Space-weather forecasting can identify active regions and eruptions, but exact particle intensity is difficult to predict before arrival. Deep-space operations therefore need passive protection and procedures that do not depend on long warning.

This is a different resilience problem from a geomagnetic storm that may take a CME one to three days to reach Earth.

Aviation sits between space and ground.

Commercial aircraft at cruising altitude receive more cosmic radiation than people at sea level because there is less atmosphere above them. Polar routes are additionally exposed because geomagnetic shielding is weaker. A major solar particle event can raise dose and disrupt high-frequency radio communication at high latitudes. Airlines can reroute aircraft, lower altitude or adjust operations, but those responses cost fuel and capacity.

Again the particle spectrum matters.

A hard event sends more high-energy particles deep into the atmosphere. Dose calculations based on one spectral assumption can differ substantially from another with the same total fluence above a low energy threshold.

The carbon-14 archive does not give aviation planners a single dose number. It gives a set of physically constrained event scenarios that should be propagated through atmospheric radiation models.

The power grid enters through another door.

Geomagnetically induced currents result from changes in Earth's magnetic field, not directly from the protons that create carbon-14. A solar eruption capable of a Miyake particle event may also launch a severe CME, but the coupling is not guaranteed. Therefore a “Miyake event” cannot be assigned a grid consequence without an assumed geomagnetic component.

If that component is severe, long high-voltage transmission lines and transformers at high geomagnetic latitudes are vulnerable. Grid topology and local ground conductivity shape induced currents. Operators can reduce load, reconfigure networks and monitor geomagnetic conditions. Transformer damage is possible but not inevitable.

The appropriate planning case is a joint distribution, not a single ancient replay.

Communications face several mechanisms. Solar X-rays can cause radio blackouts by increasing ionization in the lower ionosphere. Energetic particles can create polar cap absorption that disrupts high-frequency radio. Geomagnetic storms alter ionospheric electron density, affecting navigation and communication. Satellite anomalies can remove capacity.

Modern systems are redundant across technologies, but redundancy can share space-weather exposure.

A fiber-optic cable does not care about ionospheric absorption, but its repeaters and landing stations require terrestrial power. A satellite backup may share radiation risk. GPS timing underlies telecommunications and grid control. Cloud services depend on data centers, fiber, power and timing. System diagrams become coupled quickly.

This is where catastrophe modeling often becomes theatrical. Every dependency is drawn as a domino until civilization falls over.

Real resilience analysis asks about capacities, recovery and operator action.

How many satellites fail versus experience recoverable upsets? Which constellations have on-orbit spares? Can operators safe instruments? Which grid regions can island? Are replacement transformers available? Can airlines route around polar communication zones? Can lunar crews reach shielded volume in time? How does an event evolve over hours and days?

The ancient archive does not answer those questions. Modern engineering does.

The role of the archive is to keep the environmental input honest.

There is a useful analogy with flood design. A paleoflood deposit does not tell engineers which bridge girder will fail. It tells them the river has reached a level outside the gauge record. Engineers then run structural and hydraulic models under that scenario.

Miyake events are paleofloods of particle radiation.

The comparison breaks if taken too far, but it captures the division of labor. Natural archives expand the forcing distribution. Technology-specific models convert forcing into damage.

This separation also helps prioritize mitigation.

For astronauts, shielding and shelter are obvious because the consequence can be biological and immediate. For satellites, radiation-hard components, redundancy, safe modes and diversified orbits help. For aviation, radiation monitoring and rerouting procedures matter. For grids, geomagnetic monitoring, transformer neutral current sensors, operational protocols and hardening of vulnerable assets address the relevant variable.

No single “Miyake defense” exists.

There is also a danger of solving the last event. If the 774 particle spectrum was unusually hard, designing solely to it may neglect softer but longer-duration events. If a future CME produces extreme geomagnetic coupling with only moderate particle fluence, a particle-centric plan misses the grid. If multiple eruptions arrive in sequence, recovery windows shrink.

Resilience should be built to a family of physical environments.

The ancient catalog can help define that family as it grows.

One underexplored implication concerns satellite constellations. The number of spacecraft in low Earth orbit has increased dramatically. Constellations provide redundancy but also create correlated exposure. A particle event or thermospheric density increase can affect many spacecraft at once. Operators may face simultaneous anomaly management at unprecedented scale.

This is not automatically worse than a world with a few irreplaceable satellites. Distributed constellations can be more resilient. The answer depends on design and failure correlation.

Miyake events should therefore enter reliability engineering as a common-cause stressor.

There is a similar issue for lunar exploration. A permanent human presence on the Moon would convert solar particle risk from an occasional mission constraint into an occupational hazard. Habitats, suits, rovers and surface power systems would need procedures for rare extremes. The ancient trees provide evidence that rare extremes are not theoretical.

The rhetorical move “this could happen tomorrow” is true and useless. Any rare event can happen tomorrow.

The useful move is to ask whether current designs already accommodate the reconstructed range. If not, how expensive is the margin? If a few additional tonnes of shielding, a dedicated storm shelter, or different mission operations reduce risk dramatically, recurrence uncertainty becomes less important.

Insurance logic can be rational without precise probability.

The field also needs to distinguish societal disruption from existential risk. A severe space-weather event could damage satellites, interrupt communications, reroute aviation and stress grids. Those are serious consequences. Claims that a Miyake event would necessarily collapse modern civilization go far beyond the isotope evidence and often beyond engineering analysis.

A robust society can absorb large shocks if systems fail gracefully and repair capacity exists.

The practical value of the ancient record is that it lets us test that robustness against environments we have not personally experienced.

There is one more target the event would hit: our confidence.

Space-weather forecasting has improved enormously, but the arrival of a particle event outside the instrumental range would force models to extrapolate under operational pressure. Sensor saturation, unprecedented count rates and unfamiliar combinations could challenge systems designed around modern data.

This is another reason proxy science belongs in preparedness. It familiarizes institutions with out-of-sample conditions before the outlier arrives.

The most important preparation may therefore be conceptual. Do not build thresholds whose upper bound is “largest measured since 1956.” Do not assume Carrington defines every solar hazard. Do not assume a rare-event archive gives an exact recurrence. Do not combine particle and geomagnetic severity without a model.

The trees cannot protect a satellite.

They can stop us from pretending the satellite has seen the whole sky.
