# False Anchors and Bad Clocks

A precise method creates precise mistakes.

That is the chapter every new dating technique should be required to write before it becomes fashionable.

Miyake events can produce exact-year anchors. The phrase is true. It also creates a cognitive hazard. Once a method is associated with exactness, users begin to ignore the uncertainties around sample association, event identification and chronology because the final number lacks visible error bars.

A wrong 777 is more dangerous than an honest 770–790.

The first failure mode is a false event match.

Annual radiocarbon measurements are noisy. The carbon-14 calibration record contains ordinary year-to-year variation. A short sequence can show an increase that resembles part of a known event. If researchers know roughly where they want the chronology to land, they may align the most convenient rise.

The defense is shape, replication and external constraint.

Measure several years before and after the candidate. Compare the entire excursion with independently dated reference trees. Replicate key rings. Use conventional radiocarbon and archaeological context only to define a plausible search window, not to choose among ambiguous matches after the fact.

The second failure mode is ring chronology.

Trees are not perfect annual barcodes. Some species form missing rings under stress. Others create false rings when growth stops and restarts within a season. Tropical trees may not have reliable annual boundaries. Narrow rings can be hard to dissect without cross-contamination. Dendrochronological cross-dating is a specialized practice for a reason.

An isotope spike cannot repair a misread sequence if the ring count around it is wrong.

The third failure mode is stored carbon.

Trees do not always build a year's wood exclusively from carbon fixed that year. Stored carbohydrates can contribute to early growth, especially earlywood. Species differ. Deciduous trees and evergreens allocate reserves differently. A rapid atmospheric carbon-14 pulse can therefore appear with different timing or amplitude in different tissues.

This is manageable at annual scale for large events but becomes important for subannual dating.

Claims that an event occurred in a particular season need stronger physiological modeling than claims that a ring belongs to a known year.

The fourth failure mode is old wood.

Radiocarbon archaeologists know this problem intimately. The age of wood is not the age of use. A long-lived tree can contain heartwood centuries older than its felling. A beam can be reused. A dead tree can be collected long after death. Charcoal can preserve only inner rings.

Miyake dating can tell you exactly when a surviving ring grew and, if the outer edge survives, when the tree stopped growing. It cannot automatically tell you when a human built with it.

Context remains sovereign.

The fifth failure mode is contamination and pretreatment.

Archaeological wood can absorb conservation chemicals, humic substances and modern carbon. Radiocarbon laboratories use chemical pretreatment to isolate appropriate fractions such as alpha-cellulose. Poor preparation can shift results. Because Miyake identification depends on relative annual changes, some contamination modes may be visible as smoothing or offsets, but others can confuse weak spikes.

Replicate laboratories are valuable when stakes are high.

The sixth failure mode is event catalog error.

A newly proposed cosmogenic spike can become an archaeological anchor too quickly. If the event year is later revised, every chronology tied to it moves. This argues for confidence grades. Canonical events such as 774 and 993 have extensive independent support. Newer candidates should not be promoted to equal status until replicated across absolutely dated chronologies and, ideally, other cosmogenic isotopes.

The seventh failure mode is hemisphere and growing-season alignment.

Atmospheric carbon mixes globally but not instantaneously. Trees in opposite hemispheres grow in opposite seasons. A production pulse late in one calendar year can enter Southern Hemisphere wood differently from Northern Hemisphere wood. Reference curves must therefore respect location and biology.

The eighth failure mode is confirmation bias.

This one is not technical and may be the strongest.

An archaeologist has a disputed chronology. A famous ruler or migration sits at one end of the plausible range. A Miyake event near that preferred date offers the possibility of a definitive result. The incentive to see the match is enormous.

Pre-registration sounds absurd in archaeology until you need it.

A team can define the candidate interval, reference event curves, matching criteria and rejection thresholds before seeing the high-resolution sequence. Blind sample labels can reduce expectation effects in laboratory processing. Independent dendrochronologists can review ring counts. A second lab can measure a subset of rings.

These are not rituals. They keep a hard date from becoming a wish.

The ninth failure mode is publication asymmetry.

A successful exact-year result belongs in Nature. A timber sequence that failed to find the expected event is less glamorous. If failed searches disappear, users cannot estimate how often candidate matches are ambiguous or how often archaeological sequences turn out not to span the assumed event.

A method's reliability lives partly in its disappointments.

The tenth failure mode is semantic inflation.

“Dated to 1021” can mean several things: a tree ring grew in 1021, the tree was felled in 1021, wood was worked in 1021, an occupation occurred in 1021, a settlement was founded in 1021. Those claims sit on different rungs of inference.

The L'Anse aux Meadows study supports Norse activity involving cut wood in 1021. It does not establish first landfall that year. Por-Bajin's foundation timber supports construction beginning in summer 777 more directly because of archaeological context and outer-ring anatomy. Dispilio's anchored chronology dates construction phases, but each structural association must still be evaluated.

The number is only as good as the verb attached to it.

There are also astrophysical false anchors.

Suppose a carbon-14 spike is real but not solar. For archaeological dating, that may not matter if the event is global and securely dated. For space-weather recurrence, it matters completely. A gamma-ray burst and a solar proton event are different hazards.

This is why the chronological and astrophysical confidence grades should remain separate.

An event can be a class-one timestamp and class-two source attribution.

The 774 and 993 multiradionuclide evidence makes solar origin strong, but the principle matters for future discoveries. Do not require archaeology to wait for perfect astrophysics; do not let archaeological utility certify the astrophysics.

The same separation should apply to historical chronicles. A red-sky report can be fascinating without becoming part of the timestamp definition. The isotope event is dated from physical archives. Literary sources are then compared independently.

This prevents a circular romance in which the text “confirms” the isotope and the isotope “proves” the text refers to the storm.

Bad clocks often arise from evidence streams flattering each other.

The solution is adversarial chronology.

For an important exact-year claim, assign one team to try to break the placement. Give them the raw annual radiocarbon data, ring photographs, pretreatment records, alternative event curves and archaeological context. Ask for plausible competing alignments. If none survives, confidence rises.

This is not hostile review. It is load testing.

The cost is small compared with the cultural weight of some conclusions.

Exact dates can reorder national histories, migration narratives and climate-event correlations. Once a number enters textbooks, later correction becomes socially harder than scientific correction. A little friction at publication is cheap.

The method should also publish uncertainty in material terms. “Outer edge absent by three rings.” “Sapwood estimate broad.” “Possible reuse.” “Event identification replicated in two trees.” These statements tell readers where precision comes from.

An exact year with a long footnote is healthier than an exact year with a halo.

Miyake dating deserves enthusiasm because it has already worked in cases where other clocks were blunt. It deserves skepticism because its success will attract weaker applications.

That is not cynicism. Strong methods survive explicit failure modes.

The tree is not wrong because we can misread it.

The job is to make the misreading expensive.
