# The Event of 993

The second event made the first one useful.

That sounds unfair to 774, which had already forced a revision of the solar-particle record. But one extraordinary isotope excursion can remain an orphan. A second event with a similar physical signature changes the problem. It suggests a recurring process, creates another candidate anchor year, and gives archaeologists something the first event alone could not provide: choice.

In 2013 Fusa Miyake, Kimiaki Masuda and Toshio Nakamura reported another rapid rise in tree-ring carbon-14 around 993/994 CE. The Nature Communications paper is here: https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms2783. The increase was smaller than the 774/775 event but still far outside ordinary annual variation. Later measurements and ice-core evidence supported a solar energetic particle interpretation.

The date is fortunate for historians because 993 sits inside a world full of surviving timber and documentary argument.

It is also close enough to the Norse expansion across the North Atlantic to be useful in exactly the way a global marker ought to be useful. Greenland had been settled by Norse communities by the late tenth century. The archaeological site at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland had long provided material evidence for Norse presence in North America around the year 1000. Radiocarbon dating, artifact typology, architecture and the site's unmistakably Norse character placed the occupation securely. What remained difficult was the exact year.

The 993 event made exactness possible.

Before getting to Newfoundland, it is worth asking why the second spike was trusted. The logic repeated the first case but with some important improvements. Carbon-14 excursions were measured in annual tree rings. Beryllium-10 in Antarctic ice also showed an increase around the same interval. Multiradionuclide work by Mekhaldi and colleagues treated 993 alongside 774 and found the relative isotope production compatible with a strong solar proton event.

The event also has more tempting historical sky reports than 774.

Researchers have identified descriptions of red skies and aurora-like phenomena in late 992 and early 993 from parts of Europe and East Asia. Korean records refer to a strange appearance sometimes translated as “heaven's gate.” German and Irish chronicles contain reports of red or fiery skies. These records are intriguing because the timing is close to the radiocarbon event.

They are not particle detectors.

A historical aurora report can support the existence of strong geomagnetic activity around the same period, but it cannot by itself identify the particle event that produced the cosmogenic isotopes. Manuscript chronology can be uncertain. Descriptions are culturally mediated. A red sky could have several causes. And a strong aurora records magnetospheric disturbance, not the proton spectrum inferred from carbon-14.

The 993 event is therefore a good place to practice not becoming greedy with evidence.

The isotope spike is class one. A solar energetic particle source supported by multiple radionuclides is class two, strong enough to be the working explanation. Specific historical reports as observations of the same solar episode belong between class two and three depending on dating and interpretation. Claims about the exact flare, CME or day remain more speculative.

The archaeological use does not require any of that extra confidence.

This is the deep advantage of a physical timestamp. Archaeologists do not need to know whether an Irish chronicler saw the same event. They need the annual carbon-14 excursion to be real, global enough to appear in their timber, and securely tied to a calendar year.

Once those conditions hold, the event functions like a notch in a ruler.

The ruler analogy is better than a clock because the operation is spatial inside the wood. Researchers take annual rings from an archaeological timber and measure carbon-14 across a sequence. When they locate the 993/994 excursion, they know which ring corresponds to the event. Then they count outward toward the bark or waney edge. Each ring is one year. The final growth ring tells them when the tree stopped growing because it was cut or died.

The method has an almost offensive simplicity after the isotope work is done.

Find spike. Count rings.

But the simplicity rests on several conditions that must remain visible. The tree must have annual rings that can be separated. The sequence must include the event. The isotope signal must be strong enough to identify. The outermost wood must preserve enough anatomy to establish that the last ring is near or at felling. Reused or seasoned timber can separate felling from construction. Stored carbon can affect the exact ring in which a rapid atmospheric increase appears. Species and latitude matter.

At L'Anse aux Meadows, the wood happened to cooperate.

The 2022 Nature paper by Margot Kuitems and colleagues, “Evidence for European presence in the Americas in AD 1021,” analyzed wooden artifacts from the site that had been cut with metal tools. The relevant article is https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03972-8. In three pieces of wood, researchers identified the 993 radiocarbon anomaly. From the event ring they counted twenty-eight annual rings to the bark edge. The trees had been felled in 1021 CE.

The date is narrower than the history that surrounds it.

It does not tell us that 1021 was the first Norse arrival in North America. It does not establish how long the settlement lasted. It does not tell us which named saga character, if any, cut the wood. It does not turn literary Vinland narratives into transcripts. It establishes that Norse people were cutting trees at L'Anse aux Meadows in 1021.

A single hard date can be more useful than a large soft story.

The result also illustrates why the 993 event deserves its own identity rather than being treated as a smaller sequel to 774. The two spikes occupy different historical landscapes. The eighth-century event can date Carolingian and Central Asian contexts. The tenth-century event falls near Norse Atlantic expansion, early medieval construction, and numerous preserved timbers. Each new event year opens a different archaeological window.

This makes the search for older Miyake events partly an archaeological infrastructure project.

Suppose a floating Neolithic tree-ring chronology spans 300 years but cannot be tied to the absolute calendar. If one of those years contains a known global carbon-14 spike, the sequence can be snapped into place. The event does not have to be historically observed. Indeed, for deep prehistory it cannot be. Its value increases as written chronology disappears.

The event of 993 sits at the hinge. It is recent enough to have chronicles and old enough for the physical archive to beat them at precision.

That should make historians slightly uncomfortable in a productive way.

Written sources have always had an aura of specificity. A chronicle gives names, motives, locations, sequences. Scientific dates can look blunt by comparison. But a sentence written centuries later can be exact in language and loose in time. A tree ring can be mute about motive and exact about year.

Different archives answer different questions.

This is why the phrase “interdisciplinary research” undersells the method. The disciplines do not merely contribute adjacent expertise. They impose incompatible standards on each other. Solar physics demands a source mechanism. Radiocarbon science demands isotope production and calibration. Dendrochronology demands ring integrity. Archaeology demands context and association. History demands source criticism. A claim that survives all five is narrower than any one discipline might prefer and stronger for it.

The 993 event also provides a caution about circular dating.

If an archaeologist expects a timber to date near 1000 and looks only for a spike around 993, a noisy annual sequence could tempt overfitting. Robust identification should use several years around the event, compare the observed rise and decay with reference data, and ideally replicate measurements. The absolute event chronology must come from independent tree-ring sequences, not from the archaeological object being dated.

A tie-point is only useful if the tie is external.

The L'Anse aux Meadows study had another strength: the three pieces of wood came from different trees. The same event-to-bark offset appearing independently makes an accidental match less likely and suggests a coherent episode of woodcutting. The result is therefore not simply three radiocarbon dates. It is three annual sequences sharing one cosmogenic marker and one felling year.

This kind of structure is what raw sample size cannot capture.

Three independent trees with a global anchor can be more informative about a discrete event than dozens of conventional radiocarbon measurements spread across broad calibration ranges.

There is still room for correction. Future work could identify earlier or later Norse activity at the site. Other North American sites might eventually yield equally secure evidence. The 1021 date should not be asked to bear more than it says.

But what it says is remarkable enough.

A solar particle event in 993 changed atmospheric isotope production. Trees incorporated the change. Norse woodcutters felled some trees twenty-eight growth rings later on the far side of the Atlantic. The wood survived. A thousand years after the cutting, accelerator mass spectrometers found the solar scar.

The event did not preserve the Vikings.

It preserved the interval.
