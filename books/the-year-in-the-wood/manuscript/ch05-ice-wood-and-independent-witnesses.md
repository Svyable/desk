# Ice, Wood, and Independent Witnesses

A tree ring is persuasive. A tree ring and an ice core are troublesome.

Troublesome for bad explanations, I mean.

When the 774/775 radiocarbon excursion was first reported, almost every mundane alternative was local. Contaminated sample. Misdated ring. Species-specific physiology. Laboratory offset. A weird carbon-cycle perturbation. Those possibilities weaken as the same pattern appears in other independently dated trees, but they do not vanish entirely. Carbon-14 is still one isotope moving through one global carbon system.

Beryllium-10 changes the argument because it is not carbon.

High-energy particles generate beryllium-10 through spallation reactions in the atmosphere. The isotope attaches to aerosols and is removed by atmospheric transport and precipitation. Polar snow captures some of it, and annual or near-annual ice layers can preserve variations. Chlorine-36 supplies a third cosmogenic channel with different production sensitivity. If a proposed particle event explains the relative behavior of carbon-14, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36, it has passed a stronger test than fitting one tree-ring curve.

The key paper for the 774 and 993 events is Mekhaldi et al. 2015, “Multiradionuclide evidence for the solar origin of the cosmic-ray events of AD 774/5 and 993/4,” Nature Communications: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms9611. The authors combined ice-core and tree-ring isotope evidence and found that the events were consistent with strong solar proton events. The multinuclide ratios constrained the particle spectrum and argued against alternatives such as a gamma-ray burst.

This is where “independent evidence” needs a precise definition.

Two laboratories measuring the same annual wood sequence are independent in one sense. They can catch lab-specific errors. Two tree species on different continents are more independent because they add environmental and physiological variation. A tree and an ice core are more independent still because the isotope, archive material, transport pathway and measurement problems differ. But all remain coupled through the atmosphere and the underlying particle event.

Independence is not binary. It has architecture.

That architecture matters when evaluating any anomalous claim. A radar and an infrared sensor may appear independent but share the same tracking software. Two eyewitnesses may have discussed the event before being interviewed. Two papers may use overlapping samples. Two intelligence reports may trace back to one source. Ten citations can collapse to one observation.

Miyake-event science has an unusually legible version of the opposite problem: one cause, several archives.

The carbon-14 rise is globally mixed enough to appear in wood across latitudes. Beryllium deposition is much noisier because weather and aerosol transport matter. Chlorine-36 behaves differently again. Agreement therefore does not mean identical curves. Researchers compare timing, production ratios and model expectations rather than demanding the archives overlay perfectly.

This is what makes the case stronger rather than messier. A real atmospheric production event should look different after traveling through different physical systems.

Perfect agreement can be suspicious when instruments ought to respond differently.

Ice cores also bring chronological uncertainty. Tree rings in an absolutely cross-dated chronology can often be assigned to exact calendar years. Ice layers are counted, modeled and tied to known horizons, but errors accumulate with depth and deposition can smear a short atmospheric event. A sharp event anchored in trees can therefore help synchronize ice chronology. The ice simultaneously provides independent evidence that the tree-ring anomaly is cosmogenic.

The relationship is reciprocal rather than hierarchical.

Brehm and colleagues emphasized this in their 2022 work on 7176 and 5259 BCE. A strongly dated carbon-14 event can serve as a time marker for floating tree-ring sequences and ice-core radionuclide records. Their 7176 BCE signal helped place a floating section of bristlecone-pine chronology more securely. The same event, if found in polar isotopes, can tighten cross-archive alignment.

Imagine the chronological problem physically. A bristlecone pine grows for centuries. Another tree overlaps some of its rings. Dead wood preserves an older sequence but no longer overlaps securely with the living chronology. Dendrochronologists can sometimes bridge the gap through pattern matching, but short overlap or missing regional material can leave a floating sequence. Conventional radiocarbon narrows the placement but may not choose a single year.

Then one ring contains a globally known isotope spike.

The floating sequence acquires a bolt hole.

Ice cores suffer a related but different drift. Annual layers thin with depth. Accumulation varies. Volcanic sulfate horizons, methane changes and other markers help synchronize cores. A globally generated cosmogenic spike adds another hard feature.

The archival network becomes strongest when each material corrects something the other is bad at.

Trees are excellent annual clocks but geographically and temporally discontinuous. Ice is continuous across long periods but can have layer-counting uncertainty and noisy deposition. Carbon-14 is globally mixed but smoothed by the carbon cycle. Beryllium-10 responds more directly to production but is strongly affected by atmospheric transport. Chlorine-36 carries useful spectral sensitivity but is less commonly measured.

There is no perfect archive. There is a portfolio.

This may sound like a methodological abstraction, but the consequences are concrete. The estimated magnitude of an ancient solar particle event depends on how isotope production is reconstructed. If one archive is temporally smeared, the peak can look smaller. If geomagnetic field strength differed from today, the same incoming particle fluence could produce more or less cosmogenic isotope. If local deposition boosts an ice-core peak, a naive global extrapolation will overstate production.

Cross-archive comparison limits those errors.

The 660 BCE event illustrates the point. Researchers found evidence in high-resolution beryllium-10 from Greenland ice and modeled carbon-14 production, later constraining the tree-ring timing more precisely. Panyushkina and colleagues in 2024 placed the event between 664 and 663 BCE using larch tree rings from Yamal and Altai: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01618-x. The event was no longer merely a broad ice-core anomaly. It acquired a calendar position.

Again the tree and ice trade strengths.

The chronology also changes how historical records can be used. If an isotope event is dated to a one-year window, historians can search contemporary astronomical chronicles with a narrower target. But the physical archive should set the window rather than the text. Otherwise there is a danger of reverse engineering: finding an evocative medieval report first, then stretching isotope chronology to fit it.

This is a general rule for multi-evidence anomalies. Let the most objective clock lead.

The same applies in archaeology. A cultural interpretation may suggest a preferred historical ruler or migration. Do not use that preference to choose among possible ring placements. Locate the isotope feature, establish the dendrochronological offset, then ask what the resulting date does to the history.

Por-Bajin became more useful because the construction date was allowed to constrain the story. L'Anse aux Meadows was not dated to 1021 because 1021 was historically convenient. The 993 marker was found, rings to the bark were counted, and 1021 followed.

Independent witnesses are only independent if they are permitted to disagree.

That is the hidden standard behind the isotope work. If beryllium-10 had not supported the radiocarbon events, the solar interpretation would have weakened. If annual trees on other continents had failed to reproduce 774, a Japanese-local explanation would have remained alive. If chlorine-36 ratios had strongly favored a gamma-ray source, the mechanism story would be different. The evidentiary streams were useful because they could have broken the hypothesis.

This is why the phrase “multiple sensors” should never end an argument by itself.

A sensor network becomes evidentiary when we know which errors are shared and which are independent. A tree in Germany and a tree in Japan share atmospheric carbon but not local weather. Two accelerator mass spectrometers may share calibration standards. Carbon-14 and beryllium-10 share cosmic-ray production but not post-production chemistry. Ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica share the global event but not regional deposition.

The map of dependencies is the evidence.

Miyake events offer a rare case where this map is tangible enough to teach with. You can draw arrows: particle spectrum to atmospheric nuclear cascade; geomagnetic field to access; production to isotope inventories; atmospheric transport to deposition; photosynthesis to wood; snowfall to ice; laboratory chemistry to measured ratio; chronology to assigned year. Different archives branch from the same source and reconverge in analysis.

Each branch adds uncertainty. Each independent branch also adds leverage.

The field is still incomplete. We do not yet have equally high-resolution carbon-14, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 coverage for every candidate event. Many proposed spikes are supported by only one archive or low-resolution data. Some event lists mix confirmed and candidate episodes too casually. The oldest tree-ring records are not annually measured throughout. Ice-core dating uncertainty grows. Different studies use different carbon-cycle and production models.

This incompleteness is not a defect to hide. It tells us what to measure next.

A useful future program would identify candidate intervals from low-resolution multi-isotope records, then deliberately measure annual tree rings across several independent chronologies and high-resolution ice around the same years. Better still, a second program would sample randomly selected intervals at annual resolution to estimate the false-positive background and reduce search bias.

The first program finds events efficiently. The second tells us how surprised to be.

This division of labor is common in astronomy. Targeted surveys find interesting objects; blind surveys estimate populations. Miyake-event science increasingly needs both.

The payoff is not merely a better solar hazard frequency. Every confirmed sharp event becomes a potential chronological marker. An astrophysics survey can enlarge archaeology's toolkit. Archaeological demand for anchor years can justify new high-resolution isotope measurements. Ice-core synchronization benefits both.

The sciences are coupled because the sky was global.

There is something almost comical about how little the original event respected disciplinary ownership. Energetic particles arrived. Nitrogen nuclei changed. Carbon circulated. Trees grew. Aerosols fell onto ice. Human beings cut some of the trees and built things. Other trees died naturally and remained in bogs. Millennia later, laboratories separated cellulose and counted isotopes.

The event acquired witnesses it never asked for.

And because the witnesses are different, we can cross-examine them.
