# Back Matter

The research posture of this book is deliberately asymmetric. The existence of the best-established rapid radiocarbon excursions is much firmer than the detailed reconstruction of the solar events that produced them. Their use as chronological markers can therefore be stronger than claims about exact flare energy, CME behavior, auroral appearance or modern technological consequence.

The primary starting paper is Fusa Miyake, Kentaro Nagaya, Kimiaki Masuda and Toshio Nakamura, “A signature of cosmic-ray increase in AD 774–775 from tree rings in Japan,” Nature 486 (2012): https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11123.

The second canonical event was reported by Fusa Miyake, Kimiaki Masuda and Toshio Nakamura, “Another rapid event in the carbon-14 content of tree rings,” Nature Communications 4 (2013): https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms2783.

Independent geographic replication of the 774 event includes A. J. Timothy Jull et al., “Excursions in the 14C record at AD 774–775 in tree rings from Russia and America,” Geophysical Research Letters (2014): https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2014GL059874.

Multiradionuclide source attribution is anchored by Florian Mekhaldi et al., “Multiradionuclide evidence for the solar origin of the cosmic-ray events of AD 774/5 and 993/4,” Nature Communications 6 (2015): https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms9611.

The 660 BCE event and the importance of multiple radionuclides are treated in Raimund Muscheler and colleagues' work and in the PNAS paper “Multiradionuclide evidence for an extreme solar proton event around 2,610 B.P. (~660 BC)”: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1815725116. The annual timing was refined by Irina Panyushkina et al. in 2024: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01618-x.

The deep-Holocene events at 7176 and 5259 BCE are in Nicolas Brehm et al., “Tree-rings reveal two strong solar proton events in 7176 and 5259 BCE,” Nature Communications 13 (2022): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-28804-9.

The use of the 993 event to establish Norse activity in North America in 1021 is Margot Kuitems et al., “Evidence for European presence in the Americas in AD 1021,” Nature 601 (2022): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03972-8.

The Por-Bajin application is Margot Kuitems et al., “Radiocarbon-based approach capable of subannual precision resolves the origins of the site of Por-Bajin,” PNAS (2020): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32513700/.

The Neolithic Dispilio application is Andrej Maczkowski et al., “Absolute dating of the European Neolithic using the 5259 BC rapid 14C excursion,” Nature Communications 15 (2024): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48402-1.

The literature on the late-glacial event around 12,350 BCE is evolving rapidly and should be treated as an active research area rather than a settled ranking of “largest solar storm.” The original radiocarbon-spike work is associated with Edouard Bard and colleagues; later coupled atmosphere-production modeling strengthens a solar energetic particle interpretation. Publication-grade revision should use the latest peer-reviewed reconstruction rather than press summaries.

A useful broad review is Edward Cliver, Carolus Schrijver, Kazunari Shibata and Ilya Usoskin, “Extreme solar events,” Living Reviews in Solar Physics (2022), along with later Space Science Reviews work on extreme solar events and cosmogenic proxies.

## Chronology

1956 — Strongest hard-spectrum solar energetic particle event in the neutron-monitor era, often used as a modern reference.

1959 — Carrington's 1859 geomagnetic storm is already established as a historical extreme, but not as a Miyake-scale cosmogenic particle event.

2012 — Miyake et al. report the 774/775 annual carbon-14 excursion in Japanese cedar.

2013 — Miyake et al. report the 993/994 excursion.

2014–2015 — Geographic replication and multiradionuclide evidence establish the events as global and strongly support a solar energetic particle source.

2019 — Multiradionuclide work strengthens the case for an extreme event around 660 BCE.

2020 — Por-Bajin construction is assigned to summer 777 using the 775 marker and outer-ring anatomy.

2022 — L'Anse aux Meadows wood places Norse activity in North America in 1021 using the 993 marker. Brehm et al. report strong 7176 and 5259 BCE events.

2023–2025 — Late-glacial tree-ring work and modeling extend the event record to roughly 12,350 BCE and beyond the Holocene.

2024 — The 5259 BCE event anchors the Neolithic Dispilio chronology; annual tree-ring work refines the 660 BCE event to 664–663 BCE.

## Evidence classes

Class 1 — verified / documented: annual carbon-14 excursions at the canonical event years; independent replication across multiple tree-ring chronologies for the strongest events; companion cosmogenic radionuclide evidence in ice for several events; published exact-year archaeological applications at L'Anse aux Meadows, Por-Bajin and Dispilio.

Class 2 — credible but incomplete/model-dependent: reconstructed proton fluence and spectra; exact event duration; seasonal production timing; geomagnetic normalization; recurrence estimates from sparse high-resolution coverage; detailed mapping from isotope production to modern radiation environments.

Class 3 — disputed or unconfirmed: specific medieval sky reports claimed as direct observations of individual Miyake events; candidate event years lacking independent multi-archive confirmation; claims that a carbon-14 event uniquely determines flare energy or geomagnetic storm strength; deterministic predictions of civilization-wide collapse from a future event.

Class 4 — synthesis / open conjecture: a deliberately maintained global Miyake-event timestamp catalog for archaeology; systematic random annual tree-ring surveys for unbiased recurrence estimation; integrated archaeological/space-weather sampling programs; use of event anchors to synchronize otherwise floating natural archives at scale.

## Defeat conditions

The book's central thesis would materially weaken if the canonical 774 and 993 events failed independent annual-ring replication, if multi-isotope evidence showed that their production was local rather than global, or if exact-year archaeological applications depended on circular calibration to the historical dates they claim to establish. None of those conditions is supported by the current literature.

The stronger claim—that most canonical events were caused by extreme solar energetic particles—would weaken if improved isotope production models consistently favored a non-solar source across carbon-14, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36.

The hazard argument would weaken if modern radiation transport showed that reconstructed event spectra add no material exposure beyond existing design envelopes. The grid argument is intentionally limited because cosmogenic particle events do not uniquely determine geomagnetic-storm severity.

## Research priorities

A publication-grade pass needs a complete event-by-event evidence table; the latest 12,350 BCE modeling; raw annual reference curves where publicly available; the full event-candidate literature including failed replications; a systematic accounting of annual versus targeted measurement coverage; modern solar-particle environment standards used by NASA, ESA and commercial satellite operators; quantitative deep-space dose modeling for several reconstructed spectra; and additional archaeological applications beyond the three cases developed here.

The next prose pass should also deepen the human history of Fusa Miyake's laboratory work, the accelerator-mass-spectrometry process, dendrochronological practice, and the material biographies of the archaeological timbers. Those additions should come from primary interviews, institutional histories and methods papers rather than invented scene-setting.
