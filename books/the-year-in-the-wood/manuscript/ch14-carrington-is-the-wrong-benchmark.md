# Carrington Is the Wrong Benchmark

The Carrington event is too famous for its own good.

On 1 September 1859, Richard Carrington observed an intense white-light solar flare. Within roughly a day, Earth experienced a severe geomagnetic storm. Auroras appeared at unusually low latitudes. Telegraph systems malfunctioned; some operators reported sparks and the ability to run circuits on geomagnetically induced currents after disconnecting batteries. The event became the standard historical unit for “big solar storm.”

Then tree rings complicated the scale.

The 774 and 993 cosmogenic events appear to have delivered solar energetic particle fluences far beyond anything directly measured and beyond what Carrington left in cosmogenic isotopes. If Carrington is the most famous geomagnetic storm, and Miyake events are larger particle events, which is the bigger storm?

The question is malformed.

Solar weather has dimensions.

A geomagnetic storm measures disturbance of Earth's magnetosphere, often summarized using indices such as Dst. The disturbance depends strongly on the speed, density and especially magnetic orientation of solar plasma reaching Earth. Southward interplanetary magnetic field can couple efficiently into the magnetosphere. Geomagnetically induced currents depend on the time variation of magnetic fields, grid geometry and ground conductivity.

A solar energetic particle event is different. High-energy protons and ions arrive along magnetic field lines, sometimes within tens of minutes of a solar eruption. Their spectrum and fluence determine radiation effects, atmospheric ionization and cosmogenic isotope production.

A flare's electromagnetic radiation is different again. X-rays and extreme ultraviolet alter the ionosphere almost immediately and can disrupt high-frequency radio on the sunlit side of Earth.

A coronal mass ejection can accompany the flare and particles, but not always in a simple proportion.

Calling all of this “storm size” is like ranking hurricanes, floods and lightning strikes on one ruler because all involve weather.

The Carrington event is the wrong benchmark when the question is particle fluence.

It remains a valuable benchmark for geomagnetic disturbance.

This distinction has consequences for modern risk planning. Power grids are primarily vulnerable to geomagnetically induced currents from rapid magnetic-field changes. Satellites are vulnerable to several solar effects: particle radiation, charging, drag changes from atmospheric heating, communication disruptions and navigation errors. Astronauts beyond low Earth orbit are especially exposed to energetic particles. Aviation at high latitudes faces radiation and communications issues. A single solar eruption can stress all these systems, but the severity ranking can differ among them.

A 774-like particle event with only moderate geomagnetic coupling could be devastating for spacecraft radiation while less severe for ground grids than Carrington. A Carrington-like CME with modest high-energy proton production could reverse that ranking.

We do not know which combination occurred in 774.

Carbon-14 cannot tell us the magnetic orientation of a vanished CME.

That one sentence should sit beside every civilization-collapse simulation that starts from a Miyake isotope spike and ends with a blackout map.

This does not make the hazard benign. It makes it conditional.

The modern tendency to ask whether a future event would be “worse than Carrington” comes partly from the need for a narrative reference. Carrington has eyewitnesses, instruments, telegraph failures and dates. Miyake events are isotope curves without a machine-age impact record. Engineers prefer cases with failure data.

But the historical analogy can mislead because technology has changed.

In 1859, telegraph lines were long conductors that responded vividly to geomagnetic fields, but there were no high-voltage continental grids, no GPS constellation, no commercial low-Earth-orbit megaconstellations, no astronauts, no transpolar jet network, no global high-frequency aviation communication, no semiconductor-heavy spacecraft fleet.

A modern Carrington event would encounter a different civilization.

So would a modern 774 event.

The proper benchmark is not one historical storm. It is a matrix of physical stressors and system vulnerabilities.

For particles: spectrum, fluence, duration, anisotropy, onset time. For geomagnetic disturbance: CME speed, field orientation, magnetospheric coupling, rate of field change. For radio: X-ray and EUV output, ionospheric response. For technology: orbit, shielding, transformer design, grid topology, ground conductivity, operational procedures, spare capacity, warning and recovery.

This is less memorable than “Carrington times ten.” It is also closer to engineering.

The Miyake archives can improve only part of the matrix. Cosmogenic isotope production is most informative about energetic particles. Ice-core nitrate has sometimes been proposed as a proxy for solar proton events, but that indicator has been heavily debated and is not a reliable universal recorder. Carbon-14, beryllium-10 and chlorine-36 are stronger because their production physics is directly tied to high-energy atmospheric cascades.

Even they do not reconstruct the whole solar event.

This incomplete observability is a useful reminder that natural archives are sensors with transfer functions. They do not preserve “the storm.” They preserve effects that can be inverted with models.

Carrington itself is incompletely measured by modern standards. Dst estimates are reconstructed from historical magnetograms. Telegraph reports are scattered. Aurora observations have geographic bias. The famous event is not a perfect calibration point either.

The comparison is uncertain on both sides.

There are better modern particle benchmarks. The February 1956 ground-level enhancement was extremely hard-spectrum and well measured by neutron monitors. The August 1972 event had very high particle fluence and occurred between Apollo 16 and Apollo 17, prompting enduring discussion of astronaut risk. October 1989 produced several ground-level enhancements. January 2005 had a remarkably rapid particle onset.

These events teach operational response and spectral diversity.

None makes the ancient events unnecessary because none reaches their inferred scale.

The archive therefore asks engineers to combine incompatible virtues. Use modern events for detailed response functions. Use ancient events for tail magnitude. Do not ask one dataset to impersonate the other.

There is another reason Carrington can be the wrong benchmark: the event itself may have become a mythic maximum.

For decades “Carrington-class” often meant the largest plausible solar storm because it was the largest famous historical case. The term quietly fused observed maximum with physical maximum. Miyake events broke that fusion for energetic particles.

This is an important scientific move. Maxima in short records are usually sampling facts, not natural limits.

The 774 event did not prove Carrington was small. It proved a different solar variable had exceeded modern experience.

This should change how we talk about low-probability infrastructure threats. The correct question is not whether the next event will resemble the worst thing we remember. It is which physical dimensions our systems are vulnerable to, and whether the historical record samples the tail of each dimension.

For power grids, paleomagnetic and historical geomagnetic reconstructions remain crucial. For satellites and crewed exploration, cosmogenic particle events are directly relevant. For aviation, both radiation and communication effects matter. For pipelines and long conductors, geomagnetic fields dominate.

One storm can produce many hazards; one proxy sees only some.

The public wants a single category because emergency planning becomes easier to explain. The Sun is not obliged to cooperate.

This multidimensional view also softens some apocalyptic rhetoric. “An event ten times Carrington” is meaningless unless the dimension is specified. Ten times the proton fluence? Ten times Dst? Ten times flare X-ray flux? Ten times induced electric field? Each implies different consequences, and some ratios are physically nonsensical.

Precision in language is a resilience measure.

If policymakers think Miyake events are simply giant geomagnetic storms, they may spend in the wrong places. If satellite operators treat Carrington as the radiation worst case, they may underdesign for particles. If grid engineers are told a carbon-14 spike proves a particular induced-current scenario, they may distrust the entire field when the caveat appears later.

Good hazard communication keeps variables attached to evidence.

There is a larger lesson about historical analogies. They are useful because they give scale to unfamiliar risk. They are dangerous because they import hidden conditions.

The Carrington event happened to a nineteenth-century technological network under one solar geometry. It cannot be rerun on our system. The 774 event happened to a preindustrial world and survives mainly through isotope chemistry. Neither is a scenario. Both are constraints.

The future event will be its own combination.

This is why I would retire “Carrington-class” as a universal adjective and keep Carrington as a named case. It deserves its place in history. It simply should not be asked to define the Sun's entire tail.

The trees have already told us the tail has more than one direction.
