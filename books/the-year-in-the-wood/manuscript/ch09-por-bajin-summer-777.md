# Por-Bajin, Summer 777

Por-Bajin sits on an island in a lake in southern Siberia and looks like a place designed to manufacture hypotheses.

The rectangular enclosure has high walls, internal courtyards and traces of substantial timber construction. Its builders were associated with the Uyghur Khaganate, but the site's exact purpose and chronology long resisted agreement. Palace, monastery, fortress, ritual complex: each interpretation became more or less plausible depending on when the place had been built and how long it had been occupied.

The problem was not a lack of dates. It was a lack of the right date.

Conventional radiocarbon measurements and historical context placed the complex in the eighth century. That left room for several rulers and political moments. In a short-lived empire, a few decades can mean a different patron, religion and geopolitical setting.

Then the 774/775 event appeared inside the foundation wood.

In 2020 Margot Kuitems and colleagues published a PNAS paper using annual radiocarbon measurements to identify the Miyake event in timber from Por-Bajin. The abstract is available through PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32513700/. By locating the 775 marker and examining the outermost rings, the researchers concluded that construction began in the summer of 777 CE.

Summer, not merely year.

That extra precision came from wood anatomy. The final ring's development indicated the tree had been cut during the growing season. The cosmic marker fixed the annual sequence; cellular growth stage narrowed the season.

It is difficult to resist the elegance of the method. So resist it long enough to see the assumptions.

The timber had to be fresh construction wood rather than reused material. The ring sequence had to be correctly counted. The 775 isotope excursion had to be correctly located. The outermost growth tissue had to survive well enough for seasonal interpretation. Local tree growth had to be understood.

When those conditions held, the date altered the historical argument.

A 777 construction start placed Por-Bajin during the reign of Tengri Bögü Qaghan, a Uyghur ruler associated with the adoption of Manichaeism after contact with Sogdian religious communities. The timing strengthened proposals that the complex had a religious function or was connected with political changes around Manichaeism. It also helped explain the site's apparent rapid abandonment after a regime change hostile to that policy.

The isotope did not prove the site's purpose.

It eliminated some chronologies and favored others.

This is the difference between a date and a theory. Archaeological debates often become stuck because every interpretation can absorb a broad date range. Tighten the chronology, and some stories lose room to breathe.

Por-Bajin is therefore a more interesting demonstration of Miyake dating than a simple “mystery solved” headline suggests. The scientific contribution was not that cosmic rays revealed the purpose of an ancient fortress. It was that a physical timestamp reduced historical degrees of freedom.

That phrase sounds statistical because it is.

If a monument could have been built anytime across fifty years, several rulers, wars, religions and building campaigns may fit. If the timber was cut in summer 777, the candidate set contracts. Historical records become more discriminating because the date has become less forgiving.

Chronology is often the hidden variable in causal history.

The Por-Bajin work also shows why subannual ambition is not always foolish. Earlier I warned that stored carbon and species physiology complicate the attempt to date a solar particle event to a season. Here the seasonal claim concerns tree felling after the event, not the exact moment of the cosmic-ray pulse. The outermost ring preserves its stage of formation. That biological evidence can be more direct.

Two clocks operate in the same sample.

The internal isotope spike identifies 775. The outer ring anatomy identifies a summer cut two years later.

This is what makes trees such unusual archives. They carry both absolute markers and relative growth structure. A volcanic ash layer can anchor a sediment sequence, but it does not usually count years for you after the eruption. A tree does.

The annual count is built in.

Por-Bajin also benefits from a short construction history. Large medieval structures often incorporate reused beams, stockpiled timber and repairs from multiple periods. An outer ring date may tell you when one tree was felled, not when the whole building was constructed. Multiple timbers can reveal phases. Archaeological context remains essential.

At a site with substantial original foundation timber, the association can be stronger.

This suggests a hierarchy of good Miyake targets. Foundation piles with bark in a rapidly built structure are excellent. Reused roof beams are less clean. Charcoal is difficult because the outer rings may be gone and “old wood” can predate burning by centuries. Museum objects with uncertain provenance may offer a precise felling date with weak historical association.

The best sample is not the oldest. It is the sample whose biography is shortest.

That principle deserves wider use in radiocarbon work. A date measures the sampled material, not the human event an archaeologist cares about. The inferential gap depends on material history. Seeds have short biological lives and can closely date deposition. Heartwood from a thousand-year-old tree can predate cutting by centuries. Reused timber can outlive the building it first served.

Miyake precision cannot repair a bad association.

This is one of the method's most important epistemic boundaries. Exactness at the laboratory can coexist with ambiguity at the site.

A timber can be dated to 777 and still leave open whether the building opened that year, whether the ruler visited it, whether a religious community occupied it, or whether a later earthquake damaged it. The calendar becomes sharp while the narrative remains fuzzy.

That is not failure. It is what physical evidence usually does.

The Por-Bajin case also demonstrates why the 774 event is useful even when the solar physics remains incomplete. Nobody dating the foundation needs to know whether the particle event came from one flare or several, how bright the aurora was, or exactly how hard the proton spectrum was. They need the radiocarbon excursion to be globally reproducible and calendar-dated.

A phenomenon can be an excellent clock before it is a complete astrophysical story.

This reverses the usual anxiety around anomalous evidence. People often insist a phenomenon is useless until the mechanism is known. That is wrong. Superconductivity was technologically useful before BCS theory. Mendelian inheritance was predictive before DNA. Sailors navigated with magnetic compasses before geomagnetism was understood in modern terms.

But usefulness should not be smuggled back into mechanism. The fact that a Miyake event dates Por-Bajin does not prove every detail of the solar-source model. Different questions inherit different uncertainties.

The site itself adds a human scale missing from isotope plots.

Imagine building a large walled complex on an island in 777. Trees are felled in summer. Timber is shaped and moved. Walls rise. Political events elsewhere in the Uyghur world determine whether the complex will matter. The builders know none of the carbon-14 story. The isotope anomaly is already two rings behind them, invisible in their lumber.

The trees have been tagged by an event nobody recognizes.

A few years later the complex's political context changes. Por-Bajin never becomes what its builders intended, or does so only briefly. Centuries pass. The timber survives because archaeological fortune is uneven. Modern researchers discover that one of the most important facts about the wood occurred before it was cut.

That is the oddity Miyake dating repeatedly produces. The date stamp precedes the human event.

Unlike a coin struck in a known reign, which enters circulation because people made it, the cosmogenic marker is already inside raw material before anyone decides what to build. It is a naturally preprinted timestamp.

This opens a practical research strategy. Archaeologists with timbers spanning known event years can search for the marker even where local dendrochronological master sequences are incomplete. Once anchored, their floating chronology may extend before and after the event for centuries. One expensive isotope campaign can therefore fix many cheaper ring-width dates.

Dispilio would later demonstrate this at much greater temporal depth.

But Por-Bajin is the case that makes the operation easy to see. The event was known. The timber was approximately dated. The spike was found. Rings were counted. Seasonal growth narrowed the cut. Historical interpretation shifted.

No extraordinary mechanism had to be believed.

The extraordinary thing was that the sky had already numbered the wood.
