# 9. Five Hundred Feet at Mach Three

Every weapon system contains an image of the world it expects to survive.

SLAM's image was low and fast.

Later retellings love the spectacle: a huge nuclear-powered missile at roughly Mach 3, down near the ground, carrying multiple warheads and continuing on after releases. Some accounts add a secondary terror—that shock waves, reactor exhaust or eventual impact would themselves become weapons.

The responsible way to write this is to separate design intent from folklore.

The low-altitude supersonic mission is well documented. So is the open-cycle reactor concept. So are multi-target payload studies. The exact radiological consequences of a complete operational flight depend on reactor behavior, fission-product retention, mission duration, altitude and failure mode. Those questions should be calculated from program data, not inherited from adjectives like 'flying Chernobyl.'

The temptation to exaggerate is unnecessary. The documented system is strange enough.

At low altitude, speed converts terrain and atmosphere into adversaries. Pressure fluctuations matter. Weather matters. Thermal loads matter. The navigation system must command a vehicle with almost no time margin. The airframe has to absorb prolonged punishment that an ICBM avoids by spending most of its trajectory where the atmosphere is thin or absent.

The mission also creates political geography. A strategic missile cannot casually irradiate friendly territory on its way to the enemy. Flight-test corridors become diplomatic problems. Abort logic becomes a nightmare because the propulsion system is itself hazardous.

That is a recurring pattern in extreme weapons: operational flexibility looks large on a range diagram and shrinks when one draws the real map.

Where can it launch?

Where can it turn?

Where can it be tested?

Where can it fail?

Where can it be recovered?

Where can it never go in peacetime?

The most advanced thing about a weapon may be its engine, but the most decisive thing may be the geography of its embarrassment.
