# 11. The Requirement Disappears

Weapons are built to requirements, but requirements age faster than hardware.

The Pluto reactor was born into the late 1950s, when the future balance among bombers, cruise missiles, ballistic missiles and defenses was still unsettled. Its extreme endurance and low-altitude penetration looked like answers to a strategic problem worth solving.

By 1964 the problem had changed shape.

Intercontinental ballistic missiles had matured. Submarine-launched ballistic missiles created survivable second-strike options. Warheads became lighter. Guidance improved. The strategic value of carrying a very large payload for a long, circuitous, low-altitude mission had to be compared against systems that could arrive much faster and were already becoming central to deterrence.

Livermore's retrospective says Pluto was halted for lack of a firm military commitment. A contemporary July 1964 Air Force magazine article captured the uncertainty in real time: Pluto had a strong technical record but faced the political and budgetary jump from ground technology program to an expensive flight-test and weapons-system commitment.

This is the cruel moment in advanced development.

Before the requirement disappears, every technical success increases the case for continuing. After the requirement disappears, technical success can become evidence that the research phase has done its job and should stop.

The same test result can support opposite program decisions depending on the strategic denominator.

There is a popular mythology that bureaucracies never cancel anything that works. Pluto is one of many counterexamples. Institutions can be wasteful, self-protective and captured by sunk costs; they can also terminate technically successful work because another system has changed the opportunity cost.

For readers interested in alleged secret programs, this matters enormously. The existence of a black research effort does not imply permanent deployment. States explore technologies they later abandon. They preserve expertise. They restart ideas. They sometimes classify failures longer than outsiders expect. A program can be both real and dead.

That category—real and dead—deserves more space in our imagination.
