# 12. When Technical Success Loses

The easiest way to misunderstand Pluto is to say it was cancelled because it was too horrible.

The second easiest way is to say it was cancelled because ICBMs made it obsolete.

Both statements compress a systems decision into a slogan.

Environmental and radiological consequences mattered. Flight testing was difficult. Strategic alternatives were improving. The military requirement was uncertain. A full development program would be expensive. The system's advantages had to justify not just its own disadvantages but the existence of competitors already attracting doctrine, infrastructure and confidence.

There is rarely one cause when a large program dies.

The useful question is: what changed the sign of the expected value?

Suppose the reactor had failed. Cancellation becomes easy. Suppose ballistic missiles had failed instead. Pluto's ugly externalities might have looked more tolerable. Suppose the reactor had been shieldable at low mass. The flight-test problem changes. Suppose air defense had made ballistic trajectories uniquely vulnerable. Low-altitude persistence might regain value.

Program outcomes are conditional, not moral verdicts on technologies.

This is why historical analogies need discipline. Pluto cannot prove that today's nuclear-powered cruise missiles are useless. Modern materials, navigation, modeling and strategic defenses differ. Nor can a modern program prove Pluto was secretly wise all along. Each system exists inside a different threat model.

What Pluto does provide is a map of categories.

Physics.

Materials.

Manufacturing.

Integrated testing.

Safety.

Basing.

Doctrine.

Arms-race response.

Alternatives.

Political tolerance.

The decisive failure can migrate among them while the machine itself improves.

That is a more interesting story than technological destiny because it admits something engineers know and futurists hate: there is no single scoreboard.
