# 16. Secrecy Has Mass

We talk about classification as if it were a cloak.

A better metaphor is weight.

Every additional person who must know increases security burden. Every specialized facility creates access patterns. Every test creates telemetry. Every dangerous material creates accounting. Every contractor creates interfaces. Every failure creates an investigation. Every operational unit creates training. Secrecy can organize these things, fragment them and punish disclosure. It cannot make them weightless.

Pluto was classified during development and still required a large physical world.

This is why claims of indefinitely hidden technology should be evaluated by scale, not by disbelief.

A one-off laboratory sample can hide easily.

A small compartmented prototype can hide surprisingly well.

A fleet cannot hide in the same way.

A fleet needs spares.

A fleet needs maintenance.

A fleet produces accidents and careers and training syllabi and budgets and retired people who remember oddly specific annoyances.

The probability of public residue rises with duration, scale, hazard and operational integration. That is not a law of nature, but it is a useful prior.

The inverse matters too. Public residue can exist without proving the sensational interpretation placed on it. A strange procurement line may support an ordinary classified sensor. A secure hangar may house foreign hardware rather than alien hardware. A nuclear specialist at a missile range may be working on power, instrumentation or safety rather than a particular rumored engine.

Secrecy analysis therefore needs two disciplines at once: expect residue, and refuse to over-label residue.

Pluto is unusually generous because history eventually supplied both the weird object and the boring explanation. We know why the rails were there.
