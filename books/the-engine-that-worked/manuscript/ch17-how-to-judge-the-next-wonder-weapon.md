# 17. How to Judge the Next Wonder Weapon

The next wonder weapon will arrive as a sentence.

Someone will say a state has a propulsion system with effectively unlimited range, a craft that crosses atmosphere and ocean without meaningful drag, a material that changes inertia, a directed-energy device with impossible efficiency, or a recovered machine whose principles sit outside current engineering.

Do not begin by deciding whether the speaker is a fool or a prophet.

Begin by separating gates.

**Gate one: effect.** What physical phenomenon is actually claimed? What measurement would distinguish it from artifact, misinterpretation or known physics?

**Gate two: device.** Has the effect been embodied in hardware that operates outside one fragile laboratory configuration?

**Gate three: integration.** Can the device survive inside a vehicle or weapon with power, control, structure, thermal management and safety constraints?

**Gate four: testing.** Where and how could the integrated system be tested? What signatures would that create?

**Gate five: mission.** What operational problem does the system solve better than existing alternatives?

**Gate six: organization.** What people, facilities, suppliers, clearances, maintenance and training does sustained use require?

**Gate seven: consequence.** If the system works, what should change downstream—doctrine, adversary behavior, procurement, accidents, tactics, arms control?

Project Pluto passed enough gates to make the later gates visible. That is its value.

A patent rarely gets past gate one. A witness may provide evidence relevant to gates two or three. A declassified budget can establish organizational interest without demonstrating technical success. A crash can prove hardware existed without proving the intended mission. A test can demonstrate performance without proving deployment.

The method is annoyingly incremental.

Good.

Reality is under no obligation to be dramatic at the same speed as our stories.
