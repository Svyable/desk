# 3. Jackass Flats Builds a Wind Tunnel for a Reactor

There are facilities that explain a program better than its mission statement.

Site 401 at Jackass Flats is one of them.

The nuclear ramjet could not be evaluated by putting a prototype on a runway and hoping the neighbors were relaxed about it. The test problem had to be inverted. Instead of moving the reactor through air at supersonic speed, engineers would keep the reactor fixed and move the air through it.

That sounds ordinary until the scale appears.

Nevada histories describe a dedicated Pluto complex with control and maintenance areas, a test bunker, a disassembly building, remote operations and the machinery needed to deliver heated compressed air. One official history notes that for a five-minute full-power test, roughly a ton of air per second was forced through the engine and that the air-storage system used extraordinary lengths of oil-well casing. The facility itself became a temporary atmosphere.

This is the chapter of the book I wish people carried into every argument about secret engineering.

We have a cultural preference for the object. The aircraft. The missile. The recovered fragment. The prototype under a tarp. But large technical claims are often easier to test by looking sideways at the support world they require.

A high-power reactor test requires instrumentation. An irradiated reactor requires a post-test plan. High-temperature airflow requires energy before it ever reaches the reactor. Remote control requires channels, redundancy and stand-off distance. Dangerous disassembly requires shielding, manipulators, procedures and waste handling. None of these is secondary. They are the program.

Tory II-A's 1959 design report explicitly discussed the test locale, program and possible hazards. Instrumentation reports describe a control room roughly two miles from the unshielded reactor and the transmission of measurements from the test bunker. Those details are stronger evidence than almost any retrospective adjective because they are the unromantic commitments a real test forces people to make in advance.

Secrecy changes how we see such commitments. It can hide the name on the door. It can bury a budget line. It can compartment personnel. It can classify performance. But if a system requires large-scale physical testing, secrecy becomes a design constraint of its own. You need remoteness. You need cover stories or restricted land. You need transport patterns. You need environmental monitoring. You need people who know only their slice and a smaller number who know how the slices connect.

This is not an argument that every secret program becomes easily visible. The Manhattan Project is the obvious warning against laziness here: enormous programs can remain strategically secret while thousands of people contribute. But Manhattan also demonstrates the other half of the rule. Once historians knew where to look, the physical and administrative residue was immense.

Pluto has the same quality at a smaller scale. The reactor reports do not tell us merely that smart people had a frightening idea. They tell us how thick the walls had to become, how air was conditioned, how controls were arranged, how a hot machine would be approached after it ran.

The facility is the negative image of the technology.

You can infer the beast from the cage.
