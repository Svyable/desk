# Research Trail — Motivate & Incentivize

This folder is the evidence trail for *Motivate & Incentivize*.

The book investigates how incentives shape effort, attention, meaning, selection, gaming, culture, and long-run behavior. It should resist two equally easy stories: that people are simple economic actors who reliably respond to prices, and that external rewards inevitably poison intrinsic motivation. Both claims are too broad. The evidence is more useful when the task, measurement, context, and type of reward stay visible.

## Evidence rules

1. Separate **motivation**, **incentive**, **measurement**, **ability**, and **opportunity**. A change in observed behavior does not by itself reveal which mechanism changed.
2. Treat incentives as conditional. Ask what behavior is rewarded, how the reward is perceived, what can be measured, what cannot, and what happens after the reward is removed.
3. Distinguish **quantity** from **quality**. A system can increase output while degrading judgment, service, safety, cooperation, or work that is difficult to count.
4. Treat vivid cases as mechanisms, not prevalence estimates. Wells Fargo can demonstrate how sales pressure and targets interact with organizational controls; it cannot prove that sales incentives generally cause fraud.
5. Preserve positive evidence for incentives. Piece rates, performance pay, commitment devices, and well-designed rewards can improve effort or output in many settings.
6. Preserve crowding-out boundary conditions. Do not imply that all rewards reduce intrinsic motivation. The empirical literature finds important differences by task, baseline interest, reward type, performance criterion, and context.
7. Track **selection effects** as well as effort effects. Compensation systems may change who joins, stays, leaves, or competes for a role.
8. When discussing gaming, distinguish deliberate cheating from rational adaptation to a proxy. A system can produce distorted behavior even when every participant follows the written rule.
9. Prefer primary sources for organizational scandals, enforcement actions, compensation systems, and public claims. Prefer original papers, meta-analyses, or serious reviews for empirical claims.
10. Record what would weaken the book's argument. The manuscript should change if a claimed backfire effect fails to replicate, depends on a narrow population, or disappears under a better design.

## Chapter 1 source spine

### Wells Fargo sales practices

- U.S. Department of Justice, February 21, 2020, settlement and statement-of-facts summary: https://www.justice.gov/usao-cdca/pr/wells-fargo-agrees-pay-3-billion-resolve-criminal-and-civil-investigations-sales
- Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2016 enforcement action: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/wells-fargo-bank-2016/
- Federal Reserve, February 2, 2018 governance and growth-restriction action: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/enforcement20180202a.htm

The DOJ account is especially useful because Wells Fargo admitted that its Community Bank used a volume-based sales model, employees were pressured by onerous sales goals, and management knew that "gaming" practices were increasing. The case should be written as an interaction of targets, pressure, controls, leadership, and measurement—not as proof that compensation alone caused the misconduct.

### Incentives can improve performance

- Edward P. Lazear, "Performance Pay and Productivity," NBER Working Paper 5672, 1996; later published in *American Economic Review* (2000): https://www.nber.org/papers/w5672
- Edward P. Lazear, "Output-based Pay: Incentives or Sorting?", NBER Working Paper 7419, 1999: https://www.nber.org/papers/w7419

Lazear's autoglass study is important counterweight. The move from hourly wages to piece rates was associated with a large productivity increase, with evidence consistent with both greater effort and worker sorting. The book should not flatten this into "bonuses work"; the measured output in that setting was unusually close to the job's productive task.

### Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives

- Christopher P. Cerasoli, Jessica M. Nicklin, and Michael T. Ford, "Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives jointly predict performance: a 40-year meta-analysis," *Psychological Bulletin* 140(4), 2014: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/
- Edward L. Deci, Richard Koestner, and Richard M. Ryan, "A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation," *Psychological Bulletin* 125, 1999. Bibliographic entry and related materials: https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/research/meta-analysis/
- Marianne Promberger and Theresa M. Marteau, "When do financial incentives reduce intrinsic motivation?", *Health Psychology* 32(9), 2013: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24001245/

Cerasoli et al. provide a useful organizing result: intrinsic motivation and incentives can jointly predict performance, with intrinsic motivation more strongly associated with quality and incentives more strongly associated with quantity in their meta-analytic models. Promberger and Marteau are useful for resisting overgeneralization of crowding-out claims across different behaviors.

### When a price changes the meaning of a norm

- Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini, "Pay Enough or Don't Pay at All," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 115(3), 2000: https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/115/3/791/1828156

The famous daycare-fine result belongs in the book because it shows that an incentive can alter the frame of a decision rather than simply add a cost. It should not be treated as a universal law of fines or as evidence that penalties generally increase bad behavior.

### Multitasking and proxy distortion

- Bengt Holmstrom and Paul Milgrom, "Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design," *Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization* 7, 1991: https://www.jstor.org/stable/764957

This is the theoretical backbone for the problem of high-powered incentives applied to one measurable dimension of work when other valuable dimensions are hard to observe.

## Research lanes

### Compensation and sales

Develop cases where commissions, quotas, bonuses, rankings, or tournaments changed both effort and the definition of success. Candidate domains include banking, insurance, brokerage, retail, call centers, auto sales, and executive compensation. Avoid a parade of scandals; select cases that reveal distinct mechanisms.

### Schools and measurement

Study standardized testing, teaching to the test, attendance targets, graduation metrics, and performance-based funding. Separate evidence that accountability improved outcomes from cases where proxies were gamed or narrowed instruction.

### Health

Study incentives for smoking cessation, vaccination, medication adherence, exercise, screening, and provider reimbursement. Health is useful because it makes clear that a reward can help a person do something they already want to do but struggle to sustain.

### Status and non-cash rewards

Money is only one incentive. Titles, rankings, prestige, visibility, badges, scarce access, social proof, public recognition, and promotion opportunities can shape behavior just as strongly and often more opaquely.

### Platforms

Investigate how engagement metrics become incentives for creators, employees, advertisers, and recommendation systems. Distinguish a platform's stated social goal from the objective functions used to allocate attention.

### AI and reward functions

Use reinforcement learning and specification gaming carefully as a late-book mirror of the human problem. The machine case is valuable because it strips away moral language: an optimizer does not need greed, laziness, or malice to exploit a badly specified reward. Avoid claiming humans and machine-learning systems are psychologically equivalent.

## Falsification questions

- When do incentives improve both quantity and quality rather than forcing a tradeoff?
- How often does crowding out persist after the reward is removed?
- Are apparent incentive failures actually failures of supervision, ethics, training, or selection?
- Does the book confuse the metric with the incentive? A metric can distort behavior without money attached to it.
- Are cases of gaming representative, or selected because failure is more newsworthy than ordinary success?
- Does a proposed fix merely add another metric that can itself be gamed?
- When is a simple, high-powered incentive better than a nuanced system because complexity makes the rules illegible?
- How much behavior is driven by status, identity, norms, and career concern rather than explicit compensation?
- When does external reward enable autonomy by helping people overcome friction or self-control problems?
- What evidence would show that a system is aligned with its purpose even when participants become highly skilled at optimizing it?
