# Book Brief — Motivate & Incentivize

## Canonical identity

**Title:** *Motivate & Incentivize*

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson

**Status:** Drafting

## Central question

How do people design incentives that produce the behavior they genuinely want rather than increasingly sophisticated performance against a proxy?

## Reader promise

The reader should finish the book able to look at a workplace, market, school, platform, family rule, compensation plan, public policy, or algorithm and ask better questions about why behavior is moving.

The book will distinguish the internal reasons people act from the external conditions that change the payoff of acting. It will show why that distinction matters, why rewards sometimes increase effort cleanly, why they sometimes narrow attention or alter meaning, why measurable work attracts stronger incentives than important but invisible work, and why people learn the rules of a system faster than its designers expect.

The practical promise is not a universal reward formula. It is a way to see an incentive system before its side effects become culture.

## Distinct contribution

There are excellent books on intrinsic motivation, behavioral economics, habits, organizational culture, compensation, nudges, goals, and management. This book's contribution is to place them inside one operating problem: an incentive is never merely a payment. It is part of an information system.

It tells the participant what is being counted. It changes where attention goes. It can change who chooses to participate. It can signal trust or distrust. It can convert a social norm into a transaction. It can make a difficult but valuable behavior easier to sustain. It can also teach a person how to satisfy a metric while abandoning the purpose that gave the metric meaning.

The book then carries that logic into artificial systems. Reward hacking in machine learning is not proof that people are machines. It is a cleaner demonstration of an old design error: the optimizer receives the objective that was specified, not the intention that lived in the designer's head.

## Competing explanation

The strongest competing explanation is that the incentive problem is often overcomplicated.

Prices work. Piece rates raise output. Commissions sell products. Bonuses focus effort. Deadlines create action. Penalties deter behavior. Organizations need measurable goals because unmeasured purpose is difficult to manage. Many stories about "crowding out" are narrow, context-dependent, or overstated when translated into management folklore.

The book should treat that position as a serious constraint. It should not become an argument that money is corrupting, that intrinsic motivation is always superior, or that measurement is the enemy. The sharper thesis is conditional: the more incomplete the measure, the more multidimensional the work, and the more meaning or trust matters to performance, the more carefully the incentive must be designed and interpreted.

## Narrative engine

Begin chapters with a real system under pressure: a sales quota, piece-rate change, daycare fine, school target, compensation plan, ranking, app metric, medical payment rule, or reward function.

The recurring tension is the distance between the stated purpose and the behavior the system makes locally rational.

Do not populate every chapter with a new morality play. Incentive design is interesting precisely because many failures emerge without bad intentions and many simple incentives work extremely well. Let the evidence decide which kind of story each chapter earns.

## Chapter architecture

### Part I — What Makes People Move

**1. The Number Becomes the Job**  
Wells Fargo as the opening case. Distinguish purpose, motivation, incentive, target, metric, and control. Introduce the central problem: a score can begin by measuring work and end by defining it.

**2. Wanting and Working**  
Intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, effort, ability, opportunity, and performance. Use the empirical literature to dismantle the false choice between "people need purpose" and "people respond to rewards."

**3. The Price of a Favor**  
The daycare fine and other norm-to-market transitions. When adding a price changes the meaning of the act.

**4. Paid by the Piece**  
Lazear's autoglass evidence and the cases where strong incentives work. Output, effort, sorting, and why clean measurement changes the design problem.

### Part II — The Proxy Problem

**5. What the Score Cannot See**  
Multitask principal-agent theory. The measurable task receives attention; quality, maintenance, cooperation, safety, mentoring, or judgment may not.

**6. The Target Learns to Lie**  
Gaming, threshold effects, bunching, quota timing, sandbagging, and the difference between fraud and rational adaptation to a rule.

**7. Quantity Is Not Quality**  
Use the motivation-performance literature to examine why incentives may be more predictive of quantity while intrinsic motivation matters more for quality.

**8. The Ratchet**  
What happens when today's performance becomes tomorrow's quota. Strategic underperformance, budget games, forecast padding, and the fear of teaching management what is possible.

**9. When the Metric Becomes Identity**  
Rankings, leaderboards, grades, titles, prestige, and social comparison. Non-cash incentives can be stronger than cash precisely because they are harder to price.

### Part III — Organizations Teach

**10. Commission Changes the Customer**  
Sales systems as a design laboratory. Who is the customer when the rep is paid by the product, the account, the margin, the retention rate, or the customer's lifetime value?

**11. The Bonus Is a Message**  
An incentive says something about trust, priorities, and what management believes people would do without it. Study the signal alongside the money.

**12. Culture Is the Unwritten Contract**  
Formal compensation meets informal norms. What teams praise, tolerate, hide, and punish can overwhelm the official plan.

**13. Incentives Select People**  
Compensation changes the workforce, not only the worker. Sorting, self-selection, retention, tournament entry, and the long-run culture created by who finds the game attractive.

**14. Friction Pays Too**  
Defaults, waiting time, convenience, forms, scheduling, access, and other non-monetary features that change behavior by changing the effort price.

### Part IV — Systems That Optimize

**15. Schools, Hospitals, and Other Difficult Scoreboards**  
Public-service domains where the desired outcome matters enormously and the proxy is incomplete. Examine both accountability gains and distortion.

**16. Engagement Is an Incentive**  
Platforms create reward systems for users, creators, employees, and algorithms. Follow how one metric can propagate through an ecosystem and alter what gets produced.

**17. Reward Functions**  
Reinforcement learning, specification gaming, and reward hacking as a mirror of incentive design. The optimizer does not need malicious intent to discover the loophole.

**18. Design for What You Actually Want**  
Return to the opening problem with a practical discipline: define the purpose, identify the proxy, map what can be gamed or ignored, test selection effects, watch for meaning changes, and keep enough judgment outside the metric to correct the system when reality diverges.

## Recurring distinctions

Keep these visible without turning them into slogans:

- motivation / incentive
- target / purpose
- metric / outcome
- effort / performance
- quantity / quality
- price / norm
- reward effect / selection effect
- compliance / commitment
- explicit pay / status reward
- intended behavior / locally rational behavior
- measurement error / deliberate gaming
- short-run response / long-run culture

## Practical device worth earning

A single recurring device may be useful: an **incentive audit**.

It should not appear as a prefabricated seven-step framework in Chapter 1. The questions should accumulate from the cases until the reader can ask them naturally:

What is the real outcome? What proxy is being rewarded? What behavior becomes locally rational? What valuable work is invisible? Who does the system attract or repel? What social meaning does the reward introduce? How would a smart participant game the rule without technically breaking it? What happens after the reward disappears?

## Commercial positioning

Primary audience: managers, founders, sales leaders, product builders, economists, operators, educators, policy readers, and curious people who have watched a metric take over a system.

The book should sit comfortably near work on motivation, behavioral economics, organizational design, and technology without becoming another business fable or a catalog of cognitive biases. *Primed to Perform*, *Drive*, *Nudge*, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, *Predictably Irrational*, and serious work on principal-agent problems are nearby shelves, not templates.

The title is a command pair, which gives it energy. The manuscript earns that energy by refusing the usual promise that there is one correct way to motivate people. The point is to make the reader dangerous to bad incentive design.
