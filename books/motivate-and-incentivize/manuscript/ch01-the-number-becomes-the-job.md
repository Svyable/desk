# The Number Becomes the Job

In 2012, Wells Fargo published a sentence about selling that almost nobody could object to: “We start with what the customer needs – not with what we want to sell them.”

It was the kind of line that belongs in a values document because it sounds less like a strategy than a promise. A bank exists because customers need somewhere to keep money, borrow money, move money, protect money, and plan around it. Start there. Understand the need. Offer the useful product. The sentence made the moral logic and the commercial logic appear to be the same thing.

Inside the Community Bank, another logic was running.

Beginning years earlier, Wells Fargo had placed increasing emphasis on sales volume and cross-selling additional products to existing customers. The Department of Justice would later describe a volume-based sales model in which employees were pushed to sell large numbers of products, often without much regard for whether customers needed or would use them. The sales goals were not a decorative management device. They organized the work. Managers pressed employees to hit them. Employees learned ways to hit them. Some of those methods were called “gaming” inside the bank.

The word is almost too revealing.

Games have rules, points, boundaries, penalties, loopholes, and a definition of winning. Once a person knows the game, intelligence moves toward the score.

According to the Justice Department’s 2020 account of the case, which incorporated admissions by Wells Fargo, the pressure around sales goals contributed to conduct that included accounts opened without customer authorization, false records, unauthorized debit cards, and other practices that produced the appearance of sales without the underlying customer demand. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau had already described the role of sales targets and compensation incentives in its 2016 enforcement action. The government record is extensive enough that the broad outline no longer depends on corporate folklore or an ex-employee’s impression. The system generated a number. People learned to generate the number.

The easiest way to tell this story is as a story about greed.

That is also the least useful way.

Greed explains too much and therefore explains very little. It can be attached after the fact to almost any scandal involving money. It does not tell us why one compensation plan produces ordinary selling while another produces systematic pressure to manufacture accounts. It does not explain why misconduct can persist after internal warnings. It does not explain why intelligent managers can look at evidence of gaming and still preserve the system that created the incentive to game.

More importantly, greed invites the reader to stand comfortably outside the case.

Bad people did bad things for money.

Fine. Close the file.

The harder fact is that organizations build small games every day, usually for reasonable reasons. A chief executive cannot stand beside every employee. A sales leader cannot listen to every conversation. A board cannot observe every judgment call. A school district cannot sit in every classroom. A hospital administrator cannot watch every treatment decision. A parent cannot follow a teenager through every choice. A software platform cannot ask a billion users whether each recommendation improved their lives.

So people reach for a proxy.

Accounts opened. Revenue booked. Calls handled. Tests passed. Cases closed. Procedures completed. Minutes watched. Miles driven. Packages delivered. Bugs fixed. Papers published.

A proxy makes invisible work legible.

That is useful. It is also the beginning of the problem.

The number starts as a measurement of the job. Then compensation is attached to it. Promotion is attached to it. Shame is attached to it. Meetings begin with it. Dashboards turn red or green around it. People learn which number gets a manager off their back and which number makes a manager call. Eventually the measurement acquires gravity.

The number becomes the job.

That sentence needs qualification because it is tempting to turn every target into a villain. Targets can be excellent. A warehouse that cannot measure throughput does not become humane; it becomes blind. A salesperson who receives no feedback on whether customers buy anything is not liberated from capitalism; the salesperson is unemployed. A factory that refuses to count defects because quality is multidimensional will produce a philosophical defense of broken products.

Measurement is not the enemy.

Neither is money.

In one of the best-known studies of performance pay, economist Edward Lazear examined a large auto-glass company that changed installers from hourly pay to piece rates. Productivity rose substantially. The exact estimate depends on the comparison, but the study found effects large enough to matter commercially and evidence that two mechanisms were operating at once: existing workers changed their effort, and the compensation system changed which workers the company attracted and retained.

The incentive did not corrupt the work. It helped organize it.

That should bother anyone who wants a simple theory.

If the Wells Fargo case proves that incentives are dangerous, the auto-glass case proves that incentives are powerful. Those are not opposite conclusions. They are the same conclusion viewed from different tasks.

The design question is what the incentive makes worth doing.

Installing another windshield is close to the productive output an auto-glass company actually wants. Opening another bank account is only useful if the account belongs to a customer who wants or needs it. The difference is small enough to disappear on a spreadsheet and large enough to ruin a bank.

A metric can be beautifully correlated with the purpose until people begin optimizing the metric.

That is the central tension of this book.

I want to keep three words separate from the beginning: motivation, incentive, and measurement.

Motivation is about why a person acts. The reasons can be mixed. Money can motivate. Pride can motivate. Curiosity, fear, loyalty, status, boredom, habit, competition, guilt, craft, anger, ambition, love, and the desire to be left alone by a manager can all move behavior. People regularly carry several of these at once, sometimes pointed in different directions.

An incentive changes the consequences attached to a choice. Do this and get paid. Miss this and lose the bonus. Finish by Friday and go home. Arrive late and pay a fine. Reach this rank and gain access. Refer a friend and receive credit. Publish enough and keep the job. Increase engagement and get promoted.

Measurement determines what the system can see.

The three interact, but they are not interchangeable. A person can be deeply motivated and face no explicit incentive. A person can face a powerful incentive and feel little internal motivation. An organization can measure something without attaching money to it and still change behavior simply because visibility creates status, comparison, or fear. A poorly measured incentive can motivate enormous effort toward the wrong thing.

This distinction matters because managers often ask a motivational question when they have an incentive problem.

How do we get people to care more?

Sometimes the people care plenty.

A nurse may care intensely about patient health while working inside a reimbursement system that rewards certain procedures more reliably than prevention. A teacher may care about learning while facing a test score that determines how the school is judged. A salesperson may care about customers and still know that the monthly review will be conducted in units, not in relationships. A researcher may care about truth while understanding that publication count is visible and careful negative results are harder to place.

The person does not have to stop caring for the incentive to win.

The incentive only has to become the binding constraint.

That phrase—binding constraint—is useful because it shifts the question away from character. Suppose an employee needs the job, the promotion path depends on a metric, the manager discusses the metric daily, colleagues compete on the metric, and missing the target invites formal consequences. The employee may still believe in the larger mission. Belief does not erase the local price of disobedience.

An organization can therefore contain two value systems at once.

The stated value system lives in the mission statement, orientation deck, annual report, and town hall.

The operational value system lives in the loop between behavior and consequence.

People notice the second one quickly.

Wells Fargo’s stated customer principle and its sales machinery are a particularly stark example because the conflict became legally consequential and publicly documented. But quieter versions appear wherever management says one thing and repeatedly rewards another.

“Take your time and do it right” loses force when the person who ships fastest gets praised every week.

“We value collaboration” becomes strange when promotion depends entirely on individual production.

“Speak up” acquires a footnote when the people who raise problems stop appearing in important meetings.

“Think long term” is difficult to hear over a bonus calculated every quarter.

Nobody needs to announce that the real priorities have changed. The feedback loop does the announcing.

This is one reason incentive systems are also information systems. They tell participants what the institution is willing to count as evidence of value.

That information can be more credible than a speech.

There is a second complication. Incentives do not merely add motivation to a fixed human being. They can change the meaning of the action.

A famous field experiment by economists Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini studied day-care centers that wanted parents to stop arriving late. The centers introduced a monetary fine for lateness. Standard intuition says the cost of arriving late has increased, so lateness should decrease.

It increased.

The result has been retold so many times that it is easy to turn it into a cute behavioral-economics party trick: fine parents, get more lateness. But the deeper point is not that fines usually backfire. They do not. A large enough fine attached to a well-enforced rule can be highly discouraging.

The interesting part is what a price can do to a norm.

Before the fine, lateness imposed a social cost. A parent was keeping another person waiting. There was ambiguity around the obligation and, with it, room for guilt, courtesy, embarrassment, or reciprocity. The fine did not simply increase the cost. It also clarified the transaction. Lateness now had a number attached.

The parent could pay.

The system had converted at least part of a social relationship into a market relationship.

This is why incentives are never only arithmetic. They are interpreted.

A bonus can say, “This work matters.”

It can also say, “We do not think you would do this unless we paid extra.”

A prize can recognize mastery.

It can also turn play into evaluation.

A fine can communicate a boundary.

It can also communicate a price.

The same payment can land differently depending on what the person believed the relationship was before the payment arrived.

Psychologists have spent decades arguing about the relationship between intrinsic motivation and external rewards, and the popular version of that argument is usually worse than the literature. The slogan says rewards destroy intrinsic motivation. The evidence says something more conditional.

A 2014 meta-analysis by Christopher Cerasoli, Jessica Nicklin, and Michael Ford combined research across school, work, and physical domains and found that intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives both predicted performance. They were not simple enemies. Intrinsic motivation was more strongly related to the quality of performance, while incentives were more strongly related to quantity in the models the authors examined.

That distinction feels immediately recognizable in the Wells Fargo case, but it should not be abused.

The study does not say that paying for quantity automatically destroys quality. It does not say intrinsic motivation produces beautiful work and incentives produce junk. It says the relationship between motivation, incentives, and performance depends in part on what kind of performance is being asked for and how the reward is tied to it.

That is enough to make one managerial habit look suspicious: the search for a single number that can carry a multidimensional job.

Most jobs contain work that is easy to count and work that is expensive to count.

The easy work wins.

Consider a manager who rewards a customer-support team for reducing average call time. The metric is defensible. Customers dislike waiting. Labor is expensive. Long calls may indicate poor training, clumsy software, or unresolved processes. Average call time can reveal real operational problems.

Then the reward arrives.

Now the worker faces a slightly different problem. The goal is no longer merely to help the customer efficiently. The worker is being evaluated on the duration of the call. If the system does not equally capture whether the problem was actually solved, whether the customer had to call back, whether a vulnerable customer was rushed, or whether the agent quietly transferred difficult cases, the incentive has created a shadow price for those invisible outcomes.

The agent does not need to cheat.

The agent only needs to learn.

People are very good at learning what a system truly notices.

Economists Bengt Holmstrom and Paul Milgrom gave formal shape to this problem in their work on multitask principal-agent relationships. When a job requires several activities and some are easier to measure than others, strong incentives on the measurable activity can pull effort away from valuable work that the contract cannot observe well.

The theory is dry. The lived version is everywhere.

A police department can count arrests more easily than trust.

A university can count papers more easily than intellectual influence.

A company can count new customers more easily than whether salespeople are creating future regret.

A software team can count tickets closed more easily than architectural judgment.

A newspaper can count clicks more easily than civic value.

None of these examples proves that the easy metric should be abandoned. The point is that measurement changes the shape of attention.

Once compensation or status is attached, the shape becomes sharper.

The system designer now faces an unpleasant choice. Use a weak incentive and perhaps fail to focus effort. Use a strong incentive and perhaps cause the proxy to swallow the purpose.

There is no universal answer because the quality of the proxy varies.

This is where many discussions about motivation float away from the actual work. They become sermons about purpose. Give people autonomy. Inspire them. Build culture. Make the mission matter.

All useful.

All incomplete.

Purpose does not unload a truck.

Meaning does not calculate payroll.

Autonomy does not tell a person which of twelve urgent tasks should happen first.

Organizations need priorities, constraints, feedback, and consequences. People often want them. Clear goals can reduce uncertainty. A bonus can make an otherwise unrewarded burden worth carrying. A deadline can rescue a project from perfectionism. A commitment device can help a person act in line with an intention that loses every nightly argument with the sofa.

The problem is not that external incentives contaminate some pure human state.

Human beings arrive contaminated.

They want money and meaning. Status and autonomy. Security and freedom. Praise and privacy. They can love the work and resent the job. They can care about the customer and care about making quota before Friday.

The design problem begins there, in the mixture.

It gets harder over time because an incentive system does not merely shape the people already inside it. It can shape who enters.

Return to Lazear’s auto-glass company. Part of the productivity change after piece rates was associated with sorting: the compensation regime affected the workforce. That is obvious once stated. A job with uncapped commission attracts a different distribution of people than the same job with a stable salary. A company that celebrates internal tournaments will retain some competitors and exhaust some cooperators. A research lab that rewards publication volume may attract people comfortable with a high-output race. A nonprofit that pays below-market wages while emphasizing mission will filter for people able and willing to accept that trade, which is not always the same as filtering for the people best at the work.

An incentive therefore has a biography.

At first it changes a decision.

Then it changes habits.

Then people learn strategies.

Then managers write processes around the strategies.

Then new employees enter a culture already shaped by the game.

Eventually the organization can forget that another game was possible.

This is why the Wells Fargo story extends beyond the existence of a bonus or a sales goal. The public record describes pressure, management response, internal awareness, quality problems, and years in which the system continued despite warning signs. In 2018, the Federal Reserve took the extraordinary step of restricting the bank’s growth until governance and risk-management deficiencies were improved. The problem had outgrown a compensation spreadsheet. It had become organizational.

Incentives are often discussed as if management designs them and employees receive them.

In practice, participants redesign them from below.

They discover edge cases.

They coordinate.

They learn which manager enforces which rule.

They find out whether a threshold is real.

They shift activity across reporting periods.

They rename behavior.

They create workarounds that become customs.

They figure out what can be technically compliant while defeating the purpose.

This adaptation is usually described as gaming when management dislikes the result.

Sometimes it is fraud.

Sometimes it is intelligence.

That distinction matters.

If a rule says a salesperson gets credit for an account opened, and the organization later becomes furious that salespeople focus on opening accounts rather than building relationships, the salesperson may have done exactly what the system asked. The moral failure, if there is one, may live in conduct around the sale. The design failure lives earlier, in the assumption that an opened account was a sufficiently complete representation of a good customer outcome.

People do not game a perfect measure.

There are no perfect measures.

The practical implication is uncomfortable because it removes the fantasy of the finished system. You cannot design the correct incentive once and then admire it. The act of introducing the incentive changes behavior, which changes the informational value of the metric, which changes what the incentive is actually paying for.

The system has to be watched while people learn it.

That requires judgment.

Judgment is expensive, inconsistent, difficult to scale, and resistant to dashboards, which is why organizations keep trying to automate it away.

They never fully succeed.

The manager eventually has to ask whether the account is useful, whether the customer was helped, whether the student learned, whether the patient improved, whether the employee created durable value, whether the click represented interest or compulsion, whether the model found the solution or found the loophole.

Those questions arrive after the number because the number cannot answer them alone.

This is not a reason to return to vague supervision and favoritism. The absence of metrics creates its own pathologies. Invisible standards can be arbitrary. Discretion can hide bias. Workers deserve to know how performance is judged. Investors deserve evidence. Customers deserve consistency. Public institutions need accountability.

The answer to a bad scoreboard is not no scoreboard.

It is to remember that the scoreboard is an instrument.

The purpose lives elsewhere.

Wells Fargo is useful at the beginning of this book because the bank put the conflict in writing.

“We start with what the customer needs – not with what we want to sell them.”

That was one instruction.

The sales machinery was another.

Thousands of employees did not need a seminar in corporate philosophy to discover which instruction had teeth.

The consequences told them.

That is the first rule of incentive design worth carrying forward, though I hesitate to call it a rule because exceptions begin immediately: people learn from what happens after the behavior.

They learn what receives money.

They learn what receives status.

They learn what receives attention.

They learn what is forgiven.

They learn what gets somebody promoted.

They learn what ends a meeting.

They learn which red number makes everyone stop talking about the mission.

Then they adapt.

The adaptation can be wonderful. A good incentive can focus energy on a useful constraint and free people from ambiguity. It can reward hard work that had been invisible. It can attract talent. It can move behavior that intention alone could not move.

The adaptation can also be catastrophic.

The difference is rarely captured by asking whether people were motivated.

At Wells Fargo, motivation was not in short supply.

The problem was what the system made worth doing.
