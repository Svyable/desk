# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*The Compatibility Dividend* asks why apparently small agreements about dimensions, identifiers, interfaces, semantics and procedures can create economic effects far larger than the documents that contain them.

The strong claim — that standardization is always beneficial and fragmentation is always waste — is false. Diversity can preserve experimentation, local fit, cultural autonomy and resilience. A dominant standard can freeze weak designs, propagate common-mode failure, exclude entrants or shift power toward whoever controls definition, certification, identity, discovery or distribution.

The narrower claim worth testing is this:

> Standards create economic surplus when they reduce recurring coordination costs at a seam where independent actors need to interoperate, while preserving enough freedom on either side of that seam for competition, adaptation and local choice.

The manuscript calls that surplus the **compatibility dividend**. It is visible in pooled capacity, larger component markets, cheaper substitution, reduced translation, portable meaning, more credible complementary investment and lower permission cost for new entrants.

A second claim follows:

> The most generative standards tend to be narrow at the waist: they standardize the minimum common contract needed for independent systems to meet, while leaving implementation and higher-level purpose plural.

The final chapters extend that logic to autonomous agents. The forward-looking claim is not that one universal agent protocol should govern machine action. It is that machine-to-machine economies will need composable standards for identity, capability discovery, bounded authority, commitment states, payment, provenance, audit, revocation and dispute evidence if autonomous systems are to act across organizational boundaries without being trapped inside one proprietary stack.

## What would weaken the argument?

Evidence that should materially change the book includes:

- industries where bespoke interfaces remain cheaper over long periods than shared standards despite dense cross-organizational interaction;
- cases where forced interoperability consistently reduces welfare because security, performance or integrated innovation losses exceed gains from entry and switching;
- evidence that standards bodies systematically choose inferior designs because consensus processes are worse than proprietary platform governance for the relevant seam;
- cases where translation technology, including AI, makes incompatibility so cheap and reliable that formal shared semantics no longer add meaningful value;
- evidence that open standards regularly increase concentration because complementary economies of scale dominate any reduction in switching or entry cost;
- evidence that agent systems can safely rely on natural-language interpretation for high-consequence authority and commitment without structured permission, provenance or audit layers;
- examples where diverse implementations create more incompatibility and operational risk than the resilience or innovation diversity provides.

Counterexamples should remain in the manuscript rather than being explained away.

## Evidence lanes

### Measurement and metrology

Use BIPM, NIST and CGPM material for the Metre Convention, SI history, current definitions, calibration and traceability. Distinguish a unit definition from the institutional chain that makes measurement comparable in practice.

### Mechanical interoperability

Use NIST historical material on the Great Baltimore Fire and national fire-hose coupling work; historical engineering sources on Whitworth and Sellers thread standards; and Bureau of Standards material on railway gauge. Avoid origin myths where later conventions are falsely projected backward as inevitabilities.

### Time and synchronization

Use NIST and historical railroad sources for the 1883 North American railway-time transition and the later federal standard-time framework. Keep local solar time, railway convention and statutory time distinct.

### Freight and identifiers

Use ISO, maritime history and containerization scholarship for intermodal freight. Use GS1 and retail history for UPC/EAN and product identification. Treat Malcolm McLean as an important entrepreneur, not the sole inventor of every element of containerization.

### Electrical standards

Use IEC historical material and national electrical-safety sources. Avoid implying that one universal household plug was technically inevitable or that adapters alone solve voltage, frequency and safety differences.

### Internet and Web

Prefer primary internet standards: RFCs, IETF process documents, Internet Society history and W3C Recommendations. Distinguish the open protocol layer from concentration in browsers, search, cloud, app stores or identity infrastructure above it.

### Unicode and representation

Use Unicode Consortium histories and standards documentation plus ISO/IEC 10646 material. Preserve controversies over character identity, normalization and Han unification rather than presenting universal encoding as culturally neutral by definition.

### Governance, patents and trade

Use ISO/IEC/ITU/IETF governance documents; WTO Technical Barriers to Trade material; competition-agency work on standards-essential patents; and scholarship on standards capture, consensus and path dependence. Separate openness of a document from openness of implementation, certification and distribution.

### Competition and interoperability

Use primary law and regulator material for the EU Digital Markets Act, UK Open Banking and other mandated-interoperability examples. Test whether interoperability obligations create usable entry paths rather than merely published APIs.

### Agent standards and machine agency

Treat this as time-sensitive. Re-verify current specifications before release. Primary sources currently include Model Context Protocol specification material, A2A 1.x, OAuth security BCPs and W3C Verifiable Credentials 2.0. Distinguish current deployed protocol behavior from the book's proposed future architecture.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary standards, specifications, treaties, regulator decisions, standards-body histories and original scholarship.
- Do not invent quotations, committee dialogue or private motives.
- Separate a standard's technical design from the historical reason it won.
- Distinguish de jure standards, voluntary consensus standards, regulations, platform rules and de facto market standards.
- For claims about network effects and path dependence, preserve disagreement in the economics literature.
- For contemporary standards, record exact specification versions and dates.
- Do not treat publication of a standard as proof of adoption or interoperability.
- Do not equate machine-readable claims with truthful claims; issuer trust remains contextual.
- Do not imply that a cryptographic signature proves legal authority beyond what the signed credential or applicable institution supports.
- Re-check MCP, A2A, OAuth, Verifiable Credentials, DMA and Open Banking facts immediately before a Shelf release.

## What would make the whole book false?

The book's strongest conclusion would weaken substantially if dense modern networks showed little economic benefit from stable shared interfaces once migration, compliance, governance, common-mode risk and innovation costs were fully measured — or if adaptive AI translation made independently specified systems as safe, cheap and substitutable as systems built around explicit shared contracts.

The manuscript should remain open to that possibility. Its claim is not that standards are sacred. It is that compatibility is produced, maintained and governed — and that the terms of that production shape the size and freedom of the systems civilization can build.
