# The Right to Exit

A standard is easiest to admire when it lets things connect.

Its harder achievement is letting them separate without catastrophe.

That second property receives less attention because connection is visible and exit is mostly hypothetical. A common interface gets celebrated when a new product plugs in, a new supplier enters, or a new service reaches customers. The ability to leave an implementation, migrate to a successor, withdraw trust, move records, change providers, or retire an obsolete protocol is quieter. It matters most when the relationship has already become expensive to unwind.

This is where compatibility reveals whether it created freedom or dependence.

A healthy standard does more than enlarge the number of things that can join a system. It lowers the cost of replacing the particular thing currently occupying a role.

That is a stronger test.

A customer may technically have ten vendors available and still be trapped if data cannot move, identifiers change, credentials must be rebuilt, historical records disappear, integrations have to be rewritten, and every counterparty treats the new provider as a stranger. The market looks competitive at the moment of purchase and becomes concentrated through accumulated relationships.

The lock-in is not always contractual.

It is often architectural.

A proprietary file format is an obvious example. The software can be inexpensive. The user's real investment accumulates in the documents. Over years the files become more valuable than the application that created them. If rival software cannot interpret them faithfully, the old program acquires power through the user's own history.

The same pattern appears in less visible forms.

An identity provider accumulates account relationships. A payment processor accumulates merchant integrations. A cloud platform accumulates deployment assumptions. A social network accumulates connections. A marketplace accumulates reputation. A business-software suite accumulates workflows. An AI assistant may accumulate memories, permissions, tool relationships, policies, and a record of what the user has already delegated.

The longer the system works, the harder it becomes to replace.

Success manufactures switching cost.

Standards can interrupt that process by making some of the accumulated value portable.

This is why file formats mattered so much to personal computing. If a document could survive the program that created it, software competed more credibly for the next edit. If the file belonged effectively to one application, the application had leverage over the future because it controlled the past.

The principle generalizes: a user has meaningful choice only to the extent that the valuable state of the relationship can survive substitution.

This is not the same as demanding that everything be portable.

Some value really is specific to an implementation. A proprietary optimization may not have an equivalent elsewhere. A model's internal representation of a user may not map cleanly into another model. A reputation score generated by one marketplace may depend on behavior unique to that marketplace. A workflow can be inseparable from a product's design.

Forced portability can flatten genuine differentiation.

The important question is which parts of the relationship should belong to the participant rather than the intermediary.

Consider a bank account history. The bank's fraud model can remain proprietary. The customer's transaction history is different. A competing budgeting application does not need the bank's internal model weights to help the customer analyze spending. It needs authorized access to transaction data in a usable form.

This distinction underlies open-banking regimes. The policy significance is not merely that an API exists. The customer gains the practical ability to authorize another service to interact with financial information that previously lived behind one institution's interface.

The interface changes the bargaining position.

The bank remains free to compete on trust, products, service, risk management and pricing. It loses some ability to compete by making the customer's own financial history difficult to use elsewhere.

That is a transfer of power produced by a seam.

The same logic appears in the European Union's digital-market rules around portability and interoperability. The legal details vary by service and obligation, but the underlying competition problem is familiar: a platform can become difficult to challenge when users must abandon accumulated relationships in order to leave it.

A right written in law can still be weak if exercising it requires heroic technical work.

A person may have the formal right to receive data and receive a pile of files no rival can import. A business may have access to an interface whose undocumented assumptions make reliable integration expensive. A messaging service may claim interoperability while important functions work only inside the incumbent's own network.

Exit has to be operational.

This is one reason standards should be judged by migration, not merely conformance.

Can the user export the relevant state?

Can another implementation import it?

Do identifiers remain stable enough that counterparties still know whom they are dealing with?

Can authorization be re-established without transferring dangerous bearer secrets?

Can history remain auditable after the old provider disappears?

Does the transition require a weekend of downtime, a year of dual-running, or a complete restart?

The answers determine whether compatibility created a market or simply a larger funnel into one implementation.

This gives standards a role that looks almost constitutional.

Exit limits power.

Political institutions have long understood the difference between consent at entry and freedom after entry. A worker who may accept a job but can never leave is not free because the original choice was voluntary. A citizen who may join an association but cannot resign has entered something different from an ordinary association. A customer offered a contract in a competitive market can still become dependent if later switching requires forfeiting assets accumulated through the relationship.

Markets need recurring choice, not one choice at the beginning.

Interoperability can make choice recurring.

The effect is especially important in infrastructure because infrastructure tends to become invisible once adopted. A company builds around an API. A city buys equipment around a connector. A hospital stores records in a format. A manufacturer trains workers on a process. At first, the decision looks like procurement. Later it looks like physics.

The standard can preserve the distinction.

If the shared layer is genuinely independent of the supplier, then replacing the supplier can remain an engineering project instead of becoming institutional surgery.

This is the economic value of the narrow waist seen from the other direction.

Earlier chapters emphasized that a narrow waist lets many things connect. The same architecture also limits how much any one implementation can take hostage.

TCP/IP did not make every network provider interchangeable, but it separated applications from many details of the underlying network. HTTP did not make every web server or browser identical, but it let websites and clients evolve independently around shared semantics. Standard container fittings did not make shipping lines equivalent, but cargo did not have to be repacked simply because it crossed from one carrier or mode to another.

A narrow waist is therefore not only a compression mechanism.

It is a hostage-reduction mechanism.

The phrase sounds dramatic until you examine the alternatives.

Suppose every logistics company used containers that could be lifted only by its own cranes. The customer could still change carriers. The cargo would have to be unloaded and repacked at the boundary. That friction would become part of the carrier's moat.

Suppose every payment provider issued merchant identifiers meaningful only inside its network. Changing providers would require counterparties to rebuild references. Suppose every agent platform encoded user authorization in a proprietary format that no other agent could present. The user could change assistants, but the new assistant would arrive stripped of institutional relationships.

The nominal right to switch would exist.

The practical right would not.

This is why portability should focus on relationships, not only data.

Data is the easiest category to see because it lives in files and databases. But modern systems accumulate other forms of state.

There is identity state: who the system believes you are.

There is trust state: which credentials and issuers have been accepted.

There is authority state: what you have permitted software to do.

There is transaction state: orders, payments, subscriptions and obligations that remain open.

There is reputation state: histories that influence how counterparties treat you.

There is configuration state: policies, preferences and rules that make the system behave as expected.

There is evidence state: logs and receipts needed to prove what happened.

A migration that moves documents and leaves these other layers behind may preserve far less freedom than it appears.

The agent economy will make this obvious because an agent can become an unusually dense bundle of relationships.

Imagine using one assistant for five years.

It has learned which calendars matter, which merchants you trust, which expenses require approval, which family members may receive information, which cloud accounts it can access, which projects it may edit, which travel preferences are firm, which credentials it can present, and which recurring tasks it may perform without asking every time.

Now the provider raises prices or degrades in quality.

You choose another agent.

What exactly moves?

If only conversation history moves, most of the institutional investment remains trapped.

The new agent may understand your past and still lack every authority required to act in your future.

The obvious response is to export credentials and tokens.

That can be catastrophically wrong.

Many credentials should not be copied between providers. A long-lived bearer token is not a preference file. Moving it may transfer security risk, violate audience restrictions, or bypass the resource owner's opportunity to decide whether the new delegate should receive access.

Portability of authority therefore needs to be designed differently from portability of secrets.

The portable object should often be the principal's policy and evidence of prior consent, not the reusable credential itself.

The new agent can present the policy to the relevant resource provider, authenticate under its new identity, and request a fresh grant. The resource provider remains in control of issuing authority. The user avoids reconstructing every decision from memory.

This distinction is subtle and important.

A safe migration preserves intent without preserving unnecessary possession.

The same idea appears in ordinary institutions. Changing lawyers does not mean handing the new lawyer the old lawyer's office keys. The client preserves the underlying rights and case files while establishing a new agency relationship. Changing employees does not require transferring every password one employee used. The organization preserves policy and resources while issuing new credentials to the new person.

Software should be able to do the same.

This suggests that mature agent standards will need exportable policy schemas, portable credential references, auditable delegation histories and predictable re-authorization flows.

They will also need revocation that survives vendor failure.

If the only interface for revoking an agent belongs to the agent vendor, the principal has a problem when the vendor is unavailable, compromised or hostile. Critical authority should be terminable at the resource or authorization layer that ultimately enforces it.

That is separation of powers in technical form.

The agent can request.

The policy system can constrain.

The resource owner can revoke.

No single application needs to control the whole chain.

Exit rights improve security here because they reduce the cost of responding to distrust.

A company that discovers a vendor problem should be able to replace the vendor without rebuilding the entire surrounding system. If migration is prohibitively expensive, security teams face pressure to tolerate risks because the cure threatens operations.

Lock-in weakens discipline.

A supplier knows this too.

When customers can leave credibly, the supplier has to earn continuation. When customers cannot, the supplier can extract more value from inertia.

This does not mean switching should be effortless. Complex systems create legitimate migration cost. Data models differ. integrations have to be tested. employees need training. regulated environments require validation. New suppliers need time to learn.

The relevant distinction is between unavoidable transition cost and artificial captivity.

A standard cannot eliminate the first.

It can make the second harder to manufacture.

This is where standards and competition policy meet again.

A regulator deciding whether to require interoperability should not ask only whether rivals can technically connect to the incumbent. It should ask whether users can transfer the valuable state required for rivalry to matter.

A rival messaging service that can send text but cannot participate in group administration, identity verification, attachments or calls may be interoperable in a laboratory and irrelevant in the market.

A financial API that exposes balances but not transactions may satisfy a narrow reading of access and fail the user's actual purpose.

An agent platform that lets tools advertise capabilities but keeps user authorization, memory and transaction history proprietary may implement open protocols around a closed center.

The center is where exit has to be tested.

This suggests another way to identify the real seam in a market.

Ask what the customer would lose by leaving.

The answer often reveals the asset whose portability matters most.

If the answer is documents, the file format is strategic.

If the answer is contacts, identity and messaging relationships are strategic.

If the answer is transaction history, financial-data interfaces are strategic.

If the answer is reputation, the scoring and evidence system are strategic.

If the answer is permissions, authorization becomes strategic infrastructure.

If the answer is no single thing but an accumulated web of all of these, the platform may have become an institution rather than a product.

Standards become more consequential as that institutional density rises.

They can distribute the institution across providers.

This is difficult because distributed institutions need governance. A portable identifier needs someone to manage collisions. A credential needs trust rules. A revocation mechanism needs availability. A data format needs versioning. An export process needs privacy protections. A transaction record needs semantics stable enough for another system to understand later.

The work moves from one vendor's product team into a shared layer.

That is more cumbersome.

It is also the price of not requiring one vendor to remain sovereign forever.

There is a recurring temptation to dismiss this as architecture for people who enjoy switching suppliers.

Most users do not switch often.

That misses the effect of credible exit.

The value of an emergency exit is not measured by how often a building burns. Its existence changes the consequences when something goes wrong. The value of a backup supplier is not measured only by how often procurement changes vendors. It affects bargaining, resilience and the range of responses available during failure.

Option value matters even when the option is rarely exercised.

This was visible in Baltimore. Neighboring equipment mattered because extreme events create temporary need for capacity outside the ordinary system. Compatibility converted someone else's assets into an option.

Exit is the mirror image.

A standard converts someone else's implementation into an option.

You may never use it.

Its availability still changes the structure of dependence.

The same logic applies to archives.

A durable standard gives future software a chance to interpret records after the original creator disappears. This is exit across time rather than vendors.

Libraries and governments know the problem well. A public record may need to remain readable decades after the software company that created it has vanished. Proprietary formats turn archival preservation into reverse engineering. Open and well-documented formats separate the document's lifespan from the vendor's corporate lifespan.

The dead cannot renew a subscription.

The archive therefore becomes another silent stakeholder in standards design.

This is one reason backwards compatibility deserves respect. A specification carries obligations not only to current participants but to accumulated history. Breaking compatibility casually can destroy the exit path from old implementations because the data or identifiers users need to migrate no longer retain meaning.

Yet permanent compatibility can also prevent exit from the old standard itself.

This is the paradox at the end of the book.

We want standards stable enough that relationships survive implementation change and flexible enough that the ecosystem can eventually change standards.

Too little stability and users cannot rely on the seam.

Too much and the seam becomes a fossil everyone must carry.

The answer is not eternal compatibility.

It is staged exit.

Good infrastructure creates a successor path before withdrawing the predecessor. It defines how versions coexist, how capabilities are discovered, how translation works, where translation is unsafe, what evidence indicates readiness, and when continued support becomes an unacceptable cost or risk.

TLS deprecation followed this pattern imperfectly but recognizably. Newer protocol versions existed before old ones were formally deprecated. Operators had time to migrate. Eventually the security and maintenance cost of preserving obsolete versions justified intentional breakage.

IPv6 has demonstrated the other side: coexistence mechanisms can keep a transition alive for decades because they reduce immediate pressure to complete it.

A migration bridge can become infrastructure too.

This is why exit mechanisms need governance and sometimes deadlines.

Without a credible endpoint, every temporary adapter acquires users who depend on it. The exception becomes another constituency.

Physical infrastructure shows the same thing. Dual-gauge track, adapters and converters can make migration possible. They can also make coexistence comfortable enough that conversion stalls.

There is no technical device that removes the politics of deciding when the old system has had enough time.

Somebody bears the final breakage.

Standards governance should make that cost explicit.

Which users remain on the legacy path?

Why have they not moved?

What would migration cost them?

What risks do they impose on everyone else by remaining?

Which transition tools could lower their cost?

At what point does preserving compatibility become a subsidy from the whole ecosystem to the slowest participant?

These are uncomfortable questions because infrastructure contains unequal actors. A multinational company can migrate an API more easily than a small municipality can replace physical equipment. A well-funded bank can maintain dual systems more easily than a community institution. A technology mandate can be efficient in aggregate and brutal for organizations with thin budgets.

Exit policy therefore has distributional consequences.

A society that wants faster migration may need to fund migration.

Baltimore's lesson returns in another form. The benefits of compatibility can be shared while the costs of conversion are local. If the system wants a new standard badly enough, telling every lagging participant to “upgrade” may be less effective than helping pay for the upgrade.

This is true in cybersecurity, energy systems, public infrastructure and digital government.

Transition is part of the institution, not an embarrassing phase between specifications.

The machine economy will need to learn this early because software makes standards adoption look deceptively cheap.

Publishing a new JSON schema costs almost nothing.

Changing a functioning ecosystem does not.

The cost lives in implementations, tests, contracts, credentials, stored data, user expectations, risk models, compliance processes and the thousands of small dependencies nobody included in the architecture diagram.

An agent protocol can release version two in an afternoon.

The organizations around it may carry version one for ten years.

A mature standard therefore needs two stories at the moment it is born.

How do you join?

How do you leave?

Join without exit creates dependence.

Exit without stable joining rules creates fragmentation.

The two functions belong together because they define the real freedom produced by compatibility.

The best standard is not the one everyone is forced to use forever.

It is the one useful enough that many actors voluntarily organize around it, restrained enough that implementations can compete, explicit enough that consequences remain legible, and portable enough that participation does not require surrendering the future.

This gives the compatibility dividend its final dimension.

Earlier we saw that standards can create pooled capacity, larger markets, transferable skills, lower integration costs and more competition among complements. All of those benefits increase when participants believe they are not making an irreversible bet.

A manufacturer invests more confidently around a connector if multiple suppliers can serve it.

A developer adopts a protocol more readily if independent implementations exist.

A business entrusts data to a system more comfortably if the data can leave in a usable form.

A user delegates authority more safely if it can be revoked and reissued elsewhere.

A government preserves records more responsibly if future software can still read them.

The right to exit does not sit outside compatibility.

It is one of the reasons compatibility is valuable.

A system of strangers becomes durable not when nobody can leave, but when leaving one participant does not require leaving the system.

That is a different kind of stability.

Monopolies can be stable because dependence concentrates.

Standards can make a system stable because relationships survive substitution.

The first kind of stability belongs to the owner.

The second belongs to the network.

That is the kind worth building.
