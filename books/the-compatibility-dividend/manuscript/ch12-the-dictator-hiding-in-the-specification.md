# The Dictator Hiding in the Specification

A standard can be voluntary and still be impossible to ignore.

That is one of the reasons standards deserve to be studied as power rather than paperwork.

A government regulation announces its authority. It arrives with a statute, agency, court or enforcement mechanism. A dominant technical standard can govern more quietly. Nobody may be legally required to use it. Yet a manufacturer that refuses it can lose access to customers, suppliers, infrastructure or distribution. A software developer can choose not to implement the common protocol and thereby choose not to communicate with the network.

The compulsion comes from compatibility itself.

If everyone else has coordinated around a seam, deviation becomes expensive.

This is useful when the seam solves a real collective problem. Fire hose couplings should fit. Product identifiers should not collide. Network packets need shared rules. But the same mechanism can carry private advantage into public-like infrastructure.

The dictator hiding in the specification is not always a person.

Sometimes it is the installed base.

Sometimes it is a dominant firm.

Sometimes it is a patent portfolio.

Sometimes it is a government using a technical requirement to protect domestic producers.

Sometimes it is a committee whose participants sincerely believe they are choosing neutral engineering details while their assumptions quietly favor certain business models, languages, countries or technologies.

Standards convert design choices into participation conditions.

That conversion deserves scrutiny.

International trade law recognizes the dual nature directly. The World Trade Organization's Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade treats international standards as potentially valuable tools for production efficiency and trade, while also seeking to prevent technical regulations, standards and conformity-assessment procedures from becoming unnecessary obstacles to commerce.

The same specification can lower one barrier and raise another.

A common safety standard can let a manufacturer test once and sell into many markets. Different national standards can force the manufacturer to redesign the product repeatedly. Harmonization lowers the cost of export.

But a standard can also demand a test available only in a few expensive laboratories, require a design feature associated with domestic producers, or encode local practices that foreign firms cannot cheaply satisfy. The document looks technical. The effect looks like industrial policy.

This is why standards disputes often become trade disputes.

The border has moved into the specification.

Governments know this and often choose to borrow private standards rather than write every technical rule themselves.

In the United States, federal policy has made that preference explicit for decades. OMB Circular A-119 directs federal agencies, where practical and consistent with law, to use voluntary consensus standards instead of creating government-unique standards. The logic is sensible. Standards organizations concentrate specialized expertise. Industry already uses many of their specifications. Reusing them can reduce duplication, lower procurement costs and make federal requirements more compatible with commercial markets.

This is government benefiting from someone else's coordination machinery.

It is also a transfer of practical authority.

A private technical committee can make decisions that later become relevant to federal purchasing, regulation or program requirements even though the committee itself is not a legislature. The government has not surrendered its legal authority. It still decides whether and how to incorporate or reference the standard. But the technical content entering that decision may have been developed through an institution with different participation rules, funding, transparency norms and intellectual-property policies from public rulemaking.

The line between public and private governance becomes a membrane rather than a wall.

That membrane can work extremely well.

A federal agency that needs a specification for a mature engineering problem may gain little by assembling its own rival committee and inventing another standard. Reuse can make government less idiosyncratic and markets more coherent.

The same arrangement raises questions that become visible only when the borrowed standard matters enough.

Who could afford to participate in writing it?

Can the public read it without paying?

Who owns the copyright?

How are conflicts of interest handled?

What happens when the private body changes the standard after government has built policy around an earlier version?

These are not reasons to reject incorporation of voluntary standards.

They are reminders that efficiency does not erase provenance.

The European Union provides an even sharper example because its system of harmonised standards gives certain technical documents a defined relationship to legal compliance.

Under the EU's “New Approach” and later legislative framework, legislation can state essential requirements while European standards organizations develop harmonised technical standards supporting those requirements. When a harmonised standard is referenced in the Official Journal under the relevant legislation, a product conforming to that standard can receive a presumption of conformity with the legal requirements covered by it.

The manufacturer may still be able to demonstrate compliance by another route.

That point matters.

The harmonised standard is not necessarily a command that every producer use one exact design. The system preserves formal voluntariness while making the standard an unusually attractive path through compliance. Follow it and the law supplies a presumption. Depart from it and the burden of demonstrating conformity may become more complicated.

Voluntary does not mean inconsequential.

The economics of proof can make one path dominant without making it legally exclusive.

This architecture is elegant because it can separate public goals from technical detail. Legislators set essential safety requirements. Technical experts maintain methods and specifications that can evolve more readily than statutes. Manufacturers can innovate outside the harmonised standard if they can still prove compliance.

It also creates the paradox at the center of this chapter.

A privately developed document can acquire public legal effects while remaining governed partly by private institutional practices.

That paradox reached the Court of Justice of the European Union in a dispute over access to harmonised toy-safety standards. Public.Resource.Org and Right to Know sought access to standards referenced under EU product-safety law. In March 2024, the Grand Chamber held that there was an overriding public interest in disclosure of the standards at issue. The Court emphasized their legal effects and treated them as part of EU law for purposes of the access dispute.

The ruling did not transform every technical standard into legislation.

It did something more revealing.

It recognized that a document's institutional origin does not settle the nature of the power it later exercises.

If public law gives a private specification enough legal consequence, access to the specification becomes a public-law concern.

The question “who may read the standard?” stops being merely a publishing question.

It becomes part of the rule-of-law architecture.

This is a useful warning for the machine economy.

Suppose a regulator requires autonomous purchasing systems to satisfy “recognized industry standards” for authorization, audit and revocation. The phrase sounds flexible. If one standards consortium becomes the practical source of those requirements, its technical choices can become the easiest route to legal compliance. Firms may treat optional fields as mandatory because auditors expect them. Credential issuers may implement only the dominant profile. Insurance contracts may reference it. Procurement systems may require it.

Nobody has passed a Machine Agent Constitution Act.

A constitution-like layer can still emerge through references, contracts, conformity assessments and market expectations.

That is why provenance and governance matter before the standard becomes boring.

The most useful way to analyze this power is to ask who controls four things: definition, implementation, verification and distribution.

Definition is the ability to write or change the standard.

Implementation is the practical ability to build something that conforms.

Verification is the authority to declare that something conforms.

Distribution is access to the network or market where conformance matters.

A system can be open at one layer and closed at another.

A specification may be free to read, but the patents required to implement it may be expensive. Implementation may be open, but certification may be controlled by one organization. Certification may be competitive, but a dominant marketplace can decide which certified implementations it will accept. The interface can be standardized while the directory that makes counterparties discoverable remains proprietary.

“Open standard” is therefore a starting question, not a conclusion.

Open in what sense?

Can anyone obtain the document?

Can anyone implement it without discriminatory licensing?

Can independent implementations interoperate in practice?

Can new participants obtain identifiers and credentials?

Can users switch among implementations while preserving data and relationships?

Can the standard evolve without one vendor's veto?

Can a participant leave the ecosystem without abandoning its customers or archives?

The public/private cases add another question.

If law or procurement gives the standard public consequences, can the people governed through those consequences inspect the rules on reasonable terms?

These questions reveal where the dictator might be hiding.

Patents make the issue concrete.

Technical standards sometimes incorporate patented technology. If the standard becomes widely adopted and the patented technique is necessary to implement it, the patent becomes standards-essential. This creates a bargaining problem. Before the standard is chosen, the standards body may have alternatives. After industry invests around the chosen specification, switching can be enormously expensive.

The patent's leverage changes after adoption.

That change is called hold-up in some economic and legal discussions: the rights holder may be able to demand more because the market is locked in. Standards organizations and legal regimes have developed disclosure and licensing policies intended to reduce this risk, including commitments around licensing on fair, reasonable and nondiscriminatory terms in some ecosystems.

The balancing problem is real.

If standards bodies simply confiscate the value of patented inventions, firms may have weaker incentives to contribute strong technology. If patent owners can wait until everyone is locked in and then impose arbitrary terms, firms may fear adopting the standard.

A durable standard needs an intellectual-property settlement credible enough for complementary investment.

The shipping container provided a particularly generous historical example when key patent rights associated with McLean's system were made available in support of standardization. The ecosystem became larger than the proprietary opportunity.

Not every inventor will make that choice, nor should a healthy system depend on saints.

Governance exists because incentives need structure.

The dictator can also hide in a test.

Suppose a regulation says a product must be safe. That leaves enormous ambiguity. A performance standard might specify that the product must survive certain conditions. A design standard might specify exactly how it must be constructed.

These approaches distribute innovation differently.

Performance standards can preserve freedom by defining the outcome while allowing manufacturers to find new methods. Design-prescriptive standards can be easier to inspect but may freeze a particular solution.

The WTO's technical-barriers framework explicitly recognizes this issue, encouraging performance-based requirements where appropriate rather than unnecessary design prescriptions.

This is a general principle with enormous relevance to AI.

A safety standard for an autonomous system could require measurable outcomes, documentation, incident reporting and controls appropriate to risk. Or it could prescribe a specific model architecture, training method or vendor technique.

The first approach may preserve innovation but make evaluation harder.

The second may make compliance legible while accidentally turning today's safety technique into tomorrow's mandatory fossil.

Standards can freeze the means when they should freeze the goal.

The opposite mistake also occurs. A standard can define an outcome so vaguely that conformity becomes theater. “Use appropriate security” is not an actionable interface. “Exercise reasonable oversight” may be legally useful language and technically useless language.

The art is to make requirements specific enough to coordinate and general enough to permit progress.

That balance is where power enters.

Who gets to decide what counts as enough?

Committee composition matters because standards distribute costs unevenly.

Large firms can often send engineers, lawyers and policy specialists to meetings for years. Small firms may have one engineer who also has to ship the product. Governments from wealthy countries can maintain deep technical delegations. Developing countries may have limited resources to follow hundreds of committees even when the resulting standards will shape their export markets.

A process can be formally open and practically unequal.

This is not unique to standards bodies. Legislatures, courts and scientific institutions have the same problem. But standards add a peculiar feature: the work looks narrow enough that broader political communities often ignore it until adoption is expensive to reverse.

Power is easiest to exercise where attention is low.

A thread pitch does not attract a protest movement.

A file format does not sound like constitutional law.

A machine-readable identity schema does not sound like social policy.

Then the standard wins, ecosystems accumulate and choices that once looked technical begin determining who can participate.

This is why the politics of standards often happen too early for most stakeholders and the consequences arrive too late for easy change.

The app store offers a modern intuition even though it is better described as a platform governance system than a formal standard. Developers follow technical and policy interfaces defined by a platform owner to reach users. The platform controls APIs, review, identity, payment rules and distribution. Its requirements can function like private regulation.

Now imagine such power attached to a standard used by autonomous agents.

A dominant agent network might define what counts as an identity, which credential issuers are trusted, what payment methods are acceptable, how reputation is calculated and what behaviors trigger expulsion. If one network becomes the default route to machine customers, those definitions become economically constitutional.

The rules might be public.

The governance could still be centralized.

This is why protocol neutrality is not enough. We need governance neutrality at critical shared layers, or at least credible contestability.

Contestability means participants can build alternatives and users can move.

Interoperability can support contestability by lowering switching costs. Data portability can support it by letting users carry history. Standard identifiers can support it by preserving references across providers. Federated systems can support it by allowing multiple operators to interconnect.

But interoperability can also cement a dominant standard.

This is the standards paradox again.

The more valuable compatibility becomes, the harder it is to leave the compatibility zone.

Competition may shift from standards to implementations, which is often good. But if the standard itself contains biased assumptions, the whole market competes inside a crooked field.

The remedy is not constant fragmentation.

Competing standards can waste enormous resources. Consumers may have to choose ecosystems before knowing which will survive. Developers duplicate work. Network effects can tip markets abruptly. Losing formats strand content and devices.

The famous consumer “format wars” of electronics show the cost at smaller scale. Competing video recording systems, optical-disc formats and media standards forced buyers and producers to bet on ecosystems. Market victory eventually restored compatibility, but only after duplication and stranded investment.

A standards war can be socially useful when it allows genuine experimentation before convergence.

It can also be a rent-seeking battle over who will control the eventual tollbooth.

The difference is difficult to know in real time.

One clue is whether the competing designs create meaningful user benefits that cannot coexist through a shared interface.

Another is whether participants can implement multiple standards cheaply.

Another is whether a gateway can preserve cross-compatibility.

Another is whether the winner will be governable after victory.

Standards policy should care about the postwar constitution, not only which army wins.

The dictator can hide in backward compatibility too.

Once a standard becomes infrastructure, old participants acquire an implicit veto because changes that break them impose broad costs. This protects users from arbitrary redesign. It can also let obsolete implementations constrain everyone else indefinitely.

Internet engineers know this problem as ossification. Financial systems know it as legacy infrastructure. governments know it as administrative inertia.

A constituency of old machines cannot attend the standards meeting, but it votes through breakage.

The more successful yesterday's compatibility, the stronger its claim on tomorrow.

This is why a good standard needs a theory of change from the beginning.

What can be extended?

What must remain stable?

How are versions negotiated?

How are insecure features retired?

How long are old implementations supported?

Who decides when migration is mandatory?

What evidence justifies breaking compatibility?

These are governance questions disguised as versioning.

Artificial intelligence makes them urgent because models and capabilities evolve rapidly while institutions around identity and money evolve slowly.

Suppose an agent protocol encodes a particular model of human approval: every transaction above a threshold requires a synchronous confirmation. That may be sensible in 2026. A decade later, organizations may use sophisticated policy agents, risk engines and delegated approval hierarchies. If the protocol hard-coded the old interaction instead of expressing a more general authorization condition, the standard becomes a cage.

Standards should preserve intent across changing implementation.

This is another reason performance-oriented semantics are powerful. “This action requires authorization satisfying policy X” can survive changes in how authorization is obtained better than “display this exact dialog to a human.”

But generality has limits. A standard too abstract cannot interoperate.

The dictator's opposite is the philosopher: a specification so elegant and broad that nobody can build the same thing from it.

Practical standards live between tyranny and poetry.

They constrain enough to make independent implementations meet.

The distribution of those constraints is the politics.

Consider a hypothetical machine-commerce standard that defines a merchant credential. The obvious fields might include legal name, payment address and supported terms. Then somebody proposes a mandatory environmental score. Somebody else proposes labor-practice certification. Another wants jurisdictional tax status. Another wants sanctions screening. All of these can be legitimate concerns.

Put all of them into the core credential and the standard begins deciding which social policies every machine transaction must carry.

Leave all of them out and important users will invent proprietary extensions.

The solution is often extensibility plus profiles.

A small core establishes identity and integrity. Different sectors or jurisdictions define additional recognized claims. Recipients choose which profiles they require.

This preserves plural governance while keeping technical interoperability.

It is not perfectly simple. Nothing governing plural societies is.

The alternative—one universal schema containing every political priority—is centralized conflict disguised as completeness.

Standards can become constitutions because they allocate rights to connect.

That should make us conservative about the core and ambitious about interoperability among extensions.

The best shared infrastructure is usually boring enough that many political and commercial systems can use it without surrendering their distinct purposes.

TCP/IP does not decide tax policy.

Unicode does not decide what sentences may be written.

A screw thread does not decide what machine must be built.

The standard stays powerful by staying beneath the arguments it enables.

The coming agent economy will test this discipline.

The temptation to embed trust, safety, identity, payments and policy into one unified agent layer will be enormous because integration is convenient. The first company to provide the whole stack may produce a wonderfully smooth experience.

It may also become the sovereign of machine action.

A healthier architecture would let multiple identity systems, payment providers, policy engines and agent vendors interoperate through stable seams.

That is harder to build.

It is also harder to capture.

The dictator hiding in the specification is not an argument against standards.

It is an argument for taking them seriously enough to ask who gains the power created by everyone agreeing.

The standard will govern something.

The question is whether it governs only the seam that needs governing—or quietly annexes the world around it.
