# Compatibility as Competition Policy

A market can contain many companies and still have only one practical doorway.

This is a recurring problem in digital systems because the value of a product often depends on its connections. A new messaging service is less useful if none of your contacts can receive its messages. A new financial app is less useful if it cannot access your account data or initiate payments with your consent. A new mobile accessory is less useful if the operating system reserves important hardware features for the platform owner's own products.

Competition can fail at the interface.

Traditional competition policy often asks whether a firm has market power, whether it excludes rivals, whether a merger reduces competition or whether conduct harms consumers. In networked markets another question has become increasingly important: can rivals interoperate with the infrastructure customers have already chosen?

This turns standards from an engineering concern into a competition instrument.

Interoperability can lower the cost of entry because a challenger no longer has to reproduce the incumbent's entire network before offering value.

Consider messaging.

If every service is an island, a new entrant needs to persuade groups of users to move together. One person switching alone loses access to contacts. The incumbent's installed network protects itself even if the entrant has a better product.

Interoperability changes the migration equation.

A user can adopt a new service without immediately abandoning everyone on the old one, provided the two systems can exchange enough messages. The challenger competes on interface, privacy, features or price while relying on a shared communications seam.

This is what telephone networks learned long ago. A telephone network that could reach only its own subscribers was less valuable than an interconnected network. Regulation eventually treated interconnection as too important to leave entirely to the discretion of dominant carriers.

Digital policy is rediscovering the pattern.

The European Union's Digital Markets Act makes interoperability an explicit tool for contestability. Article 7 requires designated gatekeepers providing certain number-independent interpersonal communications services to make specified messaging functions interoperable with requesting third-party providers. The obligations are phased, beginning with one-to-one text and file sharing and extending toward group functions and eventually voice and video calls. The European Commission reported that the first third-party services interoperable with WhatsApp launched in November 2025.

The DMA also contains broader interoperability obligations for designated operating-system gatekeepers. Article 6(7), as implemented through Commission proceedings, can require access to hardware and software features available to the gatekeeper's own services, subject to proportionate measures needed to protect system integrity.

The policy theory is straightforward.

If the platform controls a seam competitors need, requiring a usable seam can create competition without requiring the platform itself to be broken apart.

That sentence sounds simpler than implementation.

Interoperability has to preserve enough functionality that a rival can compete meaningfully. A deliberately slow, unstable or incomplete interface can satisfy the appearance of openness while preserving practical exclusion. Documentation matters. Latency matters. feature parity matters. security processes matter. version changes matter. the ability to test matters.

A standard can be open on paper and hostile in operation.

This is why mandated interoperability often needs service-level governance around the technical interface.

Who answers requests when the interface fails?

How quickly must new capabilities become available to third parties?

May the incumbent charge?

Which security checks are proportionate?

Can it require rivals to expose equivalent data?

What happens when a third party causes abuse?

How are disputes resolved before the challenger runs out of money?

Competition policy enters systems engineering.

The tension with security is real.

Every new interface creates attack surface. A tightly integrated platform can sometimes protect users by controlling both sides of an interaction. Opening interfaces to third parties can create new opportunities for fraud, data leakage, spam or unsafe hardware behavior.

Incumbents emphasize this because it is true.

They can also exaggerate it because security is one of the few arguments powerful enough to justify keeping an interface closed.

Regulators face a difficult asymmetry: the platform usually knows its system better than the regulator, while also having a commercial incentive to describe interoperability as dangerous.

A good rule therefore does not pretend security and competition are opposites.

It asks for security measures that are necessary, evidence-based and proportionate rather than measures that recreate exclusion under another name.

This is exactly the kind of problem standards institutions already know how to handle.

A well-designed interoperability specification can define authentication, permission scopes, rate limits, abuse reporting, audit logs and failure modes. Independent testing can compare implementations. Public issue processes can reveal whether a limitation is genuinely technical.

Standards make the competition remedy operational.

Open banking offers another version of the same logic.

For years, a customer's financial history was technically stored by a bank and economically useful largely inside the bank's own products. Third-party financial services could ask users to re-enter information, scrape websites or negotiate proprietary integrations. The data belonged to the customer's economic life but was trapped behind institution-specific interfaces.

The United Kingdom's open banking regime created standardized APIs and security profiles through which authorized third-party providers can, with customer consent, access account information and initiate payments. The Open Banking Standard specifies read/write APIs, directories, security profiles, onboarding mechanisms and operational requirements rather than simply ordering banks to “be open.”

That implementation detail is the policy.

A legal right to data portability without a usable format can be ceremonial.

An interoperability right without identifiers, authentication and error semantics can be a PDF about freedom.

Standards turn rights into interfaces.

This is one of their most important political functions in digital markets.

The right to switch becomes more meaningful when data can move.

The right to choose a third-party service becomes more meaningful when the service can connect.

The right to control one's financial information becomes more meaningful when machines can exercise consent through standardized, secure flows.

Law establishes entitlement.

Standards make the entitlement executable.

This relationship is likely to deepen as AI agents become economic intermediaries.

A human consumer may tolerate the annoyance of exporting a CSV, opening a new account and rebuilding preferences. An agent will route around friction ruthlessly if standards make alternatives legible.

Machine customers could therefore make interoperability more economically potent.

Suppose an agent can query several insurance providers through a common interface, carry verified driving and claims credentials with the user's permission, compare contract terms in a machine-readable form and switch coverage without manually re-entering data. The competitive pressure is different from a market where every insurer controls a separate website designed partly to make comparison difficult.

Standards can reduce the value of confusion.

This will not please every incumbent.

Many businesses earn legitimate returns from better products. Some also earn returns from customer inertia, proprietary formats, incompatible data, difficult cancellation and the sheer cost of learning a competitor's system.

Interoperability attacks the second category of advantage.

That can look like hostility to innovation if the incumbent's innovation includes building an integrated ecosystem.

The distinction is not always clean.

Integration creates real value. Apple's control over hardware, operating system and services has often allowed tight product coordination. A bank's integrated security system can reduce fraud. A cloud provider's proprietary services can offer capabilities unavailable in lowest-common-denominator standards.

If regulators standardize too aggressively, they can force products toward sameness and discourage integrated innovation.

Competition policy should not require every layer to become interchangeable.

It should identify where lack of interoperability creates a bottleneck that protects market power more than product quality.

This is a seam-selection problem again.

Standardize the escape hatch, not the entire building.

Data portability is one such seam.

A user should often be able to retrieve the information they supplied or generated in a useful format. But portability becomes complicated when data includes rights belonging to other people, proprietary inferences, security-sensitive information or structures unique to the provider.

A simplistic requirement to “export all data” can expose privacy or trade secrets.

Standards can define categories.

User-provided content may be portable one way. transaction history another. inferred scores another. social graph data may require rules about contacts' privacy. cryptographic secrets should not be exported casually at all.

The interface forces policy to become precise.

This is why writing standards can improve law even before implementation. Vague rights encounter edge cases. The committee has to decide what a field means, who can request it, what consent looks like, how often access is allowed and how revocation works.

Technical specification is a form of institutional debugging.

It reveals where the legal concept was underspecified.

Interoperability also changes business strategy by moving competition upward.

If all banks expose standardized account-information APIs, simply possessing transaction data becomes less differentiating. Firms compete on analysis, advice, user experience, risk management and product design.

If messaging networks interoperate, exclusive access to a contact graph becomes somewhat less powerful. Services compete more on features and trust.

If operating systems expose common hardware capabilities, accessory makers compete on devices rather than privileged private integrations.

This is the same mechanism we saw with screw threads.

Standardize one unproductive axis of competition so more valuable competition can happen elsewhere.

But competition can move downward too.

An interoperability standard can create a new market for gateways, compliance testing, identity providers, directories and security infrastructure. Those new layers can become concentrated.

Open banking needs directories of authorized participants. Agent ecosystems may need registries of capabilities or credential issuers. Messaging interoperability may depend on bridges.

Every opened seam creates supporting infrastructure that can become the next seam worth controlling.

Competition policy must follow the stack as it evolves.

This is why one-time remedies age badly.

A regulator can require an API in 2026 and discover by 2030 that the real bottleneck is no longer the API but the identity system, ranking algorithm, payment rail or default-setting mechanism around it.

Markets reorganize around constraints.

Standards should therefore be governed as ongoing infrastructure, not static decrees.

The UK's Open Banking Standard explicitly evolves through versions, with implementation planning designed to reduce support costs and increase adoption. That is not bureaucratic tidiness. It recognizes that regulated interoperability is software in production.

Production systems change.

So do threats.

OAuth provides a useful example. OAuth 2.0 became a widely used authorization framework for letting applications receive limited access to resources without simply collecting a user's master credentials. Deployment experience exposed patterns that were vulnerable or easily misused. RFC 9700, published in January 2025 as Best Current Practice for OAuth 2.0 security, updates earlier advice and deprecates modes no longer considered safe.

A competition-enhancing interface without evolving security practice would eventually destroy trust in the interface.

Standards maintenance is therefore part of keeping markets open.

A security incident can trigger pressure to retreat into proprietary integration. If open interfaces repeatedly fail, incumbents gain a persuasive argument that only closed ecosystems can be safe.

Security excellence protects openness politically as well as technically.

This will matter enormously for agent interoperability.

Imagine a requirement that dominant commerce platforms allow authorized shopping agents to act on behalf of users. The competition benefits could be large. Agents could compare products across stores, negotiate, purchase, return items and manage subscriptions without being captive to one platform's assistant.

Now imagine the first wave produces account takeovers, malicious agents and confused delegation.

The public reaction could close the interface for a generation.

Open systems need standards for bounded authority before they need maximal reach.

This is why competition policy and safety architecture should be designed together.

An agent should not receive a user's entire account merely because interoperability law says third parties need access. It should receive the minimum rights necessary for the task, for a limited period, with auditable scope and revocation.

Granular authorization makes openness safer.

OAuth's basic insight is relevant: give the application a token representing limited access rather than the user's password. The agent economy will need a richer version because agents can delegate, plan and act across multiple services.

A token may need to represent not only “read calendar” but “schedule one meeting with these participants, before this date, without moving existing medical appointments.”

The technical challenge is expressing authority without requiring every service to understand arbitrary natural language.

This is precisely where new standards can create competition.

If every platform invents a proprietary permission model, agents become captive to platforms. If a common way exists to express bounded grants, multiple agents can compete for the user's delegation.

The user can switch intelligence while keeping institutional relationships.

That could become one of the most important portability rights of the agent era: portability of authority.

Today a person switching assistants may lose integrations, permissions, memories and learned workflows because those relationships are bound to one provider.

A future interoperability regime might separate the principal's policy from the agent implementation.

The user could say, in effect: these are the domains in which I permit software to act, these are my standing limits, these are the institutions I trust, and this new agent may inherit only these selected grants.

The standard would make delegation portable without making credentials universally shareable.

That distinction is delicate.

Permission portability can become a security disaster if migration transfers long-lived bearer tokens or opaque secrets. The portable object should probably be policy and consent that can be re-authorized through each resource provider, not a bag of reusable keys.

Again the standard determines the seam.

Competition policy in the agent era may therefore move from data portability toward relationship portability.

Can I move my history?

Can I move my preferences?

Can I move my verified credentials?

Can I move my subscriptions?

Can I move my delegated authority?

Can I move my reputation?

Can I move my social and commercial relationships without every counterparty treating the new agent as a stranger?

Each question has different privacy and fraud risks.

Not every answer should be yes.

A reputation score generated by one marketplace may not be meaningful elsewhere. A professional credential should travel if the issuer intends it to. A bank authorization should probably be reissued rather than copied. A social relationship involves another person who may have rights in the connection.

Portability is not one thing.

Standards let policy decompose it.

The broader economic argument is that compatibility can make markets more contestable without forcing all production into one firm or all infrastructure into government.

A shared seam lets independent actors connect.

That is the same institutional trick we encountered with machine threads and the internet.

In competition policy, the trick is deployed deliberately against bottlenecks.

It should be used carefully because mandatory compatibility can reduce incentives to build ecosystems if innovators believe rivals will immediately receive every complement they create. Intellectual property and security can be legitimate constraints. Integration can be valuable. Some APIs are expensive to support.

The answer is not a presumption that openness always wins.

The answer is to identify rents created by incompatibility separately from returns created by innovation.

That analytical separation is hard and worth doing.

A standard cannot tell us where the legal line belongs.

Once the line is chosen, standards can make the remedy real.

Competition law says a rival may enter.

Compatibility determines whether the door opens.
