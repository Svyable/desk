# The Fire Hose That Wouldn't Fit

On February 7, 1904, Baltimore discovered that help can arrive and still be unusable.

A fire that began in the city's business district grew beyond the capacity of local companies. Assistance came from other cities. Firefighters, engines, hose and experience moved toward Baltimore because that is what mutual aid is supposed to do: when one city runs out of capacity, neighboring systems become its reserve.

The reserve failed at the point of connection.

Fire hose couplings were not sufficiently standardized. Equipment that worked perfectly in one city could arrive in another and fail to mate with the local hydrants. The National Bureau of Standards, the institution now known as NIST, later documented the problem as part of a much larger national mess. Its researchers had encountered hundreds of sizes and variations in hose couplings. The Great Baltimore Fire became the memorable demonstration of what those variations meant when interoperability stopped being an engineer's nuisance and became a civic emergency.

The fire burned for more than a day and destroyed roughly 1,500 buildings. It would be too neat to say that incompatible threads caused the disaster. Fires of that period spread through dense commercial districts for many reasons: combustible construction, weather, the limits of firefighting equipment, the size of the event, and the ordinary difficulty of controlling a major urban fire. But the coupling problem mattered because it converted available outside capacity into stranded capacity. Men and machines had traveled to the right place. Water existed. The system failed at an interface.

That distinction is the beginning of this book.

We tend to think of capacity as something an organization owns. Baltimore had so many firefighters, so much hose, so many engines. Washington had its own. Philadelphia had its own. Add them together and, on paper, the region possessed a larger firefighting force than any city could justify maintaining alone.

But regional capacity exists only if the parts can combine.

A neighboring city's fire engine is not your backup simply because it is nearby. It becomes your backup when its crew can operate with your procedures, its hose can connect to your hydrants, its communications can reach your command structure, and its equipment can function in the environment you provide. Mutual aid is an interoperability claim.

The same principle governs far less dramatic systems. A replacement bolt in a warehouse is not inventory if it does not fit the machine that broke. A cargo container at a port is not transport capacity if the crane cannot lift its fittings. A hospital's extra records are not useful continuity if the receiving system cannot interpret them. A battery is not backup power if the connector, voltage or control protocol is wrong. A software service is not an alternative if the customer's data cannot be exported into a form the alternative understands.

We routinely count assets without counting interfaces.

The result is a subtle inflation of resilience. Organizations believe they possess options that exist only in diagrams.

The Baltimore fire is often told as a triumphal origin story for standardization. Disaster revealed incompatibility; experts responded; a national standard followed. That is broadly true. It is also incomplete in a way that matters.

The coupling problem was known before Baltimore burned.

Fire departments and fire-protection organizations understood the advantages of uniform connections. National Bureau of Standards work found extraordinary variation. The missing element was not knowledge but coordinated adoption. Each city had already invested in equipment. Each local standard had become embedded in hydrants, hose, purchasing systems, repair practices and budgets. Changing to a national connection meant spending real money today to avoid a future problem that might never become visible in that city's accounts.

This is the ordinary political economy of standards.

Compatibility produces benefits across organizational boundaries, while conversion costs land on particular organizations. The whole system may gain if everybody changes. Any one participant may prefer that everybody else change instead.

Imagine a city whose hydrants use a local fitting. Its equipment works. Its crews are trained. Its repair shops know the parts. The fire chief can ask the council for money to replace thousands of connections so that hypothetical outside firefighters will someday be able to attach hypothetical hose during a hypothetical catastrophe. Or the chief can ask for another engine that local voters can see.

The engine wins often enough that history fills with incompatible infrastructure.

Standards are therefore not merely technical agreements. They are mechanisms for distributing transition costs and future benefits.

The strongest case for standardization appears when the network value is obvious. If every city uses the same fire-hose connection, each gains access to every other compatible city's emergency capacity. The more cities adopt the standard, the more valuable adoption becomes. Economists describe this family of effects as network externalities: the usefulness of participation can rise with the number of compatible participants.

The phrase sounds bloodless next to a burning city, but that is exactly why it is useful. The fire did not create a special law of emergency equipment. It exposed a general economic structure.

Compatibility can manufacture capacity without manufacturing more assets.

Suppose ten towns each own equipment sufficient for ordinary local fires but insufficient for an extreme event. If their systems are interoperable, the region can pool capacity when trouble is unevenly distributed. They have created resilience by agreeing on interfaces. If their systems cannot connect, the same physical equipment exists but much of the shared reserve is fictional.

A standard can therefore behave like capital.

It does not look like a factory or a fleet. It may be no more than dimensions in a document. Yet it increases the productive range of existing assets. A common coupling lets an engine serve more hydrants. A common gauge lets rolling stock travel farther. A common protocol lets software reach more counterparties. A common identifier lets databases reconcile records. The agreement raises the option value of things that were already built.

This is the compatibility dividend in its simplest form.

The dividend is easy to miss because accountants usually record the cost of compliance and not the value of connection. A city pays to change fittings. A manufacturer pays to redesign a component. A software company pays engineers to implement a specification. Those are visible expenses.

The gains arrive diffusely. Procurement gets more suppliers. Customers get more choices. Repairs become easier. Mutual aid becomes possible. New entrants can build complementary products without negotiating private interfaces. Training travels between employers. Secondary markets deepen. Inventories can be shared. Someone else's innovation becomes usable inside your system.

No single invoice says: compatibility dividend, received in full.

This asymmetry helps explain why standards often look bureaucratic at the moment they are being made and obvious after they have won.

A committee discussing thread pitch can appear to be doing less important work than an inventor producing a new pump. Yet if the pump's connection becomes common across an industry, the specification around its interface may outlast the pump itself. Generations of equipment can change while the agreed boundary remains stable enough to let old and new systems cooperate.

The interface acquires a strange kind of independence from the objects it connects.

This is why a history of technology told only through inventions misses so much. It gives us the engine but not the gauge, the computer but not the character encoding, the container ship but not the corner fitting, the browser but not HTTP, the smartphone but not the cellular standards that let it enter a network. The object photographs well. The standard sits in a document.

Yet the document may determine the size of the object's world.

Baltimore also teaches a second lesson: technical superiority does not automatically defeat installed reality.

A national standard does not erase local infrastructure the moment experts agree on it. Old equipment remains in service. Municipal budgets remain finite. Replacement cycles take years. Adapters appear. Transitional arrangements become permanent. Even a century after the Great Baltimore Fire, NIST researchers examining major American cities found that national-standard hydrant connections were far from universal.

This is not evidence that standards are useless. It is evidence that standards live inside history.

The installed base is one of the great powers in technological civilization. Once physical or digital infrastructure reaches sufficient scale, any proposed standard must bargain with the past. The theoretically clean solution is often to replace everything. The economically plausible solution is usually to preserve compatibility, create adapters, phase changes in, or tolerate multiple systems longer than an engineer starting from a blank sheet would prefer.

That bargain can be wise. It can also keep bad systems alive.

A standard increases coordination by reducing variation at a chosen interface. The choice of interface matters. So does the amount of variation removed. Standardize too little and systems remain incompatible. Standardize too much and the agreement begins to dictate design decisions that would be better left to competition.

A fire-hose coupling is almost an ideal case because the value of common geometry is direct and the creative value of having hundreds of incompatible local connections is close to zero. Nobody needs a flourishing artistic ecosystem of hydrant threads. Diversity there mostly destroys optionality.

Other domains are harder.

Consider medical records. A common way to exchange core patient information can improve continuity of care. But a standard that freezes every detail of clinical workflow may burden hospitals and lock software into assumptions that age badly. Consider electric vehicles. Common charging interfaces can increase consumer confidence and infrastructure utilization, but decisions about connectors can shift competitive advantage among firms that already invested in different systems. Consider AI agents. A common way to discover another agent and exchange messages may be liberating. A standard that dictates how every agent reasons internally would be absurd.

The art is to standardize the seam, not the whole garment.

We can state the idea more precisely. A useful interoperability standard defines the minimum shared assumptions required for independent systems to cooperate while leaving as much room as practical for independent improvement on either side of the boundary.

Minimum does not mean small in page count. A modern protocol can require enormous detail. It means limited in ambition. The standard should know what it is trying to make compatible.

The fire-hose case is about mechanical connection. Two parts must mate under operational conditions. That requires dimensions, tolerances, thread form and enough shared expectation that equipment made by different parties can connect reliably.

Notice what the standard does not need to specify. It does not need to require every city to purchase the same fire engine. It does not need to prescribe the color of every station, the salary of every firefighter, or the route by which crews arrive. Shared coupling geometry can coexist with local variation everywhere else.

That is one reason standards can expand freedom by imposing constraints.

At first glance, requiring everyone to use a common connector reduces choice. A city loses the freedom to invent a proprietary thread. But that sacrificed freedom buys a much larger one: the freedom to connect to equipment made elsewhere.

Standards repeatedly make this trade. Drivers surrender the freedom to choose any side of the road. In return, traffic becomes possible at speed. Manufacturers surrender arbitrary dimensions at certain interfaces. In return, they gain component markets. Network engineers surrender arbitrary packet behavior. In return, independent machines can communicate. Writers and software developers accept character encodings. In return, text survives its journey between systems.

The useful freedom is often downstream of the constraint.

This is not always obvious to incumbents because incumbents may benefit from incompatibility.

If only one company can make a replacement part that fits its machine, incompatibility creates a captive aftermarket. If moving data out of a service is painful, switching costs become customer retention. If a platform controls the only practical interface to its users, would-be complements must negotiate access. A private standard can therefore operate as a moat.

The very feature that makes common standards socially productive—their ability to reduce the cost of connecting—can threaten firms whose profits depend on expensive connection.

This is where standardization stops being a dry engineering topic and becomes political economy.

Who has the right to define an interface? When should compatibility be voluntary? When should a regulator require it? How much should a patent holder be allowed to charge when a patented technique becomes necessary to implement an industry standard? Should a dominant platform be forced to open an interface to competitors? When does interoperability increase security by enabling substitution, and when does it increase attack surface by creating more connections?

Baltimore offers no complete answers. It gives us the cleanest statement of the problem.

Systems fail at boundaries.

The boundaries are often where ownership changes. A hydrant belongs to a city. A hose belongs to a fire company. A rail line belongs to one operator and rolling stock to another. A payment moves between institutions. A packet crosses networks. A shipment changes modes. A patient crosses hospitals. A software agent calls a tool controlled by someone else.

Inside one organization, a manager can impose conventions. Across organizations, coordination becomes harder because nobody has complete authority. Standards are one way civilization solves that authority gap without requiring a single owner for the whole system.

That is a profound institutional invention.

A common specification can create order without creating a monopoly organization. Thousands of firms can remain independent while behaving as parts of a larger machine at selected interfaces. In that sense, standards make decentralized complexity governable.

They allow strangers to become a system.

The phrase deserves care. A system is not simply a collection of things. It is a collection whose parts have relationships stable enough that behavior can propagate. If every component requires a custom negotiation before it can interact with another, the collection scales poorly. Human attention becomes the bottleneck. Connection costs rise faster than participation.

Standards compress those negotiations.

Instead of every fire department agreeing bilaterally with every other fire department about hose dimensions, participants can adopt a shared specification. Instead of every browser vendor negotiating a private web protocol with every website, the web relies on common protocols and formats. Instead of every retailer inventing a unique product identifier with every supplier, common numbering systems allow identifiers to travel.

The number of potential relationships grows much faster than the number of participants. Shared standards prevent all of those relationships from becoming separate engineering projects.

This is why compatibility matters most as networks expand.

A village can live with local conventions. A global trading system cannot rely on everyone knowing the village rule.

Scale turns convention into infrastructure.

There is an irony here. The more successful a standard becomes, the more natural it feels. Children encounter electrical outlets as facts of architecture. Programmers encounter network protocols as properties of computers. Consumers encounter barcodes as if products had always carried machine-readable identities. The negotiation vanishes from memory.

That amnesia creates two mistakes.

The first is to undervalue maintenance. Standards are not fossils. They require governance, testing, revisions, registries, compatibility work and people willing to argue over small details before those details become large failures. The second is to assume that current standards were inevitable. They were not. They emerged from particular technical possibilities, institutional interests and moments of history.

That means today's interfaces can be redesigned, but not for free.

Every standard creates a constituency of compatible things. As that constituency grows, changing the standard becomes harder. Stability is one of the benefits users came for. The standard succeeds partly by becoming difficult to change casually.

The first great question, then, is not why standards exist. Baltimore makes that easy.

The harder question is what, exactly, we are buying when we standardize.

We are buying the ability to rely on strangers.

A firefighter can arrive from another city and connect. A mechanic can reach for a part made by another factory. A shipper can trust a crane in a port never visited. A program can send a message to software written by people its authors never met. An agent can someday prove not only that it can speak, but that it has authority to act.

The shared rule reduces the amount of trust required in the individual counterparty because some of the uncertainty has been moved into the interface.

That is the first superpower of standards.

They turn compatibility from a relationship into an assumption.
