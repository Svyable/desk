# The Limit Does Not Exist

*How Much Is Enough?*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Sven Hardy Benson |
| **Status** | Drafting |
| **Chapters** | 3 of 18 drafted |

There is no obvious number after which a person becomes too rich to want another dollar, too accomplished to want another win, too admired to want another room to notice, too optimized to stop tracking, too productive to close the laptop, or too powerful to stop expanding the perimeter of control.

Sometimes more really is better. More food to a hungry person. More money to a household that cannot pay rent. More sleep to someone chronically deprived. More medicine when the dose remains therapeutic. More years with someone you love. A theory of “enough” that begins by scolding desire is not serious.

But many human and institutional systems have an asymmetry: they are extremely good at detecting **less** and surprisingly bad at detecting **enough**.

**The Limit Does Not Exist: How Much Is Enough?** asks what supplies the stopping rule when appetite, status, optimization, accumulation, and growth do not supply one themselves.

The question appears personal, but it scales quickly. A worker can keep adding hours. A company can keep seeking growth. A fund can keep seeking return. A city can keep adding lanes. An empire can keep adding territory until the cost of holding it changes the equation. A social platform can keep optimizing engagement long after another minute of attention has stopped serving the person whose attention is being harvested. A fitness tracker can turn health into a number that is always one unit short of tomorrow's target.

The book is not an argument for austerity, minimalism, anti-growth economics, or withdrawal from ambition. It is an argument that **systems without terminal conditions eventually borrow their limits from somewhere else**: biology, debt, regulation, exhaustion, competition, physical scarcity, ecological damage, revolt, death, or deliberate choice.

The empirical spine begins with one of the most famous modern arguments about satiation: whether income stops improving emotional well-being after a threshold. The answer turned out to depend on measurement, population, and which kind of well-being was being studied. Kahneman and Deaton reported a plateau in experienced well-being around $75,000 in 2010. Killingsworth's later experience-sampling work found no such general plateau. An adversarial collaboration between Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Barbara Mellers reconciled part of the disagreement: flattening appeared among the least happy participants while happiness continued rising with log income for many others.

That disagreement is useful because the point of this book is not to discover one universal number called Enough.

The point is to understand why the number moves.

## The book's lane

This is not a happiness book with a provocative title. It is a book about **stopping rules**.

The psychological literature matters because adaptation and social comparison alter subjective sufficiency. Economics matters because marginal value, positional competition, and incentives shape accumulation. Addiction science matters because some reward systems can alter the machinery of wanting itself. Operations research and machine learning matter because objective functions reveal what optimization looks like when no terminal condition is specified. History matters because states, firms, movements, and institutions also have appetites.

The book should keep asking the same practical question in different forms:

**What tells the system to stop?**

If the answer is “nothing inside the system,” then the next question is where the limit eventually comes from.

## Editorial principles

1. **Do not moralize scarcity.** A person without enough food, safety, income, housing, sleep, or care does not need a lecture about wanting less.
2. **Separate absolute from positional goods.** Some goods improve life directly; others derive part of their value from relative rank. Many contain both.
3. **Treat adaptation as empirical, not mystical.** Ask what adapts, how quickly, in which populations, and with what exceptions.
4. **Do not turn addiction into a metaphor for everything.** Use addiction science where clinically and mechanistically appropriate, and distinguish it from ordinary habit, ambition, or preference.
5. **Avoid the false claim that corporations are legally required to maximize short-term growth.** Follow actual incentives, governance, financing, competition, and strategy.
6. **Look for systems that stop well.** Maintenance organizations, finite projects, harvest cycles, mission-driven institutions, sunset clauses, and bounded games are counterexamples worth studying.
7. **Preserve cases where more remains better.** The book fails if every curve is forced to flatten for rhetorical convenience.
8. **Make “enough” operational.** The ending should offer ways to design stopping rules without pretending one number applies to everyone.
