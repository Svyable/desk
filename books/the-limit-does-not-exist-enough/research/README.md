# The Limit Does Not Exist: How Much Is Enough? — research brief

## Research question

What supplies a stopping rule when a person, organization, market, model, or state can keep pursuing more of a measurable objective after the original purpose has been substantially served?

The book should distinguish several mechanisms that are too often collapsed into one story about greed:

- **basic insufficiency**, where more of a good has large direct benefits;
- **diminishing marginal value**, where benefits remain positive but shrink;
- **adaptation**, where a gain changes the baseline against which future gains are experienced;
- **positional competition**, where part of the value depends on relative rank;
- **reinforcement and compulsion**, where the pursuit changes the machinery of wanting;
- **objective-function failure**, where a measurable proxy keeps improving after it diverges from the purpose;
- **institutional growth incentives**, where stopping can be individually rational but competitively dangerous;
- **external constraint**, where biology, debt, physics, ecology, law, or politics supplies the limit the system lacks internally;
- **chosen sufficiency**, where a person or institution deliberately adopts a terminal condition before desire or opportunity disappears.

The thesis should not require every curve to flatten. In some domains, more continues to improve outcomes across the observed range. That is part of the argument: there may be no universal natural point called enough.

## Opening evidence spine

Chapter 1 follows the modern income-and-well-being dispute because it demonstrates how a culturally attractive stopping point can dissolve into a conditional result when measurement improves.

The sequence matters:

1. Kahneman and Deaton (2010) distinguished emotional well-being from life evaluation and reported a plateau in the former around $75,000 annual household income in their data while life evaluation continued to rise.
2. Killingsworth (2021), using experience sampling, reported experienced well-being rising with log income above that level.
3. Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers (2023) conducted an adversarial collaboration and found flattening among the least happy participants while well-being continued rising for happier groups.

Do not inflate that sequence into “science proved money buys happiness forever.” The literature is observational, the populations and measures are specific, and causal interpretation needs care.

## Chapter research spine

1. **Adaptation.** Use longitudinal and review literature rather than the cartoon claim that people always return completely to a fixed happiness set point.
2. **Positional goods.** Trace status competition from classic economics into empirical work on relative income, housing, education, luxury consumption, and rank. Separate social comparison from direct utility.
3. **Reward and addiction.** Use NIDA and clinical sources to explain reinforcement, tolerance, craving, and substance-use disorder. Avoid calling ordinary ambition “addiction” unless the mechanism and diagnostic language fit.
4. **Work and time.** Use American Time Use Survey and longitudinal work-hours research. Ask when longer hours buy income, autonomy, status, or career capital and when they reduce health or well-being.
5. **Quantification.** Look for documented cases in health, education, platforms, and workplaces where a helpful metric became the target.
6. **Corporate growth.** Use actual corporate governance, competition, capital costs, returns to scale, and market structure. Explicitly reject the folk claim that U.S. corporate law imposes a universal legal duty to maximize short-term profit or growth.
7. **Objective functions.** Draw from control, operations research, machine learning, reward hacking, and specification gaming. Keep analogies anchored to real technical examples.
8. **Induced demand.** Use transport economics as a clean infrastructure case where added capacity can generate additional use, complicating the intuition that expansion permanently eliminates scarcity.
9. **Diminishing returns.** Find domains where the last increment is disproportionately expensive: reliability, purity, latency, safety, accuracy, manufacturing yield, athletic performance.
10. **Chosen limits.** Study budgets, caps, Sabbath traditions, seasons, term limits, sunset clauses, mission completion, maintenance modes, and organizations that intentionally stop growing or shut down.

## Source discipline

For any claim that “more stops helping,” specify:

- what outcome is being measured;
- over what range;
- for which population;
- whether the evidence is cross-sectional, longitudinal, experimental, or causal-inference based;
- whether the curve actually reaches zero marginal benefit or merely shows diminishing returns;
- what cost or opportunity cost is being compared with the marginal gain.

For any claim about addiction, use clinical or neuroscience sources and preserve the difference between substance use disorder, behavioral addiction where recognized, reinforcement, habit, and metaphor.

For any claim about firms, use real governance and economic evidence rather than attributing motives to an abstract entity called “capitalism.”

## Falsification questions

The book becomes a sermon if the answer is always “less.” Each chapter should look for evidence that complicates the sufficiency thesis.

- Where does more continue to produce meaningful gains with no observed plateau?
- Which people rationally prefer continued accumulation because it buys security, optionality, philanthropy, or future freedom rather than status?
- When does a chosen cap merely protect incumbents or freeze an inefficient system?
- When does growth reduce unit costs, spread risk, improve access, or fund innovation?
- Which competitive environments punish organizations that stop before rivals do?
- Where does adaptation remain incomplete for years, leaving durable gains or losses?
- Which positional goods also have large absolute benefits?
- When does induced demand represent valuable newly enabled activity rather than policy failure?
- Which forms of ambition produce meaning rather than crowd it out?

The target claim is not that wanting more is irrational. It is: **when a system has no native terminal condition, continued optimization needs an external comparison—marginal value, opportunity cost, purpose, or chosen constraint—or the stopping point will be supplied by whatever breaks first.**
