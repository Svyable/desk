# The Number After Enough

There is a peculiar kind of number that almost never appears on a financial statement, a fitness dashboard, a résumé, a growth plan, a leaderboard, or a map of an empire.

Enough.

Not the number you can afford.

Not the number you can achieve.

Not the number your competitor has.

The number after which continuing would no longer improve the thing you were trying to improve.

That number is difficult because human beings rarely encounter desire as a clean optimization problem. We want several things at once. Money buys security, convenience, status, optionality, care, time, distance from humiliation, and sometimes simply more money. Work can be a source of income, identity, craft, friendship, competition, meaning, and avoidance. A larger house can provide space, privacy, schooling access, a yard, a commute, a social signal, an investment, and another set of rooms to maintain.

A single curve called utility cannot carry all of that without losing something.

Still, one fact keeps showing up.

We are better at noticing insufficiency than sufficiency.

Hunger is loud. Satiety can be subtle.

Debt sends a bill. Financial security does not send a certificate saying the accumulation phase is complete.

Exhaustion eventually stops the body. Ambition can keep producing reasons to ignore it.

A company can measure shrinking revenue immediately. It has a harder time recognizing the point where another unit of growth adds fragility, complexity, political exposure, or managerial cost faster than value.

A government can see territory it does not control. The cost of controlling one more territory may reveal itself only after the border has moved.

A platform can measure another minute of engagement. It cannot infer from the minute alone whether the user is grateful for it.

This book began with a question that sounds almost embarrassingly old: How much is enough?

The mistake would be to answer it with a number.

Economists, psychologists, philosophers, religious traditions, political movements, parents, ascetics, advertisers, financial planners, addiction researchers, and people lying awake at three in the morning have all produced answers. The answers differ because “enough” is not one problem.

Enough for what?

Enough to survive is not enough to flourish.

Enough money to reduce financial stress is not necessarily enough money to stop caring about relative status.

Enough achievement to satisfy one identity may threaten another. The person who reaches the income target can discover that what they actually wanted was independence. The athlete who reaches the performance target can discover the target was functioning as permission to feel worthy. The executive who reaches the title can discover a new comparison group waiting one floor higher.

The finish line moves because the runner changes while running.

That is the psychological version of the problem.

There is also an institutional version.

Many systems are designed with an objective and no native stopping rule.

Revenue should rise. Costs should fall. Throughput should increase. Engagement should improve. Territory should be secured. Accuracy should get closer to one hundred percent. Response time should approach zero. Life expectancy should increase. Compute should scale. Market share should expand. The loss function should decrease.

None of those goals contains its own terminal condition.

A loss can always be smaller.

A market share can always be larger until the market is exhausted.

A model can always be trained longer if the marginal improvement is still measurable and the budget exists.

A road can always carry more cars until the additional capacity attracts more trips and congestion returns.

A bureaucracy can always add another rule to address the failure the last rule did not prevent.

A person can always produce one more revision.

This is not evidence that optimization is bad.

Optimization is one of civilization's great engines. The problem appears when the thing being optimized is easier to measure than the thing it was meant to serve.

Then the proxy keeps improving after the purpose has flattened.

This is where the idea of a stopping rule becomes useful.

A stopping rule is not a mood. It is a condition under which continued optimization no longer deserves priority.

Sometimes the rule is physical. A stomach is full. A battery is charged. A reservoir reaches capacity. A machine overheats.

Sometimes it is economic. The expected value of the next unit falls below its cost.

Sometimes it is legal. The budget is exhausted. The authorization expires. The mandate ends.

Sometimes it is social. The store closes. The season ends. The Sabbath begins. The child goes to sleep. The meeting has a hard stop.

Sometimes it is chosen. This much money. This many clients. This many days on the road. This many nights away from home. This much risk. This much fame. This much company.

Chosen limits are interesting because they compete against systems that reward ignoring them.

A lawyer billing by the hour needs a stronger stopping rule than fatigue. A fund manager paid on assets under management may have incentives to gather more capital even when the strategy's edge declines with scale. A social network optimizing time spent can treat a successful departure as failure. A founder can discover that every solved problem creates the capacity to adopt a larger one.

The system's metric does not know when the human purpose has been served.

Sometimes neither does the human.

The modern argument about income and happiness is a useful place to begin because it demonstrates how badly we want one clean number.

In 2010, Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton published a large analysis suggesting that emotional well-being rose with income up to roughly $75,000 a year and then flattened, while life evaluation continued to rise. The result escaped the paper and entered culture as a number: seventy-five thousand dollars. Earn that much and, in the simplified retelling, more money would not make you happier.

It was irresistible.

It was also not the end of the story.

Matthew Killingsworth later collected more than a million real-time reports of experienced well-being from tens of thousands of employed U.S. adults and found no general plateau at $75,000. Experienced well-being continued to rise with log income in his data.

Now the culture had two answers.

The unusually productive next step was not another round of dueling headlines. Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Barbara Mellers worked together in an adversarial collaboration. Their 2023 analysis found a more complicated pattern: for the least happy participants, happiness flattened at higher incomes; for happier groups, it continued rising, and for the happiest it accelerated.

The threshold did not disappear.

It became conditional.

That is much more interesting.

The famous question “At what income does money stop buying happiness?” had bundled together different kinds of well-being, different measurement methods, different parts of the population, and an assumption that one curve should describe everyone.

The better result was not a better universal number.

It was a better map of the problem.

This book will keep trying to do that.

There are places where more has obvious diminishing returns. There are places where returns stay positive but shrink. There are places where the return is mostly positional: the value depends on what everyone else has. There are places where consumption changes the system doing the consuming, as in tolerance and addiction. There are places where what looks like satiation is actually a measurement ceiling. There are places where scarcity is so severe that a conversation about “enough” becomes insulting.

The book has to preserve all of them.

It also has to resist one fashionable mistake: assuming that the desire for more is a defect unique to capitalism, modernity, smartphones, billionaires, or the United States.

Institutions can intensify appetite. Markets can monetize it. Technology can automate it. Advertising can cultivate it. Social media can quantify it. But status competition, territorial expansion, hoarding, prestige, rivalry, asceticism, gluttony, ambition, renunciation, and arguments about sufficiency are much older than any of those systems.

The machinery changes.

The question persists.

A strong theory of enough therefore has to work at several scales.

At the scale of the body, it must distinguish hunger from appetite and reward from compulsion.

At the scale of the person, it must account for adaptation, comparison, identity, ambition, and security.

At the scale of the organization, it must account for incentives, capital, competition, governance, and the possibility that stopping is strategically stupid while everyone else keeps moving.

At the scale of civilization, it must confront physical limits without assuming every resource problem ends in catastrophe or every efficiency gain solves demand.

And at the scale of a life, it must deal with the one terminal condition nobody gets to optimize away.

Time.

A dollar can compound.

An hour cannot be reinvested after it is spent.

That difference turns “enough” from a moral preference into an allocation problem.

Every pursuit consumes something that cannot be spent twice: time, attention, health, land, political capital, trust, risk capacity, ecological capacity, or simply the chance to be doing something else.

The reason to stop is often not that the current thing has become worthless.

It is that another use of the next unit has become more valuable.

That is a harder definition of enough because it does not offer the comfort of satiation. You may still want more when stopping is correct.

A stopping rule can arrive before desire ends.

That may be the central fact of the book.

Enough is not necessarily the moment when wanting goes quiet.

It may be the moment when judgment outranks wanting.
