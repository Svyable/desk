# The Number That Moved

For a while, happiness cost seventy-five thousand dollars.

Not literally. But that is how a research result escaped into culture.

In 2010, Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton published an analysis of more than 450,000 responses to the Gallup-Healthways Well-Being Index. They were careful about a distinction popular summaries often blurred. They separated **life evaluation**—how people judged their lives when they stepped back and considered them—from **emotional well-being**—the quality of everyday feelings such as enjoyment, stress, sadness, worry, and affection.

The two measures behaved differently.

Life evaluation kept rising with log income across the range they studied. Emotional well-being improved with income too, but in their analysis it stopped improving beyond roughly $75,000 in annual household income.

That number was almost engineered for headlines.

Seventy-five thousand dollars was high enough to feel aspirational and low enough to feel attainable. It was specific. It appeared to answer an ancient question with the authority of a regression.

How much money is enough?

About this much.

The trouble with a memorable number is that it survives the qualifications that produced it.

The data came from 2008 and 2009. The measure of emotional well-being asked about the previous day. Income was reported in bands. The paper itself emphasized the difference between life evaluation and emotional experience. The authors did not announce a universal human income target valid for all places, years, household structures, personalities, and purposes.

Culture did what culture does.

It rounded the argument into a rule.

That rule is useful to examine because it contains almost every problem this book cares about.

First, there is the temptation to turn a curve into a cutoff.

Second, there is the temptation to turn a population average into a personal prescription.

Third, there is the temptation to treat a measured plateau as a property of human nature rather than a result produced by a specific instrument, sample, and definition.

Fourth, there is the human desire for a stopping rule strong enough to compete with wanting more.

A scientific paper appeared to have supplied one.

Then the number moved.

Matthew Killingsworth took a different measurement approach. Instead of asking respondents to remember yesterday's emotional state in a small number of questions, he used experience sampling: people were prompted during their lives and reported how they felt in the moment.

His 2021 paper analyzed more than 1.7 million reports from 33,391 employed adults in the United States.

The result did not reproduce the famous plateau.

Experienced well-being rose with log income across the observed range. It did not flatten above $75,000.

Now the story could have become the familiar ritual of science by press release: old result wrong, new result right, everyone choose a side.

Something better happened.

Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Barbara Mellers collaborated directly on the disagreement. The method is worth lingering on. “Adversarial collaboration” sounds theatrical, but its purpose is almost the opposite. Researchers with conflicting results agree on analyses that can expose where their interpretations differ, rather than continuing to publish past one another.

Their 2023 paper did not restore one clean answer.

It made the answer less clean.

For the least happy portion of the sample, experienced well-being showed a flattening pattern at higher incomes. For happier people, well-being continued to increase with log income. Among the happiest group, the relationship steepened.

Money had not become infinite happiness.

The plateau had become conditional.

This matters for the question of enough because it exposes how badly we want a threshold to do philosophical work for us.

If a study had established beyond dispute that emotional well-being stops increasing at a certain income, that would still not tell a person what to do after reaching it.

Money has uses other than buying immediate positive affect.

It can reduce risk.

It can create options.

It can support children, parents, friends, employees, charities, political causes, art, travel, healthcare, education, or a future version of yourself.

It can buy distance from an abusive employer.

It can buy time.

It can buy a house in a safer neighborhood or a shorter commute or a school district somebody believes is better for their child.

It can become a score in a status competition.

It can become an identity.

It can become something a person continues accumulating because the process of accumulation itself has become the game.

A happiness plateau, even if perfectly measured, would answer only one part of the problem.

Enough is multidimensional.

That is why the most useful lesson from the income-and-well-being literature is not a dollar figure.

It is the shape of the disagreement.

Measurement changed the apparent stopping point.

Population heterogeneity changed the apparent stopping point.

The definition of well-being changed the apparent stopping point.

The scale on which income was plotted mattered.

The distinction between levels and changes mattered.

What looked like one question turned out to be several.

This is a recurring pattern.

Ask how much house is enough and the answer depends on whether the extra room means a child no longer shares a bedroom, an aging parent can move in, a worker gains a home office, or a household has begun buying square footage as a positional signal.

Ask how much work is enough and the answer depends on whether another hour keeps the lights on, completes a surgery, protects a craft deadline, secures a promotion, feeds a compulsion, or merely satisfies a workplace norm nobody remembers choosing.

Ask how much exercise is enough and the answer depends on whether the person is sedentary, training for an event, recovering from injury, managing disease risk, or chasing numbers after the health benefit has flattened.

Ask how much company growth is enough and the answer depends on returns to scale, competitive threats, investor expectations, financing, market structure, managerial complexity, and whether the next customer improves the business or makes it worse.

The word “enough” appears singular because grammar forces it to.

The underlying system is not.

There is another reason the $75,000 story is useful.

It demonstrates how quickly a stopping rule becomes normative once a number appears.

If research says happiness plateaus at a threshold, the person who continues chasing income above it can be portrayed as confused. Perhaps greedy. Perhaps trapped in hedonic adaptation. Perhaps wasting a life.

Maybe.

Or perhaps the person likes the work.

Perhaps their household has unusual obligations.

Perhaps they care more about life evaluation, security, philanthropy, status, or optionality than the measured dimension of yesterday's emotional affect.

Perhaps the marginal dollar still matters to them for reasons the study did not measure.

A descriptive average is not a command.

This should be obvious, but “enough” invites moral trespass. People like telling other people where their appetite should stop.

The rich should stop there.

The ambitious should stop there.

The consumer should stop there.

The athlete should stop there.

The state should stop there.

The company should stop there.

Sometimes that advice is excellent.

Sometimes it is a disguised preference for somebody else's life.

A serious theory of sufficiency has to be harder than that.

It needs at least three different curves.

The first is **absolute benefit**.

Does another unit improve the person's material condition, health, security, capability, or experience?

At low levels of many goods, the slope can be steep. More food to the hungry matters. More cash to a household facing eviction matters. More sleep to someone surviving on four hours matters. More medical care can matter enormously when necessary care is absent.

The second is **marginal benefit**.

Even when more remains positive, how much is the next unit worth compared with the last one?

A second bedroom may transform a household. A fourteenth bedroom probably does not provide the same kind of gain.

The third is **opportunity cost**.

What does another unit require giving up?

Another dollar of income might cost an hour of time, a move to another city, more risk, more responsibility, less autonomy, a longer commute, or nothing meaningful at all. The same nominal gain can be cheap for one person and ruinously expensive for another.

The stopping point is not where benefit reaches zero.

It is often where the next unit is no longer the best use of what it costs.

That distinction is the beginning of adulthood in many domains.

You can still want the thing.

You can still benefit from the thing.

You can still decide not to pursue more of it.

This is important because popular discussions of hedonic adaptation sometimes imply that the goal is to extinguish desire by understanding its trick. Learn that the new car becomes normal and suddenly you will stop wanting cars. Learn that the promotion resets your comparison group and status will lose its grip.

Awareness helps.

It is not a neurological off switch.

A stopping rule has to work while desire remains active.

Markets understand this better than self-help does.

Every investment decision is an argument about marginal return under constraint. Capital should not flow to a project merely because the project still produces a positive return. It should flow there if that return is better, after risk and other considerations, than the available alternatives.

A life contains the same problem, except the scarce capital is not only money.

It is time.

Attention.

Health.

Trust.

Geographic freedom.

Emotional range.

Risk capacity.

The number of evenings in a year.

The number of summers with a child at a particular age.

Those assets do not all appear on the same spreadsheet.

That makes overinvestment easy.

A person can maximize the metric that compounds and ignore the asset that expires.

Income compounds.

Prestige can compound.

Audience can compound.

Equity can compound.

Expertise can compound.

A Tuesday night does not.

This does not mean everyone should work less.

It means “more” should face a denominator.

More per what?

More money per hour?

More status per relationship neglected?

More accuracy per dollar?

More growth per unit of complexity?

More safety per restriction?

More fitness per injury risk?

More engagement per minute of somebody else's finite attention?

Once the denominator becomes visible, the curve changes.

The pursuit that looked monotonic can acquire a bend.

The income literature teaches a related lesson. Even before the 2023 reconciliation, the original 2010 paper did not say money stopped mattering. It found different relationships for different dimensions of well-being. Life evaluation continued rising. Emotional well-being behaved differently in that dataset.

That is already a warning against one-dimensional stopping rules.

The same person can be doing better on one measure and worse on another.

An executive can earn more, feel more successful, sleep less, see friends less, and report higher life satisfaction.

Which variable gets veto power?

There is no study that can answer that on behalf of the person.

But there are better and worse ways to structure the choice.

One is to notice whether the target keeps changing after it is reached.

Another is to distinguish security from scorekeeping.

Another is to identify which goods are positional: if everyone gains the same amount, does the benefit survive?

Another is to ask whether the optimization process is changing the optimizer. Addiction is the most dramatic case, but milder reinforcement loops appear everywhere.

Another is to ask whether the system has any internal signal for completion.

This last question is especially important.

Some activities terminate naturally. The patient is stabilized. The bridge is built. The harvest is collected. The game ends. The manuscript ships.

Other activities can always generate another unit.

Money.

Followers.

Market share.

Miles.

Features.

Data.

Citations.

Weight lifted.

Weight lost.

Territory.

Power.

The absence of a natural endpoint does not make the pursuit bad.

It means the endpoint has to come from judgment.

The famous $75,000 number was attractive because it appeared to save us from having to exercise that judgment ourselves.

Science would tell us when to stop.

Instead, science did something more useful.

It made the curve more complicated.

The number moved.

Now we have to think.
