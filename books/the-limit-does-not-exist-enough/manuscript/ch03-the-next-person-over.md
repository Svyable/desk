# The Next Person Over

In the Dutch Postcode Lottery, a winning postal code can make one street suddenly strange.

Some households win. Their neighbors do not. The geography barely changes. The reference group does.

That made the lottery useful to economists interested in a question people usually discuss with much less precision: what happens when the person next to you gets richer?

Peter Kuhn, Peter Kooreman, Adriaan Soetevent, and Arie Kapteyn studied households exposed to these unexpected local income shocks. Winners changed some consumption, especially durable goods and cars. More interesting for this chapter, neighbors changed too. The researchers found social effects on car consumption and exterior home renovation.

One household's windfall could alter another household's spending.

The neighbor did not take anything away.

The driveway changed anyway.

This is the basic problem of positional goods.

Some things are valuable because of what they do for you. Some are valuable partly because of where they place you relative to other people. Most interesting goods contain both elements.

A car transports you. It can also announce income, taste, age, profession, tribe, or rank.

A house shelters you. Its address, size, architecture, school district, view, and distance from other houses can carry positional value.

A job pays you. Its title can matter after the paycheck clears.

A university teaches you. Admission can also certify that you were selected.

A watch tells time with almost comic accuracy compared with what most mechanical watches cost. The remaining value has to come from somewhere else.

The existence of status value is not a revelation. Humans have been decorating rank since before written history.

The interesting question for a book about enough is what status does to the shape of satisfaction.

If the value of a good depends partly on position, then there is no quantity that can make everyone top-ranked at once.

The game contains scarcity even when production does not.

That makes positional competition fundamentally different from ordinary abundance.

If everyone in a town gets cleaner drinking water, everyone can be better off on that dimension. If every household gets reliable electricity, the improvement does not require someone else to lose electricity for the benefit to count. If every child is vaccinated against a disease, protection can spread rather than cancel itself.

Rank is different.

Not everyone can be above average.

Not everyone can have the house that is unusually large for the neighborhood after every house grows.

Not everyone can attend the school that is selective because it rejects most applicants.

Not everyone can be in the top one percent.

A positional system can improve absolutely while remaining unchanged relatively.

That distinction is easy to understand in sports. If every runner in a race becomes ten percent faster, the finishing order may be identical. Each athlete is objectively faster. No athlete gained rank.

Money often contains both kinds of improvement.

More income can buy food, safety, healthcare, time, transportation, privacy, education, and resilience. Those are not imaginary because someone else also has them.

But income is also one of the most powerful ranking systems modern societies possess.

That means the emotional return to another dollar can depend partly on what happened to somebody else's dollar.

Andrew Clark and Andrew Oswald found an early version of this pattern in data on British workers: job satisfaction was related not only to a worker's own income but negatively to the wage used as a comparison level. Erzo Luttmer later matched U.S. well-being data with local earnings and found that, holding a person's own income constant, higher earnings among neighbors were associated with lower reported well-being.

These studies are not proof that every person constantly scans the block for signs of economic rank. Reference groups are hard to observe. People compare themselves with coworkers, siblings, classmates, friends, people online, people in the same profession, and sometimes people they have never met.

But the broader literature keeps finding that comparison matters.

The Norwegian experience made that point in an unusually blunt way.

In 2001, Norwegian tax records became easy to search online. Suddenly, the income of another person was not a rumor, guess, or private conversation. It was a lookup.

Ricardo Perez-Truglia studied the change as a natural experiment and found that greater income transparency widened the well-being gap between richer and poorer people. The information did not create the income inequality. It changed how visible the ranking was.

That distinction should make anyone designing a leaderboard nervous.

Metrics do not merely report position.

They can make position salient.

The workplace offers an even cleaner example.

David Card, Alexandre Mas, Enrico Moretti, and Emmanuel Saez studied what happened when University of California employees were encouraged to look up the pay of their coworkers. The effect was asymmetric. Employees who discovered they were paid below the median for their unit and occupation reported lower pay satisfaction and job satisfaction and became more likely to look for another job. Employees above the median did not receive an equal and opposite happiness bonus.

That is a miserable little machine if it generalizes.

Falling behind hurts.

Being ahead does not symmetrically heal.

The result resembles other asymmetries in this book. A new baseline can become normal without an old baseline becoming painless to lose. A status gain can fade while a status loss remains vivid.

More recent field experiments make the comparison effect even harder to dismiss as questionnaire psychology. Zoë Cullen and Ricardo Perez-Truglia studied employees who learned information about salaries inside a large firm. When workers learned that peers earned more than they had thought, their behavior changed differently than when they learned that managers earned more. A higher-than-expected manager salary could function as information about a possible career ladder. A higher-than-expected peer salary could feel like unfavorable comparison.

Same organization.

Same money.

Different reference person.

That is important because “envy” is too crude a word for what is happening.

Comparison can motivate, demoralize, inform, reassure, threaten, or redirect ambition depending on who the target is and what their success appears to mean.

A richer founder in another industry may be irrelevant. A colleague hired six months after you at a higher salary can reorganize your afternoon.

Reference groups are selective.

This is one reason the internet changed the economics of enough without changing human nature.

The village comparison group used to be limited by geography, work, family, church, school, club, profession, or class. Those systems could be brutal, but they were bounded.

A phone can supply a new reference group every few seconds.

The person comparing houses can see homes in cities they do not live in. The person comparing bodies can see professional athletes and edited influencers. The amateur musician can compare a practice session with a child prodigy. The small-business owner can compare revenue with venture-backed companies. The academic can compare citation counts globally. The middle manager can compare compensation with an anonymous spreadsheet of people at firms that pay differently for reasons the spreadsheet does not contain.

The comparison set has become searchable.

That does not mean social media invented status anxiety. It means the cost of finding someone ahead of you approached zero.

This matters because enough is partly a function of the denominator.

A $200,000 income can feel enormous compared with a childhood household income of $40,000 and inadequate compared with a peer group earning $500,000. A 2,000-square-foot house can feel palatial after a studio apartment and small after a weekend touring new construction. Ten thousand followers can feel surreal until the relevant comparison group is people with a million.

Nothing about the absolute quantity changed during the comparison.

The meaning did.

Status goods make this especially visible because sometimes the social meaning is the product.

Leonardo Bursztyn and colleagues ran field experiments with an Indonesian bank offering high-income customers a platinum credit card. They compared demand for the status card with a visually less distinctive product carrying identical benefits. Customers showed substantially greater demand for the platinum version, and the status card was more likely to be used in social settings. When the researchers experimentally increased self-esteem, demand for the status good fell.

The card's transaction utility had been held constant.

What changed was the audience.

This can sound ridiculous until we remember how much of ordinary life is social signaling.

Clothes signal affiliation and taste. Neighborhoods signal class. Credentials signal competence. Job titles signal hierarchy. Philanthropy can signal generosity and access at the same time. Cars, phones, vacations, restaurants, schools, and hobbies all carry information whether or not the buyer purchased them for that purpose.

Humans live among other humans. It would be stranger if consumption contained no social dimension.

The problem is not that positional goods are fake goods.

The problem is that positional competition has a poor stopping rule.

If the point is rank, then satisfaction depends on a moving field of competitors.

You can win locally and then change leagues.

This happens constantly.

A student becomes the best in a high school and enters a selective university full of former best students. A lawyer makes partner and begins comparing herself with partners at larger firms. An entrepreneur becomes wealthy and gains access to people for whom that wealth is ordinary. A runner qualifies for Boston and now knows hundreds of people who are faster. A scientist gets tenure and begins comparing grants, prizes, labs, citations, invitations, or institutional prestige.

Success changes the reference group.

That is one of the least appreciated mechanisms in the moving finish line.

You do not merely approach the people you once wanted to resemble.

You gain access to a new room.

Then the room supplies a new hierarchy.

This does not make ambition pathological. New reference groups can teach. A better athlete sees what better training looks like. A young scientist encounters people doing work at a level she did not know was possible. A founder meets operators who reveal avoidable weaknesses in the company. Comparison can expand the frontier of capability.

The same mechanism can also erase arrival.

There is always another room.

This is why advice to “stop comparing yourself with others” is both sensible and unserious.

Humans learn socially. Markets communicate through comparison. Salaries need benchmarks. Students need standards. Athletes need opponents. Scientists need peer review. Consumers need reference prices. A person trying to improve needs some idea of what better performance looks like.

The goal cannot be to eliminate comparison.

It has to be to understand which comparisons are informative and which ones convert life into a tournament with no terminal state.

A useful comparison answers a question.

What is this skill worth in the market?

What does competent performance look like?

Am I being underpaid?

Is this medical outcome normal?

What is physically possible?

A corrosive comparison often answers no question except rank.

Who has more?

Who is ahead?

Who was invited?

Who is younger and already there?

Who has the larger number?

The difference can be subtle because the same metric can do both jobs.

Salary data can reveal discrimination or merely produce obsession. Race times can guide training or make every run a referendum on identity. House prices can inform a purchase or turn a neighborhood into a scoreboard.

The metric is not enough to tell us whether the comparison is useful.

We need the purpose.

There is another caution in the evidence.

The Dutch postcode-lottery study found some neighbor effects on visible consumption, but it did not find that nonwinning neighbors were less happy six months later. The social-comparison story should not be inflated into a law that every nearby success makes everyone miserable.

People are not that simple.

They can celebrate a friend's success. They can use someone else's achievement as information. They can choose different games. They can care about absolute security more than rank. They can compare downward. They can compare with their past selves. They can decide that one hierarchy is stupid and leave it.

That last ability matters most.

A positional game becomes dangerous when participation feels compulsory.

If status in the game determines access to mates, schools, employment, credit, housing, healthcare, political power, or basic dignity, leaving is expensive. If the prize is merely being the person with the fanciest watch at dinner, the exit is easier.

Enough becomes possible when a person can separate the function of a good from the tournament attached to it.

What does the house need to do?

What does the income need to protect?

What kind of work is worth doing?

What level of fitness serves the life outside the metric?

Which comparison teaches something useful?

Which league did I enter without deciding to?

These are not minimalist questions.

They are boundary questions.

The person who knows what a good is for has at least a chance of noticing when the positional layer has taken over.

Without that, more has an elegant way of surviving every victory.

The neighbor gets a new car.

Then the driveway starts talking.
