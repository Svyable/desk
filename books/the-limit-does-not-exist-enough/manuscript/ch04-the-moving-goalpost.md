# The Moving Goalpost

A promotion is a useful thing to want.

More money. More control. A better title. Greater security. Evidence that somebody noticed the work. A larger field of action.

Then the promotion arrives.

For a while the old job remains available as a comparison. The new office is better than the old office. The new salary is higher than the old salary. The meetings contain people who used to be above you. The title sounds slightly unreal when someone says it out loud.

Then the old job disappears as a reference point.

This is what a moving goalpost actually feels like from the inside.

It usually does not feel like greed.

It feels like an updated map.

Alois Stutzer studied this problem through income aspirations. Using Swiss survey data, he compared life satisfaction with people's own assessments of the income they considered sufficient or desirable. Higher aspirations were associated with lower reported well-being, holding income constant. More importantly, aspirations were not fixed. They rose with a person's own income and with the average income of the surrounding community.

The target moved with the runner.

That is a more precise mechanism than saying money cannot buy happiness or that humans always want more.

Income can improve a life. Chapter 1 already made that difficult to deny. The problem is that the income used to judge whether one has enough can change the standard against which the next income is judged.

Suppose someone earning $60,000 believes $100,000 would create real breathing room. The belief may be correct. At $100,000, debt can be paid faster. Savings can grow. Better housing may be possible. Emergencies can become less frightening.

But the person who arrives at $100,000 is not necessarily carrying the same aspiration that existed at $60,000.

The peer group may have changed. The neighborhood may have changed. The tax bill changed. The opportunities changed. New forms of consumption became visible. The next professional level became imaginable. Expenses that once looked extravagant may now look normal, prudent, or necessary.

The original goal can be reached while sufficiency remains exactly one promotion away.

This is not a paradox if aspirations are endogenous.

The word sounds technical because the mechanism is technical. Endogenous means that the variable we thought was merely judging the system is being changed by the system itself.

The aspiration is not standing outside income with a clipboard.

Income is helping to write the aspiration.

That creates a difficult problem for personal goals because goals are supposed to tell us when to stop.

A target that updates in response to progress is not a terminal condition.

It is a tracking algorithm.

Promotions show the pattern because they change several reference points at once.

David Johnston and Wang-Sheng Lee studied Australian workers before and after promotions. The promotions substantially improved perceptions of pay, job security, and overall job satisfaction in the short run. They also changed job control, stress, hours, and income. But the effects on broader health and happiness were small on average, and the researchers found evidence of worsening mental health two years later.

This should not be read as a warning to refuse promotions.

A promotion can be excellent. It can bring autonomy, money, interesting work, influence, recognition, and the ability to leave worse situations behind. Average effects do not tell an individual what to do.

The study matters for a narrower reason.

The promotion does not merely add benefits.

It changes the job being evaluated.

More authority can bring more responsibility. Higher pay can arrive with longer hours. A larger team can produce less time to do the work that originally made the person successful. Greater status can increase the number of people whose expectations now matter.

The goalpost moves partly because the prize changes the game.

A later German panel study followed promotions and pay raises across decades and found a short-term increase in job satisfaction after promotion that diminished after about a year. The authors interpret the pattern through habituation and a changing reference point: the promoted role initially looks better relative to the old role, then becomes the new ordinary.

Again, the lesson is not that advancement is futile.

The lesson is that arrival and normalization are different events.

A promotion can improve the structure of work while losing its ability to feel like a promotion.

This distinction matters because people often use feeling as evidence about value.

When the excitement fades, the mind can conclude that the gain was smaller than expected and begin searching for the next gain.

Sometimes that is accurate. The new job may genuinely be disappointing.

Sometimes the value is still there but no longer salient.

A person has more autonomy, more income, more influence, and better options than before. None of those facts need generate a daily sensation of upward movement.

The escalator can stop feeling like motion while still being one floor higher.

That makes ambition difficult to regulate from mood alone.

The same process appears in goals that have nothing to do with salary.

A writer wants to publish a book. The book is published. Then sales become the measure. Sales arrive. Reviews become the measure. Reviews arrive. The next book must be better. A scientist wants a paper accepted. Then the journal matters. Then citations. Then a grant. Then a larger grant. An athlete wants a personal record, then a qualifying time, then a podium, then a ranking. A founder wants product-market fit, then $1 million in recurring revenue, then $10 million, then category leadership, then a second product because the first market is no longer large enough.

Each target can be rational when considered locally.

The sequence can still have no ending.

This is why telling ambitious people to be satisfied is not a design.

Satisfaction is a state. A stopping rule is a decision procedure.

The two are not interchangeable.

A stopping rule might say: once the company reaches a level at which it can fund itself, the next objective changes from growth at any cost to durable cash generation.

A researcher might say: I want a body of work large enough that I can spend more time on difficult questions than on signaling productivity.

A household might say: once retirement savings reach a specified range and fixed costs are covered, additional income is allowed to buy time rather than a more expensive baseline.

A runner might say: the point of this training block is to complete the race healthy, not to turn every subsequent year into an attempt to defeat the previous one.

The important feature is not the number.

It is that the criterion was chosen before the new reference group could rewrite it.

That does not mean the criterion can never change.

A rule made at twenty-five may be ridiculous at forty-five. A company can discover a larger opportunity. A scientist can find a question worth another decade. A family can acquire new obligations. The world changes.

But there is a difference between revising a goal because circumstances changed and revising it automatically because it was reached.

The first is judgment.

The second is drift.

Drift is especially powerful because successful people often enter new comparison groups as a direct consequence of success.

The promotion moves a worker onto a management team. The successful founder gets invited into rooms with larger founders. The athlete qualifies for a race populated by faster athletes. The musician gets signed and begins comparing streams with other signed musicians. The family buys into a more expensive neighborhood and discovers a new local definition of normal.

The reward changes the denominator.

This is one reason people can become objectively better off and subjectively stationary.

They are measuring from a new origin.

Economists and psychologists sometimes call these shifts reference-dependent evaluation. The phrase covers several mechanisms, and it should not be treated as one master explanation. Adaptation, social comparison, aspiration, loss aversion, and changing information can all affect the reference point differently.

What matters here is the practical consequence.

A person may not notice the target changing because the new target arrives disguised as information.

Now that I know what is possible, why would I stop here?

Now that I work with people at this level, shouldn't I be at their level?

Now that the company is worth this much, isn't failing to double it a kind of decline?

Now that I can afford this neighborhood, isn't the next one safer, better connected, better for the children?

Each question can contain truth.

That is what makes the treadmill durable.

A stupid desire is easy to reject.

An escalating desire usually comes with reasons.

There is also a selection problem hidden inside stories of ambition. We hear disproportionately from people for whom moving goalposts produced visible achievement. A founder who never stopped expanding may build an enormous company. A scientist who remained obsessed with the next question may transform a field. An athlete who refused ordinary satisfaction may set a record.

Those cases are real.

So are the costs we often exclude from the scorecard.

Time. Health. Relationships. Stress. Opportunity cost. The projects never attempted because the old game continued consuming attention. The point is not to force every life into balance. It is to count what the chosen metric leaves out.

A stopping rule becomes necessary when success on one axis can quietly consume everything not represented on that axis.

The company can always be larger.

The portfolio can always be worth more.

The body can always be leaner, stronger, faster, or more measured.

The audience can always be larger.

The house can always have another room.

The difficulty is that these statements are mathematically true long after they stop being practically useful.

The limit does not appear by itself.

A person has to supply one.

There are several ways to do that without pretending ambition is a disease.

One is to define the function before defining the number.

What is the money for?

What is the promotion for?

What is the company for?

What is the training for?

What is the audience for?

A functional target is harder for status drift to hijack because it refers to an external purpose.

Another is to separate a floor from a frontier.

The floor is what must be enough: sleep, solvency, time with children, health, integrity, whatever the person has decided should not be continuously sacrificed.

The frontier can remain ambitious.

A researcher can pursue a harder question without allowing every grant cycle to consume the floor. A founder can chase a larger market while deciding that some personal constraints are not venture inputs. An athlete can train at an elite level while accepting that injury changes the optimization problem.

The point is not moderation.

It is explicit tradeoffs.

A third method is to revisit the forecast.

Before the promotion, what did you believe it would change?

After a year, what actually changed?

Which benefits persisted after the novelty faded?

Which costs appeared that were absent from the fantasy?

Would the next promotion solve the remaining dissatisfaction, or is the dissatisfaction being produced by the structure of the ladder itself?

This kind of retrospective accounting is uncomfortable because it interrupts narrative momentum.

It asks whether the next objective is still ours or merely next.

That is the core distinction of Part I.

Chapter 1 showed that even the supposed income threshold for happiness moved when researchers changed the measurement and population. Chapter 2 showed that adaptation can make durable gains feel ordinary. Chapter 3 showed that the person next door can change the meaning of what we already have. This chapter adds the final mechanism: success can rewrite the target that defined success.

None of these mechanisms prove that enough is impossible.

They prove that enough rarely arrives as a sensation.

The system has no reason to announce that the objective function should change.

That decision has to come from somewhere else.

Part II turns to the systems that make this harder still.

Not merely moving goalposts, but machines built to keep us playing.
