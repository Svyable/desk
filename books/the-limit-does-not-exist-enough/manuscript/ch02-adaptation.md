# Adaptation

A bath stops feeling hot after you have been in it.

You knew the water was hot when you stepped in. Ten minutes later, the same water feels neutral, even cool near the edges. Nothing changed in the water. Something changed in the person occupying it.

This is the oldest and least mysterious version of what psychologists call hedonic adaptation. The nervous system is built to notice change. It attends to what is new, what is surprising, what departs from the level it has tuned itself to expect. Once a stimulus becomes steady, the apparatus that detected it stands down. The kitchen that smelled of onions when you arrived smells like nothing by dinner. The road noise beside a house fades to silence for the people who live there and returns the moment they notice it is gone. A wage, a house, a view, a bedroom that once seemed luxurious: none of it stops being what it is because it stopped being noticed.

It stops being noticed.

Adaptation level theory, the broad framework that shaped this research decades ago, was built around exactly that observation: judgments of how much is too much or too little are made against a baseline supplied by experience. The baseline is not fixed. It drifts toward whatever has been happening. Water feels warm or cold partly against the temperature you have just been feeling. A step up in life circumstances resets the level against which the next step is measured.

Sensory adaptation is easy to accept because everybody has a bathtub.

The interesting question is how far the principle travels.

There is a famous study from 1978 about lottery winners and accident victims, and it has been cited so often that many people know its supposed moral without knowing its actual results. Philip Brickman, Dan Coates, and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman compared twenty-two major lottery winners with twenty-two people who had not won, and both groups with twenty-nine people who had been paralyzed in accidents. The popular telling is that the winners were no happier than anyone else and that awful luck failed to make people miserable, proof that we are all on a hedonic treadmill and nothing we gain or lose changes the ride.

The study found something close to that for winners.

The winners were, as a group, not significantly happier than the controls, and they reported taking less pleasure from ordinary daily events: breakfast, a kind word, a good morning's work. The peak experience of the win raised the bar for what counted as pleasure, and ordinary life came up short against it.

But the accident victims tell the real story.

They reported lower present-day happiness than the controls, markedly so. Their past happiness, recalled after the accident, was higher than the controls' memories of their own pasts. Their present ratings were lower. They did not return to the baseline the controls occupied. Adaptation helped; they were not as unhappy as the psychologists expected them to be. But they had not adapted back.

The 1978 study is remembered as proof that adaptation is total. It is better read as proof that adaptation is real and partial, and that the popular summary swapped the two.

That distinction matters for the question of enough, so it is worth being precise about the machinery.

Adaptation is what makes gains and losses feel temporary. A raise improves people's satisfaction with life when it arrives, and then the same satisfaction more or less returns. A bigger house, a better view, a newer car, a more impressive title: each produces a bump, and each bump decays. Economists found this in large panel datasets. In the German Socio-Economic Panel, a representative sample of households followed year after year, satisfaction with life rose with income gains, and then much of the rise dissipated within about four years.

Brickman and Donald Campbell had given the phenomenon its famous name in 1971, a few years before the lottery study: the hedonic treadmill. Stand on it and run and you stay in place relative to the world, because the treadmill moves with you.

The strong version of the idea took shape as set-point theory. People have a characteristic level of happiness, partly heritable, and events push them from it temporarily before they return to it, the way a thermostat returns a room to a temperature after a door opens. Twin studies suggested a substantial genetic contribution to the stable part of well-being. The set point, in the strong theory, is where you end up after the excitement of any event wears off.

That theory has a stark implication: it does not matter much what you gain, what you achieve, or what you buy, because you will adapt back to wherever you started.

It also has a stark implication for this book.

If adaptation were total and inevitable, enough would be a non-problem. Nothing would leave a trace, so there would be nothing to conserve, choose, or give up understanding. Wanting more would be slightly sad but ultimately harmless, since its costs would dissolve along with its gains.

Neither implication survived contact with better data.

Starting in the early 2000s, researchers began analyzing large longitudinal panels, the same people measured repeatedly over many years, using methods that could separate what happens around an event from who a person was before it. The results corrected the strong theory in three ways at once.

First, people do not all share one set point. Baselines differ between individuals, partly on a temperamental basis, so the word "back to baseline" hides the fact that baselines are not the same for everyone.

Second, the same person appears to have more than one set point. Cognitive well-being, how a person steps back and judges their life, behaves differently from affective well-being, the emotional quality of everyday experience. An event can move one without moving the other. The income-and-happiness argument from the last chapter is the most visible version of this, but the separation generalizes.

Third, and most important, some events change the set point itself. Marriage, divorce, the death of a spouse, unemployment, and disability do not always produce a bump and a return. Some produce a lasting shift in the level around which a person's life satisfaction subsequently fluctuates.

The careful statement of the revised view comes from Ed Diener, Richard Lucas, and Christine Napa Scollon in a 2006 article titled, accurately, "Beyond the Hedonic Treadmill." They proposed five revisions to the older model: set points are not hedonically neutral; individuals differ in their set points; a person can hold multiple set points for different components of well-being; set points can change under some conditions; and individuals differ in how much they adapt at all.

The treadmill is real.

It is not a universal law.

What determines whether an event leaves a trace? The evidence points to several features, and they map unevenly onto the kinds of things people pursue.

Speed matters. Some events are processed quickly. Studies comparing how fast people return to their characteristic level found that marriage, on average, is absorbed within a couple of years. The happiness bump of a wedding is real and then it is gone, which sounds deflating until you remember that the return is not to the exact pre-wedding state for everyone.

The asymmetry matters more. Negative events tend to adapt more slowly and less completely than positive ones. Widowhood, the onset of serious disability, and long-term unemployment are associated with lasting changes in well-being that do not simply wash out. This is the finding that makes the "we all adapt to everything" slogan true in one direction and false in the other. We adapt more fully to the good.

That asymmetry should sharpen any conversation about enough.

If positive changes fade more quickly than negative ones, then the typical asymmetry of ambition, gain slowly and lose fast, is not neutral. A life that leans too hard on gains that will fade, and underweights the losses that will persist, accumulates the error in one direction only.

Income and status adapt differently, and this is one of the most useful findings in the literature for the chapters ahead.

In the German panel, income gains lost most of their effect on life satisfaction within roughly four years. Relative status did not adapt in the same way. A one-unit improvement in rank, prestige, or standing produced an effect that persisted across the follow-up, rather than dissipating like income.

People largely get used to more money.

They do not get used to being higher than someone else.

That single difference explains a lot about why ambition has so few natural stopping points. Money is a moving floor: what felt like a fortune last year is the baseline this year. Status is worse, because its value depends on comparison, and comparison does not adapt the way a bathtub does. The person who outruns the treadmill is a rarity; the runner can simply raise the reference point and start again.

The same evaporation does not happen in the other direction.

Entering poverty is not a bump that fades. Studies using long-run panel data found that people who fell below a poverty line experienced large declines in life satisfaction, and there was little evidence they adapted back within the observed window. Adaptation, in other words, is not evenly distributed across the income scale. Gains at the top get absorbed into a new baseline; losses at the bottom persist.

The adaptation data cuts one way before any philosophy gets involved: do not moralize scarcity. If the poor do not adapt, then asking a person at the bottom to "be content with enough" is asking them to accept a baseline that does not recede the way a rich person's does. Enough, for someone with unmet basic needs, is a very different creature from enough after satiety.

So what do we actually know?

Adaptation is real. Gains fade. New goods, new titles, new levels of comfort become the standard they measured against, and the next gain is judged from the new water level.

It is also partial. Not everything fades, and not equally. Some losses persist. Some wins persist for some people. There are consistent individual differences in how quickly and how completely people adapt: some people's well-being returns; for others the event leaves a lasting change.

The thing being acquired changes the answer. Affective experience adapts faster than reflective life judgment in many cases. Income adapts; status persists. Discretionary gains fade; security and basic-need losses do not.

And adaptation is not destiny. Because part of what fades is attention, some of what protects a gain is deliberate attention paid again later.

Sonja Lyubomirsky and Kennon Sheldon's hedonic adaptation prevention model builds directly on the observation that the gains most likely to persist are those that keep producing new experiences. Variety interrupts the process by which a steady good becomes an unnoticed good. Continued appreciation, noticing the good on purpose, acts as a kind of maintenance on the pleasure a purchase or achievement once produced. The model is careful not to promise permanent upgrades; it predicts slower decay, which is a less glamorous but more honest claim.

The practical upshot is not a happiness checklist. It is a distinction.

Some of what people buy is an attempt to raise a permanent level, and adaptation means the attempt mostly fails at the level of feeling. The same money could instead be spent on variety, on experiences with enough novelty to keep producing new moments, or on the negative space where adaptation is weak: security, health, autonomy, time.

A house you have lived in for eight years does not keep feeling like the first year.

A year of stable rent and savings does not stop supplying safety because you have gotten used to it.

The word "enough" has to be answered against this background. If satisfaction were fully adapted, enough would be a number you could look up. It is not. If satisfaction were fully unadapted, enough would be visible, the point where the slope flattened, and most people could find it by feel. It is not that either. The truth is in between, and it is a responsibility.

Because the baseline moves while you are standing on it, the question "Is more still worth it?" cannot be answered by consulting the last gain you received. The last gain is already absorbed. The relevant fact is what the next unit would do now, against the baseline you now occupy.

And because adaptation is partial, the answer is not always the same.

For some goods, at some levels, the honest answer is that the next unit will quickly join the background, and the real cost is whatever it displaces. For others, the kind that restore the bottom rather than raise the top, the next unit may keep paying out for years.

The last chapter called this the difference between absolute benefit, marginal benefit, and opportunity cost.

Adaptation is the reason that difference is so hard to apply in practice.

You cannot feel marginal utility directly. You feel the contrast between a new gain and the old baseline, and that contrast is exactly what adaptation then erases. The pleasure of the gain is a report about change, not about level. It tells you that something happened, not that your total has improved.

That is why the treadmill feels so convincing. It measures the only thing the body measures well: change.

Someone who becomes worse off feels it.

Acceptance after a loss can feel like recovery and be mistaken for gain.

And once a gain has been absorbed, no feeling remains to report that it is still there, even when it is.

So the practical skill of knowing when you have enough is partly a skill of correcting for your own adaptation. You have learned to stop feeling the water's warmth. The water is still warm. Deciding what counts as enough, for income, house, work, pursuit, often means learning to read the water level instead of trusting the feeling.

That is harder than it sounds, because the feeling is the only instrument most of us were issued.

And it is worth doing, because adaptation does not mean desire is pointless.

It means desire is a signal about change, not a report on the total.

Wanting something, getting it, and then calibrating to it is a normal way for a mind to work. The failure mode is not that you wanted the thing. It is that you believed the wanting was an accurate reading of your level, and that you spent the whole evening measuring how the water feels instead of checking whether the water level rose.

In the chapters that follow, the same shape keeps returning.

A promotion resets the comparison group, and the reset is a form of adaptation. For status, the baseline that moves is other people.

A bigger house fills with obligations, and the obligations are a form of adaptation: the thing you accumulate slowly stops being seen as part of the house and becomes part of the work of keeping it.

An engagement score, a followers' count, a return on capital, a territory, a list of features: each is a metric that can keep generating another unit. Each is a metric whose satisfaction fades on contact. The system that tracks the metric has no term in it for the fact that the baseline has moved, which is why the system can keep wanting more forever, even after the person inside it has stopped feeling anything at all.

Adaptation does not tell you to stop wanting.

It tells you that wanting will keep producing exactly one more step, because the step is measured against a baseline you are always standing on.

The treadmill is real, partial, and uneven: quick for gains like money, slower and more stubborn for losses, weak for status, strong for the ordinary pleasures that make up daily life.

Enough, against that machinery, is not the point where wanting stops.

It is the point where you stop treating the wanting as a meter and start judging it against what it is costing, including the fact that much of what you gain will, within a few years or a few weeks, simply join the water you no longer feel as hot.

The bath was always the right temperature for a while.

Then it is just your bath.
