# Saturated

*Too Smart To Meter*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

In September 1954, Lewis Strauss, chairman of the United States Atomic Energy Commission, stood before the National Association of Science Writers and made a prediction that followed nuclear power for decades. He imagined a future in which electrical energy would become so cheap that households would no longer bother measuring how much they used.

The line became more famous than the argument around it. “Too cheap to meter” sounded like the purest form of technological optimism: abundance arriving so completely that the measuring device itself became unnecessary.

History was less obliging. Electricity remained metered. Nuclear power became important without becoming free. The phrase survived anyway because it captured a recurring fantasy about technology. Push a capability far enough and scarcity disappears. Push the curve high enough and ordinary accounting breaks.

Artificial intelligence has revived that fantasy in a stranger form.

The cost of useful machine intelligence has fallen quickly. Systems that once required specialized infrastructure can now be called through an API, bundled into office software, run locally, or placed inside products that barely mention AI at all. But cheapness is not the most interesting measurement problem.

The more unsettling development is that some of the meters themselves are beginning to fail.

A benchmark is a meter. An exam is a meter. A leaderboard is a meter. A coding task with an automated grader is a meter. So is a job interview, a professional credential, a risk threshold, a productivity statistic, a test suite, a credit score, a safety case, and every other device that compresses a complicated capability into a number somebody can use to make a decision.

Meters work because the world stays inside their useful range.

Put a bathroom scale under a truck and the problem is not that the truck weighs exactly whatever number appears at the end of the dial. The problem is that the instrument has saturated. It can no longer resolve the thing placed on it. A thermometer at the top of its range does not prove the temperature stopped rising. A camera sensor blown white by too much light has captured less information, not more.

This book uses saturation in that technical sense.

Artificial intelligence is moving through measurement regimes faster than many institutions can rebuild them. Academic benchmarks that once separated leading models from the rest are now routinely described by researchers as saturated. New tests appear specifically because the old ones no longer discriminate. Some of those new tests start aging almost immediately. Public questions leak. Answers become searchable. Training data absorbs examples. Tool-using agents recognize the evaluation environment. Automated scoring rewards shortcuts. The score remains legible even after the meaning has begun to drift.

That last part matters.

A broken meter does not usually look broken from a distance. It often looks wonderfully precise.

The leaderboard still has decimals. The report still has confidence intervals. The dashboard still sorts models from top to bottom. The applicant still has a grade. The vendor still has a pass rate. The organization still has a KPI. The number can become more polished at the exact moment the underlying measurement becomes less trustworthy.

The easy response is to keep inventing harder tests. That is necessary and insufficient.

Every test contains a theory of what matters. An academic question assumes that answering the question tells us something useful about the system. A coding benchmark assumes that passing the test suite approximates producing a change a maintainer would accept. A time-horizon measure assumes that human completion time is a useful way to scale task difficulty. A safety evaluation assumes that behavior under the evaluation says something about behavior outside it.

Those assumptions can be good. They can also be temporary.

The central question in *Saturated* is therefore not whether artificial intelligence is “smart.” The word is too broad to carry the burden placed on it. The question is what evidence should count when familiar proxies stop separating the systems, people, and outcomes we actually care about.

That question moves quickly beyond AI laboratories.

Education depends on meters. Schools assign problems, grade answers, award credentials, and infer learning from performance. If a student can obtain a competent answer instantly, the old assignment may still measure something, but perhaps not what the institution thinks it measures.

Hiring depends on meters. Resumes, take-home exercises, interviews, degrees, portfolios, references, and work samples are all proxies for future performance. Generative systems can now help produce many of those signals. That does not make every signal worthless. It changes the cost of producing the signal and therefore changes what the signal means.

Management depends on meters. Revenue per employee, tickets closed, lines of code, calls handled, documents produced, time billed, and units shipped become inputs into decisions about compensation, staffing, and investment. When a small team can call large amounts of machine labor, headcount can become a poor denominator. When output becomes cheap, counting outputs can reward volume rather than consequence.

Safety depends on meters most of all.

A system that passes a test can still fail in a setting the test did not represent. A system that fails a test may still be useful if the failure is obvious, bounded, and recoverable. Reliability at ninety percent means something very different when the task is suggesting a headline than when it is changing production infrastructure. The average score hides the distribution of consequences.

This is the measurement problem hiding inside the intelligence debate.

The temptation will be to treat it as another story about exponential progress. That is not the argument. Progress can slow tomorrow and the metrology problem would remain. A benchmark can saturate because systems improve, because the questions leak, because the grader is weak, because the test was badly designed, because everyone optimizes against it, or because the capability being measured has moved somewhere the test does not look.

The strongest counterargument to this book is that none of this is new.

Tests have always been gamed. Metrics have always distorted incentives. Goodhart’s law predates large language models. Psychometrics has dealt with ceiling effects for generations. Software engineers have always known that a passing test suite does not prove a program is good. Economists have spent decades separating measured productivity from the underlying thing they care about. Scientists already know that instruments have ranges, noise floors, calibration requirements, and error bars.

All true.

What may be new is the speed at which the target is changing, the degree to which the tested system can participate in the testing process, and the number of institutions importing machine-generated performance into meters built for human-generated work.

A model can search the public web for material related to its own benchmark. An agent can inspect the repository containing its task. A system can sometimes infer that it is being evaluated. An organization can use one model to generate the work and another model to grade it. The same technology can sit on both sides of the measurement boundary.

That changes the geometry of the problem.

The meter is no longer necessarily outside the machine.

The chapters ahead begin with the most literal version of saturation: AI benchmarks that reach their ceilings, leak their answer keys, or lose their discriminating power. From there the book follows the measurement problem into longer tasks, real-world reliability, human supervision, education, hiring, management, insurance, and governance.

The goal is not to abolish measurement. Quite the opposite.

When a meter pegs at the top, the serious response is not to declare the truck weightless. It is to build a better instrument, with a wider range and a clearer understanding of what the number can and cannot tell us.

Artificial intelligence does not need to become infinitely smart for the old meters to fail.

It only needs to become too smart for the meter we brought.
