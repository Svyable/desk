# Protocols Get Better

A field should be judged partly by what it does after being criticized.

Remote-viewing research did not freeze in 1974. The obvious weaknesses of early work—monitor knowledge, transcript cueing, informal judging, small samples, target-pool structure—became part of the methodological conversation. Later experiments, particularly work associated with Edwin May at Science Applications International Corporation, were designed with tighter controls than the famous first-generation studies.

This is important because skeptical histories often make a subtle mistake of their own. They identify a flaw in an early experiment and then let that flaw explain the entire later literature without checking whether the procedure changed.

That is not skepticism. It is stale criticism.

The correct question is whether the effect survives the improved design.

The 1995 AIR evaluation focused heavily on this later work. Jessica Utts concluded that the statistical evidence from the best experiments was strong enough to support the existence of anomalous cognition. Ray Hyman agreed that the later experiments were better conducted than much of the earlier literature but argued that the evidence did not justify claiming a paranormal phenomenon. Their disagreement became famous because it offers both camps a credentialed champion.

The more useful fact is that protocol improvement did not produce a simple convergence to zero that ended the subject.

If every tightened control had made the effect disappear, remote viewing would be an easy historical chapter about leakage. The reason the subject remains awkward is that proponents can point to later datasets they argue remained statistically anomalous. Critics then have to move beyond the cartoon of sloppy 1970s experiments and ask harder questions about randomization, multiplicity, analytic independence, publication selection, effect-size stability, and whether the same laboratories and researchers generated too much of the evidentiary base.

This is progress, even if the final answer remains no.

A scientific claim becomes more interesting when the criticism has to become more specific.

The 2023 remote-viewing meta-analysis by Patrizio Tressoldi and colleagues illustrates the contemporary continuation of the argument. The authors reviewed studies through 2022 and reported an average positive effect across included remote-viewing tasks. That paper is Class One evidence that a published meta-analysis found such an effect under its inclusion and analytic choices. It is not Class One evidence that anomalous cognition exists.

Meta-analysis can aggregate signal. It can also aggregate shared methodological bias.

If many studies descend from similar protocols, research communities, scoring traditions, or publication incentives, their errors are not independent. A forest plot can look like replication while containing a family resemblance.

The cleanest next step is therefore multi-laboratory preregistered replication designed jointly by proponents and skeptics. Not a debate after the data. A protocol signed before the data.

The parties should agree on target generation, trial count, participant selection, blinding, exclusion rules, scoring, primary outcome, secondary outcomes, stopping rule, analysis code, and what result would change each side's belief. Raw timestamped responses should be deposited before target revelation through a cryptographic commitment or trusted third-party system. Targets should be generated after the response when the design permits, which closes an enormous class of ordinary leakage explanations.

That last feature is conceptually powerful.

If a participant completes and cryptographically commits a response before a future random target is selected, then ordinary sensory cueing about the target becomes impossible by construction. The claim becomes precognitive rather than merely clairvoyant, which is metaphysically more radical but experimentally cleaner in one specific respect.

Of course new confounds remain: random-number integrity, file handling, judge blinding, analytic flexibility, participant selection. The point is not to create a perfect experiment. It is to force the conventional explanation into narrower territory.

This is how frontier claims should be handled. Do not ask whether the subject is respectable. Ask what experiment would make ordinary explanations expensive.

Remote viewing has spent half a century in a peculiar position because some protocol improvements have been real while social replication has remained narrow.

The next improvement should therefore be institutional.

The experiment must leave the people who invented it.
