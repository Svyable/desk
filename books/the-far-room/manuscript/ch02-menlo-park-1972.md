# Menlo Park, 1972

Menlo Park was already a strange place to stage a quarrel about the limits of perception. The peninsula south of San Francisco was filling with electronics firms, defense contractors, semiconductor laboratories, and the institutional residue of wartime physics. Stanford Research Institute had the right smell for the Cold War: technical, contract-driven, close enough to academia to borrow prestige and close enough to government to understand classified curiosity.

Russell Targ was a laser physicist. Harold Puthoff had worked in electrical engineering and quantum electronics. They did not arrive from a Victorian séance room. That fact did not make their claims correct, but it mattered socially. A claim about anomalous information transfer sounded different when it came attached to oscilloscopes, shielding, statistical tests, and an institute accustomed to federal contracts.

By 1973 CIA documentation records a final report presentation on parapsychology studies at SRI. The report was to include work with Uri Geller and Ingo Swann. This is Class One evidence of agency engagement and experimental activity. It is not Class One evidence that Geller or Swann possessed paranormal abilities. That distinction becomes almost comically important later because the existence of a declassified CIA file is now often circulated as though classification were a peer-review process.

Classification means somebody thought the information should be controlled. It does not mean the information was true.

The early SRI period contains the ingredients that would define the field. There were unusually vivid subjects. There were experiments designed to remove direct sensory access. There were target descriptions interpreted after the fact. There were statistical arguments. There were critics alert to procedural leakage. And there was a sponsor operating under an adversarial premise: what if the Soviets are doing this too?

The 1974 Nature paper is worth reading slowly because it is neither the smoking gun its admirers sometimes make it nor the obvious joke its detractors sometimes prefer. It reports work on information transmission under sensory shielding and concludes that an unidentified perceptual channel can be studied in laboratory conditions. That is an extraordinary claim. Publication in Nature did not certify it; it exposed it.

Marks and Kammann's later critique and replication attempt targeted precisely the vulnerable bridge between raw descriptions and judged matches. If the judging material contains cues that allow target order or identity to leak, the experiment can produce apparently impressive matching without any remote perception. This is the recurring nightmare of free-response psi research. The response is rich enough to feel human, which is precisely why it gives the judge so many degrees of freedom.

A forced-choice experiment is ugly but clean. Guess red or black. Guess one symbol from five. The outcome space is simple. A free-response protocol is psychologically natural but statistically dangerous. A viewer can say cold, vertical, metallic, open, movement, water, echo, bright, angular. The target might be a dam, a shipyard, a skyscraper, a radio telescope, a bridge, an airport, or an industrial plant. Afterward, intelligent people can perform extraordinary acts of resemblance.

Proponents understood this and developed formal ranking methods. Critics replied that formal scoring cannot rescue contaminated data. Both were right in principle.

The deeper historical question is why the work survived these early methodological fights. Part of the answer is that the signal, if signal it was, did not vanish cleanly. Some later experiments continued to produce statistical departures from chance under protocols proponents argued were improved. That prevented the simple ending skeptics expected. Another part is that government programs do not require scientific consensus to continue. They require sponsors, budgets, plausible utility, and a reason not to close the door.

The Cold War supplied the last ingredient in abundance.

If anomalous cognition were real at even a weak level, it had obvious intelligence implications. Distance and shielding are not minor obstacles in espionage; they are the entire business. A sensor that ignored both would be absurdly valuable. No satellite orbit. No border crossing. No intercepted transmission. No recruited source. Just tasking and perception.

That promise created an asymmetry. The upside was enormous. The cost of a small exploratory program was trivial by defense standards. A rational sponsor could therefore continue testing a very improbable claim without being irrational.

This is how fringe ideas sometimes persist inside serious institutions: not because everyone believes them, but because disbelief is not quite cheap enough to become closure.

The lesson generalizes well beyond remote viewing. When the possible payoff is strategic surprise, the decision threshold for research can be much lower than the evidentiary threshold for belief.

Menlo Park was not proof that the mind could cross distance.

It was proof that distance between possibility and proof can itself attract money.
