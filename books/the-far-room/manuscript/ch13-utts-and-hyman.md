# Utts and Hyman

In 1995 the government finally staged the argument remote viewing had been waiting for.

The CIA commissioned the American Institutes for Research to evaluate the research and operational record of the program. Statistician Jessica Utts and psychologist Ray Hyman were brought in as prominent experts with sharply different priors. They reviewed the later laboratory work and wrote assessments that have since been quoted like rival scripture.

Utts concluded that the statistical evidence was strong enough that continued proof-oriented experiments were no longer the most useful question. In her view, anomalous cognition had been demonstrated and research should shift toward mechanism and conditions.

Hyman disagreed. He accepted that some experiments produced anomalous-looking statistical results and that later work was methodologically improved, but he did not believe the evidence established psychic functioning. He emphasized the need for independent replication and the possibility that unidentified methodological problems could remain.

The internet usually stops there.

It should not.

The interesting thing about the Utts-Hyman split is how much common ground existed underneath the opposing verdicts. Both took the data seriously enough to analyze. Both recognized the methodological history. Both treated the later experiments as more sophisticated than the early caricatures. Neither claimed that the operational program had delivered a magical, reliable spy sensor.

The dispute was about what burden remains after repeated statistical departures from chance.

Utts placed more weight on the improbability of the results under the null and on consistency across the selected experimental series. Hyman placed more weight on the danger of hidden methodological commonalities and the absence of robust independent replication by unaffiliated laboratories.

These are not merely philosophical temperaments. They correspond to two real failure modes in science.

The first failure mode is endless skepticism. An effect repeatedly appears under improved controls, but critics keep inventing unspecified flaws because the conclusion feels impossible. Science can protect itself from discovery by moving the goalposts.

The second failure mode is premature ontology. A research group produces stable anomalies across increasingly careful studies and begins treating the favored interpretation as established before outsiders reproduce it. Science can mistake local craftsmanship for universal reality.

Remote viewing may be one of the cleanest historical examples of the tension.

The AIR report also separated research from operations. That separation is essential. Even if one accepts Utts's scientific conclusion, it does not follow that the program was useful for intelligence. A small statistical effect under structured blind judging does not automatically scale to open-ended operational tasking. The AIR evaluation concluded that the operational record did not justify continued use as an intelligence tool.

That finding is often treated by believers as though it contradicts Utts. It does not have to.

Imagine a pharmaceutical compound that changes a laboratory biomarker reliably but has no clinically meaningful benefit. The laboratory effect can be real and the treatment useless. Imagine a visual illusion that is scientifically robust but offers no engineering application. Nature is full of effects that matter to theory more than to operations.

Remote viewing could occupy that category.

Or the laboratory anomaly could still be methodological.

The 1995 review could not end the question because the decisive experiment was sociological as much as statistical: independent groups with no investment in the program needed to reproduce the best effects under jointly agreed controls. That never happened at the scale that would force a broad scientific re-evaluation.

The story is therefore not that Utts proved psi and Hyman denied it. Nor is it that Hyman debunked the program and Utts was fooled.

The story is that two experts looked at an unusually strange dataset and disagreed about the remaining probability of ordinary explanation.

That is a respectable disagreement.

It also tells us what should have happened next.

Instead of another generation of arguments over the same files, the field needed adversarial collaboration: common protocol, independent labs, locked analysis, large sample, public misses, and a result threshold negotiated in advance.

Twenty-nine years after the AIR review, the most frustrating part is not that the debate survived.

It is that the obvious decisive institutional experiment was never made large enough to kill one side's favorite explanation.
