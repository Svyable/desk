# The Sheep-Goat Problem

Parapsychology has long had a vocabulary problem that is also a causality problem.

Participants who believe in psi have sometimes been called sheep. Skeptics are goats. The old literature contains reports that belief, expectation, personality, experimenter relationship, or altered state can correlate with performance. To an outsider, this can sound like the perfect escape hatch: the effect works better around people who believe in it and worse around critics.

That is not automatically absurd.

Psychological variables change ordinary performance constantly. Anxiety alters memory. Expectation alters pain. Rapport changes interview quality. Expertise changes visual search. If anomalous cognition is a human cognitive effect rather than a machine-like sensor, participant state might genuinely matter.

But a moderator becomes scientifically useful only when it predicts independent data.

The sheep-goat problem matters because it exposes a conflict between two kinds of replication. Physics prefers procedural invariance: give competent laboratories the same apparatus and they should recover the effect. Psychology often deals with populations, contexts, and interactions that make replication noisier. Psi claims sit at the extreme edge of that difficulty because the underlying effect, if present, is small and the proposed moderators can be socially entangled with the experiment itself.

An experimenter who expects success may behave differently. A participant who believes may persist longer, produce richer responses, or tolerate the strange task better. A skeptical experimenter may be more meticulous about controls—or create a colder session. These ordinary psychological pathways can generate moderator effects without anomalous information transfer.

So the experiment must distinguish them.

This is why automated protocols are useful but not sufficient. Automation can remove interpersonal cueing, yet it may also remove whatever social conditions proponents believe facilitate performance. That is not an argument against automation. It is a testable prediction. Run both conditions under preregistered procedures. If interpersonal warmth matters, measure it. If belief matters, stratify participants. If experimenter expectation matters, blind experimenters to the study hypothesis where possible and distribute identical protocols across labs with different orientations.

The field has occasionally done pieces of this. It has not done enough of it at the scale required to convert decades of moderator speculation into a stable causal model.

Remote viewing adds another complication: talent selection.

Government programs did not recruit random citizens forever. They looked for people who appeared unusually good. That makes practical sense. Intelligence agencies select pilots, linguists, cryptanalysts, and shooters for aptitude. But selective recruitment complicates scientific inference because apparent high performers can emerge from noise when many candidates are tested.

This is regression to the mean wearing a name badge.

Suppose one hundred people each perform twenty noisy trials. Several will look exceptional by chance. If the program selects the top five and follows them, their future average will likely decline even without fraud or methodological change. The decline can then be interpreted as loss of motivation, psi missing, hostile observers, or a mysterious effect. The boring explanation is selection.

The remedy is prospective validation. Identify high performers in one dataset and test them under a new frozen protocol with no overlap in targets or scoring. Repeat enough times to estimate individual reliability.

If a small subset continues to outperform chance across independent trials, the scientific problem changes dramatically. The question is no longer whether population averages contain a statistical wrinkle. It becomes why certain individuals repeatedly carry information under sensory isolation.

That would be worth taking very seriously.

If the high performers revert to chance, the talent story weakens.

The government history is full of named viewers because personalities are memorable. Names are dangerous data structures. They encourage us to evaluate a career through famous episodes rather than a complete trial record.

A modern study should anonymize that prestige during analysis. Viewer 17's celebrated submarine story should not affect how Viewer 17's next thousand trials are scored. The protocol should be capable of humiliating legends and discovering unknowns.

The sheep-goat problem is not whether belief creates reality.

It is whether a field can model human variability without turning every failure to replicate into evidence that the replicator was the wrong kind of human.

That is a much harder test of scientific maturity than any single spectacular session.
