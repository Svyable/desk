# The Misses in the Archive

The best remote-viewing document I know is not a famous hit.

It is a failure report.

A declassified 1982 training session, number 876, says post-session analysis indicated the viewer had very little correlation with the target. An earlier training record is blunter: the target images appeared to show absolutely no target correlation. The sentences are almost beautiful in their bureaucratic flatness.

No target correlation.

These documents do something that decades of argument rarely manage. They restore the denominator.

Anomalous claims are naturally selected for surprise. Nobody tells the story of the psychic who described a warehouse when the target was a waterfall. Nobody builds a documentary around the session in which the impressions were vague and wrong. The failed file remains in a box while the hit becomes a career anecdote.

Declassification can reverse that selection because archives preserve paperwork without regard to narrative quality. A file search returns the dull sessions too. That makes the STAR GATE collection more scientifically valuable than many later popular accounts by program participants.

Failure data answer questions success stories cannot.

How variable was performance within the same viewer? Did high-confidence sessions outperform low-confidence ones? Were certain target types easier? Did training improve average results or merely create more elaborate descriptions? Were misses recognized immediately, or reinterpreted after feedback? Did operational customers stop tasking after poor performance? Did the same viewers who produced famous hits also produce long stretches of ordinary noise?

A genuine anomalous effect should not require perfection. Human vision fails under fog. Radar fails under clutter. Memory fails under stress. The demand is not that remote viewing always work. The demand is that its failures follow a measurable structure.

This is where psi research has often been weakest conceptually. Explanations for failure can become too flexible. The viewer was tired. The target was boring. The target had poor entropy. The monitor interfered. Analytical overlay overwhelmed the signal. The participant lacked motivation. The experimenter was skeptical. The effect declined under observation. The phenomenon resisted exploitation.

Any of these could be true. A system with enough post hoc failure modes can also fit any data.

The solution is prediction. If fatigue matters, specify a fatigue measure before the session and predict the performance gradient. If target type matters, preregister the target categories and test them. If belief or interpersonal rapport matters, quantify it prospectively. If repeated trials suppress the effect, predict the curve before collecting the next dataset.

A theory earns flexibility by forecasting where flexibility will matter.

The misses are also essential for evaluating operational utility. Intelligence work does not care about metaphysical excuses. A source that fails unpredictably must be treated as unreliable even if the failures are philosophically fascinating. A commander cannot plan around an effect that announces its own weakness only after the target is revealed.

This distinction between scientific reality and practical utility is uncomfortable because it allows an odd middle position: remote viewing might produce a real laboratory anomaly while remaining useless for espionage.

That possibility should have received more attention.

The 1995 AIR review effectively moved toward it. Utts argued that the accumulated laboratory evidence supported anomalous cognition. Hyman resisted that conclusion. Yet the broader evaluation found insufficient operational value to justify continuing the program as an intelligence capability. One can disagree with either expert on the scientific question and still accept that an operational source has to clear a different bar.

The archive's failed sessions support this separation.

If an effect exists, the misses may eventually teach us more about it than the spectacular matches. They define the conditions under which whatever is happening disappears. In conventional science, that is often how mechanisms emerge. Superconductivity became intelligible through temperature and material dependence. Enzymes reveal themselves through inhibition and saturation. A psi effect that merely comes and goes according to narrative needs is not yet a scientific object.

Remote viewing needs a phase diagram.

Not literally temperature versus pressure, perhaps, but an empirical map of participant, target, protocol, time, feedback, confidence, and performance. Where does the anomaly persist? Where does it collapse? Which variables predict the collapse before the trial?

The old program never produced that map convincingly enough.

The failure reports are not ammunition for ridicule. They are invitations to do better science.

A field that hides its misses becomes a story industry.

A field that studies them may eventually discover what it has actually been measuring.
