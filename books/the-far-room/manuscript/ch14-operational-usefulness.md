# Operational Usefulness

An intelligence product does not get points for being ontologically interesting.

It gets points for changing a decision in the right direction.

That is why the operational record of STAR GATE should be judged more harshly than the laboratory record. The program was not merely asking whether human beings can produce above-chance descriptions under sensory shielding. It was sometimes being asked to help find people, characterize facilities, identify activities, and answer questions that mattered to government customers.

The 1995 operational review summarized more than two hundred tasks received from military organizations from 1986 into 1995. A later formal scoring effort generated accuracy and value ratings from forty tasks conducted by three viewers for five operational organizations.

This is a better evidence base than a collection of favorite stories, but it still faces a basic question: what would useful have looked like?

For a collection source, useful information should be specific enough to distinguish among live hypotheses, timely enough to matter, and reliable enough that analysts can weight it. It should ideally provide something not already encoded in the tasking context. Most importantly, it should have a known error profile.

A satellite image can be ambiguous, but the analyst understands resolution limits. A human source can lie, but the case officer can assess access and motivation. A signals intercept can be spoofed, but technical metadata and network context constrain interpretation.

Remote viewing offered descriptions without a mature error model.

That is lethal to calibration.

Suppose one viewer says a facility contains underground space, another says water, and a third says rotating machinery. An analyst knows the target is a suspected military installation. Underground space is common. Water may refer to cooling, coastline, pipes, or nothing. Rotating machinery could describe almost any industrial site. The product feels suggestive while remaining difficult to falsify.

The strongest operational cases should therefore be those in which viewers generated low-base-rate details that were unknown to the taskers at the time and later independently confirmed.

Those cases deserve close archival reconstruction. Many famous accounts do not yet meet that standard publicly because the original tasking, full transcript, contemporaneous evaluation, later confirmation, and conventional-information environment are not all available in one clean chain.

This is why testimony cannot substitute for records. A participant may accurately remember that a session astonished a customer. That is Class Two. The claim becomes much stronger if we can inspect what the viewer wrote before feedback, what the customer knew before tasking, and what later evidence established.

Operational intelligence is full of stories that become cleaner after success.

There is also a selection problem on the customer side. A unit may task remote viewers when conventional collection has failed, which means targets are unusually difficult and ground truth may be unavailable. Poor feedback then makes the source hard to evaluate. Conversely, a customer may remember the one session that happened to help and forget the nineteen that produced nothing.

The program's own formal customer evaluations were an attempt to escape this trap. The overall result did not show a source with sufficient accuracy and actionable value to justify continuation.

That conclusion should not be softened because the subject is exciting.

It should also not be exaggerated into proof that every operational session was useless. Weak aggregate utility and occasional striking hits can coexist. The real scientific question is whether the distribution of hits exceeds what selection, ambiguity, and chance would generate.

A modern operational test could answer this more cleanly than historical anecdotes. Give viewers real-world targets embedded in a prospectively scored decision environment. Analysts receiving the products should record decisions before and after seeing the remote-viewing material. Some analysts should receive sham material matched for style. The experiment should measure whether real viewing improves decisions relative to placebo information.

That is a brutally practical test.

It also sidesteps some metaphysics. If remote viewing helps, the decision metric should move. If it does not, an intelligence service should not care that a laboratory score remains slightly above chance.

Science may continue caring.

Spies should not confuse the two.
