# The Cueing Problem

In the folklore of remote viewing, the miracle happens at the viewer. In the laboratory, the miracle can happen anywhere information crosses a boundary.

That is why cueing deserves an entire chapter.

A viewer does not need to consciously cheat for a protocol to fail. An interviewer who knows something about the target can change cadence, repeat a question, become animated, or linger when the response moves closer to expectation. The viewer may pick up these signals without awareness. This is the old Clever Hans problem dressed in classified stationery.

The monitor can be blind and the experiment can still leak elsewhere. Target folders can differ physically. Randomization can be predictable. Transcripts can contain administrative markings. Judges can infer target order from comments. Feedback from previous trials can train participants toward the structure of a target pool. Analysts can ask leading follow-up questions. Operational customers can reveal what kind of intelligence problem they are working even when they conceal the exact target.

Every one of these pathways is ordinary. That makes them more important than any exotic mechanism until they are excluded.

The early SRI controversies demonstrated how devastating a small downstream cue can be. Marks and Kammann argued that transcript information in remote-viewing experiments allowed successful matching without paranormal perception. Targ, Puthoff, and defenders disputed aspects of the critique and later procedures were modified. The history is often retold as an ideological duel. A better reading is engineering: one group discovered a potential side channel, and the burden shifted to protocols that could survive it.

This is exactly how cryptography improves. Nobody says an encryption algorithm is metaphysically false because somebody found a timing attack. They close the timing attack and test again. Anomalous cognition deserves the same unsentimental treatment.

The difficulty is that remote viewing has no established physical channel whose signal can be isolated from noise. If a radar system performs better after shielding a cable, we understand why. If a remote-viewing experiment remains above chance after one cue is eliminated, we do not know whether we removed the relevant confounder or merely the easiest one to see.

That uncertainty creates a protocol arms race.

Double blinding becomes necessary. Independent target generation becomes necessary. Automated randomization becomes attractive. Responses should be time-stamped before target revelation. Judges should receive materials scrubbed of metadata and presented in randomized order. Scoring should be specified before the session. The target pool should be published or at least characterized. Analysis code should be frozen. Failed sessions should remain in the denominator.

These are not bureaucratic decorations. They are the experiment.

Operational remote viewing was much harder to protect. Intelligence tasking is intrinsically rich with context. A viewer working repeatedly with the same unit may know what kinds of problems are active. A monitor may know whether the customer is interested in hostages, facilities, narcotics, weapons, or fugitives. Even broad knowledge changes priors. If the United States is in the middle of the Tehran hostage crisis and a viewer is asked about a human target in Iran, a description of guarded buildings and anxious people has a different evidentiary value than the same description under a target-blind random protocol.

This does not make operational sessions worthless. It changes the comparison class.

The best intelligence systems exploit priors. An analyst should use context. But then the product cannot be cleanly interpreted as evidence for anomalous perception. The same feature that may improve utility damages the scientific test.

This is one of the deepest tensions in the program. Research wants the viewer ignorant. Operations want the viewer useful. Research removes context to estimate whether an effect exists. Operations add context because context can make weak information actionable.

A capability can therefore appear to improve as a spy tool while becoming harder to study as a phenomenon.

The cleanest answer is to separate the missions. Use rigorously blinded laboratory trials to estimate whether there is a residual information channel at all. Use operational trials only after that effect is characterized, and score them prospectively against concrete decision outcomes. STAR GATE repeatedly blurred those worlds because the same small community supplied both research and application.

That blending is understandable. It is also exactly the sort of arrangement that should make us cautious.

The cueing problem is not an accusation. It is a reminder that human beings are exquisitely sensitive instruments connected by more wires than we can see.

Before inventing a new sense, count the old ones carefully.
