# The Far Room

There is a temptation, when writing about remote viewing, to start with the most astonishing story in the file cabinet. A submarine described before it was photographed. A hostage located from thousands of miles away. A sketch that resembles the target after the envelope is opened. The temptation is understandable. Astonishment is efficient.

It is also the wrong unit of analysis.

The stranger fact is not that somebody once drew something that looked like a crane, a bridge, a gantry, a silo, a church, a road, a coastline, or a room. Human beings are prolific resemblance machines. The stranger fact is that agencies responsible for expensive satellites, signals intelligence, nuclear warning, foreign weapons analysis, and war plans spent roughly two decades repeatedly testing whether people sitting in quiet rooms could acquire information by a route not recognized by ordinary sensory physiology.

That happened. It is documented. The documentation does not prove psychic perception. It proves institutional attention.

This book is about the distance between those two sentences.

The public argument has usually been flattened into a referendum on belief. Either STAR GATE was a suppressed success and the government quietly demonstrated a human capability that would overturn neuroscience, or it was an embarrassing Cold War carnival kept alive by credulous officers and contractors. The declassified record is less convenient. It contains experiments that produced statistically interesting results, methodological vulnerabilities serious enough to alter interpretation, operational sessions that appear striking in retrospect, sessions with little or no target correlation, customers who sometimes found the material suggestive, and a final external review whose most famous experts agreed on more facts than their opposing conclusions imply.

I use four evidence classes throughout. Class One is documented fact: declassified tasking, contracts, reports, protocols, contemporaneous memoranda, published experiments, recorded evaluations. Class Two is credible but incomplete evidence: testimony from participants, retrospective accounts, operational anecdotes whose underlying files are partial, and claims that have some documentary support without an adequate independent test. Class Three is disputed allegation: stories of decisive intelligence successes, foreign breakthroughs, exotic mechanisms, or suppressed demonstrations that cannot be independently established. Class Four is conjecture: models offered to explain what the first three classes do not settle.

The categories are not a ranking of emotional plausibility. They are a way to stop one kind of evidence from dressing up as another.

The central question is narrower than whether minds can transcend distance. It is whether the remote-viewing record contains a residual effect that survives the controls needed to exclude cueing, judging artifacts, sensory leakage, optional stopping, target-set structure, selective memory, and post hoc matching—and, if it does, whether that effect can ever be made useful enough for an intelligence service that must decide what to believe before the answer is known.

A laboratory can tolerate a weak anomaly. Intelligence work cannot tolerate being wrong in the wrong direction.

That difference turns out to be the whole book.
