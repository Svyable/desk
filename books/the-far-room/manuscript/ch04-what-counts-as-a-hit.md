# What Counts as a Hit

Suppose I ask you to describe a place you cannot see.

You write: large open space, hard vertical surfaces, water nearby, metallic structure, movement, bright sky, curved form.

I reveal a photograph of the Sydney Opera House.

How did you do?

The answer feels obvious until you try to score it. Water nearby: yes. Large open space: arguably. Hard vertical surfaces: yes, but almost every built target has them. Metallic structure: maybe, depending on what counts as metallic. Movement: ferries? people? clouds? Curved form: certainly. Bright sky: photograph-dependent. A believer can see convergence. A skeptic can see generic language.

Both can be sincere.

Remote viewing lives or dies in the machinery that converts qualitative descriptions into quantitative evidence. The core problem is not that humans are dishonest. It is that humans are excellent at post hoc compression. Once the target is known, ambiguous fragments reorganize themselves around it.

The solution developed in serious remote-viewing research was blind judging. A judge who does not know the correct target compares a response against a set of candidate targets and ranks the matches. If the actual target is ranked first more often than chance predicts across many trials, the claim becomes statistical rather than anecdotal.

That is an enormous improvement. It is still not enough by itself.

Target pools matter. Imagine a pool containing a waterfall, a suspension bridge, a lighthouse, a stadium, and a cathedral. The targets are perceptually distinct. A few salient descriptors may separate them. Now imagine a pool containing five industrial buildings. The same response becomes harder to score. Experimental results can change depending on target heterogeneity, judge strategy, descriptor coding, and whether each response is matched independently or in a set.

Transcript handling matters too. Marks and Kammann's critique of early SRI work became famous because it showed how unintended cues in transcripts could aid matching. Even if the viewer had no access to the target, the judge might acquire structure from the record. The paranormal hypothesis then gets credit for an ordinary information channel opened downstream.

This is a general law of anomaly research: closing the front door means little if information leaks through the scoring room.

Statistics create another layer. A p-value can tell us how surprising a result would be under a specified null model. It cannot tell us whether the null model captures all the ways the experiment was actually conducted. Optional stopping, unreported pilot sessions, flexible exclusions, multiple analyses, and target-pool quirks can all make chance look more impressive than it is.

None of this means that every positive remote-viewing result is explained by leakage. It means leakage and analytic flexibility are competing models that must be experimentally squeezed until they cannot plausibly carry the effect.

This is why the later SAIC work reviewed in 1995 matters more scientifically than the most famous early stories. By then the researchers had lived through years of criticism. Procedures were more explicit. Jessica Utts, a statistician asked to assess the evidence in the American Institutes for Research review, concluded that the laboratory results represented a real anomalous effect. Psychologist Ray Hyman disagreed that the evidence justified that conclusion, while acknowledging that some results were statistically unusual and that the later experiments were methodologically stronger than much earlier work.

The disagreement is often presented as believer versus skeptic. That wastes the useful part.

The useful part is that they were arguing over a residual after many obvious flaws had already been identified. This is where anomalous science becomes interesting. If a claim survives the removal of crude leakage, the next task is not celebration. It is adversarial refinement.

A hit should therefore mean more than resemblance.

It should be a result generated under a protocol that fixes the target-selection method in advance, separates viewer from target information, prevents monitor cueing, prevents judge cueing, timestamps and preserves the raw response, specifies the scoring rule before outcome inspection, characterizes the target pool, includes enough trials for stable estimation, and reports the misses with equal fidelity.

For intelligence applications, the bar rises again. A laboratory judge can choose among five known candidate targets. An analyst in a crisis may face an open world. There is no answer sheet containing the correct submarine base alongside four decoys. The analyst must determine which fragments are true, which are false, and what action follows.

That is not merely a harder version of the same task.

It is a different statistical universe.

A laboratory effect can be genuine and still be operationally useless. A viewer might beat chance when forced into structured comparison but produce too many unsupported details for open-world intelligence. Conversely, an occasional extraordinary operational hit can coexist with no stable average capability if selection and memory preserve only the dramatic cases.

The entire STAR GATE story sits between those possibilities.

Before asking whether the viewer saw the target, ask what the experiment allowed us to call seeing.
