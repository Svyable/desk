# The Square Does Not Know

A square does not know it is a square.

Start there.

Not with enlightenment.

Not with God.

Not with neurons, Sanskrit, quantum mechanics, simulated universes, or the suspicion that reality has been hiding an instruction manual in sacred geometry.

Four lines will do.

A square is one of the most primitive things we can draw.

It has an inside.

It has an outside.

That distinction looks almost insultingly simple until you notice how much of organized existence depends on some version of it.

Here.

Not here.

Contained.

Excluded.

This side.

That side.

A cell begins with a problem like this.

Before anything recognizably alive can maintain its chemistry, repair itself, reproduce, sense, move, or join a larger organism, there has to be some meaningful distinction between the system and its surroundings.

The membrane is not decorative.

Boundary is part of the trick.

Erase the distinction completely and the organized system ceases to be the same system.

The logic survives at larger scales.

Skin.

Immune systems.

Organs.

Territories.

Categories.

Computer memory.

Legal persons.

Private rooms.

Names.

Every one of these is more complicated than a square, but each depends on drawing a line that makes one state different from another.

This is why the square at the bottom of the drawing matters.

Historically, square forms appear in chakra and tantric diagrams in contexts that carry specific religious and cosmological meanings. Those meanings deserve to be handled on their own terms. I am not going to pretend that geometry erases history.

But geometry can still be read.

And whatever else a square has meant, it is an unusually efficient picture of constraint.

Closed.

Bounded.

Located.

Cornered.

A thing occupying some region rather than every region.

Rotate it and the orientation becomes obvious. Stretch it and the distortion becomes visible. Break an edge and the enclosure changes.

It is object-like.

The square says:

**something is here.**

That is enough to begin.

It is not enough to become alive.

The square has a serious limitation.

It tells us almost nothing about transformation.

The symbol simply sits there.

So the drawing moves.

Above the square appears a crescent.

Now the geometry contains absence.

A crescent implies a circle that is partly present and partly missing. It is difficult to look at one without importing phase.

Waxing.

Waning.

Arrival.

Departure.

Fullness approaching.

Fullness receding.

The square gave us distinction.

The crescent gives us state.

And once there are states, there can be transitions between them.

That sounds small.

It is not.

A thing can be described by what it is.

A process has to be described partly by what it was and what it is becoming.

Life is full of this second kind of description.

Heartbeat.

Respiration.

Sleep.

Digestion.

Electrical oscillation.

Cell division.

Hormonal cycles.

Repair.

Growth.

Decay.

Nothing living is merely a thing.

A living system survives in part by refusing to remain materially identical to itself.

Matter enters.

Matter leaves.

Molecules are assembled and dismantled.

Cells die and are replaced.

Memories are altered by retrieval.

The pattern persists while the material participating in the pattern changes.

You are much less like a statue than like a whirlpool.

The whirlpool exists.

You can point to it.

You can measure it.

It can knock something over.

But the identity of the whirlpool does not depend on keeping the same water.

This gives us the second term of the formula.

First:

**BOUNDARY**

Then:

**CHANGE**

But change is everywhere.

A coastline changes.

A glacier changes.

A star changes.

A dead body changes.

Change by itself does not give us mind and it does not even give us life.

Something else has to enter.

Above the crescent is a triangle.

And the triangle points.

A square occupies.

A crescent cycles.

A triangle aims.

The moment direction enters the diagram, a new kind of question becomes available.

Not merely:

*What changed?*

But:

*Which way?*

Toward.

Away.

Increase.

Decrease.

Preserve.

Correct.

Return.

Living systems are saturated with direction long before they acquire anything we would recognize as conscious intention.

Bacteria move in ways sensitive to chemical gradients.

Plants orient growth around light and gravity.

Bodies regulate temperature, hydration, blood chemistry, and countless other variables without waiting for the person inhabiting the body to form an opinion about them.

We should be careful with the word *purpose*. It is too easy to smuggle a homunculus into any system that maintains itself.

But we do not need a little thinker hidden inside the cell.

Control is already strange enough.

A system can occupy a state.

Detect a difference.

Respond to the difference.

Reduce or amplify the difference.

Do it again.

The world now matters to the system in a new way.

Some conditions are compatible with continued organization.

Others are not.

Direction creates the possibility of failure.

That may be one of the most consequential developments in the history of organized matter.

A stone cannot misunderstand its environment.

An adaptive organism can.

It can move toward poison.

Fail to detect danger.

Spend energy at the wrong time.

Treat noise as signal.

Treat signal as noise.

Act as if the world were one way when the world is another.

To be wrong, in any useful sense, requires a relationship between an internal state and something beyond that state.

We can fight later about what deserves to be called a representation.

Philosophers should fight about it. Neuroscientists should be careful about it. Computer scientists should not assume that every variable in a control loop amounts to a miniature belief.

But the structural point remains.

The internal organization of the system begins to matter because of what is happening outside it.

Inside becomes coupled to outside.

Now look at the next symbol.

Two triangles.

One downward.

One upward.

Interpenetrating.

The previous symbol gave us direction.

This one gives us opposed directions occupying the same center.

That is different.

Up and down.

Acting and receiving.

Sensing and predicting.

Organism and environment.

Self and world.

Bottom-up and top-down.

The reason this symbol becomes interesting is not that a six-pointed star secretly encodes neuroscience.

It does not need to.

The interesting fact is that any sufficiently capable adaptive system has to solve some version of the same geometric problem.

It has to reconcile influences moving in different directions.

Incoming signals constrain what the system can sensibly believe.

Existing models change what the system notices.

Action changes the environment.

The changed environment generates new signals.

Memory alters interpretation.

Expectation changes attention.

Attention changes sampling.

Sampling changes evidence.

Evidence changes expectation.

Already the ladder is breaking.

This is not a clean ascent.

It is a loop beginning to tighten.

A system capable of surviving in a complicated environment benefits from compressing regularities in that environment.

Where is food usually found?

What follows this sound?

Which movement produced that visual shift?

What kind of object continues behind an obstruction?

Which face belongs to the person who usually returns?

What happens after I do this?

At some point it becomes convenient to use a dangerous word:

**model.**

Not necessarily a tiny picture in the head.

Not necessarily a symbolic map.

A model in the functional sense: organized internal states that allow a system to respond to what is not immediately given.

The future.

The hidden cause.

The missing object.

The likely consequence.

The next move.

Once a system can do this well, it inhabits more than the present stimulus.

It inhabits a world.

Not the world in total.

A world available from somewhere.

A tick has one.

A bat has another.

An octopus has another.

You have another.

Reality may not care about these boundaries.

Experience does.

A particular body samples a particular range of signals through particular receptors while carrying a particular history and a particular repertoire of possible actions.

The world for that organism is not simply everything that exists.

It is the subset of existence that can make a difference to that kind of system in that kind of body.

And then something happens that changes the problem completely.

The model begins to include the modeler.

This hand is closer than that wall.

This movement was caused by me.

That pain belongs to this body.

That memory happened to me.

That threat is approaching me.

That object can be reached by me.

That person is looking at me.

The self enters the map.

Or perhaps the map constructs the self.

That possibility should make us uncomfortable.

Because the intuitive story most of us carry around is that there is already a self in there somewhere, receiving information and making decisions.

A little witness.

A central executive.

The one who is having the experience.

But if the self is at least partly constructed from the same machinery used to model the body and the world, then the grammar changes.

The observer is not merely using the model.

The observer may be one of the things represented by it.

This does not mean the self is unreal.

A hurricane is not unreal because it is a dynamically maintained pattern.

A corporation is not unreal because no single molecule contains the corporation.

A melody is not unreal because it cannot be found inside one note.

"Constructed" is not another word for "fake."

It means we have to ask what maintains the construction, what it does, what can alter it, and what happens when the construction begins to model its own operation.

That is the next turn.

A system represents something in the world.

Then represents itself in relation to that thing.

Then represents something about the quality of its own representation.

I see.

I think I see.

I am not sure that I saw.

I know that I am unsure.

The levels are not infinitely clean. Human introspection is unreliable. Confidence can be wrong. We invent explanations after the fact. We mistake familiarity for certainty and certainty for truth.

But the capacity itself is extraordinary.

The system can inspect, estimate, report, or regulate aspects of its own cognitive activity.

This is the territory of metacognition.

And this is where the drawing becomes difficult to dismiss as merely a stack.

At the top is the element associated with Ajna, often translated through the familiar language of the "third eye."

That phrase invites supernatural theater.

Ignore it for a moment.

The cognitively interesting distinction is simpler.

There is a difference between:

**I see something.**

and:

**I know that I am seeing something.**

The second statement contains a representation of a representational act.

The model bends.

That is recursion.

And recursion is where this book is going.

Not because recursion automatically creates consciousness.

It plainly does not.

Software can call itself.

A thermostat can participate in feedback.

A camera can photograph a monitor displaying the camera's own feed.

None of those facts entitles us to announce that a conscious subject has appeared.

But recursion changes the architecture of the problem.

A system can now contain information about itself containing information about something else.

The world enters the organism.

The organism enters the model of the world.

The model enters a model of the modeling.

Then those models alter behavior.

Behavior changes the world.

The changed world becomes the next input.

The loop closes.

This is why the long curving line around the original stack matters more to me than any individual symbol.

It does not behave like a ladder.

It crosses.

Returns.

Folds around earlier regions.

The same line that appears to bound one level becomes part of the route toward another.

That is closer to the systems we actually inhabit.

Perception changes thought.

Thought changes attention.

Attention changes perception.

Memory changes prediction.

Prediction changes action.

Action changes the environment.

The environment changes perception.

The boundaries remain, but causation runs both ways.

Matter does not disappear when sensation appears.

Sensation does not disappear when cognition appears.

Cognition does not disappear when metacognition appears.

The earlier levels remain active inside the later ones.

Nested.

Entangled.

Dependent.

This gives us a first version of the formula:

**FORM → CHANGE → DIRECTION → INTEGRATION → MODEL → SELF-MODEL**

But the formula is incomplete.

Because the self-model can act.

A belief about a cliff changes muscle tension.

An imagined insult changes heart rate.

A memory changes a route through a city.

An equation changes the design of a bridge.

A law changes the movement of bodies.

A story changes an army.

Representation, once embodied in a system capable of action, pushes matter around.

So the final arrow points back:

**SELF-MODEL → ACTION → FORM**

Now close it.

**WORLD → MODEL → SELF-MODEL → ACTION → WORLD**

That is not proof of a metaphysics.

It is a causal loop.

And it gives us a different question from the one consciousness books usually begin with.

Not:

*What extra thing must be added to matter to produce mind?*

But:

**What happens when matter becomes organized enough to model the world, include itself in that model, model aspects of its own modeling, and use those representations to alter the world that produced them?**

That question will not solve consciousness by itself.

Good.

A question this old should not collapse because we found a clever diagram.

The square still has one advantage over us.

It does not have to wonder what it is.

We do.

And the fact that matter can be arranged into something capable of wondering about matter remains, even before we add anything mystical, one of the strangest facts in the universe.
