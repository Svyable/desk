# Chapter 1 research audit — a boundary is not yet an individual

## Why this pass exists

Chapter 1 currently gets useful narrative leverage from a simple move: the square becomes boundary, boundary becomes the first requirement of organized life, and the book can then climb toward regulation, modeling, self-modeling, and recursion. The opening is memorable, but it risks letting three different propositions slide into one another:

1. contemporary cells require selectively permeable membranes;
2. some form of compartmentation was probably important in the origin of cellular life;
3. drawing a boundary is enough to establish a biologically meaningful individual, much less the first rung toward mind.

The first two have substantial biological support. The third is exactly where the book should become more interesting.

## What the evidence supports

Pierre-Alain Monnard and David Deamer's review of membrane self-assembly argues that some form of compartmentation is a necessary prerequisite for maintaining interdependent molecular systems and for permitting the variation required for speciation. It also makes the mechanism concrete: amphiphiles can self-assemble into vesicles that retain products while allowing some environmental exchange. A boundary is useful not because it creates a metaphysical inside by fiat, but because selective permeability changes what chemistry can persist together.

- Monnard, P.-A. & Deamer, D. W. (2002), “Membrane self-assembly processes: Steps toward the first cellular life,” *The Anatomical Record* 268(3):196–207. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.10154
- PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12382318/

Deamer's later review keeps the claim appropriately conditional: if cellularity was required for life's origin, a source of amphiphilic compounds capable of assembling into compartments was necessarily required. That is stronger than saying membranes are decorative, but weaker than claiming that a membrane by itself supplies life or individuality.

- Deamer, D. (2017), “The Role of Lipid Membranes in Life's Origin,” *Life* 7(1):5. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/life7010005

## The counterexample the chapter needs

A vesicle can have a perfectly good inside and outside without being alive. Compartmentation is therefore not a sufficient transition from matter to organism. The square can do less work and become a better symbol.

That matters because the present chapter says that “every” example in a heterogeneous list — cells, skin, immune systems, territories, categories, computer memory, legal persons, private rooms, names — depends on a line that makes one state different from another. As metaphor this is suggestive. As mechanism it is too permissive: the same abstraction covers a lipid bilayer, a jurisdiction, a category boundary, and a name only by discarding most of what makes each boundary causally distinctive.

The stronger biological story is **selective coupling**. A living membrane does not merely separate. It regulates exchange, supports gradients, hosts receptors and transport machinery, and participates in the maintenance of the organization it bounds. Even primitive-membrane research is interesting precisely because the boundary must be permeable enough to admit useful molecules while retaining products and allowing internal chemistry to persist.

That gives Chapter 1 a sharper progression:

> The square can distinguish here from there. Biology needs a stranger boundary: one that separates without severing, preserving an inside by controlling its traffic with the outside.

This is not proposed manuscript wording; it is the factual/narrative burden the revision should satisfy.

## A second counterargument: which boundary?

Later theoretical work is useful because it prevents the chapter from equating a visible membrane with the full boundary of an autonomous system. Kirchhoff et al. model biological organization with nested statistical boundaries (Markov blankets) and explicitly argue that such system boundaries need not be co-extensive with an organism's biophysical surface. That framework is influential but theoretical, not an empirical discovery that settles organism or mind boundaries.

- Kirchhoff, M. et al. (2018), “The Markov blankets of life: autonomy, active inference and the free energy principle,” *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 15:20170792. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2017.0792

The framework also has direct criticism. Menary and Gillett argue that neither the Pearl nor Friston versions of a Markov blanket is sufficient to demarcate the boundary of the mind. That is valuable counterevidence for *The Formula*: a mathematically definable statistical boundary should not be laundered into a solved ontology of selfhood.

- Menary, R. & Gillett, A. J. (2022), “Markov blankets do not demarcate the boundaries of the mind,” *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 45:e201. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X22000371

## Publication burden for Chapter 1

Before the square becomes the book's first causal step, the chapter should distinguish at least four senses of boundary:

- **geometric boundary:** an edge that partitions a drawing;
- **physical compartment:** a structure that restricts diffusion or movement;
- **biological boundary:** a selectively coupled interface participating in the persistence of an organism or protocell;
- **statistical/cognitive boundary:** a model-dependent partition used to describe conditional dependence, autonomy, or cognition.

The chapter does not need a taxonomy on the page. It does need prose that stops the reader from mistaking these senses for interchangeable evidence.

A useful concrete calculation/example for the eventual revision would compare a passive lipid vesicle with a contemporary cell membrane: what crosses unaided, what requires channels or pumps, how gradients are maintained, and what fails when selective permeability collapses. That would let mechanism replace the current rapid list of metaphorical boundaries.

## Strongest counterargument to the book's use of the square

The square may be doing no explanatory work at all. Once “boundary” is abstracted enough to include membranes, countries, names, categories, legal persons and statistical partitions, almost any organized phenomenon can be made to fit. The sequence may therefore be a retrospective mapping imposed on the source diagram rather than a discovery about emergence.

The book should allow that objection to win if later stages require similarly elastic reinterpretation. The way to survive it is not to insist that boundary is universal. It is to make each transition pay a mechanistic bill. For this first stage, that bill is selective coupling and maintained compartmentation, not mere enclosure.

## Editorial consequence

Do not broaden Chapter 1. Narrow it. Keep the excellent opening object — the square — but cut or compress the catalog of skin / immune systems / territories / categories / computer memory / legal persons / private rooms / names. Move quickly into the biological fact that makes a membrane unlike a drawn box: life must remain materially open while maintaining enough separation to preserve gradients, reactions, and organization.

That creates a better live question for the next move in the chapter: **how can something keep an identity when the material inside the boundary is continually being exchanged?** The crescent/change section can then answer a pressure generated by the square section rather than simply becoming the next symbol in a prepared ladder.
