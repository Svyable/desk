# 17. The Experiment the Storm Owes Us

The field has enough observations to stop being satisfied with discovery language.

The decisive next experiment should be prospective, synchronized and deliberately adversarial among mechanisms.

Choose storm regions with frequent deep convection and existing lightning-mapping infrastructure. Deploy a layered network: ground gamma-ray and neutron spectrometers; electric-field instruments; broadband VLF/LF and fast antennas; optical photometers; radar access; at least one high-altitude aircraft or balloon platform; and coordinated satellite overpasses when available. Synchronize clocks to microsecond accuracy or better and publish timing calibration as data, not metadata.

Before observing, register mechanism-specific predictions.

A leader-dominated TGF model should predict a tight relationship between leader development, field enhancement and gamma onset. A large-volume relativistic-feedback model should permit or require gamma activity before ordinary leader signatures under defined field conditions, with specific spectral and pulse behavior as feedback gain approaches unity. A hybrid model should state where the transition occurs. Photonuclear events should predict neutron/de-excitation signatures and delayed isotope annihilation components when photon spectra cross threshold with sufficient fluence.

Then blind the analysis where blinding is possible. One team reconstructs radiation events without lightning labels. Another reconstructs lightning stages without gamma triggers. A third performs joint association under preregistered timing and spatial windows. Event classification changes only under documented rules.

The central dependent variable should not be 'number of interesting events.' It should be predictive likelihood across a held-out storm set.

Measure nulls aggressively. Storm volumes with strong electric fields but no high-energy emission are informative. Lightning leaders without TGFs are informative. Gamma glows that terminate without lightning are informative. Weak convection provides controls. Different storm types supply natural perturbations.

For aviation, add passive calibrated spectrometers to routine aircraft outside convective cores and research aircraft near them, so hazard can be translated into encounter distributions. For isotope physics, deploy neutron-sensitive detectors and capture delayed spectra long after the fast trigger.

The source data should be public after calibration and safety review. High-energy storm physics has benefited enormously from cross-instrument reanalysis; opaque event curation would recreate the threshold problem this book criticizes.

No single observation will 'solve lightning.' That phrase is a grant-writing trap. The achievable goal is more useful: determine which high-energy discharge mechanisms operate, their parameter regimes, how much charge and energy they move, and whether they predict transitions in storm electrical behavior.

The storm does not actually owe us this experiment. We owe ourselves an instrument suite capable of noticing what the storm has been doing.
