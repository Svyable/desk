# 11. Which Came First

Lightning and gamma rays occur close enough together that causality can hide inside clock error.

This is not a philosophical complaint. It is an instrumentation problem measured in microseconds.

ASIM was built to observe high-energy radiation and optical lightning from the same platform. Early results found strong statistical support for TGFs being produced during upward leader propagation just before a large current pulse heats the channel and creates a strong optical pulse. A later analysis of 221 TGFs and associated optical data found that TGF onset was before or at the same time as optical-pulse onset after accounting for cloud scattering, with some events showing delays too long to explain by scattering alone.

A 2024 ASIM study reported a bright long-duration TGF whose gamma emission was clearly distinct from and earlier than the associated optical current pulse. The authors concluded that the current surge producing the optical pulse could not itself be the generator of that TGF; the surge might instead have been conditioned by the high-energy discharge or helped terminate it.

This is a small causal reversal with large implications.

For years, the intuitive sentence was 'lightning produces TGFs.' It remains true for some event architectures if 'lightning' means the leader process broadly enough. But a bright optical/current pulse can be effect rather than cause relative to the gamma emission. The storm can enter a high-energy discharge state before it enters the visible state our eyes recognize.

This does not mean gamma rays 'cause lightning' in general. That claim is too coarse. Energetic particles can alter ionization and conductivity; feedback discharges can modify fields; leaders can trigger or enhance runaway avalanches; avalanches may in turn change local conditions for leaders. Causality may be reciprocal rather than one-directional.

The experimental requirement is therefore a causal graph, not a slogan. Nodes should include large-scale electric field, runaway-electron population, gamma emission, positron/backscatter feedback, streamer/leader propagation, narrow bipolar events, current surge, optical pulse and field collapse. Each candidate mechanism predicts a partial ordering and measurable delays.

The difficulty is cloud scattering. Optical photons can be delayed and smeared. Absolute spacecraft timing can have offsets. Radio source location has finite uncertainty. Gamma rays propagate differently. A few hundred microseconds of calibration error can reverse a causal sentence.

The field has improved because instrument teams have treated timing calibration as physics rather than housekeeping.

That is a useful lesson far beyond thunderstorms. When two dramatic observables occur almost together, the measurement of 'almost' may contain the mechanism.
