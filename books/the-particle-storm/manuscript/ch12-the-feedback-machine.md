# 12. The Feedback Machine

Feedback is where the storm stops being merely a target for energetic particles and starts looking like a device assembled by no engineer.

In the relativistic feedback model, a forward avalanche of energetic electrons emits bremsstrahlung photons. Some photons scatter backward. Others create electron-positron pairs. Positrons can be accelerated opposite the electron avalanche by the electric field, travel substantial distances, and seed new runaway electrons near the upstream end of the region. Backscattered photons can do the same through Compton interactions or photoelectric processes. New avalanches produce more photons and positrons. Above a feedback threshold, the process can sustain or amplify itself.

The geometry is exquisite because the charge sign matters. The field that accelerates electrons one way accelerates positrons the other. Antimatter becomes part of the return path.

This is not perpetual motion. Energy comes from the storm's electric field, which comes from convective charge separation. Feedback converts stored electrostatic energy into energetic particles, ionization and radiation. A sufficiently intense discharge should therefore alter the field that powers it.

Dwyer's 2025 simulations emphasize precisely this point: energetic-particle ionization can partially discharge some regions while intensifying field at a propagating front. Under rapid charging, feedback glows become dynamic and can develop into pulse trains. The process can potentially seed narrow bipolar events or lightning.

The model makes the storm sound computational: charge, threshold, amplification, saturation, reset. Resist that metaphor just enough. The particles obey local field equations and stochastic collision physics, not a program. Yet the system-level behavior really can include threshold, gain and feedback.

A decisive field campaign would need to measure the gain. If a gamma-ray glow brightens by a factor, what happens to the local electric field before, during and after? Does the inferred ionization current account for the field change? Can a reconstructed feedback factor cross unity before a pulse train appears? Do positron-sensitive or spectral signatures change at the transition?

This is where theory should become embarrassingly quantitative. 'Feedback may matter' is easy. Predicting a threshold surface in field-density-length space, then watching storms cross it, is hard.

The atmosphere offers repeated trials, which is both gift and curse. Every storm differs. Temperature, altitude, charge geometry, hydrometeor structure and seed-particle flux vary. The solution is not to wait for a perfect natural laboratory but to build enough synchronized observatories that storm diversity becomes a parameter sweep.

Nature has already provided the high voltage. We still need the oscilloscope.
