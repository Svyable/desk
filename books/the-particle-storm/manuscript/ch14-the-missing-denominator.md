# 14. The Missing Denominator

How many terrestrial gamma-ray flashes occur each day?

For years, a commonly repeated estimate was about five hundred worldwide. The number was never a census. It was an inference from instruments that see only a fraction of storms, over limited geometries, through strong atmospheric attenuation, with trigger thresholds optimized for bright bursts.

Weak-event aircraft observations now make the old estimate less useful as a statement of the underlying population. Some events with source brightnesses of 10^12–10^15 photons would be invisible from orbit even when a space detector views the same region. Gamma-ray glows can persist over large storm areas. Flickering events create another class of subthreshold activity.

The denominator problem is not merely 'there are more than we thought.' It is that *what counts as an event* becomes unstable when sensitivity improves.

A TGF detector with a fixed trigger asks whether counts exceed a threshold within a window. A nearby aircraft can resolve substructure and see events that merge or vanish at orbital distance. A ground array samples downward emission through different atmospheric columns. A glow has no obvious unit count if it flickers across a storm for hours.

The correct population object may therefore be a distribution over emitted energy, duration, altitude, direction and storm state rather than an event rate.

This matters for every downstream claim. Global isotope production requires integrated high-energy photon fluence, not TGF count. Aviation exposure requires spatial fluence at flight levels, not satellite triggers. Lightning causality requires timing relative to discharge stages, not daily global rate. Spacecraft particle-beam encounters depend on geomagnetic connectivity.

The field needs something analogous to a luminosity function in astronomy: a corrected source distribution incorporating detection efficiency. ALOFT provides an anchor close to source regions. ASIM and Fermi provide broad orbital exposure. Ground arrays provide downward and winter-storm samples. Lightning networks provide a denominator of electrical activity. Radar provides storm volume and phase.

The statistical work is ugly because each detector selects different physics. That is why it is important.

An attractive but dangerous conclusion is that 'every thunderstorm glows.' The 2024 tropical campaign found gamma-ray glows common in the storms sampled; that does not establish universality across continental, tropical, maritime, winter, shallow or weak convection. The source function likely depends strongly on charge geometry and altitude.

The book's recurring rule applies: a surprising phenomenon becomes more scientifically valuable when the numerator gets less mystical and the denominator gets more honest.
