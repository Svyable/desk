# 6. The Minute After Lightning

A millisecond is easy to romanticize. A minute is harder.

The delayed 511-keV emission in the 2017 Japanese event lasted long enough for the radioactive cloud to move. That fact gave the investigators geometry. Positrons were not simply being produced at the detector by an instantaneous shower. The beta-plus-emitting isotopes occupied air, and the air moved with the storm.

A nuclear decay clock therefore became an atmospheric tracer.

This is one of the neglected pleasures of the result. The isotope is not only proof of a reaction; its lifetime couples nuclear physics to meteorology. Wind advects the activated volume. Gamma-ray attenuation depends on distance. The changing annihilation signal contains information about where the radioactive material was created and how it passed relative to the detector.

The experiment also shows why temporal resolution should span absurdly different scales. The initiating TGF-like flash can unfold in microseconds. Neutron capture signatures evolve over milliseconds. Nitrogen-13 decay continues for minutes. Storm charge structures evolve over seconds to minutes. Radar volumes update on still another cadence. If one instrument stores only the immediate trigger window, the causal story is amputated.

A future high-energy storm observatory should therefore behave less like a single detector and more like a forensic timeline. Fast scintillators for gamma rays. Neutron-sensitive channels. Spectroscopy around annihilation and de-excitation features. Electric-field mills. broadband RF. Lightning mapping arrays. Optical photometers. Doppler radar. Meteorological state. If feasible, airborne or balloon sampling to constrain isotope transport.

The point is not instrumentation maximalism. It is causal discrimination. A leader-generated burst, a large-volume relativistic feedback event, a photonuclear afterglow and a drifting isotope cloud predict different time-frequency-spatial signatures. The storm can answer only the questions for which timestamps are synchronized.

This chapter is also where a seductive claim must be killed early. Detection of nitrogen-13 and positrons does not imply that ordinary thunderstorms leave dangerous radioactive zones on the ground. The isotopes are short-lived and the measured quantities are small compared with familiar radiological scenarios. Risk claims require dose, frequency, geometry and population exposure. 'Radioactive' is not a dose unit.

The better implication is scientific. Natural systems can move across domains faster than our institutions do. A lightning physicist needs nuclear decay data. A nuclear physicist needs cloud motion. A detector engineer needs meteorology. An atmospheric chemist needs the high-energy source function.

The minute after lightning is where those fields finally have to share a clock.
