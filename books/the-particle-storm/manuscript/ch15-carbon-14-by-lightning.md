# 15. Carbon-14 by Lightning

Carbon-14 is usually introduced as a cosmic-ray gift.

High-energy particles strike the atmosphere, generate neutrons, and nuclear reactions convert nitrogen into radiocarbon. That production underwrites radiocarbon dating and records solar and cosmic events in tree rings. Thunderstorm photonuclear reactions add another pathway to the atmospheric nuclear ledger.

The key reaction observed in the Japanese lightning event involved nitrogen-14 losing a neutron to become nitrogen-13. Related neutron production and subsequent reactions can contribute to carbon-14. The existence of the channel is not controversial. Its global significance is.

This is where novelty routinely outruns arithmetic. Once a paper demonstrates a previously underappreciated natural isotope-production mechanism, headlines ask whether dating systems, climate proxies or atmospheric chemistry must be revised. Usually the correct first response is: integrate the source.

How many qualifying high-energy events occur? What is the photon spectrum above photonuclear thresholds? How many neutrons are produced per event? At what altitudes? What fraction enters carbon-14-producing channels rather than capture elsewhere? How does the resulting production compare with the well-characterized cosmic-ray background, both globally and locally?

The answer may be 'small globally, interesting locally.' That is not scientific disappointment. Localized radiocarbon production tied to intense thunderstorms could become a tracer of extreme atmospheric electricity or a correction in specialized contexts even if it does not move the global budget.

There is also a deep-time implication. Lightning activity depends on climate, atmospheric composition, continental configuration and convection. On early Earth or other planets, photonuclear thresholds, density profiles and storm potentials would differ. A minor modern isotope source could have different relative importance elsewhere.

That remains Class IV until models earn it.

A robust program would combine measured TGF/FGF/glow source spectra with Monte Carlo photonuclear transport and isotope chemistry, then validate against direct neutron and annihilation observations. Ice cores and tree rings are probably too integrated and noisy to attribute ordinary storm contributions event by event, but targeted atmospheric sampling after intense winter lightning might test local production.

The larger lesson is that the atmosphere is not chemically downstream of particle physics. High-energy radiation can alter nuclei, and the products re-enter atmospheric chemistry. The boundary between weather and nuclear geochemistry is real only in university departments.
