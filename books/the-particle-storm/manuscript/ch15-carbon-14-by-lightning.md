# 15. Carbon-14 by Lightning

Carbon-14 is usually introduced as a cosmic-ray gift.

That description remains basically right.

High-energy cosmic rays strike the atmosphere, build particle cascades and produce neutrons. Once slowed, some of those neutrons are captured by nitrogen-14 through the reaction

`14N(n,p)14C`.

The radiocarbon oxidizes and enters the carbon cycle. This background process is why carbon-14 exists in living material at all, why radiocarbon dating works, and why sharp production excursions such as solar-particle events can be preserved in tree rings.

Thunderstorms add a second neutron factory.

That fact is now experimentally grounded.

The February 2017 winter-lightning event observed by Teruaki Enoto, Yuuki Wada and colleagues in Japan showed that a lightning-associated gamma-ray flash reached photonuclear energies. Gamma rays above the threshold for nitrogen photodisintegration drove

`14N(γ,n)13N`.

The immediate products included energetic neutrons and radioactive nitrogen-13. The neutrons moderated and interacted in the atmosphere. The nitrogen-13 beta-plus decayed, producing positrons whose annihilation generated the delayed 511-keV signal that made the event such a beautiful chain of evidence.

That event does **not** mean the nitrogen-13 becomes carbon-14.

This distinction is easy to blur because the isotopes sit next to each other in the story. The demonstrated `14N(γ,n)13N` reaction is one photonuclear branch. Lightning-related carbon-14 is primarily a **secondary neutron-capture problem**: neutrons produced by the high-energy gamma field can slow down and some can convert atmospheric nitrogen through `14N(n,p)14C`.

So there are two ledgers.

The event ledger asks whether lightning can create photonuclear neutrons.

Yes.

The planetary ledger asks whether those neutrons produce enough carbon-14, often enough, to matter beside the continuous cosmogenic source.

That answer is much less dramatic.

## Put the cosmic denominator on the page

A modern Monte Carlo calculation of atmospheric cosmogenic production by Kovaltsov, Mishev and Usoskin estimated a global modern carbon-14 production rate of about **1.64 atoms per square centimetre per second**, rising to about 1.88 atoms cm⁻² s⁻¹ for the preindustrial epoch in their model.

That number is the denominator every lightning-radiocarbon claim has to face.

It is not a local detector count. It is an area-normalized global source integrated over the atmospheric cosmic-ray cascade.

Multiplying 1.64 atoms cm⁻² s⁻¹ by Earth's surface area gives an annual production scale on the order of **10²⁶ carbon-14 atoms per year**.

The exact number depends on which production model, solar modulation and epoch one chooses. The scale is what matters.

Lightning does not merely have to produce carbon-14.

It has to compete with a nuclear factory that is running over essentially the entire atmosphere all the time.

## An old estimate that is still useful

Long before the 2017 Japanese event closed the photonuclear observational loop, L. P. Babich and collaborators asked whether reported thunderstorm neutrons could matter for radiocarbon.

A 2007 estimate used an assumed global lightning rate, a fraction of flashes producing neutrons, and a neutron yield per qualifying discharge. Under those assumptions, the estimated thunderstorm carbon-14 production was about **two orders of magnitude below** the production attributed to cosmic irradiation when averaged globally.

That was not a measurement of the modern thunderstorm source.

It was a model built on uncertain neutron yields and an uncertain fraction of neutron-producing lightning.

Its usefulness is conceptual: even relatively spectacular event-level nuclear physics can average into a minor planetary source when the event is rare.

The relevant expression has the shape

`global lightning production ≈ lightning rate × qualifying fraction × neutron yield × 14C yield per neutron`.

Every factor can move by orders of magnitude.

That is why one dramatic gamma-ray flash cannot settle the global question.

## The 2017 argument moved the question locally

Babich revisited thunderstorm radiocarbon production in 2017 using the much larger body of gamma-ray and neutron observations accumulated since the earlier estimate.

The conclusion was deliberately unresolved.

Available neutron and gamma-ray data were not sufficient to give a final global thunderstorm contribution. But in regions of severe thunderstorm activity, the neutron fluence required for thunderstorm radiocarbon production to become comparable to the cosmic source was not obviously inconsistent with measured or modeled thunderstorm neutron fluences.

That sounds like a contradiction with “globally small.”

It is not.

Lightning is geographically concentrated.

Cosmic-ray production is global and modulated by latitude, altitude and geomagnetic shielding. Thunderstorms cluster strongly over particular land regions and seasons. A source that contributes only a small fraction to the global atmospheric inventory can temporarily matter in a small volume of air or a local deposition environment.

The correct distinction is therefore:

**global inventory significance** versus **local event significance**.

Those are different hypotheses and need different observations.

## What the 2017 Japanese event actually lets us calibrate

The Enoto event is valuable because it replaces several speculative arrows with measured ones.

There was a high-energy gamma flash.

A short neutron-related radiation component followed.

A longer annihilation component appeared with the 511-keV signature expected from positrons.

The time evolution and spectra were consistent with photonuclear production of unstable atmospheric nuclei and subsequent beta-plus decay.

Later Wada and colleagues modeled the reaction and propagation chain with evaluated nuclear data, extending the accounting beyond the headline reactions.

This gives the global radiocarbon problem a more disciplined route.

For a measured event, infer or constrain the gamma-ray spectrum and angular distribution.

Use photonuclear cross sections to estimate neutron production in nitrogen and oxygen.

Transport those neutrons through realistic air density and humidity.

Calculate moderation, escape and competing captures.

Count the fraction entering `14N(n,p)14C`.

Then repeat over a representative population of storms rather than multiplying one exceptional winter event by every lightning flash on Earth.

That last step is the trap.

The high-energy tail of thunderstorm activity is not every lightning stroke.

## The missing denominator is the event population

By 2024 and 2025, airborne work such as ALOFT had radically expanded the observed phenomenology of high-energy thunderclouds. Gamma-ray glows, flickering gamma-ray flashes and TGFs appear embedded in a more dynamic electrical environment than the early satellite-trigger view suggested.

That should make us more interested in the isotope budget.

It should not make us assume the budget is larger.

We need the distribution:

`P(gamma spectrum, duration, altitude, geometry | thunderstorm)`.

From that distribution we can derive the fraction of storms whose photons cross photonuclear thresholds with enough fluence to matter.

Then radiocarbon production becomes an integral over the actual storm population instead of an argument from spectacular examples.

A useful future campaign would therefore pair aircraft gamma spectroscopy with ground lightning mapping, neutron-sensitive detectors and atmospheric transport modeling in the same storms. The aim would not be merely to find more photonuclear events. It would estimate the **rate distribution** of neutron-producing episodes.

That is the denominator the radiocarbon question lacks.

## Could lightning contaminate radiocarbon dating?

At the global scale, nothing in the current evidence justifies rewriting ordinary radiocarbon calibration around thunderstorms.

The cosmogenic source is large, continuous and already built into atmospheric carbon-cycle calibration. The older global thunderstorm estimate was much smaller under its assumptions, and later work has not closed the global budget strongly enough to overturn that.

The more interesting possibility is local.

Suppose an extreme thunderstorm produces an unusually intense neutron field over a limited region. Some locally generated carbon-14 could oxidize and mix before global circulation smooths it away. If biological material fixes carbon during or shortly after that perturbation, could a measurable local excess survive?

That is a testable question.

It is also much harder than saying “lightning makes carbon-14.”

Atmospheric mixing dilutes the signal. The carbon pool is large. Photosynthetic uptake integrates over time. Cosmic production continues simultaneously. Fossil-fuel carbon, reservoir effects and ordinary carbon-cycle variability can swamp small excursions.

A useful experiment therefore needs a before-and-after design near a documented extreme high-energy storm, not a retrospective hunt for mysterious tree-ring wiggles.

Collect atmospheric CO₂ and precipitation where feasible.

Sample short-lived plant material with tight growth timing.

Measure stable carbon isotopes and local carbon-cycle controls alongside carbon-14.

Most importantly, require independent evidence that the storm actually produced a strong photonuclear episode.

Without the gamma/neutron measurement, the isotope anomaly has too many parents.

## A tracer before a correction

The best near-term scientific use of lightning-produced carbon-14 may be as a **tracer of extreme atmospheric particle physics**, not as a correction to archaeology.

If event-linked radiocarbon can be measured locally, it would integrate neutron production over a chemically meaningful pathway. Gamma detectors observe photons. Neutron detectors observe part of the cascade. Carbon-14 would record the fraction of the cascade that entered a specific nuclear reaction and survived into atmospheric chemistry.

That is a different instrument made out of the atmosphere itself.

The idea remains experimentally difficult, but it has a clean falsification structure.

If well-characterized high-energy storms repeatedly show no radiocarbon excess above calibrated background and transport noise, the local-significance hypothesis contracts.

If excesses scale with independently measured photonuclear activity, the isotope becomes a new event proxy.

## Early Earth is a different denominator

The temptation to leap from modern lightning to early Earth or exoplanets should come last.

Atmospheric composition changes photonuclear target nuclei.

Density profiles change gamma and neutron transport.

Storm frequency changes the event population.

Magnetic shielding and cosmic-ray flux change the competing cosmogenic source.

A lightning contribution that is minor on modern Earth could occupy a different fraction of the isotope budget elsewhere.

That is not evidence that it did.

It is a parameter study waiting to be run.

The modern terrestrial problem should be closed first.

The thunderstorm has already done the hard conceptual work for us: it proved that weather can initiate nuclear reactions in ordinary air.

Now the boring question earns priority.

**How many atoms?**

Until that denominator is measured across the storm population, “lightning makes carbon-14” is a real mechanism without a settled planetary importance.

That is exactly where the evidence belongs.
