# 7. The Glow Before the Flash

Terrestrial gamma-ray flashes attract attention because they are brief and violent. Gamma-ray glows are less cooperative with the human appetite for event boundaries.

A glow can last seconds to minutes. Detectors see elevated high-energy radiation while a storm region remains strongly electrified. Some glows end abruptly when lightning discharges the field. Others evolve, brighten, weaken or recur. Ground experiments in Japan and Armenia, balloon measurements, and aircraft observations have built a long record of these extended emissions.

The old working distinction was useful: glows are quasi-steady acceleration in large thundercloud fields; TGFs are impulsive radiation connected to lightning leaders. It gave the field two boxes.

ALOFT damaged the boxes.

High-altitude aircraft observations over tropical storms in 2023 found gamma-ray glows that were highly dynamic, structured on one-to-ten-second and even subsecond scales. Rather than isolated patches, emission could extend over thousands of square kilometers and persist in evolving form for hours. The storms looked less like a lamp and more like a boiling pot.

That phrase comes from the 2024 *Nature* paper because it earns its keep. A boiling pot is not steady, but it is not a sequence of unrelated explosions either. Local instability sits inside a persistent energetic state.

This changes the mechanistic question. If a large fraction of an electrified storm can sustain relativistic electron production repeatedly, then the high-energy component may participate in charge regulation. Ionization from energetic particles increases conductivity. Runaway avalanches move charge. Feedback can partially discharge one region while increasing field gradients elsewhere. The gamma rays become an observable of an electrical metabolism.

That is Class II inference, not yet Class I system identification. The measurements show widespread dynamic radiation. The theory tells us what energetic particles should do to conductivity and fields. Demonstrating that this feedback controls storm evolution requires synchronized field and radiation measurements with predictive power.

One candidate prediction is termination. If a glow is powered by a large-scale field, a lightning discharge that collapses that field should quench the glow promptly. This has been observed in multiple cases. A stronger test asks whether the pre-lightning gamma evolution forecasts where and when the discharge begins.

The 2025 Japanese observation of one glow quenched by lightning and another developing minutes later as the storm's electrified core descended is exactly the kind of event needed. It turns 'storm gamma rays' from cataloguing into state estimation.
