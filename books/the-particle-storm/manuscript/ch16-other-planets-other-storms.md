# 16. Other Planets, Other Storms

If thunderstorms accelerate particles on Earth, the astrobiological question is not whether aliens have lightning. It is what different atmospheres do to the same basic electrodynamic game.

Jupiter and Saturn host enormous storms and lightning. Venus may have electrical activity, though observational interpretation remains contested. Dust storms on Mars create triboelectric charging even if classic water-cloud lightning is absent. Volcanic plumes on Earth provide another particulate electrification laboratory.

Runaway thresholds depend on density and composition. Bremsstrahlung spectra depend on target nuclei and electron energies. Pair production depends on photon energy and matter. Photonuclear channels depend on nuclear thresholds and atmospheric constituents. A high-energy discharge in hydrogen-helium atmosphere is therefore not a scaled terrestrial TGF. Nor is one in carbon dioxide.

This creates a technosignature-adjacent but entirely natural remote-sensing problem. High-energy flashes from planetary atmospheres could reveal deep electrical activity inaccessible optically through clouds. Gamma rays and energetic particles may encode field strengths and discharge altitudes. In principle, sufficiently sensitive instruments around giant planets could map high-energy atmospheric electricity as ASIM and Fermi have done incidentally for Earth.

The astrobiological relevance is chemical. Lightning has long been invoked as a source of reactive molecules in prebiotic environments. High-energy electron avalanches and gamma-ray-induced nuclear/chemical processes expand the energy spectrum available to atmospheric chemistry. Ionization changes reaction networks. Energetic particles can penetrate regions ordinary UV cannot.

Again, scale matters. It would be easy to turn 'thunderstorms make antimatter' into science-fiction prose about exotic biospheres. The serious question is narrower: given atmospheric composition, pressure, convective electric field and seed particle environment, what spectrum of energetic electrons, photons, ions and radionuclides should a storm produce, and does that materially alter chemistry?

Earth provides the only place we can test the full chain in situ. That makes terrestrial gamma-ray physics a calibration problem for planetary science.

There is a pleasing reversal here. For decades, gamma-ray astronomy looked outward and accidentally found thunderstorms. A mature field may eventually use thunderstorm gamma physics to look outward deliberately.
