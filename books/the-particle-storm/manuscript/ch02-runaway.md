# 2. Runaway

An ordinary electron in air is a miserable projectile. Push it with an electric field and it collides with molecules, loses energy, scatters, heats things, and generally behaves like the microscopic employee of a resistor.

A sufficiently energetic electron can enter a stranger regime. At relativistic energies, the frictional energy loss in air does not rise monotonically in the way intuition from slow particles suggests. Under a strong enough field, an energetic electron can gain more energy from the field between collisions than it loses. It runs away.

This is the conceptual hinge behind relativistic runaway electron avalanches, usually shortened to RREA. A seed electron—cosmic-ray secondaries are an obvious source—enters a sufficiently strong thunderstorm field. It accelerates. Collisions knock additional electrons into the runaway region. Their number grows approximately exponentially over characteristic avalanche lengths set by density and field strength. As the electrons bend and decelerate in the Coulomb fields of atmospheric nuclei, they emit bremsstrahlung x rays and gamma rays.

The cloud has become an accelerator without a beam pipe.

That sentence can become too romantic if left unattended. A conventional accelerator is engineered to control field geometry, vacuum, bunch timing and target conditions. A thunderstorm does none of these cleanly. Its field is distributed through turbulent, wet, mixed-phase air. Charge layers move. Ice particles collide. Leaders form. Density changes strongly with altitude. The accelerator volume is therefore inferred indirectly from radiation, electric fields, lightning mapping and atmospheric models.

The great advantage of the runaway model is that it makes quantitative predictions. It produces characteristic spectra. It relates source brightness to field strength, avalanche length and atmospheric attenuation. It explains how MeV electrons can exist in fields that may be lower than the classical breakdown field for cold electrons. This last point is essential. Thundercloud electric fields measured in situ have often seemed too weak to explain conventional large-volume breakdown. Runaway physics changes the threshold because the relevant electron is already energetic.

But RREA by itself is not the whole storm. The hard questions begin when one asks how the avalanche starts, how it becomes bright enough to explain the strongest TGFs, whether lightning leaders locally provide the decisive field enhancement, and whether feedback from positrons and backscattered photons can make the process self-sustaining.

The word 'runaway' also tempts sloppy metaphor. The electrons run away in an exact physical sense; the storm does not become uncontrollable in a colloquial one. The discharge can reduce the electric field that created it. High-energy ionization may be part of a regulatory loop. A system can be explosively amplifying on microsecond scales and stabilizing on larger scales.

That possibility becomes important later. If gamma-ray production is common across large storm regions, then energetic particles are not merely reporting the electric field. They may be participating in its evolution.

The distinction between tracer and actor is where this book lives.
