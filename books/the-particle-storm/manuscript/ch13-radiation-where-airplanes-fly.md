# 13. Radiation Where Airplanes Fly

Aviation is where an interesting physical effect can become a bad risk headline faster than anywhere else in the book.

Aircraft already operate in an ionizing-radiation environment. Galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles create altitude- and latitude-dependent dose. Crew exposure is monitored and modeled. Thunderstorm radiation adds a source that is spatially concentrated, intermittent and difficult to sample because commercial aircraft correctly avoid the strongest convection.

Older calculations showed that an aircraft directly intersecting an intense TGF or energetic-electron beam could receive a nontrivial dose. Numbers such as tens of millisieverts appear in scenario studies and aviation-safety summaries. They are useful as conditional physics, not as estimates of routine passenger exposure.

Three denominators are missing: how often strong events occur at flight-relevant altitudes; how close normal routes pass to the relevant source regions; and what the aircraft structure plus path geometry does to dose.

ALOFT makes the first denominator more interesting. It found high-energy emissions far more common over tropical thunderstorms than the space-based TGF count implied. But ALOFT intentionally sampled convective systems for research. Commercial operations use weather radar, dispatch, air-traffic constraints and pilot judgment to route around severe cells. A common source inside avoided airspace can still imply rare exposure.

The right research program is therefore epidemiologically boring and physically exact. Place calibrated radiation spectrometers on research aircraft and selected commercial routes. Record aircraft position, altitude, shielding geometry, storm-radar context and lightning data. Build exposure distributions rather than worst-case anecdotes. Separate gamma dose from energetic electrons and secondary particles. Compare with normal cosmic-ray background and occupational limits.

There is also an avionics question. Single-event effects from ionizing particles are familiar in aerospace electronics. Whether thunderstorm particle bursts materially change that risk depends again on fluence and geometry. One should not infer operational failures from the existence of particles, but neither should certification assumptions silently rely on an outdated source distribution.

The public-health epistemology here is straightforward: hazard is not risk. A TGF can be an intense local radiation hazard. Risk requires encounter probability.

Aviation researchers have an advantage that rare-event astrophysics often lacks. Aircraft produce dense operational data. Storm avoidance is recorded. Flight paths are known. Weather reanalyses exist. If radiation instruments were deployed widely enough, the denominator could be repaired quickly.

That would turn 'thunderstorms emit ionizing radiation' from an alarming sentence into an exposure map, which is what safety engineering actually needs.
