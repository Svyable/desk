# 3. The Avalanche

The phrase 'electron avalanche' sounds like one event. In a storm it is better understood as a population process.

A cosmic ray strikes the atmosphere and creates secondaries. One secondary reaches an electrified region with the right energy and direction. Under a supercritical field it gains energy rather than thermalizing. Collisions spawn more energetic electrons. Those electrons emit bremsstrahlung photons. Some photons scatter, some escape, some pair-produce, some create secondary energetic electrons. The chain can remain a small local enhancement or climb many e-foldings into something a satellite notices through kilometers of air.

The mathematics is less cinematic than the result. Exponential multiplication is unforgiving. A small difference in field or available path length can turn a negligible source into an enormous one. That makes the observed brightness distribution both physically revealing and experimentally treacherous. Detector threshold slices an exponential process in ways that can make the rare tail look like a distinct species.

This may be one reason the field spent years with a neat taxonomy: long gamma-ray glows on one side, short terrestrial gamma-ray flashes on the other. The labels were observationally sensible. They did not necessarily correspond to separate engines.

Recent aircraft measurements complicate that division. The ALOFT campaign found intense dynamic glows, flickering gamma-ray flashes and weaker TGFs inside or above tropical convection. Joseph Dwyer's 2025 modeling argues that relativistic feedback can produce a continuum: storm electrification pushes the system toward feedback, gamma-ray glows become unstable and oscillatory, and the emission can develop into intense pulse trains. Lightning leaders can then push already prepared regions farther above threshold.

That is a very different causal picture from 'lightning makes gamma rays.' It suggests that the storm may be operating a high-energy discharge before the familiar optical discharge announces itself.

Evidence needs to be kept in order. Class I: bright gamma-ray glows and flashes are observed; their spectra are compatible with runaway-electron bremsstrahlung; some gamma emission precedes large optical pulses. Class II: feedback models reproduce important qualitative and quantitative features, and the ALOFT phenomenology fits a continuum better than a strict two-box taxonomy. Class III: no single model has been shown to explain every event geometry, polarity, intensity and timing. Class IV: high-energy feedback may be part of the storm's route into lightning rather than a side effect.

The distinction matters because lightning initiation remains one of atmospheric electricity's stubborn problems. We know large-scale storm charge structures. We know leaders and streamers. Yet measured fields have historically created tension with simple breakdown pictures. Runaway avalanches do not magically solve that problem; they introduce another channel for ionization and feedback that has to be measured alongside it.

The useful experiment is not to ask whether RREA is 'true.' It plainly occurs. The useful experiment is to measure when a storm volume crosses from passive acceleration to self-amplifying discharge, and whether that crossing predicts a specific class of lightning or narrow bipolar event before the radio and optical signatures reveal it.

If it does, gamma rays become not just radiation from weather but a diagnostic of the moment the storm changes state.
