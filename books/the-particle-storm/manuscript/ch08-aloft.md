# 8. ALOFT

The ALOFT campaign committed an act of scientific impatience: instead of waiting for the storm's radiation to escape into orbit, it flew an instrumented aircraft above thunderstorms in Florida, Central America and the Gulf of Mexico.

Distance had been part of the instrument all along. Orbital detectors preferentially see the brightest events whose photons survive atmospheric attenuation and geometric dilution. Move the detector closer and the hidden population appears.

ALOFT did not merely collect more TGFs. It altered the ontology of the field. Researchers found gamma-ray glows to be far more common and dynamic than prior sparse aircraft observations suggested. They found flickering gamma-ray flashes—events longer than canonical TGFs, shorter and more structured than traditional glows, with multiple pulses and hard spectra. They found weaker TGF-like events whose estimated source brightness could fall orders of magnitude below the space-detection threshold.

This is what threshold bias looks like when it becomes a physical theory problem.

Imagine studying earthquakes only with an instrument that detects magnitude eight and above. One might construct a taxonomy in which earthquakes are rare, catastrophic and globally observable. Lower the threshold and an entire distribution appears. The analogy is imperfect—storm radiation involves strong geometry and attenuation—but the epistemic error is the same.

ALOFT's 2024 *Nature* result estimated gamma-emitting regions spanning up to thousands of square kilometers, with emissions lasting in changing form for hours. A 2025 spectral analysis of 24 flickering gamma-ray flashes placed source altitudes around 8–16 km and source brightnesses across roughly 10^12 to 10^17 photons above 100 keV, with spectra consistent with developed runaway avalanches under varying attenuation. Most were far below typical orbital thresholds.

The important negative detail is that some flickering events did not coincide with ordinary leader signatures at the moment of gamma emission. Strong radio events followed later. This weakens any universal story in which a lightning leader must always be the immediate gamma-ray engine.

It does not prove relativistic feedback is the only alternative. It means source classes need causal fingerprints.

ALOFT also raises an aviation question because it sampled radiation at aircraft-accessible altitudes. The campaign aircraft was deliberately studying thunderstorms under controlled research conditions; commercial aircraft avoid convective cores. Translating source measurements into passenger or crew dose therefore requires route geometry, shielding, storm avoidance, event rate and spectrum. The existence of intense radiation does not automatically imply routine hazard.

But the old comfort—that TGFs are rare things seen mainly from space—cannot be used as a risk premise anymore. The denominator moved.
