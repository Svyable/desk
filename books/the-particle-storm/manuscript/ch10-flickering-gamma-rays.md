# 10. Flickering Gamma Rays

The newly named flickering gamma-ray flash is scientifically useful because it occupies the territory between categories.

ALOFT observations identified events typically lasting roughly 15–250 milliseconds, composed of multiple hard-radiation pulses. They were not simply long TGFs by convention, nor smooth gamma-ray glows. Spectral analysis published in 2025 found their spectra compatible with developed relativistic runaway electron avalanches modified by atmospheric attenuation. Their inferred source brightness covered about five orders of magnitude.

Taxonomy can either clarify mechanism or delay it. The correct question is therefore not whether the acronym FGF survives the decade. It is whether these events expose a continuous instability linking glow physics to TGF physics.

Joseph Dwyer's 2025 modeling offers a strong candidate. In sufficiently electrified storm regions, relativistic feedback can make the gamma-ray glow dynamically unstable. Backscattered photons and reverse-moving positrons seed new avalanches. The discharge oscillates. Pulses emerge. As conditions strengthen, pulse trains can resemble TGFs. A lightning leader entering or creating a highly supercritical region can push the system into a still brighter regime.

The model has a seductive virtue: it turns several observational classes into phases of one nonlinear process.

That virtue is also the danger. Unification can become aesthetic overreach. The proper test is predictive separation. Given measured storm field, source altitude and charge geometry, can the model predict pulse spacing, spectral hardness, radio silence during the gamma emission, and the timing of the subsequent narrow bipolar event? Can competing leader-based models do better? Do events exist that violate the feedback sequence?

The 2025 FGF spectral study reports an especially awkward feature for simple leader-first stories: the gamma emission itself can lack visible-light or radio signatures, while a strong radio event follows milliseconds later. The high-energy discharge may therefore precede the conventional electrical signature we usually treat as cause.

A 2026 ground-based TGF observation near Los Alamos adds another piece from a different geometry. A powerful negative cloud-to-ground stroke, estimated peak current −237 kA, was followed by a TGF roughly 35 microseconds after the return-stroke start. Modeling inferred a source on the order of 10^17 photons. This is not the same sequence as the ALOFT FGF population. It may represent a leader/stroke-driven class or a different phase of the same system.

Good theory must earn both.
