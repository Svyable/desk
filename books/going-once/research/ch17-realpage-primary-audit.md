## Corrections to preserve in the next Chapter 17 prose pass

### 1. The “nobody needed to talk” contrast is too clean

The 2024 complaint makes the common-algorithm / pooled-nonpublic-data theory central, but the January 7, 2025 amended complaint also alleges direct communications among landlord executives and regular competitor “call arounds” or market surveys involving rents, occupancy, pricing strategy, discounts, and other competitively sensitive information. DOJ’s November 2025 RealPage settlement announcement additionally says RealPage hosted meetings attended by competing property managers where sensitive information was shared.

**Claim boundary:** the public case is not a pure demonstration that an algorithm replaced the human meeting. It is evidence for a hybrid system in which software allegedly scaled and operationalized coordination while familiar human information-sharing channels also existed.

That complication improves the book’s argument. The important technological change is not that people disappear from collusion. It is that a shared data-and-recommendation system can make coordination routine, continuous, and embedded in ordinary workflow rather than episodic and visibly conspiratorial.

### 2. Cortland’s settlement timing is wrong in the draft

DOJ filed the amended complaint against six landlords on **January 7, 2025** and simultaneously filed a proposed consent decree with Cortland. The current chapter says Cortland settled in April 2025.

### 3. Do not describe the RealPage decree as a finalized judgment yet

DOJ filed a **proposed** settlement with RealPage on November 24, 2025. The Antitrust Division’s public case index, updated September 4, 2026, still lists a Proposed Final Judgment, Competitive Impact Statement, and Stipulation and Order for RealPage; unlike Greystar, it does not list an entered “Final Judgment” for RealPage.

**Claim boundary:** the company agreed to proposed restrictions, but the public DOJ index does not support saying the RealPage judgment itself had become final as of September 4, 2026.

### 4. The landlord resolutions have continued through 2026

The case did not stop with the 2025 deals described in the manuscript. The current DOJ docket records:

- Greystar: proposed settlement August 8, 2025; **final judgment entered March 2, 2026**.
- LivCor: proposed consent decree December 23, 2025.
- Willow Bridge: proposed settlement July 6, 2026.
- Pinnacle Property Management Services: proposed consent decree September 4, 2026.

The September 4 DOJ announcement describes Pinnacle as building on settlements with RealPage, Cortland, Greystar, LivCor, and Willow Bridge. This is useful current evidence that the enforcement theory has continued to generate negotiated restrictions, while still not supplying an appellate merits ruling on the core Section 1 theory.

### 5. Keep settlement evidence separate from proof on the merits

Consent decrees are strong evidence of enforcement posture and real changes in business constraints. They are not equivalent to an appellate holding that every pleaded theory was legally correct, nor do they independently validate every estimate of tenant overcharge. The absence of a merits judgment is substantive counterevidence to any sentence that treats the litigation as having definitively established algorithmic collusion as fact.

At the same time, “no merits verdict” should not be turned into “nothing happened.” Greystar has a final judgment; multiple other defendants accepted proposed restrictions on competitor-sensitive data, common pricing algorithms, meetings, or information sharing; and the RealPage proposal includes a court-appointed monitor and cooperation duties if entered.

### 6. FTC “surveillance pricing” is adjacent but not the same theory

The FTC’s 2024 Section 6(b) orders investigate intermediaries that use consumer characteristics and behavior—location, demographics, browsing or shopping history, channel, time, and related data—to tailor prices or promotions to individuals. The January 2025 staff perspective reports broad use of personal data in those systems.

That is a distinct problem from RealPage’s alleged competitor-data coordination. One concerns individualized pricing based on information about the buyer; the other concerns competing sellers feeding nonpublic business information through a common pricing system. The chapter can connect them as examples of opaque algorithmic pricing, but should not present the FTC study as direct confirmation of the RealPage collusion theory.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter’s thesis

The most important skeptical reading is that the RealPage record may ultimately tell us less about autonomous algorithmic collusion than the chapter suggests. DOJ alleges human calls, executive communications, market surveys, RealPage-hosted meetings, contractual data sharing, software rules, and pricing recommendations all at once. If those allegations are sustained, ordinary hub-and-spoke information exchange may do substantial explanatory work without requiring a novel story in which profit-maximizing software independently discovers cartel behavior.

The chapter should therefore distinguish at least three mechanisms:

1. **human agreement or information exchange assisted by software**;
2. **a common vendor pooling competitors’ nonpublic data and producing aligned recommendations**;
3. **independent pricing algorithms learning tacitly collusive behavior without competitor data sharing or human agreement**.

RealPage is strongest evidence for the first two as alleged by DOJ. The German gasoline literature and laboratory / simulation work are better places to examine the third.

## Editorial implication

The clean historical symmetry with the 1961 electrical-equipment conspiracy should be weakened, not abandoned. A more accurate opening would let the symmetry fail in an interesting way: the old cartel left hotel rooms and meetings; the RealPage complaint alleges that landlords still talked, but now those conversations sat beside a continuously operating shared data system. Software did not abolish the social machinery of coordination. It potentially made that machinery less exceptional—part of the normal pricing stack.

That is a sharper bridge to the book’s AI-agent chapters because it avoids claiming that automation has already eliminated human agreement. It asks the harder prospective question: which forms of coordination become possible when the recommendation, negotiation, and execution layers increasingly operate without a human decision at each step?
