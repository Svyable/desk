A history of the auction as a piece of social engineering, from a contested account in Herodotus's *Histories* to the infrastructure now emerging for AI agents to bid, negotiate, and transact without a human reviewing each individual decision.

The working thesis is that an auction is not a genre of event but a piece of engineering: a specific procedure for finding out what something is worth by asking a group of people to disclose, through their behavior, information they would often rather keep private. The book traces that procedure through the London saleroom trade that became Sotheby's and Christie's, William Vickrey's 1961 proof that a single rule change could make honest bidding a dominant strategy, the FCC's spectrum auctions and the psychological laboratory eBay accidentally became, into the digital advertising exchanges that now run more auctions in a single day than Sotheby's has run in its entire history, and the earliest infrastructure for AI agents to transact on a person's behalf.

Throughout, it holds two claims in tension rather than collapsing them into one: auctions, well designed, are one of the more remarkable pieces of social technology humans have built, reliably extracting real information from people who have no independent reason to disclose it honestly. Auctions, carelessly designed or deliberately abused, have produced some of the more embarrassing and occasionally predatory episodes in commercial history.
