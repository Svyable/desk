# Treasuries, Carbon, and Water

The United States government borrows money every week through a mechanism most citizens never think about despite it being, in aggregate dollar volume, almost certainly the largest recurring auction on Earth: in 2025 alone, the Treasury held 444 separate public auctions and issued approximately $29.7 trillion in marketable securities across bills, notes, bonds, inflation-protected securities, and floating-rate notes — a figure that dwarfs, by a factor of several hundred, the combined annual output of every art auction house, spectrum sale, and digital ad exchange this book has examined put together, and that flows continuously, week after week, through a mechanism most Americans whose retirement accounts, mortgage rates, and government services ultimately depend on it have never once thought to examine closely. the weekly and monthly sale of Treasury bills, notes, and bonds, through which the federal government raises the cash needed to fund the gap between what it spends and what it collects in tax revenue. The mechanics, refined over decades, work through a sealed-bid process in which authorized financial institutions — primary dealers, along with a wider set of institutional and even individual investors able to bid directly — submit bids specifying how much of a given security they want to buy and, for competitive bidders, at what yield they are willing to accept it. For most of the twentieth century, the Treasury settled these auctions using a discriminatory, or multiple-price, format in which each winning bidder paid the specific yield it had bid, the same first-price logic this book's fourth chapter examined in the context of oil leases and government procurement, with all the same strategic bid-shading incentives that format reliably produces. Beginning in 1992, at the urging of economists including Milton Friedman, who had argued for decades that the format was needlessly costing the Treasury money by encouraging exactly this kind of defensive shading, the Treasury began experimenting with a uniform-price, or single-price, format instead, in which every winning bidder, regardless of the specific yield they had individually bid, ends up paying the same single clearing yield — the highest yield among all the bids the Treasury actually accepted to fill that week's offering, directly echoing the second-price logic this book traced back to Vickrey three decades earlier in this same fourth chapter.

The uniform-price reform's actual results, once economists had years of before-and-after auction data to study, turned out to be more ambiguous than either its advocates or its skeptics had confidently predicted going in. Treasury's own internal 1995 evaluation of the format change, covering the period from the multiple-price era through the initial uniform-price experiment, found the evidence on which format actually served taxpayers better to be genuinely inconclusive rather than a clean vindication of either camp's theoretical priors. Subsequent academic research complicated the picture further: economists studying uniform-price auctions in general, not merely the Treasury's own specific implementation, identified a theoretical problem Vickrey's original single-item analysis had never had to confront, because it only arises once an auction sells multiple identical units simultaneously rather than one single item — a bidder wanting several units of a security has a real incentive to shade down their bid on the marginal, additional units beyond the first, specifically because bidding aggressively on those extra units risks pushing up the single clearing price that bidder will end up paying on every unit it wins, including the units it would have happily paid full price for anyway. This "demand reduction" problem, as the literature came to call it, means a uniform-price multi-unit auction does not automatically inherit the clean, dominant-strategy truthfulness Vickrey proved for a single item; it introduces its own, more subtle set of strategic considerations that sophisticated institutional bidders, including the same large financial institutions that dominate Treasury auction participation, have every incentive to exploit.

The single most consequential episode in the modern history of Treasury auctions had nothing to do with which pricing format the government was using, and everything to do with a bidder who simply decided the rules themselves were optional. In May 1991, the Wall Street firm Salomon Brothers submitted unauthorized bids, in the names of its own customers and without those customers' knowledge or consent, across a series of Treasury note auctions, a scheme orchestrated by Paul Mozer, the head of Salomon's Treasury trading desk. The most brazen instance came in the Treasury's May 1991 auction of $12.29 billion in two-year notes, where Salomon's unauthorized bidding allowed the firm to gain control of at least eighty-six percent of the entire issue — a staggering concentration that violated the Treasury's own explicit rule, adopted specifically to prevent exactly this outcome, capping any single bidder at thirty-five percent of a given auction. Cornering that much of a freshly issued security gave Salomon the ability to attempt what traders call a short squeeze: because so many other market participants had, in the ordinary course of business, sold these notes short — borrowing and selling securities they did not yet own, expecting to buy them back later at a lower price to return to the lender — and because Salomon now controlled such an overwhelming share of the actual available supply, the firm could effectively dictate the price at which those short sellers were forced to buy back the notes they owed, extracting a toll from a substantial share of the entire Treasury-securities trading ecosystem through what amounted to engineered scarcity in a market that, by design, was never supposed to allow any single participant that much control. Once the scheme became public in August 1991, the fallout was severe and swift: the Treasury Department immediately banned Salomon Brothers from bidding at its auctions, the firm's chairman and several senior executives resigned within days, Salomon ultimately paid $290 million to settle federal securities charges and a further $27 million to resolve related Justice Department antitrust allegations, and the scandal nearly destroyed one of Wall Street's most storied investment banks entirely, saved in substantial part by an emergency capital injection and a management overhaul led by Warren Buffett, one of the firm's largest shareholders, who took over as interim chairman for an annual salary of one dollar specifically to restore the confidence of regulators and counterparties who had, quite reasonably, begun to doubt whether Salomon could be trusted to participate honestly in the government securities market at all. Buffett fired the firm's top executives, including its chief executive, cooperated openly with federal investigators, and, in testimony before a House subcommittee on September 4, 1991, delivered a line that has outlived the scandal itself in American business culture: "Lose money for the firm, and I will be understanding. Lose a shred of reputation for the firm, and I will be ruthless." The firm avoided criminal charges and eventually survived, though considerably diminished, and the episode remains a standard teaching case in business ethics precisely because it demonstrates how quickly an institution's carefully built, decades-long reputation for honest participation in a market as consequential as Treasury auctions can be destroyed by a single trader's decision to treat a thirty-five-percent bidding cap as a suggestion rather than a rule.

Carbon markets extend this book's auction framework into territory the format's ancient and early-modern originators could never have imagined needing: selling the right to emit a specific, invisible, intangible externality rather than any physical object at all. The European Union's Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005 as the world's first major multinational carbon market, operates on a cap-and-trade principle in which the European Union sets a declining overall limit, or cap, on the total tonnage of greenhouse gas emissions permitted across covered industries each year, then distributes tradable emissions allowances — each one conferring the right to emit one metric ton of carbon dioxide or its equivalent — partly through free allocation to certain industries considered at risk of relocating production to countries with looser emissions rules, and increasingly, over the system's two decades of operation, through auctions run by the European Energy Exchange. As of the current multi-year trading phase, up to fifty-seven percent of the system's general allowances are distributed through these auctions rather than given away, with the resulting revenue flowing directly to the thirty participating European countries under a predetermined formula and, since mid-2023, required by European rules to be spent specifically on climate-related and energy-transition purposes rather than absorbed into general government budgets. The cumulative scale is genuinely enormous: the system has generated more than €245 billion in total auction revenue for European governments and climate initiatives since 2013 alone, a sum that transforms what began as an abstract environmental-economics proposal — assign a genuine market price to a previously unpriced externality, and let competitive bidding discover what that price should actually be — into one of the largest, longest-running, and most consequential auction programs operating anywhere in the world today, generating a quarter-trillion euros in cumulative public revenue since 2013 alone, not by selling anything anyone can touch, weigh, or physically inspect the way a Sotheby's specialist inspects a painting's canvas for damage and overpaint, but by selling the legal right to do measurably less environmental harm than a covered company would otherwise have been permitted to do.

The EU Emissions Trading System's compliance allowances, auctioned under close regulatory oversight and legally required to be surrendered by covered industrial polluters, sit at the more rigorously verified end of carbon-market instruments — but a separate, much larger and considerably less regulated segment of the broader carbon economy, the voluntary offset market, has produced exactly the kind of integrity crisis this book's eighth chapter examined in the context of forged art provenance, applied here to an intangible, unusually difficult-to-verify commodity: a claimed reduction in greenhouse gas emissions that, unlike a barrel of oil or a painting, cannot be physically inspected at all. The compliance side of the European carbon market, meanwhile, continues to expand in scope even as the voluntary offset market's credibility has come under sustained pressure. The European Union agreed to launch a second, separate emissions trading system, generally referred to as ETS2, specifically to cover fuel combustion in buildings, road transport, and smaller industrial sources the original 2005 system never reached, with fuel suppliers rather than individual households or drivers bearing the direct obligation to report emissions and purchase allowances upstream. After originally targeting a 2027 start, EU institutions agreed in March 2026 to push the system's first compliance surrender back to 2028, while trading itself was still expected to begin the year before, a delay driven substantially by political concern over the direct cost increases the new system would impose on ordinary households heating their homes and fueling their cars — concern serious enough that the legislation pairs the new trading system with a dedicated Social Climate Fund, financed from a portion of the auction proceeds, specifically to cushion vulnerable households and small businesses from the resulting price increases. The expansion illustrates a pattern this book's chapters on spectrum and matching markets have already shown repeating in very different domains: a mechanism that survives its first, narrower application under real-world pressure tends, over time, to get extended into adjacent territory its original designers left untouched, not because the underlying theory demanded it but because the first version's institutional survival gave everyone involved enough confidence to try the harder version next. A joint investigation published in January 2023 by *The Guardian*, the German newspaper *Die Zeit*, and the nonprofit newsroom SourceMaterial examined the rainforest-protection carbon credits certified by Verra, the world's largest voluntary carbon-standard body, and concluded that more than ninety percent of the credits examined were likely "phantom credits" that did not represent any genuine additional reduction in deforestation at all, drawing in part on a University of Cambridge study finding that the underlying threat to the forests in question had, on average, been overstated by roughly four hundred percent in the project documentation used to justify issuing the credits in the first place. Major global corporations, including several that had purchased the credits specifically to support public claims of carbon neutrality, found themselves publicly implicated in the resulting controversy; Verra disputed the investigation's methodology and conclusions at length, but the episode did lasting damage to corporate and public confidence in voluntary carbon offsetting, illustrating a problem considerably harder to solve through better auction design alone than any this book has examined so far: a compliance allowance market can verify, with real regulatory teeth, that a covered polluter actually holds enough allowances to cover its own physically measured emissions, but no comparable verification infrastructure has yet proven itself capable of reliably confirming that a voluntary rainforest-protection credit represents a ton of carbon dioxide that would genuinely otherwise have entered the atmosphere.

Water markets supply this chapter's final and, in some ways, most ethically fraught extension of the auction principle, because water sits closer than almost anything else this book has examined to the specific category of goods this book's earlier chapter on kidney exchange described as "repugnant" — a resource many people instinctively believe should never be allocated primarily through price, given its status as a literal precondition for human survival, even as physical scarcity in specific regions makes some form of rationing mechanism genuinely unavoidable. Water rights markets have nonetheless developed in several water-stressed regions, most prominently in parts of Australia's Murray-Darling Basin and in California, where formal systems allow farmers, municipalities, and other water-rights holders to buy, sell, and in some structures bid competitively for water allocations, particularly during drought years when available supply falls well short of the total rights nominally held on paper by every claimant in a given river basin or aquifer system. Australia's Murray-Darling Basin, covering the country's largest and most agriculturally significant river system, has operated one of the world's most extensively developed formal water markets for over two decades, and its history supplies real, empirically documented evidence about how such a market behaves under genuine, sustained stress rather than only in theory. Water trading across the Basin moves on the order of several billion Australian dollars annually in ordinary years, involving both permanent transfers of underlying water entitlements and shorter-term trading of specific seasonal allocations, with up to sixty percent of the Basin's irrigators participating in the market in a given year according to Australian government research. The system faced its most severe test during the so-called Millennium Drought, a prolonged, multi-year dry period stretching through the 2000s that sharply reduced the total volume of water physically available across the entire river system. Economic research examining that period concluded that the ability to trade water allocations meaningfully reduced the drought's overall economic cost, because it let irrigators growing permanent, high-value plantings like fruit orchards and vineyards — crops that die if left completely unwatered even briefly, unlike an annual crop a farmer can simply choose not to plant in a dry year — purchase additional water from irrigators elsewhere in the Basin growing more flexible, lower-value annual crops who could profitably sell their own allocation instead of using it. That flexibility, exactly the kind of efficient reallocation toward highest-value use this book's earlier chapters have credited well-functioning auction and market mechanisms with producing elsewhere, came with real, documented costs of its own: water prices in the southern Basin climbed to some of their highest recorded levels during the worst drought years, and researchers and affected farming communities have separately raised concerns about speculative trading by investors with no direct agricultural stake in the water at all, and about smaller, less well-capitalized farming communities losing out systematically to larger, better-financed operations able to outbid them for scarce allocations exactly when water mattered most to everyone's survival.

These markets aim to solve a genuinely difficult allocation problem — directing scarce water toward its highest-value uses, whether that means a almond orchard, a municipal water system, or an environmental flow requirement protecting a river's ecological health, during years when there is simply not enough water to satisfy every legally recognized claim in full — through mechanisms that range from relatively thin, occasional bilateral trading to more formal, auction-like allocation processes in some jurisdictions' most acute drought years. California's own experience with water trading, developing along a somewhat different track than Australia's, illustrates how much a formal market's actual development depends on the surrounding legal and institutional plumbing being built out first. The state's 2014 Sustainable Groundwater Management Act authorized local Groundwater Sustainability Agencies to set pumping allocations for individual wells and to permit those allocations to be traded among users within a given groundwater basin, extending the market principle already well established for California's surface-water rights into the considerably murkier legal territory of water pumped from underground aquifers. Surface-water trading in California has functioned as an active, if geographically uneven, market for decades, letting agricultural users with more ample or lower-value water rights sell or lease supply to farms, cities, and environmental water managers facing more acute shortages or higher-value uses. Groundwater trading under the newer 2014 framework has developed far more slowly, with agricultural policy researchers tracking the rollout finding that, years after the law's passage, most groundwater basins had gotten only as far as setting pumping limits without actually establishing functioning trading markets on top of them — evidence that authorizing a market on paper and having it actually clear real trades at a meaningful volume are two genuinely different accomplishments, separated by exactly the kind of patient, unglamorous, and frequently unheralded institutional development work this book's twelfth chapter argued was the real secret behind market design's genuine and lasting successes.

The controversies surrounding these markets track almost precisely the objections this book's earlier chapter on kidney exchange catalogued in the human-organ context: critics argue that pricing a literal survival necessity inevitably advantages wealthy, well-capitalized buyers — typically large agricultural operations — over smaller farmers, indigenous communities, and environmental interests that cannot compete on price alone, while defenders argue that some rationing mechanism is unavoidable once physical scarcity is severe enough, and that a transparent, price-based mechanism at least makes the resulting allocation visible and auditable in a way opaque administrative rationing, historically the alternative in most of these same water systems, conspicuously was not.

What unites Treasury auctions, carbon allowances, and water rights, despite their obvious surface differences, is that each represents an application of the same basic auction machinery this book has traced since ancient Babylon to a resource whose scarcity is not merely commercial, the way a painting's scarcity is commercial, but structurally load-bearing for an entire society's functioning — a government's ability to fund itself, a planet's tolerance for a warming atmosphere, a region's capacity to grow food and sustain human settlement at all. Getting the mechanism wrong in any of these three domains carries consequences considerably graver than an art collector overpaying for a disputed Leonardo or an oil company absorbing a bad quarter from the winner's curse; a poorly designed Treasury auction risks destabilizing the government-debt market underpinning the entire global financial system, a poorly designed carbon market risks either failing to reduce emissions at all or imposing unsustainable costs on covered industries, and a poorly designed water market risks starving vulnerable communities of a literal survival necessity in the name of economic efficiency. That these three domains have, nonetheless, each independently converged on some version of the auction as their preferred rationing tool, despite the manifestly higher stakes involved, is itself among the strongest evidence this book has assembled that the mechanism, whatever its well-documented flaws, keeps getting chosen by serious people facing genuinely hard problems because the available alternatives — a queue, a lottery, an administrator's discretion, an uncontrolled scramble — have, across enough real-world tests, generally proven worse.
