# The Algorithm That Colluded

The 1961 heavy electrical equipment conspiracy this book examined in detail several chapters ago required its human participants to do something genuinely risky and, in hindsight, genuinely careless: meet in person, repeatedly, over years, coordinating specific numbers for specific sealed bids in a way that left behind exactly the kind of evidence — hotel reservations, travel records, eventually a cooperating witness — that unraveled the entire scheme once federal investigators started pulling threads. Sixty years later, the United States Department of Justice brought a civil antitrust lawsuit alleging that a Texas software company called RealPage had found a way to produce a strikingly similar economic outcome, coordinated pricing among nominally competing businesses, without any of them ever needing to meet at all. Filed on August 23, 2024, and joined by attorneys general from eight states, the government's complaint alleged that RealPage's revenue-management software, marketed to landlords and property-management companies as an analytical tool for setting optimal rental prices, functioned in practice as a mechanism for competing landlords to share commercially sensitive information and align their pricing in ways that would have been transparently illegal price-fixing had the same landlords simply picked up the phone and agreed on numbers directly.

The scale of RealPage's reach into the American rental market helps explain why the case drew the level of sustained government and press attention it did. Independent analysis found the company's pricing algorithm in use across roughly one in every four multifamily rental units nationally, with penetration exceeding half of all units in several major metropolitan markets including Atlanta and Dallas. The White House Council of Economic Advisers published its own estimate of the resulting financial harm to tenants, calculating that RealPage's software had cost American renters approximately $3.6 billion in a single recent year, with affected tenants paying an average of roughly $70 more per month than they would have absent the alleged coordination, and renters in six metropolitan areas — Atlanta, Denver, Dallas, Washington, Riverside, and Tampa among them — facing estimated overcharges exceeding $100 a month. Academic economists examining rent data across nearly fifty major metropolitan areas separately estimated the algorithmic overcharge at somewhere between two and thirteen percent depending on the specific market, with Dallas tenants facing an estimated seven percent premium attributable to the software. Numbers at that scale explain both why government prosecutors judged the case worth pursuing at all and why the private tenant litigation running parallel to it produced settlements exceeding $140 million even before RealPage's own settlement with the Justice Department was finalized: a mechanism generating a few dollars of harm per household would never have drawn this level of sustained legal attention, but one plausibly costing renters billions of dollars a year, concentrated in specific cities where a single software vendor's pricing recommendations touched more than half the local rental market, was large enough to reshape how regulators think about algorithmic pricing across every industry this chapter has surveyed.

The mechanics the DOJ described, and that RealPage itself has never fully denied even while disputing the government's legal characterization of them, worked roughly as follows. Landlords using RealPage's software fed the company detailed, competitively sensitive, non-public data about their own properties — current rents, vacancy rates, upcoming lease expirations, and the prices at which specific units had actually leased — into a shared analytical system. RealPage's software then used that pooled data, drawn from a large share of the rental units in a given local market, to generate specific rent-pricing recommendations for each individual landlord using the platform, recommendations informed not merely by that landlord's own property but by real-time, granular knowledge of what every other participating landlord in the same market was charging and how quickly their units were leasing. The government's complaint alleged that this pooling arrangement allowed RealPage's algorithm to functionally replicate what a room full of landlords secretly agreeing on prices together would have produced — coordinated upward pressure on rents across an entire local market, achieved not through any explicit verbal agreement between the landlords themselves, who in many cases never directly communicated with each other at all, but through a shared algorithmic intermediary processing all of their private data simultaneously and issuing pricing guidance that, prosecutors alleged, landlords using the software adopted at a strikingly high rate.

This structure, if the government's characterization holds up, exploits a specific, longstanding gap in traditional antitrust doctrine that predates algorithmic pricing by nearly a century and that legal scholars had been anticipating software might eventually widen into a genuine loophole. American antitrust law, built substantially around the 1890 Sherman Antitrust Act, has always drawn a sharp legal line between two economically similar but legally very different phenomena: an explicit agreement among competitors to fix prices, which is per se illegal regardless of the specific mechanism used to reach or implement it, and independent, parallel pricing decisions that merely happen to converge on similar outcomes because competitors are all rationally responding to the same observable market conditions — a legally protected form of what economists call tacit collusion, in which competitors need no actual communication at all to arrive at higher, less competitive prices than a fully competitive market would produce, simply by each independently recognizing that undercutting a rival would trigger a price war nobody wants and settling, without ever discussing it, into a comfortable, mutually beneficial equilibrium. Courts have historically required prosecutors to show something closer to an actual meeting of the minds — direct evidence of communication and agreement, or at minimum strong circumstantial evidence like the identical sealed bids that unraveled the 1961 electrical equipment conspiracy — to sustain a price-fixing claim, precisely because parallel pricing alone, without more, has never been considered sufficient proof of an illegal agreement under American law.

RealPage's software, prosecutors argued, sat in a legally novel space between these two established categories, and the government's theory of the case rested on treating the software itself as the mechanism of agreement rather than treating each landlord's independent decision to follow the algorithm's recommendation as merely parallel, independently rational behavior. The distinction the DOJ drew was that landlords using RealPage were not independently observing public market conditions and rationally reaching similar conclusions, the traditional, legal form of tacit collusion; they were instead feeding a shared intermediary their own private, non-public, competitively sensitive data, and receiving back pricing guidance informed by that same pooled, private data from their direct competitors — an arrangement the complaint characterized as functionally equivalent to landlords sharing confidential pricing information directly with each other, with the software serving as a convenient technical intermediary that let each individual landlord maintain a form of plausible deniability, since no landlord ever had to personally pick up a phone and call a rival competitor to fix a price. Legal scholars writing about the case in the months following its filing generally treated it as a genuinely significant test of how existing antitrust doctrine, developed almost entirely around human, verbal, and documentary evidence of collusion, would handle a defendant whose alleged coordination mechanism was an algorithm processing data at a scale and speed no human cartel of the 1961 electrical-equipment variety could ever have matched, updating its pricing recommendations continuously rather than through periodic, discoverable meetings.

The government's litigation strategy targeted not only RealPage itself but a widening circle of the landlords who had actually used its software, and tracking how that second front resolved reveals almost as much about the underlying economics as the RealPage case itself. In January 2025, the Justice Department amended its original complaint to add six major apartment-management companies as co-defendants alongside RealPage: Greystar, the largest property manager in the United States; along with Cushman & Wakefield and its subsidiary Pinnacle Property Management Services, Willow Bridge Property Company, LivCor, Camden Property Trust, and Cortland Management. Several of these landlords settled relatively quickly rather than litigate the underlying legal theory to a verdict — Cortland in April 2025, LivCor for a reported $7 million, and Greystar itself reaching both a Justice Department settlement in August 2025 and a separate $7 million multistate settlement in November of that year, in each case agreeing to stop using algorithmic pricing tools that incorporated competitors' non-public data without admitting the underlying antitrust violation. A parallel track of private litigation, brought directly by tenants rather than by government enforcers, produced settlements of its own running past $140 million in aggregate across the various defendant landlords by the time RealPage's own settlement was filed — real money changing hands, in other words, well before any court had issued a definitive final ruling on whether the underlying algorithmic-pricing arrangement actually violated federal antitrust law, a pattern that lets defendants exit costly litigation on their own terms while leaving the deeper legal question this chapter has been describing formally unresolved for the next company, in the next industry, that tries something similar.

The case reached a resolution considerably short of a full trial verdict, in a pattern common to complex civil antitrust litigation against a defendant facing existential business risk from a prolonged court fight. RealPage agreed to a proposed settlement filed with the court in late November 2025, under which the company committed to limiting the specific categories of competitively sensitive data it would collect and pool across landlords going forward, and to cooperating with the government's continuing, separate legal actions against several of the individual property-management companies that had used its software — all without RealPage formally admitting wrongdoing, a standard feature of civil settlements that lets a defendant end costly litigation while preserving its ability to argue, in any future proceeding or in its own public statements, that it never actually violated the law. The settlement left several of the deeper legal questions the case had raised formally unresolved by any binding appellate court ruling: whether an algorithm processing pooled private data genuinely constitutes an unlawful "agreement" among the businesses feeding it that data, how directly a plaintiff must trace a specific price increase to a specific algorithmic recommendation to prove real economic harm, and how antitrust doctrine more broadly should treat the growing category of shared, third-party pricing software increasingly common not just in rental housing but across hotels, ride-hailing, and other industries where competing businesses now frequently rely on the same handful of specialized pricing vendors.

Regulators have made increasingly explicit, in the years following the RealPage complaint, that they view the underlying legal theory as generalizable well beyond apartment rents. The Federal Trade Commission issued formal information-gathering orders to multiple companies beginning in 2024 seeking details on what regulators termed surveillance pricing — dynamic, personalized pricing systems that adjust in response to detailed consumer behavior data, competitor activity, or market conditions in ways that can be difficult for outside observers or even the pricing company's own customers to audit. Airlines drew particular regulatory attention: the European Commission opened its own inquiry into whether shared pricing and distribution platforms used across the airline ticketing industry were functioning to harmonize fares among nominally competing carriers, echoing the RealPage theory in an industry that had already, independently, spent decades building some of the most sophisticated dynamic-pricing infrastructure in any consumer market. American regulators drew the connection explicitly rather than leaving it implicit: legal analysts tracking the Justice Department's own public statements noted that its November 2025 RealPage settlement, restricting how much competitively sensitive data could flow through a shared pricing vendor, was widely read as establishing a direct template for future cases against any industry where multiple competitors rely on the same third-party pricing algorithm, airlines very much included, and the department signaled in mid-2026 that criminal, rather than merely civil, enforcement of this specific theory — algorithmic tacit coordination achieved through a shared vendor rather than through any direct communication between competitors — remained a live possibility for prosecutors going forward. Legislators, rather than waiting for courts to fully resolve whether existing Sherman Act doctrine can reach this kind of software-mediated coordination at all, have begun drafting new statutory language specifically designed to close the gap. Senator Amy Klobuchar and a bipartisan group of co-sponsors introduced the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act in the U.S. Senate in January 2025, reviving an earlier version of the bill that had stalled in the previous Congress; the proposed legislation would make it unlawful outright to use or distribute a pricing algorithm that incorporates non-public competitor data, explicitly targeting the RealPage fact pattern this chapter has described without requiring prosecutors to prove the kind of formal, courtroom-provable agreement traditional antitrust doctrine has always demanded, and would expose violators to civil penalties running into thousands of dollars per day the violation continued or the total value of every sale made using the offending algorithm, whichever proved larger. California introduced closely parallel state-level legislation, its own Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act, in February 2025, similarly targeting pricing algorithms trained on competitor data and adding reporting requirements for larger companies using them. Neither the federal nor the state bill had been enacted into law as of this book's drafting, both having stalled in committee in earlier legislative sessions before their respective reintroductions, and their ultimate fate remains genuinely uncertain — but their very existence, drafted in direct, traceable response to the RealPage litigation this chapter has spent its length examining, is itself strong evidence that lawmakers, not merely litigators, now regard the specific software-mediated collusion mechanism this chapter has described as a real and urgent gap in existing law rather than a purely hypothetical concern confined to academic journals and law-review footnotes.

Academic economists studying the underlying game theory have reached a genuinely unsettling conclusion that helps explain why regulators are moving so aggressively: formal models of dynamic pricing algorithms have shown that software systems, trained merely to maximize their own operator's profit with no explicit instruction to coordinate with anyone, can nonetheless learn to sustain collusive-looking outcomes on their own, using remarkably little shared information, simply because sustained tacit cooperation turns out to be a more profitable long-run strategy than aggressive competition, and a sufficiently capable algorithm can discover that strategy through nothing more than repeated trial and error against its own market environment.

The German gasoline market supplies a rigorous, carefully studied natural experiment that illuminates exactly the mechanism RealPage was accused of exploiting, examined years before RealPage itself became a household name in antitrust circles and studied for entirely independent academic reasons that turned out to prefigure the American housing case with striking precision. Economists Stephanie Assad, Robert Clark, Daniel Ershov, and Lei Xu examined what happened after algorithmic gas-station pricing software became widely available across Germany beginning around 2017, using detailed station-level price data to detect, statistically, exactly when individual gas stations had adopted the new pricing technology. Their central finding, published in the *Journal of Political Economy*, was that algorithmic pricing adoption increased profit margins, but the effect was conditional in a way that mapped precisely onto the underlying collusion mechanism the theory predicted: margins rose meaningfully only in markets where every competing station in the local area had adopted the software, and specifically in markets with just two or three competing stations, the kind of small, concentrated local market where an algorithm's pricing signals from one station could plausibly and quickly propagate to influence its handful of direct rivals' own algorithmic responses in a tight, observable feedback loop. A single station adopting algorithmic pricing in isolation, surrounded by rivals still setting prices the old-fashioned way, showed no comparable margin increase at all — strong evidence that the profit gain was not simply a matter of better individual optimization, an algorithm being smarter than a human station manager at setting its own price in isolation, but a matter of *coordination* among multiple algorithmically-priced competitors, each one's software effectively learning to read and respond to its neighbors' pricing in a way that pushed the whole local market's prices upward together, exactly the dynamic RealPage stood accused of engineering deliberately, at much larger scale, across an entire national rental-housing market.

RealPage was not the only algorithmic-pricing case regulators pursued in this same period, and a second, structurally different example is worth including because it shows the same underlying anxiety about software and pricing power surfacing even without any allegation of coordination between separate competing companies at all. The Federal Trade Commission's own 2023 antitrust complaint against Amazon, filed jointly with seventeen state attorneys general, alleged that the company had used an internal pricing tool known as Project Nessie to raise prices across large swaths of its retail catalogue by predicting whether competing retailers would follow Amazon's own price increases rather than undercut them, then, once Amazon's algorithm judged a price hike would likely stick uncontested, raising prices accordingly. Unredacted portions of the complaint that later became public alleged the tool had generated more than $1.4 billion in additional profit for Amazon over roughly five years of use, including tens of millions of dollars in extra profit from books alone and meaningful additional margin across at least a dozen other product categories the complaint specifically named. Amazon disputed the FTC's characterization, describing the tool publicly as a defensive mechanism meant to prevent its own automated price-matching systems from spiraling into unsustainably low prices rather than as an instrument for extracting extra profit from shoppers, and stating that the project had been discontinued years before the lawsuit was filed because it had not worked as intended. Unlike RealPage, Project Nessie involved no pooling of data across competing companies and no shared software used by multiple independent sellers — it was, on the FTC's own telling, a single company's unilateral pricing algorithm, raising a different and in some ways more fundamental question than RealPage's alleged horizontal coordination: whether a sufficiently large, sufficiently dominant retailer's own algorithmic pricing decisions, entirely unilateral and entirely legal in form, can themselves constitute the kind of harmful market power antitrust law exists to check, once that retailer controls enough of a market to predict, with real confidence, how rivals will respond to its own price moves.

Taken together, the RealPage litigation, the Amazon Project Nessie allegations, and the German gasoline research point toward a genuinely uncomfortable structural conclusion this book's earlier chapters on shill bidding and bid-rigging have been building toward without quite stating outright until now: software has not eliminated the temptation toward auction and pricing manipulation that has apparently accompanied every competitive-pricing mechanism this book has examined, from Roman sealed bids under the spear to nineteenth-century tobacco chants to twenty-first-century apartment leases. It has, if these cases are representative of a broader pattern rather than isolated anomalies, made a certain specific and historically difficult category of manipulation — tacit coordination among many separate, formally independent competitors, achieved without the risky, discoverable meetings that have historically been prosecutors' most reliable form of evidence — measurably easier to achieve and, prior to RealPage's own high-profile prosecution, measurably harder to detect and legally punish. The 1961 electrical-equipment conspirators needed hotel rooms, travel expense reports, and eventually a cooperating witness willing to testify against former colleagues; a landlord using RealPage, or an airline participating in a shared fare-distribution platform, or a gas station owner in a small German town running an off-the-shelf pricing package, needed only to accept the software's own automated recommendation without asking too many questions about where its numbers actually came from — an action indistinguishable, from the outside, from any other business owner's entirely legal, entirely ordinary decision to trust a sophisticated, data-driven analytical tool over their own slower, less rigorous intuition. Whether antitrust law, criminal enforcement, and consumer-protection regulation can adapt quickly enough to police that distinction at the scale software now allows it to operate is a question this book's remaining chapters, turning from pricing algorithms toward the emerging infrastructure for AI agents that bid and transact with even less human oversight still, cannot yet answer with any real confidence.
