# The Algorithm That Colluded

In 1961, executives in the heavy-electrical-equipment business had a coordination problem. They wanted the benefits of competition without quite so much competition. The solution was cumbersome. Men met in hotel rooms. They used a rotation system to decide who would submit the low bid. They carried the arrangement through enough airports, expense accounts, telephone calls, and breakfasts that when the conspiracy finally broke open, prosecutors had a trail to follow.

The attraction of software is that it can make cumbersome things routine.

That is the more useful way to understand the Justice Department's case against RealPage than the cleaner story I originally wanted to tell. The clean story was irresistible: the electrical-equipment conspirators had to meet, while apartment landlords sixty years later could allegedly coordinate through an algorithm without speaking to one another at all. It had the pleasing symmetry of a technology parable. It was also too neat for the government's own complaint.

When the United States sued RealPage on August 23, 2024, joined by several states, it alleged that the Texas software company had built a system through which competing landlords supplied nonpublic information about rents, vacancies, lease terms, and other commercially sensitive facts. RealPage then used data from many participants to generate pricing recommendations back to the landlords. The antitrust theory was not that computers had spontaneously invented a cartel. It was that a shared data-and-recommendation system could become part of the machinery through which competitors softened competition.

Then the government made the story less futuristic.

On January 7, 2025, the Justice Department amended its complaint to add six large landlords. The amended case did not describe a world in which software had eliminated human coordination. It alleged direct communications among landlord executives, regular competitor "call arounds," market surveys, and exchanges of sensitive information alongside the common pricing system. Later government filings went further. In announcing a proposed settlement with Pinnacle in September 2026, the department said competing landlords had conferred about pricing strategies, rents, and parameters used in RealPage's software, and that RealPage itself had hosted meetings attended by competing property managers.

Hotel rooms had not disappeared after all. They had acquired dashboards.

That makes the case more interesting, not less. A cartel run entirely by autonomous software would be a dramatic break with economic history. A hybrid system is more plausible and probably more consequential: humans still talk, still compare notes, still have incentives they understand perfectly well, but software can turn episodic coordination into infrastructure. It can collect information every day instead of at the next dinner. It can turn a competitor's private leasing conditions into an input to an ordinary workflow. It can make the recommendation appear not as a favor from a rival but as the output of a professional system purchased precisely because it claims to know the market better than any one landlord does.

The government's theory therefore depends on something more specific than the existence of an algorithm. Landlords have always watched competitors. Hotels check neighboring rates. Airlines watch fares. A shop owner can walk across the street and read the price in the other shop's window. Antitrust law does not generally prohibit firms from reacting intelligently to public information, even when everybody's intelligent reaction produces an uncomfortable degree of parallel pricing.

The line DOJ tried to draw in RealPage was between observing a market and jointly feeding a machine with private competitor information. According to the complaint, participating landlords did not merely look at public asking rents. They supplied RealPage with nonpublic data from their own operations, and the system incorporated competitors' data when producing recommendations. The government treated that shared pipeline as part of an alleged agreement to restrain competition.

That distinction matters because American antitrust law has long had to live with a maddening fact: competitors can sometimes behave alike without conspiring. In a concentrated market, each firm can learn that cutting price is likely to provoke retaliation and that holding the line may be more profitable. Economists call variants of this tacit coordination. Courts, for good reason, have usually demanded more than parallel prices before calling the behavior an illegal agreement. Otherwise ordinary competition in an oligopoly could become evidence of a crime merely because the competitors are watching one another.

RealPage sits in the uncomfortable middle. If ten landlords independently buy the same weather forecast and all raise rents after a hurricane damages housing stock, common software is not a cartel. If ten landlords secretly hand each other tomorrow's intended prices and promise not to undercut them, calling the spreadsheet an "algorithm" changes nothing. The difficult cases are the ones between those poles, where a vendor becomes the place competitors' private information is pooled, translated, and returned as a recommendation that each participant can describe as its own independent decision.

The scale of the rental market made that question difficult to dismiss as law-school speculation. RealPage's products were widely used across multifamily housing, and government and academic work attempted to estimate the consequences for rents. The precise numbers depend heavily on model choices and should not be mistaken for damages proved at trial. The important fact for this chapter is simpler: regulators came to believe the alleged mechanism was large enough to matter in actual household budgets, and the case quickly ceased to be about one software contract.

The settlement chronology is useful because it shows both how seriously enforcers treated the theory and how much of the underlying law remains unresolved. Cortland agreed to a proposed consent decree on January 7, 2025, the same day the amended complaint was filed. Greystar reached a proposed settlement later in 2025, and the court entered a final judgment against it on March 2, 2026. Willow Bridge reached a proposed settlement on July 6, 2026. Pinnacle followed with a proposed consent decree on September 4, 2026. LivCor also entered the settlement process. These are real enforcement outcomes, not hypothetical warnings.

But they are not all the same legal event, and the distinction matters. A proposed final judgment is not a trial verdict. A settlement can impose meaningful restrictions while leaving the defendant free to deny liability. As of the Justice Department's case index updated September 4, 2026, RealPage's own November 24, 2025 decree was still listed as a proposed final judgment, while Greystar's had been entered by the court. The case therefore should not be written as if an appellate court has already blessed a general doctrine that "algorithms can conspire." It has not.

That unresolved status is not a technicality. It is exactly where the future law will be made. The government has already demonstrated that it can extract commitments restricting the use of competitors' sensitive data and common pricing tools. What remains less settled is how far those principles travel when the facts become cleaner: when there are no call-arounds, no RealPage-hosted meetings, no executives comparing local pricing strategies, and no obvious human conversation to place beside the software.

The strongest counterargument to using RealPage as proof of algorithmic collusion is therefore hidden inside the government's own success. DOJ alleged too many ordinary forms of coordination for RealPage to be the clean experiment many people want it to be. If the case contains direct conversations, shared market surveys, contractual data flows, meetings of competitors, and a common recommendation engine all at once, then it cannot tell us which piece was necessary. The software may have scaled the alleged conduct. It may have disciplined it. It may have made it easier to sustain. But RealPage is not good evidence that independent algorithms, left alone, learned to behave like a cartel without human help.

For that more ambitious claim, the German gasoline market is a better place to look.

Stephanie Assad, Robert Clark, Daniel Ershov, and Lei Xu studied the spread of algorithmic pricing among German gasoline stations after commercial pricing software became widely available around 2017. The setting is useful because gasoline stations post prices publicly and change them frequently, leaving a much denser behavioral record than apartment leasing. The researchers did not know adoption dates directly, so they inferred adoption using structural breaks in pricing patterns and used headquarters-level adoption as an instrument to address the obvious problem that stations choosing algorithmic tools may differ from stations that do not.

Their result is awkward in exactly the right way. Algorithm adoption raised margins at non-monopoly stations, but in duopoly and triopoly markets the increase appeared when all competing stations had adopted. One station becoming more sophisticated in isolation did not generate the same pattern. The paper's conclusion is cautious: algorithmic pricing had a significant effect on competition in these concentrated local markets.

That does not prove a robot cartel either. The study identifies market effects from adoption, not the internal objective function of every vendor's code. The algorithms may be faster at recognizing one another's moves, more consistent in retaliating against price cuts, or simply better at learning a profitable pattern that human managers previously implemented less reliably. The German evidence is valuable precisely because it does not require a science-fiction claim about intent. Competition can soften even when nobody has instructed the software to exchange a secret handshake.

There is a further complication. Information sharing is not uniformly bad for competition, and attempts to restrict it can have counterintuitive effects. A 2025 *Journal of Political Economy* study by David Byrne and colleagues examined an Australian gasoline antitrust settlement that changed which firms had access to high-frequency rival-price information. When one firm lost access and information became asymmetric, price competition softened and margins increased. In that market, making one competitor strategically ignorant did not produce a cleaner competitive outcome; it helped create a commitment problem that raised prices.

That is the kind of counterevidence policy arguments about algorithmic pricing need. "More transparency" and "less information sharing" are not universal instructions. The competitive effect depends on who sees what, how quickly they see it, whether consumers also gain information, how concentrated the market is, and what the pricing system is optimized to do. The mechanism matters more than the moral label attached to the data.

This is also why the Federal Trade Commission's separate inquiry into surveillance pricing should not be casually folded into RealPage. They are neighboring problems with different structures. RealPage concerns allegations that competitors' sensitive information flowed through a shared pricing system. Surveillance pricing concerns the possibility that a seller uses detailed information about a particular consumer to estimate that person's willingness to pay and tailor a price accordingly. One is principally about coordination among sellers. The other is about discrimination between buyers. A company can do one without doing the other.

The distinction is easy to lose because both arrive on the screen as a number produced by software. From the customer's side, a rent recommendation, a personalized airline offer, and an auction bid can all look like prices. Economically and legally they are different machines.

Amazon's alleged Project Nessie illustrates a third machine. In the FTC's 2023 case against Amazon, the agency alleged that an internal pricing system tested whether rivals would follow Amazon price increases and, when the system expected them to do so, allowed higher prices to persist. Amazon disputed the characterization and said the tool had been intended in part to prevent automated price matching from driving prices too low. Whatever the merits of that dispute, Nessie is conceptually different from RealPage because the allegation involves one large firm's own pricing behavior rather than multiple competitors pooling private operational data through a common vendor.

Put the three examples beside one another and "algorithmic pricing" stops being a useful diagnosis by itself. In one case, a common intermediary allegedly pools competitor data. In another, multiple firms adopt pricing software and market outcomes change when all of them do. In a third, one dominant platform allegedly predicts and exploits rivals' likely responses. Add surveillance pricing and the system is estimating the customer rather than the competitor. The algorithms are the visible common element, but the economic mechanism keeps changing underneath them.

This is where the history of auctions becomes useful again. An auction is not just "a computer choosing a price." It is a set of rules about what information participants reveal, who receives it, when they receive it, and how those signals are converted into an allocation. Change one of those pieces and you can change bidder strategy even if the interface still shows the same button labeled BID. The same is true of pricing software. The consequential question is not whether a machine touched the number. It is what information structure the machine creates.

That is what RealPage adds to the history in this book. The electrical-equipment cartel had to build its information structure by hand. Someone had to know whose turn it was, what number should win, what the cover bids should look like, and how to pass those instructions among people who were supposed to be competitors. The arrangement was labor-intensive because coordination itself was labor-intensive.

Software can lower that cost. A common vendor can ingest data continuously. A recommendation can arrive as part of normal revenue-management work rather than as an exceptional call from a rival. Compliance can be measured. Deviations can be noticed. None of this proves that a particular vendor has created an illegal agreement. It changes the engineering cost of coordination, which is a different and in some ways more durable claim.

The old cartel left evidence because it had metabolism: flights, meetings, witnesses, notes, and people who had to remember the scheme. The new systems have metabolism too. They produce contracts, data schemas, access permissions, recommendation logs, override rates, vendor meetings, training materials, and code. That is why the RealPage case did not remain a philosophical argument about whether an algorithm could possess conspiratorial intent. The government went looking for the ordinary interfaces around the software.

And found humans.

That should be reassuring to anyone tempted by the most dramatic version of the story. We are not yet living in a market where autonomous pricing agents have made human conspirators obsolete. It should also be unsettling. The historical novelty does not require autonomous collusion. It is enough that the machinery of coordination can become easier to operate, harder for outsiders to see, and ordinary enough that each participant can experience its own action as nothing more remarkable than following a recommendation on a screen.

The next chapter moves into markets where the machine is no longer merely recommending a price to a person. Software agents are beginning to shop, negotiate, bid, and transact on a person's behalf. That transition raises a sharper version of the RealPage problem. Once the recommendation and the execution happen inside the same system, the question will no longer be only what the algorithm told the human to do.

It will be what the market's machines learned to do with one another.
