# Bidding Fever

When eBay launched in September 1995 as AuctionWeb, a side project of the programmer Pierre Omidyar, it inherited a design choice that would end up generating one of the more counterintuitive bodies of research in behavioral economics: rather than asking bidders to enter and re-enter individual bids by hand every time they were outbid, the site let a bidder enter a single maximum value once, up front, and then had its own software automatically bid on that person's behalf, in small increments, only as much as necessary to stay the current highest bidder, up to the ceiling the bidder had specified. This system, called proxy bidding, was explicitly modeled on the logic William Vickrey had worked out in 1961: a bidder who entered their true maximum valuation once, honestly, and then simply walked away should, in theory, do exactly as well as a bidder who compulsively checked the auction every few minutes to bid the smallest possible increment above the current leader, because the software was quietly doing that compulsive checking for them, automatically, for free. If Vickrey's theorem held in practice the way it held on paper, eBay's millions of users — and within a few years of its founding, the site genuinely did have millions of them, transforming what had begun as a hobbyist marketplace for collectibles like Beanie Babies and Pez dispensers into one of the most heavily trafficked auction venues in human history, processing more individual auctions in a single year than Sotheby's and Christie's combined had run across their first two centuries — should have overwhelmingly behaved like the site's own design encouraged: enter a maximum once, log off, and let the outcome resolve itself in the auction's final seconds without a single additional keystroke.

That is not remotely how eBay's users actually behaved, and the gap between the theory and the observed reality turned out to be one of the richest natural experiments behavioral economists have ever gotten for free. Beginning in the early 2000s, the economists Alvin Roth and Axel Ockenfels ran a series of studies directly comparing bidding behavior on eBay, where auctions end at a fixed, publicly announced deadline, against bidding behavior on Amazon's own now-discontinued auction platform, which used a "soft close" rule extending the auction by several extra minutes every time a new bid arrived in the final moments, specifically to discourage last-second bidding. The comparison was close to a controlled experiment running out in the wild: essentially the same population of internet shoppers, bidding on similar goods, under two subtly different rule sets. Roth and Ockenfels found a dramatic difference. On eBay, with its hard, unmovable deadline, a substantial share of all bidding activity — in one dataset the researchers examined, roughly eighteen percent of auctions received a bid within the final sixty seconds — arrived in a last-second rush that bidders in the trade have long called "sniping," submitted deliberately late enough that a rival has no realistic chance to see the bid and respond before the clock runs out. On Amazon's soft-close auctions, where a late snipe accomplished nothing because any bid in the closing moments simply extended the deadline further, late bidding was, by comparison, far less prevalent. Bidders were adapting their strategy precisely to the rules of the specific platform they were using, in a way that a pure, dominant-strategy reading of Vickrey's theorem said should not matter at all.

The explanation Roth and Ockenfels eventually settled on reframes sniping not as an irrational tic but as a rational, if unglamorous, defensive response to *other people's* irrationality — a distinction worth taking seriously, because it changes what the finding actually says about human behavior. If every bidder on eBay entered their honest maximum value once and then genuinely walked away, as the proxy system's design intended, sniping would accomplish nothing, since a snipe only works by denying a rival the chance to respond, and a rival who was never going to respond anyway cannot be usefully denied anything. But not every bidder behaves that way. Some bidders, watching an auction unfold in real time, revise their stated maximum upward mid-auction as they become progressively more emotionally invested in winning — a pattern the researchers termed "incremental bidding," distinct from the one-shot, walk-away behavior the proxy system was designed around. A sophisticated bidder who suspects they are competing against this kind of incrementally escalating rival has a genuine strategic reason to snipe: bidding early tips off an emotionally engaged opponent that competition exists at all, inviting that opponent to keep raising their own maximum in response, whereas bidding only in the auction's final seconds denies the incremental bidder any chance to notice and react before time simply runs out. Sniping, in other words, is not a rejection of game-theoretic reasoning. It is what game-theoretic reasoning actually recommends once you stop assuming every bidder around you is behaving as coolly as the theorem assumes, and Roth and Ockenfels found, consistent with this account, that snipers on eBay tended to be more experienced users — measured by their accumulated feedback scores — while incremental, repeatedly-revised bidding was far more common among newer, less experienced participants still learning how the system, and their own reactions to it, actually worked.

That emotional escalation has its own name in the experimental literature, developed independently of the eBay research but describing much the same underlying phenomenon: auction fever, a well-documented tendency for competitive bidding, once underway, to draw bidders into paying more than their own pre-auction valuation of an item would justify, driven less by new information about the item's worth than by the simple, visceral experience of being in a contest and wanting to win it. Researchers studying this effect in controlled laboratory settings — assigning subjects a private value for a fictional item, then observing how their actual bidding behavior in a live auction compared to that assigned value — have found bidders reliably overshoot their own stated valuations once a competitive dynamic takes hold, particularly in auctions involving a visible rival bidder rather than an anonymous, abstract field of competitors, and particularly under time pressure of the kind a live ascending auction or a closing eBay countdown clock deliberately manufactures. The competitive framing itself, separate from any information the bidding process conveys about the item's actual worth, appears to activate something closer to a desire to defeat a specific opponent than a calm assessment of value for money — the same psychological terrain, dressed in laboratory clothing, that Sotheby's and Christie's learned to cultivate deliberately, two and a half centuries earlier, with pre-sale estimates, sequenced lot ordering, and a well-timed chandelier bid.

The researchers behind the competitive-arousal framework, Gillian Ku, Deepak Malhotra, and J. Keith Murnighan, found a way to test the theory against real money rather than laboratory tokens, using an item whose "correct" value nobody could plausibly dispute: gift certificates, with a face value printed on them, sold at live and online auctions. If bidders were behaving with pure rationality, a rational bidder would never pay more for a fifty-dollar gift certificate than fifty dollars, since the certificate is worth exactly its printed face value and nothing more; any bid above face value is a bid that guarantees a loss the instant the auction ends, with no possible upside. The researchers found exactly this irrational overbidding happening at real, non-trivial scale: in one analysis of eBay auctions for Amazon.com gift certificates, 41.1 percent of completed auctions closed above the certificate's own face value, meaning more than four in ten winners had, in the most literal and easily verifiable sense possible, paid extra money for the simple experience of winning a bidding contest against another person. Their competitive-arousal model traces this to a specific, identifiable bundle of situational triggers baked into the auction format itself — a visible or implied rival, real or perceived time pressure, an audience of some kind watching the outcome, and the simple social-psychological pull of a live contest — each of which independently nudges bidders toward overbidding, and which compound powerfully when several are present at once, as they reliably are in almost any competitive auction format this book has covered so far.

A closely related but conceptually distinct bias compounds the problem once a bidder gets close to actually winning, and it has nothing to do with competition against a rival at all. The endowment effect, documented most famously in a series of experiments Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler ran in the late 1980s, describes a simple but remarkably persistent tendency for people to value something more highly once they feel they possess it than they valued the identical thing moments before possessing it. In the researchers' best-known version of the experiment, university students were randomly given a coffee mug and then asked either the minimum price at which they would sell it back or, for students who had not received a mug, the maximum price they would pay to acquire one. Classical economic theory, embodied in a result known as the Coase theorem, predicted that roughly half the mugs should change hands in a resulting market, since random assignment should have left buyers and sellers with roughly overlapping valuations. Actual trading volume came in far below that prediction, because the students who had been given mugs consistently demanded roughly twice as much to give them up — a median asking price of $5.25 — as the students without mugs were willing to pay to acquire one in the first place, a median of $2.75. Mere possession, or even the anticipation of possession, had measurably inflated the mug's subjective value to its temporary owner. Applied to an auction, particularly a live, ascending one, the implication is unsettling for anyone trying to bid rationally: a bidder who has spent the last several minutes as the *current* highest bidder — psychologically, if not yet legally, the mug's temporary owner — may experience a small but real endowment-driven inflation of how much the item is worth to them, precisely at the moment a rival's counter-bid threatens to take that provisional ownership away, adding one more psychological thumb on the scale pushing the price above where a purely rational, pre-auction valuation would have settled.

James Heyman, Yesim Orhun, and Dan Ariely gave this specific auction-context version of the effect its own name — quasi-endowment — in a 2004 paper that measured it directly rather than merely inferring it from the gap between overall theory and observed eBay behavior. Running both a hypothetical-bid experiment and, more persuasively, an experiment involving real money and real goods, they found that participants who spent more cumulative time occupying the position of current high bidder, even in auctions they ultimately went on to lose, bid measurably more aggressively in the auction's closing moments than participants who arrived late and had never occupied that psychologically inflated position at all. Merely *imagining* oneself as the provisional owner of an item, the researchers concluded, was enough to trigger a scaled-down version of the same possession-driven overvaluation Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler had documented with physical coffee mugs — no literal ownership required, just the accumulated subjective sense, built minute by minute across an ascending auction, of being the person about to win. Heyman, Orhun, and Ariely's paper linked this quasi-endowment effect directly to the same late-bidding patterns Roth and Ockenfels were independently documenting on eBay at almost exactly the same time, proposing that sniping functions, among its other benefits, as a defense against quasi-endowment as much as against an emotionally escalating rival: a bidder who enters the auction only in its final seconds never has the chance to spend extended time feeling like the item's provisional owner, and therefore never accumulates the inflated attachment that time in the lead position reliably produces in everyone else.

A more recent, considerably more expensive episode shows the same closing-minutes dynamic playing out on a stage even Sotheby's and Christie's traditional saleroom rarely occupies. In March 2021, Christie's ran a ten-day online-only auction for *Everydays: The First 5,000 Days*, a digital collage by the artist Mike Winkelmann, known professionally as Beeple, sold together with a non-fungible token intended to certify the work's authenticity — the first time a major auction house had offered a purely digital artwork on those terms, and the first time cryptocurrency had been accepted as payment for a work sold at one of the two historic houses this book's third chapter traced back to eighteenth-century London. The auction opened at a token $100 and climbed, over its first several days, past $1 million and then past $9 million, a trajectory that, by the standards of this book's earlier chapters, already looked like a fairly ordinary rising sale. The real escalation arrived in a compressed final burst entirely consistent with the sniping and auction-fever patterns this chapter has spent its length documenting: with thirty-three bidders having competed across the auction's full ten-day run, the price began jumping in ten- and fifteen-million-dollar increments in the sale's final two minutes alone, closing at a winning bid of $60.25 million, or $69.3 million once Christie's buyer's premium was added — a final-moments acceleration that mirrors, at a radically larger dollar scale and over a radically longer overall auction window, the exact same late-stage intensity Roth and Ockenfels documented in ordinary eBay listings closing in under a minute.

Taken together, these three findings — Roth and Ockenfels's evidence that a platform's closing rules shape whether sniping or incremental escalation dominates its auctions, Ku, Malhotra, and Murnighan's evidence that more than four in ten winners of a gift-certificate auction paid above the certificate's own printed value, and Heyman, Orhun, and Ariely's evidence that mere time spent in the lead measurably inflates a bidder's willingness to keep paying — describe a single coherent picture rather than three unrelated curiosities. An ascending, competitive, real-time auction is not simply a neutral information-gathering device that happens to be vulnerable, on occasion, to the odd irrational bidder. It is closer to a psychological pressure chamber, purpose-built by two and a half centuries of institutional refinement, in which ordinary, otherwise sensible people can be reliably, predictably, and legally induced to pay more than their own calm judgment would ever have sanctioned, simply by being placed in a room, or on a website, that makes winning feel like it matters more than it actually does.

Sniping proved popular enough, once bidders discovered how effective it could be, that an entire small industry grew up to automate it, which is itself worth noting as an early, low-stakes preview of a theme this book returns to repeatedly in its later chapters on machine bidding. Rather than trust their own reflexes to place a bid in the closing three or four seconds of an eBay auction — a window narrow enough that a slow internet connection, a moment's hesitation, or simple bad luck could cost a bidder the item entirely — increasing numbers of eBay users turned to third-party sniping services and browser tools that would submit a pre-set maximum bid automatically, at a precisely timed instant just before the countdown clock reached zero, removing the human reflex from the equation altogether. eBay itself never built this functionality into the platform; the company's own proxy-bidding system, after all, had been explicitly designed on Vickrey's logic to make such last-second maneuvering unnecessary in principle, which made the popularity of these unofficial sniping tools a kind of quiet, continuous referendum, cast by millions of ordinary bidders, on how much they actually trusted that principle to hold up against real human opponents in practice. The tools worked by removing exactly the psychological vulnerability the rest of this chapter has been describing — auction fever's slow, arousal-driven escalation, the endowment effect's growing attachment to a provisional lead — by ensuring a bidder's true maximum was submitted once, mechanically, at the latest possible moment, with no opportunity for the bidder's own emotions to intervene and revise that number upward in the heat of a closing few minutes. A human being had, in effect, hired a small piece of software to protect them from their own foreseeable irrationality, a full two decades and more before "AI agent" ever entered the popular vocabulary as a phrase describing something anyone would seriously trust with an actual purchasing decision made on their behalf, with real money, against real strangers, in a real and genuinely competitive auction.

None of this machinery — sniping as a rational defense against emotionally escalating rivals, auction fever as a genuine physiological response to live competition, the endowment effect's quiet inflation of a bidder's sense of ownership — requires any dishonesty or manipulation on the part of an auction house to operate. It operates on perfectly honest bidders, bidding in perfectly honestly run auctions, simply because the ascending, competitive, real-time structure of the format itself reliably triggers these responses in ordinary human psychology. That is precisely what makes the format so commercially durable and, from a consumer-protection standpoint, so genuinely worth scrutinizing: an auction house does not need to rig anything to extract more money from its bidders than those bidders' own calm, pre-auction judgment would have paid, because the room, the rising number, the countdown clock, and the visible presence of a determined rival are already doing that work on their own, night after night, entirely within the rules. Sotheby's and Christie's, as the earlier chapter on their history showed, learned to cultivate this dynamic deliberately, through pre-sale estimates and careful lot sequencing and a strategically placed phantom bid. eBay, somewhat more accidentally, built a platform whose specific closing-time rules turned out to shape which flavor of this same psychology — cool, defensive sniping or hot, escalating incrementalism — would come to dominate its marketplace, without anyone at the company necessarily setting out to run that particular experiment on human nature at global scale.

It is worth being precise, in closing, about what this chapter has and has not argued. It has not argued that eBay, or Sotheby's, or any other honestly run auction house is secretly defrauding its bidders; every mechanism described here operates in full public view, under rules any bidder can read in advance, and produces outcomes that are, in the strict legal sense, entirely fair. What it has argued is that "fair" and "free of psychological distortion" are not the same property, and that an institution can satisfy the first without coming anywhere close to the second — a distinction that matters enormously once the next chapter turns from psychology that merely exploits an honest auction's structure to techniques that cross, sometimes quietly and sometimes quite deliberately, into outright deception.

The next chapter follows this same territory into darker terrain, where the line between exploiting predictable psychology and actively defrauding a bidder gets considerably harder to locate, and where the tools available to a dishonest seller — a chandelier bid raised at exactly the wrong moment against a real bidder rather than merely to protect a disclosed reserve, a shill account bidding against a genuine buyer with no intention of ever actually paying for the item, a website explicitly engineered from the ground up to weaponize the sunk-cost instinct rather than merely benefit from it as an incidental byproduct of ordinary competitive bidding — move from the gray zone of legal-but-aggressive psychological pressure into outright fraud, sometimes prosecuted as such by regulators and sometimes, remarkably and for reasons this book will examine closely in the pages immediately ahead, not prosecuted at all.
