## A note before the bidding starts

Every claim of fact in this book is drawn from a cited source: an academic
paper, a court filing, a regulator's own record, a company's own numbers, or
contemporaneous reporting. Where a number is an estimate, the text says so.
Where a study has been disputed, the dispute is in the text, not buried in a
footnote. Nothing here is a dramatized reconstruction; where the record does
not say what someone was thinking, the book does not guess.

The subject turned out to be larger than it first looked. An auction is not
a niche institution for art and livestock. It is a general-purpose way of
asking a crowd what something is worth, and refusing to accept the first
answer. Once you start looking for that pattern, it is hard to stop finding
it: in the price of an antenna slot over Manhattan, a two-year Treasury
note, a click on a search results page, a kidney, a ride home on a Friday
night, and a ton of carbon that no one can see or touch. The book follows
that pattern from a Babylonian marriage market to a data center executing
bids in under a tenth of a second, because the machinery underneath those
two events is closer to identical than either era would have guessed.
