# When the Bidder Is a Machine

Every auction this book has examined so far, however automated its pricing mechanics became, has ultimately rested on a human decision somewhere upstream of the bid itself: a person deciding how much they were willing to pay, even if a piece of software then executed that decision at superhuman speed. eBay's sniping tools placed a bid a human had already chosen. A real-time bidding platform's algorithm evaluated an impression against targeting criteria a human advertiser had configured. Even the RealPage software this book's seventeenth chapter examined was, formally, only ever issuing a *recommendation*, which a human property manager retained the legal authority to reject. The infrastructure now emerging for artificial intelligence agents to transact directly on a person's behalf represents something the rest of this book's history has been quietly building toward without quite reaching: not software executing a human's already-formed decision, but software forming the decision itself, evaluating options, and completing a transaction, potentially including a competitive bid against other bidders, with a human involved only at the level of the original, general instruction rather than the specific, individual transaction.

High-frequency trading supplies the closest existing precedent for what this transition actually looks like once it matures, and it is worth understanding as a preview before turning to the genuinely new infrastructure built over the past two years. Modern stock and derivatives exchanges operate as continuous double auctions, formally described through a mechanism called the central limit order book: buy orders and sell orders arrive continuously throughout the trading day, are ranked first by price and then, among orders at the same price, by the simple order in which they arrived, and are matched automatically the instant a buy order's price meets or exceeds a sell order's price, all without any human auctioneer ever calling out a number or bringing down a gavel. Beginning in the 2000s and accelerating through the 2010s, an increasing share of the orders flowing into this continuous auction stopped being placed by human traders looking at a screen and started being generated by algorithmic trading systems, executing strategies at speeds — sometimes measured in millionths of a second — no human nervous system could physically match, competing against each other for the same fleeting pricing opportunities in what researchers studying the phenomenon have explicitly termed a technological arms race, one serious enough that some market-design economists, including several of the same researchers whose work this book has traced through earlier chapters, have proposed replacing the continuous limit order book entirely with frequent batch auctions — discrete, uniform-price auctions held every tenth of a second or so, specifically to blunt the pure speed advantage that a purely continuous, millisecond-by-millisecond matching system inevitably rewards. High-frequency trading's bidders have been, for the better part of two decades now, overwhelmingly software rather than people; what has changed only very recently, and what this chapter's remaining pages take up in detail, is software beginning to act as a bidder not merely in the narrow, specialized world of securities trading, but across the entire ordinary landscape of everyday consumer and business commerce.

Google's September 2025 launch of the Agent Payments Protocol marks, as clean a starting point as any for dating this transition, the moment the infrastructure for machine-initiated commerce moved from scattered, proprietary experiments into an open, broadly adopted industry standard. AP2, as the protocol is generally known, defines a cryptographically verifiable way for an AI agent to prove to a merchant or a payment network that a real human genuinely authorized a specific purchase, structured around what the protocol calls mandates — digitally signed statements defining precisely what an agent has been authorized to do, whether creating a shopping cart, completing a single specific purchase, or managing an ongoing subscription on a person's behalf. The protocol launched with more than sixty companies already signed on as partners, spanning payment networks including Mastercard and American Express, payment processors including PayPal and Coinbase, and major enterprise software providers including Salesforce, a breadth of immediate industry buy-in that signaled the major players across payments infrastructure had concluded, roughly simultaneously, that agentic commerce was arriving quickly enough to require a shared technical standard rather than dozens of incompatible proprietary systems each trying to solve the authorization problem independently. Weeks earlier, OpenAI and the payments company Stripe had jointly released a complementary open-source specification, the Agentic Commerce Protocol, focused more narrowly on standardizing the actual checkout flow between an AI agent and a merchant once a purchase decision has already been authorized — the practical mechanics of completing a transaction, distinct from AP2's broader focus on proving the authorization itself was legitimate. A related but distinct protocol addresses a problem upstream of payment authorization altogether: how two AI agents, potentially built on entirely different underlying models by entirely different companies, discover each other's capabilities and coordinate a task in the first place. Google introduced its own Agent2Agent protocol, generally abbreviated A2A, in April 2025, an open standard letting one agent publish a machine-readable description of what it can do and letting other agents discover, authenticate with, and delegate work to it across vendor and framework boundaries, all without a human writing custom integration code to connect any specific pair of agents together. Google donated the specification to the Linux Foundation for neutral, vendor-independent governance in June 2025, and by the middle of that year more than a hundred fifty organizations, spanning software vendors, enterprises, and competing AI developers, had signed on as supporters — evidence that A2A had, like AP2 and ACP before it, achieved the kind of broad, cross-company adoption that separates a genuine industry standard from one company's isolated proprietary experiment. A2A is generally described, in the technical literature that grew up around it, as solving agent-to-agent coordination specifically, complementing rather than replacing a separate protocol called the Model Context Protocol that the AI company Anthropic had introduced some months earlier to solve a related but distinct problem: how a single agent retrieves relevant information and invokes tools from external systems it needs to complete a task. Together, this fast-consolidating stack of interlocking standards — coordination, tool access, payment authorization, and checkout — supplies most of the raw infrastructure a genuinely autonomous, multi-agent commercial auction would need to run, even though, as this chapter has taken care to note, no comparably standardized infrastructure yet exists for the bidding logic itself. A third protocol, promoted by the cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase and known as x402, standardizes machine-to-machine settlement using stablecoins over ordinary internet infrastructure, aimed specifically at the case where one piece of software needs to pay another piece of software directly, with no human-facing checkout page involved in the transaction at all.

OpenAI's own early attempt to build a fully agentic shopping experience directly into ChatGPT supplies a useful, humbling corrective to any assumption that this transition will arrive smoothly or on the schedule its own builders initially expected. In January 2025, the company demonstrated an agent called Operator capable of autonomously navigating a web browser to complete real tasks — in one widely circulated demonstration, reading a handwritten grocery list held up to a camera, opening Instacart, building a complete shopping cart, and scheduling delivery without further human intervention. Building on that foundation, OpenAI launched a feature called Instant Checkout inside ChatGPT in September 2025, letting users complete a purchase directly within the chat interface through a "buy" button, beginning with Etsy and planned Shopify-merchant integrations. The feature did not survive long: by March 2026, less than six months after launch, OpenAI quietly discontinued Instant Checkout, citing an inability to offer the level of flexibility the company wanted the experience to have, and shifted ChatGPT's shopping functionality back toward product recommendations that route users to a merchant's own conventional checkout rather than completing the transaction autonomously inside the chat itself. Consumer research conducted around the same period helps explain why full autonomy proved harder to sustain commercially than the underlying technology alone: one survey found that while a large majority of shoppers, over three-quarters, had used AI to help inform a purchase decision, a meaningful share described themselves as openly skeptical or actively suspicious of AI tools that could complete a purchase entirely on their own, with roughly two-fifths registering some form of active distrust. The underlying payment and authorization infrastructure this chapter has described — the Agentic Commerce Protocol chief among it — survived the retreat and continued developing as an open industry standard even as OpenAI's own first consumer-facing implementation of full autonomous checkout did not, a useful reminder that infrastructure and adoption are separate questions, and that the first company to build the pipes is not guaranteed to be the first to successfully run water through them.

By early 2026, these protocols had moved from announcement to operational infrastructure carrying real, if still comparatively small and, on close inspection, somewhat ambiguous transaction volume. Coinbase reported that AI agents had executed more than two hundred million individual transactions through its x402 protocol by the spring of 2026, spanning tens of thousands of active agents and running at an annualized pace the company put in the hundreds of millions of dollars, processed with zero protocol fees and settled almost entirely in dollar-pegged stablecoins rather than in more volatile cryptocurrencies. That headline transaction count, though, came with an important caveat that researchers tracking the protocol's actual adoption were careful to flag: independent analysis suggested a substantial share of the reported transaction volume, by some estimates close to half, reflected small-scale testing, demonstration, and what researchers termed "gamified" activity rather than genuine commercial transactions with real economic stakes behind them, with actual day-to-day real-money volume running to a comparatively modest daily figure once that activity was excluded. The gap between an impressive headline number and a more modest, closely audited reality is itself a useful closing data point for this chapter, and arguably for this book as a whole: agentic commerce infrastructure, as of this writing, is unmistakably real, unmistakably growing, and still meaningfully smaller and less mature than its own most-cited statistics, read uncritically, would suggest — a gap between promise and current substance this book has now encountered often enough, across enough different auction formats and enough different eras, to treat as the rule rather than the exception whenever a genuinely new pricing mechanism is still this early in its working life. Coinbase launched a platform called Agent Market in April 2026, explicitly framed as an app store for autonomous software agents, letting agents built by different developers and different companies discover, transact with, and pay each other directly using stablecoins settled through the x402 protocol, with no human approving each individual machine-to-machine payment as it occurred. Salesforce made its own Agentforce Commerce platform generally available around the same period, including a business-to-business buyer agent capable of handling reordering conversations conducted over ordinary messaging channels like WhatsApp and text messaging, comparing the resulting request against a company's existing negotiated contract pricing and completing rebuy transactions with minimal human review. Industry research firms tracking enterprise adoption projected that a meaningful share of business-to-business sellers — one widely cited 2026 forecast put the figure at roughly one in five — would face at least some agent-led price negotiation from their own customers' purchasing systems by the end of that year, a genuinely rapid diffusion for infrastructure that had not existed in any standardized form eighteen months earlier. Forecasts for how large agentic commerce will actually become vary widely enough, across different research firms using different definitions and methodologies, that the honest response is to treat any single number with real caution rather than false precision. McKinsey's analysts projected agentic commerce could orchestrate as much as $1 trillion in U.S. retail revenue by 2030, and as much as $3 trillion to $5 trillion globally, framing the technology as one of the more consequential shifts in retail's underlying structure since the arrival of e-commerce itself. Morgan Stanley's own analysts, working from more conservative assumptions about how quickly consumers and merchants would actually cede purchasing decisions to autonomous software, put a considerably narrower range on the same U.S. market: somewhere between $190 billion in a base case and $385 billion in a more optimistic scenario by the same year, representing perhaps ten to twenty percent of total American e-commerce. That the same underlying technological trend can generate credible institutional forecasts differing by an order of magnitude or more says less about any individual analyst's competence than about how genuinely early and unsettled this particular chapter of the auction's long history still is — a level of forecasting uncertainty this book's own historical chapters, covering auctions with two and five hundred and two thousand years of settled track record behind them, never once had to contend with.

None of this infrastructure was built with the specific, narrow purpose of running auctions, and it is worth being precise about that distinction before drawing the chapter's central implication, because overstating the current state of the technology would repeat exactly the kind of unearned certainty this book's human-prose principles have tried to avoid throughout. AP2, ACP, and x402 solve an authorization and settlement problem — proving an agent is allowed to spend a specific amount of a specific person's or company's money, and completing that payment once authorization is established — rather than solving a bidding problem in the formal, game-theoretic sense this book has spent its length examining. But the two problems converge naturally and, on the evidence already available by 2026, quickly, the instant an agent is deployed into any setting where multiple sellers, or multiple competing buyers, are present simultaneously. An AI shopping agent instructed to "find trail-running shoes under $150 that arrive by Friday," to take the illustrative example several industry explainers of agentic commerce have used, is already, functionally, running a private, informal auction among the merchants carrying eligible inventory, evaluating their prices, shipping options, and available stock against the stated criteria and selecting a winner — a process that differs from the real-time bidding auctions this book's fourteenth chapter described mainly in that the "bidders," for now, are merchants' existing posted prices rather than merchants actively submitting live, competitive bids to the specific agent doing the shopping. It requires no great speculative leap, only the ordinary continuation of a trend every previous chapter of this book has documented, to expect merchants' own selling systems to begin bidding directly and dynamically for a given agent's specific, well-defined purchase intent, the same way advertisers' demand-side platforms already bid, in well under a tenth of a second, for a specific visitor's specific ad impression.

The genuinely new element this transition introduces, distinct from every prior wave of auction automation this book has traced, is the disappearance of the specific psychological vulnerabilities this book's seventh chapter spent an entire chapter cataloguing. An AI purchasing agent does not experience auction fever; it has no visceral, arousal-driven pull toward winning a competitive contest for its own sake, the specific mechanism Ku, Malhotra, and Murnighan's research linked to more than four in ten winners of a gift-certificate auction paying above face value. It does not experience the quasi-endowment effect Heyman, Orhun, and Ariely documented, since it never occupies the psychologically loaded position of "current high bidder" feeling a rising, unearned attachment to an item it does not yet own. It does not fall prey, at least not for the same reasons a human bidder does, to the winner's curse, since a properly designed purchasing agent can be built from the ground up to apply exactly the kind of disciplined, probability-adjusted bid-shading Capen, Clapp, and Campbell's 1971 paper prescribed and that human bidders, across five subsequent decades of behavioral research, have reliably struggled to apply consistently even after having the underlying logic carefully explained to them. If the auction's long history, as this book has told it, is substantially a history of a genuinely useful information-aggregation mechanism running headlong into the predictable, well-documented limitations of human psychology — limitations Sotheby's chandelier bids, Swoopo's sunk-cost trap, and Ku, Malhotra, and Murnighan's gift-certificate overbidders each exploited in their own way — then an agent immune to those specific limitations should, in principle, make auctions work considerably better, extracting genuinely efficient prices without the wasteful emotional overshoot this book has spent several chapters documenting.

Whether that optimistic reading survives contact with reality depends heavily on a genuinely open question this book cannot yet answer with the confidence its earlier, more historically settled chapters could offer, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise: an agent immune to human auction fever is not thereby immune to every other failure mode this book has catalogued. The RealPage case, decided using software that never claimed any capacity for anything resembling human-like reasoning at all, already demonstrates that algorithmic intermediaries pooling data across formally competing parties can produce coordination antitrust law struggles to reach using doctrine built for human cartels; a more sophisticated AI agent, negotiating and bidding on behalf of millions of individual users simultaneously while trained on shared or similar underlying models and data, raises the same structural concern at a considerably larger scale and with considerably less transparency into how any individual bidding decision was actually reached. An agent is also not immune to a fabricated signal the way a human bidder's own senses might, with effort, eventually catch — a corrupted or manipulated data feed, a spoofed merchant listing, or a deliberately poisoned bid request could exploit an agent's own blind trust in its inputs in ways a suspicious, experienced human negotiator might have caught through simple, hard-to-formalize intuition. And the shill-bidding, chandelier-bidding, and provenance-fraud problems this book's eighth chapter catalogued in loving historical detail have no obvious reason to disappear simply because the bidder reading a fraudulent signal happens to be software rather than a person; if anything, an agent operating at real-time bidding's characteristic speed and volume, evaluating and acting on thousands of signals a second with no human in the loop to apply the kind of skepticism a Beltracchi forgery or a Swoopo sunk-cost trap eventually provoked in at least some of the humans who encountered it, may prove considerably easier to systematically deceive at scale than any individual human bidder ever was, precisely because deceiving it requires fooling a pattern-matching system rather than a suspicious, experienced human being.

Regulators have not waited for the technology to fully mature before beginning to build a legal framework around it, though the resulting rules remain, as of this writing, considerably less settled than the antitrust doctrine this book's seventeenth chapter described applying to RealPage. The European Union's AI Act, the most comprehensive AI-specific regulatory framework any major jurisdiction has yet enacted, does not treat autonomous agents as a wholly separate legal category from other AI systems, instead folding them into its existing risk-tiered framework based on what the agent actually does and in what context it operates; provisions banning outright manipulation and the exploitation of a user's vulnerabilities became enforceable as early as February 2025, well before the Act's broader high-risk obligations, which govern AI systems used in contexts like hiring, credit decisions, and critical infrastructure, phase in on a staggered schedule extending into 2027 and 2028. Legal analysts examining how the framework applies to a shopping or negotiating agent specifically have concluded that responsibility falls primarily on whichever company deploys the agent in a given commercial context, rather than on the company that built the underlying general-purpose model, meaning a retailer or a payment company implementing an AP2-based or ACP-based agent bears meaningful regulatory exposure regardless of how thoroughly the model itself was tested before that specific deployment. No comparably comprehensive American federal framework existed as of this book's drafting, leaving agentic commerce's legal landscape in the United States considerably more fragmented, governed for now by the same general-purpose consumer-protection, antitrust, and payments regulation this book's earlier chapters have already shown straining, with only partial success, to keep pace with automated pricing and bidding systems that predate AI agents by more than two decades — the same real-time bidding infrastructure this book's fourteenth chapter described, still running its own hundred-millisecond auctions today, largely outside any AI-specific regulatory framework at all, simply because it arrived, scaled, and matured well before regulators anywhere had settled on a name for the broader category of problem it actually represented.

This book's final chapter does not attempt to resolve that tension, because the evidence available as of this writing does not yet permit a confident resolution either way, and any claim to the contrary would be exactly the kind of unearned certainty the historical chapters preceding this one have tried, deliberately, to avoid. What the evidence does permit is a clear statement of the stakes involved in getting the answer right, stakes this book's entire five-thousand-year historical arc has been quietly assembling the case for from its opening pages in Herodotus's contested Babylon to its closing pages in a Coinbase press release: the auction, in whatever form it takes next, will keep allocating an outsized share of what people actually get, at what price, for as long as scarcity itself persists — which is to say, for as long as there is a civilization left to keep needing one.
