# Shill Bidders and Ghost Paddles

Chandelier bidding, the practice this book's third chapter introduced — an auctioneer calling out a bid from no actual bidder, typically to protect a confidential reserve price — sits at one end of a spectrum of auction manipulation that runs from the fully legal to the flatly criminal, and the line separating those two ends turns out to be narrower, and more procedurally technical, than most bidders assume. In New York, where a large share of the world's major art sales take place, the practice is explicitly permitted so long as the phantom bids the auctioneer calls stay strictly below the item's disclosed reserve, on the theory that the auctioneer is merely protecting a seller's already-agreed minimum rather than deceiving a genuine buyer about the item's true market demand. City regulations require auction houses to disclose, in their catalogues or other published sale materials, whether a given lot carries a reserve at all — a disclosure obligation that exists precisely because regulators recognized how easily an unconstrained version of the same basic technique could slide from protecting a seller's floor into actively inflating a buyer's sense of how much competition they were really facing. Cross that reserve-price line — have the auctioneer, or a confederate working with the auctioneer, call out a phantom bid *above* the disclosed reserve, purely to drive a genuine bidder higher than that bidder would otherwise have gone — and the same basic technique stops being a permitted protective device and becomes shill bidding, a form of fraud that has occupied regulators, prosecutors, and civil litigants for as long as auctions have existed to defraud people at.

The rise of the internet, it should be said plainly, did not invent shill bidding, but it did something almost as consequential: it made the practice dramatically cheaper to attempt and, for a while, dramatically harder to detect, by replacing a single auctioneer's voice in a physical room, whose behavior at least a room full of witnesses could observe and later recall, with an anonymous username submitting bids from a keyboard that could, in principle, belong to the seller's own second account, a friend's account, or software written for the specific purpose. eBay's own history includes a long, well-documented string of shill-bidding enforcement actions and civil lawsuits, spanning the platform's first two decades, in which sellers were caught using secondary accounts — sometimes their own, registered under a different name, and sometimes accounts belonging to friends or family members recruited specifically for the purpose — to bid up their own listings, driving genuine bidders to pay more than they would have paid in an honestly competitive auction, without those genuine bidders ever having any way to know, from inside the auction itself, that one of their apparent rivals was not a rival at all. The practice is difficult to prosecute for a structural reason that recurs constantly in this book's later chapters on digital and algorithmic markets: a shill bidder leaves behind exactly the same kind of evidence a genuine, enthusiastic bidder leaves behind — a rising bid, submitted in good time, for a plausible amount — and distinguishing the fraudulent pattern from ordinary competitive behavior after the fact requires either a confession, a subpoenaed paper trail connecting the shill account back to the seller, or a statistical pattern-of-life analysis sophisticated enough to notice that one particular "bidder" only ever appears on this one seller's listings and never, in years of activity, actually wins and pays for anything.

That statistical detective work has, over the past two decades, become a genuine subfield of applied economics and computer science in its own right, and it supplies some of the clearest quantitative evidence available that shill bidding, far from being a rare bad-apple problem, has been a persistent, measurable feature of large online marketplaces. Researchers studying historical eBay bidding data have developed detection methods that look for exactly the fingerprint described above — an account that repeatedly bids on a narrow, suspiciously specific set of listings, almost always from the same small group of sellers, that reliably drives the price upward before withdrawing or losing just before the auction actually closes, and that essentially never appears as a winning, paying bidder anywhere on the platform. Studies applying these detection techniques to large samples of completed eBay auctions, particularly in categories prone to high-value, hard-to-verify goods like jewelry, collectibles, and electronics, have consistently identified a meaningful minority of auctions bearing statistical fingerprints consistent with shill activity, even though the exact prevalence estimates vary considerably depending on which detection method a given study used and how conservatively it was calibrated to avoid false accusations against innocent, merely enthusiastic bidders. Prosecutions, when they happen, tend to turn on exactly the kind of paper trail a fully anonymous digital shill scheme is supposed to avoid leaving. North Yorkshire trading standards officials in England secured a conviction against an eBay seller named Paul Barrett after tracing a pattern in which he had used a second account to bid up dozens of his own listings — mobile phones, a cash register, a car among them — inflating the prices genuine buyers ultimately paid; he pleaded guilty to ten separate offenses under the country's consumer protection regulations and was ordered to pay several thousand pounds in fines and costs alongside two hundred fifty hours of community service, a sentence that reads, next to the nine-figure sums changing hands at Christie's evening sales, almost comically modest for a crime built on the exact same underlying mechanism. American prosecutors have reached for a correspondingly wide range of legal tools against similar schemes: New York State prosecutors have used the Donnelly Act, the state's core antitrust statute against bid-rigging and price-fixing, to bring charges against online shill-bidding rings, carrying penalties that can run into felony prison time and six-figure fines for individuals, while federal prosecutors have separately pursued wire fraud and related charges against organized shill-bidding operations spanning multiple sellers and accounts working in coordination. The available penalties, on paper, look serious. The actual number of prosecutions relative to the scale of activity detection researchers have statistically identified looks, by comparison, quite small — a gap this book's later chapters on algorithmic collusion will show recurring, in a considerably larger and more consequential form, in modern rental-pricing and gasoline markets.

eBay's own policies have evolved substantially in response, explicitly prohibiting sellers from bidding on their own listings or arranging for associates to do so, building automated detection systems into the platform's back end, and reserving the right to cancel bids, suspend accounts, and refer serious cases to law enforcement — an institutional response that mirrors, in miniature, the much larger regulatory apparatus later chapters of this book will show governments building around algorithmic pricing collusion in far higher-stakes markets like housing and gasoline.

The traditional physical-world art trade has produced its own, more recent scandals that combine the old chandelier-bidding gray zone with the newer, harder-edged reality that digital records now make certain kinds of manipulation far easier to reconstruct after the fact than they would have been in Samuel Baker's or James Christie's era. Legal scholars and art-market journalists tracking the issue have documented repeated controversies over the precise line between legitimate reserve protection and outright fraud, and several U.S. states, including New York, have periodically seen legislative proposals aimed specifically at tightening disclosure requirements around chandelier bidding, reflecting an ongoing, unresolved tension between an auction house's commercial interest in a dramatic, competitive-looking sale and a buyer's legitimate interest in knowing whether the "competition" they are bidding against is entirely real. The fact that this tension remains unresolved after roughly two hundred and fifty years of continuous auction-house operation is itself an important data point for this book's larger argument: shill bidding and its variants are not an artifact of any particular technology, easily solved by moving from a physical room to a digital platform or back again. They are a structural temptation built into the ascending-auction format itself, which rewards a seller, at the margin, for making a room or a bidding page look more competitive than it actually is, and every institutional response this book has traced — New York's reserve-disclosure rules, eBay's statistical detection systems, criminal fraud prosecutions on both sides of the format divide — is best understood as an ongoing, never fully finished attempt to suppress a temptation the mechanism itself keeps regenerating.

Shill bidding inflates a price by faking demand that does not exist. A mirror-image tactic, known in the eBay-era literature as bid shielding, instead suppresses a price by faking demand that is deliberately withdrawn at the last possible moment, and it illustrates a different, subtler kind of collusion this book's taxonomy needs to make room for. In a bid-shielding scheme, two colluding accounts work together against every other genuine bidder rather than against the seller: one account submits a very high bid early in the auction, intimidating honest bidders into believing the item has already attracted more competition than it is worth pursuing further and causing many of them to drop out rather than keep raising their own offers, while the second, colluding account sits quietly with a low bid underneath that intimidating ceiling. In the auction's final moments, the high "shield" bid is withdrawn — a maneuver eBay's own rules have, at various points in the platform's history, restricted or required justification for, precisely because the tactic was well understood and actively abused — leaving the low colluding bid to win the item at a price far below what honest competitive bidding would have produced, with the profit from the underpriced win typically split between the two colluding accounts after the fact. Bid shielding never became as prominent a subject of academic detection research as shill bidding, in part because its economic harm falls on the seller rather than on other bidders, and sellers of individual consumer goods on a platform like eBay have historically had far less organized lobbying and enforcement muscle behind them than the buyer-protection advocacy that shaped much of the platform's anti-shill-bidding policy. The asymmetry is worth noting for what it reveals about which side of an auction transaction tends to attract institutional protection: buyer-side fraud, which threatens the perceived fairness of the entire marketplace and therefore threatens the platform's own reputation and revenue, draws sustained enforcement attention; seller-side fraud, which mostly just costs individual, often unsophisticated sellers money one transaction at a time, has historically drawn considerably less. Bid shielding also requires no forged catalogue, no fabricated provenance document, and no statistical detection system to defeat; it works simply because an honest bidder, watching an early number leap unexpectedly high, reasonably concludes that real competing demand exists and adjusts their own behavior accordingly, never suspecting that the number was engineered specifically to produce exactly that reasonable, entirely predictable reaction.

A related but genuinely conceptually distinct category of manipulation targets not the bidding process itself but the information bidders rely on to form their valuations in the first place, and it is worth distinguishing carefully from shill bidding because the legal and ethical analysis differs in important ways. Auction catalogues, whether printed by Christie's in the eighteenth century or displayed on a digital listing page today, are themselves a form of testimony about an item's authenticity, condition, and provenance, and the history of the auction trade includes a long, uncomfortable record of catalogue descriptions that turned out to be wrong — sometimes through honest scholarly error, since attribution of an old or damaged work is a genuinely difficult and evolving science, as the *Salvator Mundi*'s own contested history amply demonstrates, and sometimes through the kind of willful overstatement that shades from optimistic salesmanship into straightforward misrepresentation. Provenance fraud specifically — fabricating or embellishing an object's ownership history to inflate both its perceived authenticity and its market value — has produced some of the art world's most notorious modern scandals, cases in which forged documents, invented prior owners, and fictional exhibition histories were used to give forged or misattributed works a paper trail convincing enough to survive an auction house's own due diligence. These cases differ from shill bidding in an important structural respect: they distort the private valuation every bidder in the room brings to the auction, rather than manipulating the competitive process among bidders who already share roughly accurate information, which makes them, in the vocabulary this book has been building, closer to attacks on the object's assumed common value than attacks on the honesty of the bidding mechanism itself — a distinction with real practical consequences, since a bidder defrauded by a fabricated provenance has, in principle, a claim against the party who lied about the object, while a bidder defrauded by shill bidding has a claim against the seller or auctioneer who manipulated the auction process, and the two categories of claim have historically traveled through very different legal channels.

Beltracchi was hardly the first forger to demonstrate how far a convincing fake could travel through the upper reaches of the art world before detection, and an earlier, stranger case from the mid-twentieth century shows that the underlying vulnerability long predates any of the digital or statistical tools this chapter has otherwise focused on. The Dutch painter Han van Meegeren spent much of the 1930s and early 1940s producing forged paintings in the style of Johannes Vermeer, one of Dutch art's most revered and, because so few authenticated works of his survive, most difficult masters to verify with confidence. Van Meegeren's forgeries were convincing enough to fool the era's leading connoisseurs and, in the case of one painting titled *Christ with the Woman Taken in Adultery*, convincing enough that Hermann Göring, one of the most powerful figures in Nazi Germany and an avid, well-resourced art collector, traded some 137 genuine paintings from his own collection to acquire it, reportedly prizing the piece as one of his most treasured possessions. The forgery unraveled only after the war, when an Allied commission tracing looted and misattributed art back to their rightful owners identified the painting in Göring's collection and traced it to van Meegeren, who now faced a considerably graver charge than art fraud: collaboration with the Nazi occupation, a crime that could have carried the death penalty in liberated Holland. Van Meegeren's defense was, in its own way, as audacious as the forgeries themselves — he confessed publicly to being a forger rather than a collaborator, insisting he had tricked Göring rather than aided him, and to prove the claim he agreed to paint an entirely new "Vermeer" under direct observation by court-appointed witnesses, using the same techniques and materials, demonstrating in real time the exact skill that had fooled Europe's most sophisticated collectors and connoisseurs for the better part of a decade. The demonstration worked; he was convicted only of fraud rather than treason, and died a curious kind of folk hero to a Dutch public that preferred to remember him as the clever forger who had swindled a Nazi leader rather than as a man who had, for years, deceived his own country's most respected art experts for profit.

The German forger Wolfgang Beltracchi supplies one of the most thoroughly documented modern illustrations of how far this kind of provenance manipulation can go, and how long it can survive scrutiny from institutions with every professional incentive to catch it. Working with his wife Helene over several decades, Beltracchi did not copy existing masterpieces, the crude and comparatively easy-to-detect approach most people picture when they hear "art forger." He painted original works in the genuine style of early-twentieth-century modernists, then invented convincing, richly detailed fictional histories for them — claiming they had been rediscovered from a real family's private collection, complete with fabricated old photographs staged to look like archival evidence of the paintings hanging in a period-appropriate interior — and let major auction houses' own specialists supply the final layer of credibility by cataloguing and selling the works as genuine. Christie's, Sotheby's, and other respected dealers all handled paintings that were, unknown to them, Beltracchi's inventions; one fake even appeared on the cover of a Christie's sale catalogue, an ordinary marketing choice that, in retrospect, functioned as an extraordinary institutional endorsement of a painting that did not actually exist before Beltracchi made it. German investigators eventually documented sales connected to the scheme totaling in the tens of millions of dollars before it collapsed, and it collapsed for a reason that has nothing to do with connoisseurship or provenance research at all: forensic analysis of one painting found traces of a titanium-based white pigment that, chemically, had not existed at the time the work was supposedly created, a level of scientific verification no eighteenth-century auctioneer, and remarkably few even quite recent ones, had ever thought to apply. Beltracchi was sentenced to six years in a German prison in 2011. The episode is a useful reminder that an auction's price discovery only works as well as the information entering the room in the first place, and that the most sophisticated shill-bidding detection system in the world cannot catch a fraud built not on fake bidders, but on a fake object convincing enough to fool the very experts responsible for vetting it.

Penny auctions and bidding-fee auction sites, the subject of a later chapter in their own right, push a version of this same manipulative logic to an extreme the traditional art and collectibles trade never really approached, because their entire business model depends on extracting revenue from losing bidders rather than merely from the eventual winner — a structural inversion that makes certain forms of shill-like behavior almost redundant, since the format itself is designed to guarantee the house a profit on nearly every auction regardless of who actually wins it. That chapter will examine a specific, well-studied site in detail, but it is worth flagging here, in the context of this chapter's broader taxonomy of manipulation, that bidding-fee auctions represent something genuinely new in the format's long history: not a corruption of an otherwise honest mechanism, in the way shill bidding corrupts an eBay listing, but a mechanism deliberately redesigned, from first principles, around the specific psychological vulnerabilities this book's previous chapter catalogued — auction fever, quasi-endowment, the sunk-cost instinct — as its primary and in some analyses its *only* reliable source of revenue.

None of this should obscure a genuinely important counterpoint, one this chapter would be incomplete without stating plainly: the overwhelming majority of auctions run by the overwhelming majority of auction houses, on every platform this book has discussed, close honestly, with no shill bidder, no fabricated provenance, and no manipulated reserve anywhere in the transaction. Fraud detection research exists, and finds real fraud, precisely because fraud is detectable at all against a large, stable background of predominantly honest, unremarkable bidding behavior; a marketplace in which every single bidder secretly suspected every other bidder of being fake, every catalogue entry of being fabricated, and every phantom bid of quietly hiding above rather than safely below the disclosed reserve, would collapse from a simple failure of trust long before any regulator ever had occasion to intervene, and eBay, Christie's, Sotheby's, Aalsmeer's flower cooperative, and their many smaller competitors across every format this book has examined have each survived for years or centuries specifically because enough bidders, enough of the time, have judged the mechanism trustworthy enough to keep using despite knowing, in the abstract, that it can be gamed. That background trust is itself a fragile and valuable asset, cultivated deliberately by institutions with every commercial incentive to protect it, and the next chapter turns to a sale that tested exactly how far that trust — in an attribution nobody could fully verify, in a price nobody could sensibly benchmark against any comparable prior sale, and in two anonymous telephone bidders whose true identities and motives the public still does not fully know, and relayed to the room by Christie's own specialists, following a script of whispered updates and telephone confirmations, rather than spoken aloud by the bidders themselves — could actually stretch before it broke, and what, if anything, that November evening's astonishing final number really tells anyone, then or now, about what a five-hundred-year-old, heavily restored, disputedly attributed panel painting is actually worth.
