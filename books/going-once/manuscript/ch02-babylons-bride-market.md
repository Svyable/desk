# Babylon's Bride Market

The oldest written description of anything resembling an auction comes from a Greek historian who almost certainly never saw one. Herodotus, writing his *Histories* around 440 BC, roughly a century after the practice he is describing would have ended, reports that in every Babylonian village, once a year, all the women of marriageable age were gathered in one place while men crowded around. An official, Herodotus says, would call the women forward one at a time, beginning with the one judged most beautiful. He would put her up and ask who wished to buy her as a wife, and the bidding would rise until a price was reached and a suitor secured. As the most desirable women were sold off, the price for each successive woman fell, and when the crowd reached the least conventionally attractive, the mechanism reportedly reversed: rather than commanding the highest price, she would be offered together with a dowry, awarded to whichever man would accept her for the smallest payment. The money raised from the wealthier auctions, in Herodotus's account, subsidized the payments made to men willing to marry the women who received no bids at all. He calls the whole system the best of Babylonian customs, and adds, a little sourly, that it had by his own time fallen into disuse and been replaced by prostitution born of poverty after a conquest devastated the country.

Historians have argued about this passage for a very long time, and the argument is worth pausing on before treating the story as simple historical fact, because the caution generalizes to a good deal of ancient economic history. Herodotus is a genuinely valuable source and also, by his own era's standards and certainly by modern ones, an unreliable one: he wrote decades to centuries after many of the events he describes, relied on secondhand informants and local guides whose motives and accuracy he could not independently verify, and had a documented taste for the vivid, morally pointed anecdote over the dry administrative record. No Babylonian cuneiform tablet has surfaced that describes this specific annual ritual in the terms Herodotus lays out, and contemporary Assyriologists treat the account with real skepticism about its literal accuracy, while allowing that some kind of formalized, publicly witnessed marriage-price negotiation plausibly existed in Mesopotamian society, since Babylonian and Assyrian marriage contracts recording bride-prices do survive in the archaeological record. The most honest way to use the passage, then, is not as proof that this exact ritual happened this exact way, but as the earliest surviving written testimony, however imperfect, that the idea of publicly bidding for something scarce, ranked by desirability, with the proceeds cross-subsidizing the harder-to-place items, was already recognizable enough to a fifth-century-BC audience that Herodotus expected them to understand it without much explanation. Whatever else it was, it was not a strange or foreign concept requiring translation. Something like it was already legible.

That legibility matters more than the specific ritual, because it points to something almost structurally inevitable about auctions once a society reaches a certain level of complexity: wherever you have goods whose value differs from buyer to buyer, and no reliable way to know in advance who values a given item most, ranking by revealed willingness to pay is one of a very small number of procedures that actually solves the problem. You could assign the item by lottery, which is fair but wastes information — the person who wants it most and the person who wants it least have an equal chance. You could assign it by queue, first-come-first-served, which rewards whoever can show up earliest rather than whoever values the item most. You could have an official simply decide, based on status or favor, who deserves it — the method that dominated the allocation of scarce goods across most of human history and still dominates a great deal of it today. Or you could ask everyone interested to disclose, through actual behavior rather than mere claims, how much they are willing to give up to get it, and give it to whoever discloses the most. That fourth option is the auction, in its most stripped-down form, and it appears independently across enough unconnected ancient and early-modern societies — Babylonian marriage markets if Herodotus is even roughly right, Chinese and Roman commercial sales, medieval European "candle auctions" timed to a burning wick, colonial-era slave auctions whose brutal history this book will not minimize by treating it as a mere mechanism footnote — that it looks less like an invention anyone can claim credit for and more like something societies kept independently rediscovering, because the underlying coordination problem it solves shows up everywhere trade does.

Rome supplies the clearest ancient evidence of the auction as routine, documented state business rather than folk custom, and it also supplies the word. When Roman legions defeated an enemy and seized property, war booty, and captives, the spoils were often disposed of at *auctio*, a formal public sale conducted, in a striking piece of ceremonial theater, under a spear driven upright into the ground — *sub hasta*, "under the spear." The spear was a literal military standard marking the sale as legitimate state business rather than private looting, a visible signal to onlookers that whatever was being sold had passed through proper legal channels of conquest and confiscation, and the phrase *sub hasta* survived in Roman legal and commercial vocabulary long after any actual spear was involved, becoming shorthand for any forced or judicially ordered public sale, including sales of property seized from debtors. Cicero's speeches and Roman legal texts refer to sales *sub hasta* as an established institution, run by state officials called *magistri auctionum*, with rules governing bidding, deposits, and settlement that would be recognizable, in their basic shape, to anyone who has watched a modern estate sale or a sheriff's foreclosure auction. The Latin word for the process itself, *auctio*, derives from *augere*, meaning "to increase" — the same root that gives English "augment" — because the defining feature of the procedure, to a Roman ear, was not the sale but the rising. An *auctio* was, by definition, a price that grew as it went, bid by bid, in front of witnesses, until it stopped growing. That etymology is the single cleanest thread connecting the ancient practice to the modern one: for roughly two thousand years, in language after language, the word for this transaction has meant, at its root, "the increasing," because the increase — the visible, public act of the price climbing under competitive pressure — is what makes an auction an auction rather than merely a sale.

It is worth sitting with how much of the modern auction's structure Rome had already worked out, because the temptation, looking at a nineteenth-century American auction barn or a Christie's saleroom, is to assume the format is a comparatively recent, sophisticated invention layered onto older, cruder practices of simple barter. The evidence points the other way. Roman *sub hasta* sales had a designated official running the proceedings, functionally an auctioneer, empowered to accept and reject bids and to declare a sale final. They had public notice requirements, so that interested buyers would know in advance what was being sold and when. They had, in at least some documented cases, minimum reserve prices below which the magistrate conducting the sale would not let an item go, protecting the state or the creditor from a catastrophically low bid in a thinly attended sale — a concept that reappears, essentially unchanged in function, in every reserve price set by a modern auction house today. They even had a documented problem with collusive bidding, since Roman legal sources discuss penalties for bidders who conspired to suppress the price of goods sold *sub hasta*, the ancient-world cousin of a problem modern antitrust regulators are still actively litigating, as a later chapter of this book will show in uncomfortably close detail. None of this means Roman auction law was identical to a modern auction house's terms and conditions. It means the core mechanical problems an auction has to solve — how to run an orderly ascending sale, how to protect a seller from an unfairly low price, how to deter bidders from quietly agreeing not to compete with each other — were already recognized as problems worth writing rules about, roughly two thousand years before anyone thought to give the discipline that studies them a name like "market design."

Rome's most extraordinary auction by far, and certainly the strangest and most consequential one this book will examine anywhere in its ancient chapters, was not a routine sale of confiscated goods under the spear at all. It was the sale of the empire itself. On March 28, 193 AD, the Praetorian Guard — the emperor's own household troops, and by that point in Roman history the single most dangerous power broker in the state, having already murdered one emperor, Pertinax, earlier that same year — announced from the walls of their camp that the imperial throne was for sale to whoever would pay them the most. Two candidates came forward: Titus Flavius Sulpicianus, the murdered emperor's father-in-law, who opened by offering the guards a substantial cash bounty per soldier, and a wealthy senator named Didius Julianus, who arrived and began topping Sulpicianus's number, reportedly bidding from outside the camp walls while the guards relayed each new offer between the two rivals inside. The bidding climbed for some time, according to the contemporary and near-contemporary Roman historians who recorded it, before the guards settled on Julianus, whose final offer worked out to several thousand drachmas for every soldier in the Guard. He was proclaimed emperor that same day. The Roman public's reaction, by every surviving account, was disgust: a position that supposedly rested on lineage, merit, and the Senate's consent had just been sold, transparently and in public, to whichever rich man offered the most cash to his own bodyguards. Julianus did not enjoy his purchase for long. Septimius Severus, a general commanding legions on the Danube frontier, marched on Rome within weeks, and the same Praetorian Guard that had auctioned Julianus the throne abandoned him almost as quickly as they had sold it to him; he was killed in the palace after a reign of roughly two months, one of the shortest and most transparently mercenary in the empire's history. The episode is usually taught, when it is taught at all, as a symbol of imperial Rome's late-second-century political rot — a system so hollowed out that its most sacred office had become, in effect, a commodity. It is worth reading, for this book's purposes, as something narrower and stranger: proof that the ascending-bid auction, the exact mechanical procedure Sotheby's would later use to sell rare books and Christie's would later use to sell a disputed Leonardo, was already familiar enough as a concept in the second century AD that a group of soldiers with no economic training could improvise one, on the spot, to solve their own problem of deciding between two rival buyers for the one asset they controlled absolutely: their loyalty.

The word's path into English tracks the practice's spread through the parts of Europe most shaped by Roman law and, later, by English commercial custom. "Auction" entered English in the sixteenth century, borrowed via Latin *auctio* with the same "increasing" sense intact, initially describing sales by increasing bid rather than the fixed haggling of an ordinary market stall. Early modern English auctions, particularly in the book trade — the same trade that would eventually spawn Sotheby's, the subject of the next chapter — sometimes ran on a genuinely peculiar variant called "auction by candle" or "auction by inch of candle," in which bidding stayed open only as long as a burning candle stub remained lit; the moment the flame guttered out, whoever held the standing bid won, with no advance warning of exactly when that instant would arrive. The mechanism sounds whimsical from a distance and was, in practice, a remarkably clever solution to a problem every auction designer eventually confronts: how do you end an ascending auction fairly, without letting the very last bidder game the closing instant by waiting to bid until the literal final second? The London diarist Samuel Pepys recorded several candle auctions he personally witnessed in the 1660s, describing, among other sales, ships and naval goods auctioned "by an inch of candle," and his diary preserves a detail that undercuts the format's supposed unpredictability in a way worth noting for this book's larger argument: a highly successful bidder Pepys observed had learned that a candle's wick reliably flares up slightly in the instant just before it finally goes out, and had trained himself to watch for that flare and shout his final, winning bid at exactly that moment, converting what was meant to be an unmanipulable, purely random closing mechanism into something a sufficiently observant, experienced bidder could time with real precision. Most bidders in the room had no such trained advantage and experienced the candle's exact extinguishing moment as genuinely unpredictable, which made the format, for everyone besides Pepys's unusually observant acquaintance, an early and unusually elegant defense against a specific kind of last-second sniping — a problem this book will meet again, in a wildly more technological form, when it reaches eBay auctions in the 1990s and the almost identical debate over how, and whether, to let bidders snipe. That one attentive seventeenth-century bidder could still find and exploit a hidden edge inside a mechanism specifically designed to eliminate exactly that kind of edge is itself a small, early preview of a much larger pattern this book will trace across every subsequent era of auction technology: no design, however cleverly engineered against a specific known exploit, has yet proven permanently immune to a sufficiently patient, sufficiently observant participant finding some new angle its designers failed to anticipate.

The persistence of that same handful of design problems across two and a half thousand years — how to end a sale fairly, how to protect against a catastrophically low price, how to deter collusion among bidders, how to signal that a sale is legitimate — is the real argument for starting a book about twenty-first-century auction technology in ancient Babylon and Rome rather than in a Silicon Valley conference room. The specific technologies keep changing almost beyond recognition: a spear in the dirt, a burning candle, a synchronized clock with thirty-five dials in a Dutch flower warehouse, a server executing a real-time bid request in under a tenth of a second. The underlying coordination problem the technology exists to solve — how do strangers, who each hold private information about how much they value something and who do not trust each other enough to simply announce it, arrive at a price everyone will accept as legitimate — has not changed nearly as much as the technology built to solve it. Rome could not run a real-time bidding exchange. It could, and repeatedly did, wrestle with the exact questions a real-time bidding exchange's designers had to answer eighteen centuries later: who gets to bid, what stops them from lying about how much they want the item, what stops them from quietly agreeing with each other not to compete, and what makes the final price something the loser, as well as the winner, can accept as fair.

There is a final reason the ancient record is worth taking seriously rather than treating as pure color before the "real" history starts with Sotheby's in 1744. It sets, very early, the terms of a tension this entire book keeps returning to: an auction is simultaneously one of the more transparent pricing mechanisms humans have invented — the price is set in public, by observable competing behavior, rather than behind closed doors — and one of the more easily weaponized ones, because publicity and competitive pressure are themselves powerful psychological tools that a skilled operator of the mechanism can manipulate. Rome's own jurists worried about bidders colluding to suppress a price *sub hasta*. A society that, per Herodotus's account, organized an entire annual marriage market around competitive bidding was also, on his telling, using the proceeds from the desirable auctions to subsidize marriages nobody would otherwise bid for at all — a form of the same cross-subsidy logic that appears, dressed in modern clothes, in everything from tiered advertising-auction pricing to the reserve-price guarantees modern auction houses extend to nervous consignors. None of that is coincidence, and none of it is because modern market designers went digging through Herodotus for inspiration. It is because the auction, wherever and whenever it appears, is doing the same small number of jobs, under the same small number of constraints, and human societies facing those constraints keep arriving at structurally similar answers, separated by millennia and oceans, because there are only so many workable answers to give.

The Dutch Republic got there first, in the specific commercial niche that would eventually make Samuel Baker's fortune. In 1599, a bookseller in Leiden ran what is generally credited as the first used-book auction in the Netherlands, selling the library of the recently deceased statesman Philips van Marnix van Sint Aldegonde and, in the process, producing what historians believe is the earliest surviving printed auction catalogue — a small pamphlet listing the library's contents in advance, so interested buyers could plan which lots to pursue before the sale itself began. The innovation caught on with startling speed for a pre-industrial economy: by the middle of the seventeenth century, Leiden alone was hosting twenty to twenty-five book auctions a year, and across the Dutch Republic as a whole, thousands of auction catalogues survive from that single century, evidence of a book-auction trade operating at a scale and regularity no other European market had yet matched. Amsterdam, Leiden, and The Hague became the trade's three principal hubs, connected by a printing and bookselling industry that was, by several economic-historical accounts, the most developed in Europe at the time — which made the Dutch Republic not only the source of the descending flower-clock auction this book returns to later, but very plausibly the specific commercial culture that handed England the printed-catalogue, scheduled-sale auction format Samuel Baker adopted, more or less wholesale, when he sold his own first library a century and a half later. The auction, in other words, did not arrive in London as an English invention needing only an English institution to formalize it. It arrived as an import, refined over generations in Dutch printing houses, that London's booksellers were shrewd enough to recognize and adopt.

By the time the story picks up again in eighteenth-century London, the auction had already survived two thousand years of continuous reinvention across at least three civilizations that, as far as the written record shows, never directly borrowed the idea from one another. What London added was not the concept. It was an institution — a permanent, branded, professionally staffed house built around running auctions as a business in their own right, rather than as an occasional tool a state or a temple used when it needed one. That institutional step, small as it sounds, turned out to matter enormously, because it is the difference between an auction happening whenever a Roman magistrate or a Babylonian village elder decided one was needed, and an auction happening on a predictable schedule, run by specialists who had every commercial incentive to make the mechanism work as well as it possibly could. The next chapter is about the two Englishmen who built that institution, and about how a rare-book sale that raised £826 in 1744 became the direct ancestor of a nineteen-minute bidding war for a five-hundred-year-old painting.
