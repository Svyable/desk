# A Hundred Milliseconds

Open almost any ordinary news website on an ordinary laptop and, in the brief time it takes the page to finish loading, an entire competitive auction has already opened, run its complete course, and quietly settled on a final price, somewhere you will never see it happen and never even think to look. The mechanism is called real-time bidding, and its basic sequence, though it happens far too fast for any human participant to consciously observe, is not conceptually different from anything this book has already described: a seller has something to sell, in this case a single advertising impression — the right to display one ad, to one specific visitor, in one specific space on one specific page, for the few seconds that visitor's browser renders it — and a number of potential buyers compete to win it, each submitting a bid based on how much that particular impression, to that particular visitor, is worth to them. What makes real-time bidding different from every auction format examined so far is not its underlying logic but its almost unimaginable compression of time and its near-total elimination of the human participant from either side of the transaction. Industry technical explainers of the process describe the entire sequence — the publisher's ad server detecting an available impression, broadcasting a bid request describing that impression and whatever can be inferred about the visitor viewing it, dozens or hundreds of potential advertisers' automated systems evaluating that request and submitting silent bids, an exchange determining and notifying the winner, and the winning ad actually rendering on the visitor's screen — as typically completing in under one hundred milliseconds, a window shorter than the time it takes a human eye to complete a single blink, embedded invisibly inside the ordinary, unremarkable act of a web page finishing its load.

The elaborate infrastructure making this possible grew directly out of the same underlying commercial pressure that had already reshaped search advertising a few years earlier: publishers with advertising inventory to sell, advertisers wanting to reach specific audiences rather than buy generic, undifferentiated exposure, and a recognition that an automated, continuously running auction could match the two far more efficiently than the older model it replaced, in which advertising space was sold through negotiated contracts, insertion orders, and account representatives working relationships over weeks or months for placements that a modern real-time auction now clears in under a tenth of a second. Display and video advertising's version of this system runs through a considerably more elaborate technical stack than the single-auctioneer search-advertising model this book's previous chapter described: a publisher's inventory typically flows through a supply-side platform, which packages available impressions and offers them to one or more ad exchanges; those exchanges broadcast the resulting bid requests out to numerous demand-side platforms, software systems that advertisers use to manage automated bidding across potentially dozens of different exchanges simultaneously; and each demand-side platform's own bidding algorithms evaluate every individual bid request against whatever targeting criteria and value models the advertiser has configured, deciding, independently and without any human involvement in the individual decision, whether and how much to bid for that one specific impression, to that one specific visitor, on that one specific page, all within the roughly hundred-millisecond window the entire chain has to complete before the page finishes loading and the moment to bid has passed forever.

How much of an advertiser's own money actually reaches the publisher whose inventory it was meant to buy, after passing through this entire multi-layered auction chain, turned out to be a genuinely difficult question even for the advertising industry's own trade bodies to answer with confidence. A landmark 2020 study commissioned by the UK's Incorporated Society of British Advertisers and conducted by the accounting firm PwC set out to trace, in granular detail, exactly where a pound of advertiser spending actually went as it moved through the real-time bidding supply chain described in this chapter, and the results startled even industry veterans who had spent careers inside the system: on average, only fifty-one percent of advertiser spending in the study's sample ultimately reached the publisher, with the remainder consumed by the various supply-side platforms, demand-side platforms, data providers, and verification vendors each taking a cut somewhere along the chain. More troubling still to the study's own authors was a residual gap of roughly fifteen percent of total spending that could not be traced to any identifiable fee, service, or intermediary at all in the available transaction data, a category PwC itself labeled the "unknown delta" and attributed to some combination of data-set limitations, invisible fees buried in opaque contractual arrangements, post-auction bid shading, and undisclosed inventory reselling between vendors passing the same impression through multiple hands before it ever reached a genuine bidder. The finding gave rise to an entire subsequent industry practice, supply-path optimization, in which sophisticated advertisers and their agencies work to identify and eliminate redundant intermediary hops between their own budget and a publisher's actual inventory — a direct, practical echo of this book's argument that an auction's design details determine who captures how much of the value it generates, here applied not to the winning bid itself but to the largely invisible plumbing connecting a buyer's money to a seller's inventory in the first place.

The companies operating the demand-side platforms this architecture depends on have grown, over roughly fifteen years, into some of the digital advertising industry's most consequential and least publicly recognized power centers, occupying a role closer to a modern trading desk than to anything a Sotheby's specialist would recognize as advertising sales. The Trade Desk, founded in 2009 and now one of the largest independent demand-side platforms operating outside the major walled-garden ecosystems Google and Meta each control internally, manages automated bidding across trillions of individual auction opportunities annually on behalf of major advertisers, its own software making the moment-to-moment decisions about which impressions to bid on, at what price, and against which inferred audience characteristics, at a volume and granularity no advertiser's own internal marketing team could plausibly review by hand. Criteo, a French company built initially around retargeting — showing a specific user an ad for a product they had previously viewed but not purchased — occupies a related niche, running its own automated bidding infrastructure specifically tuned to identify and re-engage individual shoppers across the same real-time bidding exchanges this chapter has described. Neither company is a household name in the way Google or Amazon are, precisely because their entire function is to operate invisibly, inside the hundred-millisecond window between a page load and a rendered ad, making bidding decisions no human being downstream of the advertiser's original budget and targeting instructions will ever individually review.

The scale this architecture now processes is difficult to state in a way that feels intuitively graspable, because the numbers involved are simply outside the range of ordinary human experience with markets. Industry market-research estimates for the real-time bidding advertising technology sector alone — the software and infrastructure layer, not the much larger pool of total digital ad spending it allocates — put annual market size for 2025 somewhere in a wide range depending on methodology, with various research firms' estimates spanning roughly $16 billion to more than $33 billion for that year, figures that already dwarf the total annual sales volume of Sotheby's and Christie's combined by a considerable margin, while describing only the plumbing rather than the full river of advertising dollars flowing through it. More than seventy percent of digital display advertising is now estimated to be delivered through real-time bidding mechanisms rather than negotiated directly, and programmatic buying generally — the broader category encompassing real-time bidding alongside related automated-purchasing formats — is projected, in industry forecasts for the coming years, to account for the overwhelming majority of all global digital display advertising spending. Each individual auction inside that torrent is trivially small, often worth a fraction of a cent to the winning advertiser; the aggregate, run continuously across billions of web page loads every single day, adds up to one of the largest, fastest-moving, and least visible markets that has ever existed, operating entirely without the theatrical apparatus — the room, the gavel, the applause — that every previous chapter of this book has treated as practically inseparable from the very concept of an auction.

The Interactive Advertising Bureau's Transparency and Consent Framework, the very standard the Belgian regulator would later rule unlawful, had by the time of that ruling become close to ubiquitous across the European open web, embedded in the consent-management pop-ups that greet nearly every visitor to a European news site, retailer, or blog before any advertising auction involving that visitor's data is permitted to run at all — infrastructure operating, by the time regulators intervened, across a scale of websites large enough that a single adverse ruling against the framework's core design threatened to disrupt the technical plumbing beneath a meaningful share of the entire European digital advertising economy in one stroke, which helps explain why IAB Europe fought the finding through years of appeal rather than simply redesigning the mechanism from scratch.

Header bidding, a specific technical refinement that spread rapidly through the publisher side of the industry beginning around the mid-2010s, illustrates how quickly this infrastructure continued to evolve even after real-time bidding itself had become standard practice, and it is worth understanding briefly because it demonstrates a recurring theme this book keeps returning to: an auction's design details, however obscure and technical they sound, translate directly into who captures how much of the resulting revenue. In an earlier, simpler version of programmatic advertising, a publisher's ad server would typically offer an impression to one exchange first, in a kind of waterfall sequence, moving on to a second exchange only if the first failed to produce an acceptably high bid — a structure that meant a publisher's very best potential buyer, sitting on a different exchange further down the waterfall, might never even get the chance to bid on a given impression at all, simply because a lower-value bidder on the first exchange in the sequence had already claimed it. Header bidding restructured this process so that a publisher's inventory is instead offered to multiple exchanges genuinely simultaneously, all competing directly against each other in real time for the same impression before the page's primary ad server makes its final decision, a change publishers adopted specifically because it consistently produced measurably higher revenue per impression — direct, empirical evidence, playing out across an industry processing billions of transactions a day, that a genuinely simultaneous, broadly competitive auction extracts more value for a seller than a sequential, artificially restricted one, precisely the lesson this book's earlier chapters on Dutch flower clocks and FCC spectrum design already established in far slower, far more visible settings.

Real-time bidding's speed and near-total automation made it an unusually attractive target for a specific, industrialized form of fraud that the cybersecurity firm White Ops uncovered and publicly documented in December 2016, in an operation researchers dubbed Methbot after references buried in the underlying computer code. The scheme built a botnet controlling more than half a million distinct IP addresses, six thousand domains, and a quarter million individual URLs, each engineered to look, to an automated ad exchange evaluating a bid request, like a legitimate publisher's video-advertising inventory; rather than relying on ordinary malware infecting unsuspecting home computers, the operation ran on hundreds of dedicated servers in real data centers in the United States and Amsterdam, using a custom-built web browser specifically engineered to fool the fraud-detection systems this book's previous chapter described, and to simulate as many as three hundred million fake video-ad views a day. Investigators estimated the operation was extracting somewhere between $3 million and $5 million daily from advertisers paying for video-ad impressions that no human being had ever actually watched, making it, at the time of its discovery, the largest and most profitable digital-advertising fraud scheme cybersecurity researchers had documented, run not by amateurs but by a sophisticated criminal enterprise sufficiently well-resourced to build real physical server infrastructure purely to fool an automated auction that no human being was watching closely enough, in real time, to catch happening.

None of this infrastructure was originally built with privacy regulation in mind, and the tension between real-time bidding's fundamental mechanics and an ordinary person's reasonable expectation of privacy has become one of the format's most persistent and least resolved controversies. A bid request, to let potential advertisers evaluate how much a given impression is worth to them, necessarily carries some description of the visitor viewing it — at minimum the web page's content and category, and frequently far more granular signals inferred from tracking cookies, device identifiers, location data, and browsing history compiled by data brokers and advertising networks over months or years of accumulated online activity. That bid request is broadcast, in the ordinary and unremarkable course of running the auction, to dozens or hundreds of demand-side platforms simultaneously, the overwhelming majority of which will lose the auction and do nothing further with the impression, but which have, in the process of even evaluating whether to bid, already received a detailed behavioral profile of the specific person who happened to load that particular page. European and other regulators examining real-time bidding's data-protection implications have specifically scrutinized this broadcast-to-everyone structure as a potential source of large-scale, largely invisible personal data exposure, since a request sent to hundreds of potential bidders is, definitionally, shared with far more parties than the single winning advertiser who ultimately displays an ad, and the losing bidders' subsequent use, retention, and potential resale of whatever profile data arrived in that bid request has proven considerably harder for regulators and privacy researchers to audit than the comparatively simple, single-buyer transaction a Sotheby's or Christie's sale represents.

That scrutiny produced one of the more consequential European regulatory rulings the real-time bidding industry has faced. On February 2, 2022, the Belgian Data Protection Authority ruled that the Transparency and Consent Framework — a widely adopted industry standard, built by the trade group IAB Europe, for recording and communicating a website visitor's advertising-consent choices across the entire real-time bidding chain — itself violated the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation. The regulator's central finding was structural rather than incidental: it held that the consent signal itself, a short string of data meant to travel alongside each bid request and tell every downstream bidder what the visitor had and had not agreed to, qualified as personal data in its own right, and that IAB Europe and the many publishers, consent-management platforms, and advertising-technology vendors implementing the framework were jointly responsible, as co-controllers, for how that signal and the underlying bid-request broadcast were actually handled across an industry-wide system none of them individually controlled end to end. Acting as lead supervisory authority for the entire European Union, the Belgian regulator's decision carried consequences well beyond its own borders, and IAB Europe's subsequent appeal made only partial headway: Belgium's Market Court upheld a €250,000 fine against the trade group in May 2025, more than three years after the original ruling, confirming that the industry's own flagship consent mechanism — built specifically to make real-time bidding's data flows compliant with European privacy law — had itself become one of the auction format's most durable legal liabilities, still working its way through Europe's courts a full quarter-century after Vickrey's second-price logic first found its way into a search-advertising auction.

Google's own long, unresolved struggle to reform the third-party cookie — the small tracking file that has, for two decades, supplied much of the behavioral data feeding real-time bidding's targeting decisions — illustrates how deeply the auction's underlying economics have become entangled with the privacy debate surrounding it, to the point where the company running the world's largest advertising auction has found it genuinely difficult to reform its own data-collection practices without disrupting the auction's core value proposition to advertisers. Google announced plans, beginning in 2020, to phase out third-party cookies in its Chrome browser, the dominant browser by global market share, and to replace the targeting data they supplied with a set of privacy-preserving alternatives developed under the umbrella name Privacy Sandbox. The company repeatedly delayed its own announced deprecation timeline across the following several years, citing pushback from advertisers, publishers, and regulators worried about the transition's effect on competition in the ad-tech market, before ultimately reversing course in 2024 and announcing it would not, after all, force a full deprecation of third-party cookies, offering browser users an alternative choice mechanism instead. The reversal drew criticism from privacy advocates who had spent years pushing for exactly the change Google had originally promised, and it drew a different, more particular kind of criticism from competition regulators, including the UK's Competition and Markets Authority, which had spent much of the same period scrutinizing whether Google's original cookie-deprecation plan would itself entrench Google's own dominant position in the ad-tech stack by disadvantaging rival bidders more than it disadvantaged Google's own first-party data advantage — a genuinely difficult regulatory problem in which the same underlying policy change could be criticized, by different well-informed parties, as both too disruptive to the existing auction ecosystem and not disruptive enough to Google's own market power within it.

What makes real-time bidding the natural hinge point of this book's larger argument, more than its scale or its privacy implications, is what it represents as a milestone in the auction's long migration away from human participation on either side of the transaction. Every earlier chapter's auctions, however automated their pricing mechanisms became, still ultimately had a human decision-maker setting the parameters of any individual bid in something close to real time: a Christie's telephone bidder authorizing a specific number in the moment, an FCC spectrum bidder's corporate team huddling between rounds to decide the next increment, even a search-advertising manager, in the format's earliest years, manually adjusting a keyword bid based on yesterday's performance report. Real-time bidding's demand-side platforms removed that last remaining layer of moment-to-moment human judgment years before "artificial intelligence" reentered the mainstream technology conversation as a phrase describing anything beyond narrow, specialized statistical models: an advertiser sets a broad strategic goal — a target cost per acquisition, a maximum daily budget, an audience definition — and then a piece of software makes millions of individual, irreversible bidding decisions a day entirely on its own, at a speed and volume no human supervisor could meaningfully review even after the fact, let alone in the fraction of a second before each individual auction actually clears. The bidder, in the largest, fastest-growing, and now single most voluminous auction format human civilization has ever operated, had already stopped being a person in any meaningful sense years before this book's final chapters take up the question of what happens once the *buyer's* underlying intent, not merely the mechanical act of bidding, but the underlying decision of what to buy in the first place, also starts to be delegated to a machine that never sleeps, never blinks, never gets distracted, and never once feels the pull of the crowded, gasping room this book opened inside.
