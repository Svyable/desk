# The Machine That Sells Your Attention

Bill Gross, the entrepreneur and founder of the internet incubator Idealab, is generally credited by historians of the industry with introducing the specific idea that would eventually fund most of the modern commercial internet, and he unveiled it in a form so simple it barely looked like an innovation at the time: instead of charging advertisers a flat fee to appear in search results, or a fee based on how many times an ad was merely displayed, his company GoTo.com — which later became Overture and was eventually absorbed into Yahoo — let advertisers bid, in an open, continuously running auction, for placement next to specific search keywords, paying only when a user actually clicked their listing rather than merely seeing it. Jeffrey Brewer, a GoTo executive, presented the pay-per-click concept publicly at a TED conference in February 1998, and the underlying auction GoTo built to allocate those clicks was, in its original form, a straightforward first-price sealed-bid mechanism of the kind this book's fourth chapter already examined in some detail: the advertiser bidding the most for a given keyword won the top position and paid exactly the amount it had bid, every time a user clicked. The format worked, in the sense that it generated real advertiser revenue and real search traffic, but it inherited every strategic headache a first-price format imposes on its bidders, amplified by the sheer, relentless frequency with which search advertising auctions actually run. An advertiser bidding on a competitive keyword could not simply set a value and walk away, the way a Sotheby's bidder eventually could once a gavel fell; the auction for that same keyword would run again for the next searcher, and the one after that, and the one after that, effectively continuously, all day, every day, which meant GoTo's advertisers found themselves locked in a genuinely exhausting, largely unproductive war of constant micro-adjustment, nudging their bids up by a single cent at a time to reclaim a position a rival had just nudged them out of, in a cycle contemporary accounts of the format describe as inherently unstable and prone to precisely this kind of wasteful, continuous one-cent escalation.

Google entered the paid-search business several years behind GoTo, launching its own advertising product, AdWords, in October 2000 on a simple pay-per-thousand-impressions basis that bore little resemblance to what the product would become. The pivotal redesign arrived in February 2002, with a relaunch called AdWords Select, and the single most consequential decision Google's product team made in that relaunch was one this book's readers are, by this point, well equipped to recognize: rather than copying GoTo's first-price format, Google built its auction around a generalized version of the second-price principle William Vickrey had proven, four decades earlier, induces bidders to reveal their true valuations rather than engage in wasteful strategic guessing. In Google's mechanism, formally described in later economic literature as a generalized second-price auction, an advertiser winning a given ad position does not pay its own bid; it pays, roughly, the minimum amount needed to beat the bid of the advertiser occupying the position just below it, plus a small increment — meaning the advertiser in the top slot pays a price set by the second-highest bidder, the advertiser in the second slot pays a price set by the third-highest bidder, and so on down the page, a direct multi-slot extension of exactly the logic Vickrey had worked out for a single item in 1961. Because the price an advertiser actually pays depends on a rival's bid rather than its own, the format removes much of the incentive for continuous, wasteful one-cent nudging that had plagued GoTo's first-price system, and within roughly five years of AdWords Select's 2002 launch, according to industry accounts of the period, the generalized second-price format had become the de facto standard across the entire search-advertising industry, adopted by Google's rivals as thoroughly as it had been pioneered at Google itself.

The mechanism's real sophistication, though, lay in a second design choice layered on top of the pricing rule, one that had nothing to do with Vickrey's original theorem and everything to do with a problem specific to advertising that a Sotheby's painting never has to contend with: not every advertiser's dollar bid should count equally toward winning a position, because not every ad is equally likely to be useful, relevant, or ultimately clicked by the searcher looking at it. Google's ranking formula multiplied an advertiser's bid by a separate, continuously updated "quality score," derived from the ad's historical click-through rate, its relevance to the specific search query, and the quality of the landing page it directed users toward, meaning an advertiser offering a lower raw dollar bid but a much more relevant, higher-quality ad could, and frequently did, outrank a rival bidding more money for a worse, less relevant advertisement. This was, from Google's own commercial perspective, a genuinely elegant piece of self-interested design as much as a piece of consumer protection: an auction that rewarded raw bid size alone would eventually fill search results pages with irrelevant, high-budget spam from advertisers wealthy enough to outbid everyone regardless of relevance, degrading the search experience users relied on and, over time, driving those users toward a less cluttered rival search engine; an auction that rewarded relevance alongside price kept the ads genuinely useful enough that users kept clicking them, which is precisely the outcome that maximizes Google's own long-run advertising revenue. The economists Benjamin Edelman, Michael Ostrovsky, and Michael Schwarz, along with Hal Varian — who would go on to become Google's own chief economist — published influential formal analyses of the generalized second-price mechanism in the mid-2000s, working out its equilibrium properties in detail and noting an important theoretical wrinkle: unlike Vickrey's original single-item second-price auction, the multi-slot generalized version does not, in general, make truthful bidding a dominant strategy for every advertiser in every situation, meaning some of the theoretical cleanliness Vickrey's original insight promised gets partially lost once you generalize it across multiple ranked positions rather than a single item — a genuinely important qualification that did nothing, in practice, to slow the format's rapid conquest of the entire digital advertising industry.

Yahoo's own experience supplies a useful counterfactual for how much this specific design choice mattered, because Yahoo ended up owning the format Google had just outcompeted, and ended up, for years, unable to close the resulting gap. Yahoo acquired Overture — the renamed successor to Bill Gross's original GoTo.com — in 2003, inheriting the first-price, pay-per-click auction model that had once looked like the industry's obvious future, and spent much of the following decade trying to modernize that inherited technology against a Google AdWords platform that, by the time of the acquisition, had already been running its more sophisticated generalized second-price, quality-weighted mechanism for roughly a year. Yahoo did not lack for search traffic or advertiser interest in the mid-2000s; it lacked, by most industry accounts of the period, the specific combination of pricing-mechanism sophistication and quality-relevance weighting Google had built into AdWords Select from the start, a gap that search-advertising analysts at the time and in retrospective accounts since have identified as one of the more consequential, if underappreciated, causes of Yahoo's long competitive decline against Google through the following decade — a reminder that the specific rules governing an auction are not a minor technical footnote to the business built on top of it, but can themselves be the entire competitive advantage.

The quality-score mechanism this chapter has already described also created its own quiet secondary industry, one worth flagging briefly because it recurs in a different guise throughout this book's later chapters on manipulation and fraud. Because an advertiser's effective rank depended on relevance and historical click-through performance as much as on raw bid size, an entire consulting and software industry grew up around helping advertisers artificially inflate exactly those signals — building landing pages engineered to maximize measured relevance scores rather than genuine user value, structuring ad copy and keyword matching with the auction's own scoring formula as the primary audience rather than the actual human searcher, and in some documented cases generating artificial click activity specifically to improve an ad's historical performance metrics ahead of a real campaign launch. Google's own enforcement teams have spent two decades refining detection systems and policy enforcement against exactly this kind of gaming, banning accounts and adjusting scoring algorithms in a continuous, largely invisible arms race that mirrors, in miniature, the shill-bidding detection efforts this book's eighth chapter described in the context of eBay and fine-art auctions — further evidence, gathered across a genuinely wide range of unrelated industries by this point in the book, that wherever a mechanism rewards a measured signal rather than a directly and reliably observed outcome, someone, somewhere, motivated by exactly the same ordinary profit incentive driving the honest majority of participants, will eventually work out how to manufacture that signal more cheaply than the underlying outcome it was originally designed to represent.

The scale this single design choice eventually enabled is worth stating in plain figures, because the search-advertising auction, individually unremarkable and largely invisible to the billions of people who trigger one every time they type a query into a search box, aggregates into one of the largest revenue engines in the history of commerce. Google's parent company Alphabet derives the overwhelming majority of its total revenue from advertising: the company's own reported 2025 results put total advertising revenue at roughly $294.7 billion for the year, of which search and related advertising alone accounted for approximately $224.5 billion, against total consolidated Alphabet revenue of about $402.8 billion — meaning advertising, the large majority of it flowing through auctions descended directly from the AdWords Select mechanism this chapter has just described, made up roughly seventy-three percent of the entire company's revenue in a single recent year. That revenue is generated overwhelmingly through auctions run at a speed and frequency no human auctioneer, however fast a chanting tobacco-sale veteran, could ever hope to match: every single search query that returns a paid advertisement triggers its own fresh, self-contained auction, resolved among however many advertisers have bid on relevant keywords, completed and settled before the search results page has finished rendering on the user's screen. That volume transformed the underlying economics of the auction format in a way this book's earlier chapters, built around single, discrete, occasionally-repeated sales, could not have anticipated: where a Sotheby's evening sale might auction fifty or sixty individual lots across several hours, a single popular search keyword might be auctioned, independently, billions of times over the course of a year, each individual auction settling in a fraction of a second, each one generating a sliver of revenue too small to notice in isolation and collectively adding up to one of the largest revenue streams any company on Earth has ever built. The generalized second-price auction, in other words, did not merely borrow Vickrey's mathematics; it proved those mathematics could survive being run at a scale and speed the theorem's own author, writing in 1961 about oil leases and sealed paper envelopes, almost certainly never imagined for a single moment.

A pay-per-click auction, whatever else its virtues, created an entirely new category of fraud that neither a Sotheby's saleroom nor an FCC spectrum sale ever had to worry about, because it tied real money directly to a single, cheaply repeatable action: a click. Click fraud — the practice of generating fake or malicious clicks on a paid advertisement, whether to directly drain a rival's advertising budget, to fraudulently generate ad-network payouts on low-quality websites hosting the ads, or through automated botnets designed to simulate genuine user behavior at scale — became a persistent, well-documented cost of doing business across the entire pay-per-click industry almost from its inception, and Google, Yahoo, and their rivals have spent two and a half decades building increasingly sophisticated detection systems, using behavioral signals, device fingerprinting, and machine-learning classification to try to distinguish a genuine, commercially valuable click from a fraudulent one before an advertiser is ever billed for it. The arms race has never fully resolved in either side's favor: security researchers and ad-tech industry reports have periodically estimated that a meaningful share of all clicks on some categories of online advertising, particularly on lower-quality publisher networks with weaker fraud controls, are illegitimate, even as the platforms' own detection and refund systems have grown considerably more sophisticated since AdWords Select's earliest years. The problem is, in a structural sense, the exact same information-asymmetry problem this book's earlier chapters on shill bidding and provenance fraud described in a physical-auction context, translated into a purely digital register: an auction's integrity depends on the signals feeding into it — a bid, a click, a claimed provenance — being genuine, and any sufficiently motivated and sufficiently well-resourced bad actor has, across every single format this book has examined, eventually found some way to fake at least a portion of those signals.

That speed and scale created a new competitive landscape entirely of its own, distinct from anything the earlier chapters on art, spectrum, or matching markets described, because it meant an advertiser's success no longer depended merely on picking the right maximum bid, the way a Sotheby's paddle-holder or an FCC spectrum bidder had to. It depended on building and continuously refining an entire automated bidding operation — software systems, increasingly running with minimal human oversight, that monitored auction results in real time, adjusted bids algorithmically in response to observed win rates and conversion data, and optimized spending across potentially millions of distinct keyword auctions running simultaneously, all day, every day, at a pace no team of human media buyers could realistically track by hand. Google itself, along with its major advertising rivals, eventually built and offered exactly this kind of automated bidding management directly into its own advertiser tools, in effect handing advertisers software that would bid on their behalf, informed by the advertiser's stated goals — target cost per acquisition, target return on ad spend, a maximum daily budget — rather than requiring a human being to set an individual bid for every individual keyword by hand. This is worth flagging explicitly, because it represents the clearest and earliest large-scale instance, anywhere in this book's entire historical arc, of what its final chapters will treat as the central open question of the coming decade: not merely software helping a human bidder decide what to bid, the way a modern eBay sniping tool automates a single, occasional, human-authorized action, but software making millions of independent, continuous, real-time bidding decisions on a human's behalf, at a volume and speed no human could meaningfully supervise in real time even if they wanted to, adjusting strategy in response to results a human would need weeks to notice and process by hand. The bidder, in the most literal and commercially important auction format most people encounter every single day of their online lives, had already stopped being a person, in any meaningful moment-to-moment sense, years before anyone started using the phrase "AI agent" to describe the phenomenon.

Google's dominance over this entire auction infrastructure eventually drew exactly the kind of sustained antitrust scrutiny this book's later chapters will show falling on algorithmic pricing tools in a very different industry entirely. The United States Department of Justice and a coalition of state attorneys general sued Google in 2023, arguing that the company had illegally monopolized three distinct but interlocking layers of the digital advertising stack: the software publishers use to manage their ad inventory, the tools advertisers use to buy it, and the ad exchange sitting in between matching the two — a degree of control that, the government argued, let Google both run the auction and, in effect, sit as an interested participant on more than one side of the table simultaneously. Judge Leonie Brinkema of the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia agreed with the core of the government's case in an April 2025 ruling, finding that Google had broken the law to build and maintain its dominance specifically over the publisher ad server and ad exchange markets, and that it had unlawfully tied those separate products together in a way that disadvantaged rival ad-tech companies trying to compete for the same auctions on equal footing. The case then moved into a lengthy remedies phase, during which government lawyers pushed for a structural breakup that would have forced Google to divest parts of its advertising technology business entirely; a federal judge ultimately declined to order that sale, leaving Google's ad-tech empire largely intact while the parties continued litigating narrower behavioral remedies instead. Whatever its final shape, the ruling confirmed, in a federal court's own carefully considered judgment, something this chapter has been arguing from a different angle throughout: the specific rules governing who gets to run an auction, and who gets to compete inside it on fair terms, are themselves an enormously valuable and fiercely contested piece of market infrastructure, worth years of costly federal litigation, extensive expert testimony, and prolonged appellate argument for a company to control.

European regulators pursued a parallel but distinct legal theory against the same underlying conduct, applying the European Union's Digital Markets Act, a 2022 law imposing direct behavioral obligations on the largest online platforms the law designates as "gatekeepers." Rather than the American case's tying and monopolization theories, European enforcers focused on self-preferencing — the practice of a dominant platform ranking or promoting its own products ahead of competing offerings it also helps intermediate — arguing that Google had placed its own vertical search services, including Google Shopping and Google Flights, more prominently in search results than comparable third-party services the same auction-adjacent ranking system was supposed to treat fairly. The European Commission found Google in breach of the Digital Markets Act's self-preferencing rules in 2026, issuing a fine reported at roughly €890 million, part of a broader pattern of DMA enforcement against the company that, combined with an earlier related penalty, pushed the total EU fines assessed against Google under the new framework past $1 billion. The DMA's self-preferencing ban, unlike the American antitrust case's remedy phase, does not require years of litigated remedies negotiation to take effect; it imposes an ongoing, forward-looking obligation that gatekeeper platforms treat their own services no more favorably in ranking than third-party competitors, enforced through the kind of rolling fines and compliance orders more typical of financial or environmental regulation than of traditional American-style antitrust litigation — a genuinely different regulatory philosophy applied to the identical underlying question this chapter has spent its length examining: who gets to write, and who gets to police, the rules of an auction that now decides how a meaningful share of the world's commercial attention gets allocated.

The next chapter follows that same auction mechanism one layer deeper, past the search results page and into the far larger, far less visible market for the display and video advertising that follows internet users around the rest of the web — a market whose defining feature is not merely that its bidders are software, a condition search advertising had already normalized by the mid-2000s, but that the entire auction, from the instant a web page begins loading to the instant a winning ad actually renders on the screen, now typically completes in less time than it takes a human eye to finish a single blink.
