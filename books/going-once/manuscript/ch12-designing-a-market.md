# Designing a Market

Not every scarce resource this book has discussed can be sold to the highest bidder, and the reasons why turn out to be as economically interesting as the auctions themselves. Nobody seriously proposes selling kidneys to the wealthiest patient on a transplant waiting list, or assigning medical residency positions to whichever hospital offers a new doctor the largest signing bonus, or letting a public school system auction off its most sought-after seats to the highest-bidding parents. These are markets, in the sense that they involve scarce goods that many people want and only some can have, but they are markets where a price mechanism is either illegal, as with organs in every country but Iran, or considered so ethically corrosive that essentially no policymaker anywhere seriously entertains it, or simply impossible in the most literal sense, since a hospital cannot pay a desirable new doctor enough to overcome the fact that only one doctor can occupy one residency slot regardless of price. And yet all three of these settings share the exact underlying structural problem every auction in this book has been trying to solve: a scarce set of things, a larger set of people who want them, and a need to assign the things to the people in some way that both sides will accept as reasonably fair and that does not leave obviously mutually beneficial trades sitting on the table afterward. Economists call the branch of theory built specifically to handle this exact category of problem, allocation without any workable role for prices at all, matching theory, and the two economists most responsible for turning it from an elegant mathematical curiosity into working public infrastructure, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, published the paper that started it all in 1962, under a title whose sheer plainness thoroughly undersells the paper's eventual, decades-later influence on organ transplantation, public education, and military and medical personnel assignment alike: "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage."

Gale and Shapley's paper, remarkably brief by the standards of the dense, notation-heavy economics literature that would eventually grow up around it, posed a deceptively simple question that a careful reader could follow without any specialized mathematical training at all, which is a meaningful part of why the paper's central idea managed to travel so far beyond the narrow academic economics journals that first published it, eventually into hospitals, transplant wards, and public school boardrooms whose administrators had never read, and would never need to read, a single page of formal game theory in their entire working lives. Imagine a group of men and a group of women, each with a private, ranked list of preferences over everyone in the opposite group — who they would most like to marry, who they would tolerate, who they would refuse outright — and ask whether there exists some way of pairing everyone off that leaves no man and woman who are not married to each other but would both, given the chance, prefer to leave their current partners and marry each other instead. A matching with no such pair is called stable; a matching containing even one such pair is unstable, because that pair has both the desire and, presumably, the ability to defect from the assigned pairing and form their own, mutually preferred match, unraveling whatever assignment the matchmaker had proposed. Gale and Shapley proved something that was not obvious in advance: a stable matching always exists, for any set of preferences whatsoever, and they proved it by describing an explicit, mechanical procedure — now called the deferred acceptance algorithm — that reliably finds one. In its simplest form, each man proposes to his first-choice woman; each woman receiving one or more proposals tentatively holds onto her favorite among them while rejecting the rest; each rejected man proposes to his next-choice woman; each woman, upon receiving a new proposal, compares it against whatever proposal she is currently holding and keeps whichever she prefers, rejecting the other; and the process repeats, round after round, until nobody has an unanswered proposal left to make. The word "deferred" carries the algorithm's key insight: nobody's acceptance is ever final until the very last round, which is precisely what lets the procedure correct earlier mistakes and converge, provably, on a stable outcome rather than locking in an early match that a later round reveals to be suboptimal.

The paper's marriage-market framing was, from the start, a deliberately accessible illustration of a far more general mathematical structure, and Gale and Shapley themselves flagged the more practically urgent application directly in their title: college admissions, where students rank colleges and colleges rank applicants, face exactly the same stability problem a marriage market does, with an admitted student and a college that would both prefer each other over their assigned matches representing exactly the same kind of unstable, self-defeating outcome a jilted couple represents in the romantic version of the puzzle. The deeper insight, though, sat dormant in the economics literature for more than two decades before anyone connected it to a real institution actually suffering from exactly this problem. That connection was made by Alvin Roth, an economist who, in a 1984 paper, examined the algorithm quietly used since the early 1950s by the National Resident Matching Program — the American clearinghouse that assigns graduating medical students to hospital residency positions, a market that had, decades earlier and entirely independently of Gale and Shapley's academic work, stumbled through its own painful institutional history of unstable, self-undermining assignment processes before arriving, largely through trial and error, at a mechanism strikingly similar in its core logic to deferred acceptance. Roth's paper demonstrated that the NRMP's homegrown algorithm was, in essence, a close cousin of Gale and Shapley's mathematical construction, and that the specific reason it had succeeded where earlier, less carefully designed matching processes had repeatedly failed — failures serious enough that, in the years before the modern algorithm, substantial numbers of medical students and hospitals had simply abandoned the official clearinghouse and arranged placements through separate, chaotic private deals — was precisely its stability property. A stable match gives neither a hospital nor a student any incentive to defect from the official system and strike a private side deal, because no better mutual arrangement exists for them to defect *to*; an unstable one all but invites exactly that kind of unraveling, and the pre-algorithm history of American medical-residency assignment amounted to a long, expensive natural experiment in precisely how badly an unstable matching market can fail before its participants abandon the official process altogether.

Roth's demonstration that a real, high-stakes American labor market had independently rediscovered Gale and Shapley's mathematics opened the door to a research program that would occupy much of his subsequent career: identifying other markets suffering from the same structural affliction — scarcity, no workable role for prices, and a real risk of unraveling into chaotic, unstable private dealmaking — and redesigning them, deliberately and explicitly, around the deferred-acceptance principle. Kidney transplantation supplied the most viscerally consequential application. A patient in kidney failure who has a willing living donor cannot always receive that donor's kidney directly, because blood type or tissue compatibility may rule out a direct transplant between the two of them even though the donor is perfectly healthy and perfectly willing to give up a kidney for someone they love. Roth, working with the economists Tayfun Sönmez and Utku Ünver, realized in the early 2000s that this incompatibility problem could be solved the same way a matching market solves any other allocation problem: by finding chains and cycles of exchange among multiple incompatible donor-recipient pairs, so that, for example, one patient's incompatible donor gives a kidney to a stranger's compatible recipient, while that stranger's own incompatible donor gives a kidney to yet another patient, potentially extending across many linked pairs simultaneously, with every donor in the chain giving to somebody other than their own loved one while every recipient in the chain nonetheless receives a compatible organ. Building and clearing these exchanges at national scale requires exactly the kind of centralized, algorithmically computed matching Gale and Shapley's mathematics makes possible, and kidney-exchange programs built directly on this research, operating across dozens of American hospitals and eventually replicated in several other countries, have since facilitated thousands of transplants that would never have happened under a system limited to direct, pairwise donor-recipient matches — patients alive today, in a literal and unambiguous sense, because of an algorithm first described in a 1962 paper about the stability of marriage. The mechanism has since traveled well beyond the American hospitals where Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver first built it, adopted in some form by kidney-exchange programs in the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and South Korea among other countries, each adapting the underlying matching logic to its own national transplant regulations and hospital networks while preserving the same basic mathematical insight: a chain of reciprocal, in-kind exchanges among incompatible pairs can accomplish what no single pairwise donation ever could. South Korea's own transplant programs, notably, had experimented with informal kidney-paired exchanges as early as the 1990s, working out a rough practical version of the same underlying idea independently and without direct knowledge of the American academic literature that would later formalize it — a small but telling echo of this book's second chapter, where ancient Rome and Herodotus's Babylon each seem to have arrived, independently, at recognizably similar solutions to a shared underlying coordination problem, long before anyone had a name for market design at all.

School choice supplied a third, differently structured application, and one whose specific redesign history offers this book's clearest illustration yet of the gap between a mechanism that looks fair on paper and one that is actually strategy-proof in the sense this book's fifth chapter defined for auctions. Boston's public school system, before a 2005 reform, let families submit ranked lists of preferred schools and then ran an assignment process — commonly called the Boston mechanism in the academic literature that grew up studying it — that gave priority, at each school, first to students who had ranked that school as their *first* choice, before considering students who had ranked it second or lower, regardless of how strongly a second-choice student might have preferred that school to whatever they eventually got assigned. The mechanism sounds intuitively reasonable, even generous toward families who commit clearly to a top preference, but it created a serious, well-documented strategic trap: a family whose true first choice was a highly competitive, oversubscribed school, but who suspected their child had little realistic chance of admission there, faced real pressure to *not* rank that school first at all, because doing so and failing to get in could cost them priority at their realistic second-choice school as well, a genuine risk of ending up worse off for having told the truth about their preferences. Sophisticated families, aided by informal community knowledge, private consultants, or simply savvier friends, learned to game this dynamic by strategically ranking a realistic, slightly less desired school first to lock in priority there, while unsophisticated families, disproportionately lower-income and immigrant households without the same informal information networks, more often ranked their genuine first choice honestly and sometimes paid a real price in worse outcomes for having done so. Economists studying Boston's actual application data, including Atila Abdulkadiroglu, Parag Pathak, Alvin Roth, and Tayfun Sönmez in research that directly informed the city's eventual 2005 reform, documented this exact pattern of unequal strategic sophistication in the real record of who ranked what and who got assigned where, building an empirical case that the mechanism was not merely theoretically imperfect but was actively, measurably redistributing good school placements away from families who told the truth and toward families sophisticated enough to game a system that punished honesty.

Boston's School Committee voted in 2005 to replace its old mechanism with a deferred-acceptance-based system explicitly designed, this time, to be strategy-proof: under the new design, ranking your true first choice first could never hurt your chances at your true second choice, removing the specific strategic trap that had rewarded sophistication over honest preference reporting. New York City implemented a related redesign around the same period for its own, even larger and more complicated high school choice system, addressing a somewhat different but related failure: under the city's earlier process, so many students were failing to receive a match at any of their listed choices at all — Abdulkadiroglu, Pathak, and Roth's research on the pre-reform system documented that a substantial share of applicants, in some analyses reaching close to half of those who applied outside their immediate neighborhood, were rejected by every single school they had listed — that the city was effectively failing at the most basic function a matching system exists to perform, simply because the old process was not actually designed around the stability and completeness guarantees Gale and Shapley's mathematics can provide when properly implemented. Both reforms drew directly, explicitly, and by the researchers' own public account on the Nobel-winning theoretical apparatus this chapter has traced from a 1962 mathematics paper through a 1984 empirical rediscovery to a functioning twenty-first-century public school bureaucracy — one of the clearest possible demonstrations that market design, as an applied discipline, produces results measurable not in dollars raised for a government treasury, the metric the spectrum-auction chapters emphasized, but in something closer to a direct, countable change in how many real families got a real school placement they could actually use.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences recognized this body of work in 2012, awarding that year's Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences jointly to Alvin Roth and Lloyd Shapley — Gale had died in 2008 and Nobel prizes are not awarded posthumously — "for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design," a citation that, read carefully, credits both the abstract 1962 mathematics and its decades-later translation into kidney exchanges, medical-residency matching, and school-choice systems as a single, continuous intellectual achievement rather than treating theory and application as separate accomplishments. It is worth pausing on how different this prize's underlying logic is from the auction-theory prizes this book's earlier chapters described, even though both descend from closely related branches of mechanism design and even though the same handful of economists — Roth prominent among them — moved fluidly between both literatures across their careers. An auction asks how to let willing buyers and sellers discover a price through competitive bidding. A matching market, by design, asks how to achieve a comparably efficient and fair allocation in a setting where a price is either forbidden, as with organs, or simply absent, as with a school seat a family cannot legally purchase outright. The two problems turn out to share a great deal of underlying mathematical DNA, both requiring careful attention to what happens when a participant might benefit from lying about their true preferences, both vulnerable to unraveling if the mechanism's stability or strategy-proofness properties are not carefully engineered in from the start, and both, in their most successful real-world applications, requiring exactly the kind of patient, iterative, empirically grounded redesign work that turned Boston's broken school-assignment process and the NRMP's own troubled pre-algorithm history into working, trusted, high-stakes public infrastructure rather than an elegant theorem gathering dust in an economics journal.

Roth himself gave the underlying constraint a name, in a widely cited 2007 paper written well before his Nobel recognition, that helps explain why kidney exchange had to be solved through elaborate matching chains rather than through the far simpler mechanism this book has spent most of its length describing: repugnance. Many societies, Roth observed, refuse to let certain goods and services be bought and sold for money even when a straightforward market in them would plausibly increase efficiency and even save lives — organs, certain forms of paid surrogacy, indentured labor, and, in most jurisdictions, votes all fall into this category, not because a market in them is technically infeasible but because enough people find the very idea of pricing them morally offensive, a repugnance that operates as a real, binding constraint on market design regardless of what a purely welfare-maximizing calculation would recommend. A market designer confronting a repugnant transaction has, broadly, two options: try to build public support for lifting the taboo, a slow and often unsuccessful project, or accept the constraint as fixed and design the most efficient possible allocation mechanism that still respects it — which is exactly what kidney exchange does, achieving much of the efficiency gain a straightforward cash market in kidneys might in principle offer, through a mechanism of reciprocal in-kind exchange that never requires a single dollar to change hands for an organ, and that has, as a direct result, faced none of the fierce ethical and legal opposition a proposal to simply pay kidney donors has consistently encountered in essentially every country that has debated it, Iran's unusual regulated compensation system being the frequently cited exception that public-health researchers still argue about today.

That infrastructure, once built, tends to disappear from public view in a way that stands in sharp contrast to the theatrical, headline-generating spectacle of a nine-figure art auction or a multi-billion-dollar spectrum sale. No newspaper runs a breathless account of this year's residency match with the same drama it might lavish on a Leonardo selling for $450 million, even though the residency match quietly determines, every single year, where roughly forty thousand new American doctors will spend the next several years of their training, shaping the geographic and specialty distribution of the entire country's physician workforce for a generation. No television crew documents the moment a kidney-exchange chain clears and a dozen strangers, scattered across a dozen hospitals, receive transplants they could never have gotten through a simple pairwise donor match. The absence of spectacle is not incidental; it is close to the whole point. A market-design mechanism succeeding at its job looks, from the outside, like nothing happening at all — no bidding war, no gasping room, no record-setting hammer price — merely a scarce and genuinely valuable thing quietly, reliably, and demonstrably fairly finding its way to the specific person who actually needs it most, through a process almost nobody watching ever has to think about. That quiet, unglamorous reliability is itself the strongest possible advertisement for taking market design seriously as engineering rather than as abstract theory — a discipline whose successes are measured not in gasps from a saleroom crowd but in the number of medical students who never had to abandon an official clearinghouse for a chaotic private scramble, the number of transplant chains that cleared without a single donor backing out mid-sequence, and the number of families who told a school system the truth about which classroom they actually wanted their child sitting in, and were rewarded for it rather than punished.
