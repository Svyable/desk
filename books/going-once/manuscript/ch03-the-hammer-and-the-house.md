# The Hammer and the House

Samuel Baker was an eighteenth-century London bookseller who, in March 1744, put together a catalogue titled *Several Hundred Scarce and Valuable Books in all Branches of Polite Literature*, drawn from the library of a recently deceased member of Parliament, and sold the lot by auction over the course of several days. The sale brought in £826, a respectable sum for a mid-eighteenth-century book dealer but nothing that would have struck a contemporary as historic. Baker had no way of knowing that the business he was founding, initially trading as Baker & Leigh after he took on his brother-in-law George Leigh as a partner, would still exist under a version of his surname two hundred and eighty years later, or that it would eventually sell a five-hundred-year-old panel painting for nine figures on live television in a country that, in 1744, was still a British colony. The firm did not become "Sotheby's" until 1778, when Baker's nephew John Sotheby inherited a share of the business after Baker's death, and it would remain, for its first century and a half, primarily a house for books and manuscripts rather than the paintings and jewels people associate with the name today.

Twenty-two years after Baker's first sale, in 1766, a Scottish auctioneer named James Christie held his own first recorded auction in a saleroom on Pall Mall, and the timing was not coincidental: London in the mid-eighteenth century was accumulating exactly the conditions under which a professional auction trade could flourish. The city's wealth was growing quickly enough, and its estates were changing hands quickly enough through death, debt, and divorce, that there was a steady supply of valuable household contents — libraries, paintings, silver, furniture — needing to be converted into cash or redistributed among heirs, faster and more reliably than a private dealer negotiating one-on-one sales could manage. An auction solved a problem a private sale could not: it created, in a single afternoon, a public, competitive, time-limited event that could liquidate an entire estate's worth of disparate goods to whichever buyers in London happened to want them most, at prices nobody had to negotiate item by item. Christie built his reputation quickly on the fine-art side of that business, cultivating relationships with the aristocracy and, within a dozen years, negotiating art sales that reached as far as the court of Catherine the Great of Russia. By 1786 his house was significant enough to be entrusted with dispersing the library of Samuel Johnson, the writer who had compiled the first great English dictionary a generation earlier — a sale that functioned, in its own quiet way, as a form of cultural endorsement no advertisement could have purchased.

What Baker, Leigh, Sotheby, and Christie actually built, underneath the branding, was something closer to a standing piece of market infrastructure than a shop. A conventional eighteenth-century retailer set a price and waited for a customer willing to pay it, adjusting the price occasionally based on how sales were going, with the whole process invisible to anyone outside the transaction. An auction house instead committed, in advance, to a specific date, a specific venue, and a specific set of published rules, and then let a room full of interested buyers — who did not need to trust the seller, or even know who the seller was, since consignors were routinely kept anonymous — compete openly against one another under those rules. The house's core service was not really finding a buyer; a sufficiently well-connected dealer could often find a single buyer without an auction at all. The service was running a credible, repeatable *procedure*, one buyers could trust not to be rigged against them and sellers could trust to surface the true depth of market demand rather than whatever a single dealer felt like offering that week. That distinction — selling an item versus running a trustworthy procedure for discovering its price — is the thread connecting an eighteenth-century London book sale to a modern digital ad exchange, and it is worth holding onto, because almost everything interesting about auction history is really a history of institutions trying to make that procedure more trustworthy, faster, or harder to cheat.

The specific format both houses adopted and refined — what economists now call the English auction, though neither Baker nor Christie would have used that term — is the one most people picture by default when they hear the word "auction": an auctioneer opens with a starting price, bids rise in increments from an audience that can see and hear each other's offers, and the sale closes when no one is willing to top the last bid, typically marked by the auctioneer's gavel coming down. The format has obvious virtues. It is easy for participants to understand without training. It reveals information progressively, letting bidders update their own sense of an item's value as they watch how aggressively others are willing to compete for it — a genuinely useful feature when, as with art, the item's worth is uncertain and different bidders may hold different pieces of relevant information, about provenance, about a rival collector's determination, about what similar works have recently commanded. And it produces real-time theater: a rising number, called aloud, with witnesses watching each other react, is simply a more emotionally compelling spectacle than a sealed envelope, and both Sotheby's and Christie's discovered early and have never forgotten that spectacle sells. A gasping room, it turns out, is itself a marketing asset, one auction houses have spent two and a half centuries learning to cultivate deliberately.

That cultivation eventually became an entire discipline of its own, well documented in the modern art-market literature, and it is worth being specific about the tools involved, because several of them recur, almost unchanged, in this book's later chapters about digital and financial auctions. Auction houses learned to publish pre-sale estimates — a projected price range printed in the catalogue — which function less as a genuine forecast than as an anchor, priming a room's sense of what a "reasonable" bid looks like before a single hand is raised; behavioral economists have since documented, across many contexts, how powerfully an arbitrary initial number can distort subsequent judgments even when everyone involved knows the number was arbitrary. Houses learned to sequence a sale carefully, often opening with a few modest, reliably sellable lots to warm a room into a competitive rhythm before presenting the evening's genuine marquee item, timing that has nothing to do with the intrinsic value of any individual lot and everything to do with managing the emotional state of the buyers in the room. And houses learned, more controversially, the value of a bid that isn't quite real: an auctioneer's practiced habit of announcing a phantom increment, glancing toward an empty corner of the room or an anonymous point on the wall, to suggest competing interest exists even when, at that instant, it might not. This last technique has a name in the trade — chandelier bidding, so called because the auctioneer's gaze is said to land somewhere near the ceiling fixtures, on no bidder in particular — and its legal status is stranger than most people assume: in New York, where a large share of the world's major art auctions take place, the practice is explicitly legal so long as any phantom bids stay below the item's confidential reserve price, on the theory that the auctioneer is merely protecting the seller's minimum acceptable price rather than defrauding the eventual buyer. New York City auction regulations do, however, require houses to disclose in their sale materials whether lots carry a reserve at all, a disclosure rule that exists precisely because regulators recognized how easily an unrestrained version of the practice could shade into outright deception, and legislators have periodically pushed, with only partial success, to tighten the rules further.

It would be a mistake to read that last paragraph as evidence that Sotheby's and Christie's were, from the start, running a rigged game rather than a legitimate market institution, and it is worth resisting that conclusion for the same reason the rest of this book resists treating any single auction format as either purely virtuous or purely predatory. The houses that survived nearly three centuries did so substantially because enough buyers and sellers, across enough generations, concluded that the procedure was trustworthy enough to keep using, even knowing it involved theatrical techniques designed to influence them. A market institution does not need to be perfectly transparent to be broadly functional; it needs to be transparent enough, and policed enough, that the participants who understand its quirks still find it worth their while to keep showing up, and the ones who don't understand its quirks aren't cheated badly enough, or often enough, to poison the well for everyone else. Sotheby's and Christie's both grew, over two and a half centuries, into globally dominant institutions specifically because they managed that balance well enough, most of the time, to keep attracting both the sophisticated collectors who understood exactly how chandelier bidding and pre-sale estimates worked and the less sophisticated ones who did not.

The balance broke, spectacularly, at the very top of both houses in the 1990s, in an episode that remains the most serious criminal scandal in either firm's history and a useful corrective to any temptation to romanticize the saleroom as a pure meritocracy of taste and money. Between 1993 and 1999, according to United States Department of Justice antitrust prosecutors, Sotheby's chairman A. Alfred Taubman and his counterpart at Christie's secretly agreed, at a series of private meetings, to fix the commission rates both houses charged sellers — the exact fee schedule the two firms had spent two centuries publicly, and expensively, competing on. Sotheby's chief executive, Diana "Dede" Brooks, whom Taubman had personally elevated to run the company's day-to-day operations, eventually admitted her own role in carrying out the scheme and agreed to testify against him in exchange for avoiding prison time herself. Prosecutors estimated the conspiracy had allowed the two houses to overcharge American sellers by at least $400 million across the six years it ran. Taubman was convicted, sentenced to a year and a day in federal prison, and fined $7.5 million; both houses separately paid hundreds of millions more in civil settlements to the sellers they had overcharged. The scandal is a near-perfect illustration of a distinction this book keeps returning to. An auction, properly run, is a mechanism for preventing *bidders* from colluding against a seller. Nothing in that mechanism protects a seller from the two houses they are choosing between colluding against *them* instead, at a level one rung above the auction floor, where no bidder in the room could ever have seen it happening.

A subtler and entirely legal modern practice raises a related question about how much of what looks like open competitive bidding in a saleroom is genuinely open. The guarantee this book's first chapter mentioned in passing — Christie's promise to *Salvator Mundi*'s consignor of roughly $100 million regardless of how the sale actually went — has become, in the twenty-first century, one of the central financial instruments of the major auction houses' most important evening sales, and it works in a way that quietly reshapes the room before a single bid is called. A house, or increasingly an outside guarantor working with the house, agrees in advance to buy a given lot for a fixed, undisclosed price if the room's actual bidding fails to reach it, in exchange for a fee paid whether or not that guarantee is ever called upon; guarantors are then typically permitted to bid in the live sale itself, effectively competing to buy the very item they have already agreed to purchase as a backstop. Trade publications tracking the practice found that, in the first half of one recent year, nearly seventy-three percent of lots by total value at the major houses' postwar and contemporary evening sales carried some form of guarantee, a record share representing hundreds of millions of dollars committed before the auctioneer had said a word. Guarantees give sellers welcome certainty and give houses a powerful tool for luring the biggest, most anxious consignors away from a rival house. They also, by removing the risk of an embarrassing unsold lot, quietly change what the room's applause is actually responding to: a headline hammer price that, for a heavily guaranteed lot, may reflect less spontaneous competitive fervor than a carefully financed floor with a live auction layered artfully on top of it.

The two houses' long duopoly at the top of the fine-art auction world also illustrates a structural feature of auction markets that recurs throughout this book in far more consequential settings: liquidity attracts liquidity. A seller with a valuable painting wants to sell it wherever the largest pool of serious buyers will show up, because a bigger, more competitive room produces a better price. A serious buyer wants to bid wherever the most desirable items are being sold, for the same reason in reverse. That mutual attraction creates a powerful, self-reinforcing advantage for whichever venue already has the deepest pool of participants on both sides — precisely the dynamic that would later let a small number of digital ad exchanges, and a small number of national stock exchanges, and eventually a small number of AI-agent commerce platforms, come to dominate categories of auction that, on paper, anyone with a server could try to run. Sotheby's and Christie's spent much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries locked in exactly this kind of network competition with each other and with smaller regional houses, occasionally outright cooperating in ways that, decades later, would land both firms in serious legal trouble: in 2000, U.S. antitrust prosecutors charged both houses' leadership with a price-fixing conspiracy in which the two supposedly rival auction houses had secretly agreed, over several years, to fix the commission rates they charged sellers, rather than competing on price the way their advertised rivalry implied. Sotheby's former chairman was convicted; the company paid substantial civil settlements; and the episode remains one of the starkest reminders in this book's entire history that an institution built to *prevent* collusion among bidders is not automatically immune to collusion among the people running it.

A third house, founded far more modestly than either Sotheby's or Christie's and never quite matching their scale, nonetheless carved out a durable place in the same market and is worth a brief mention for what its more recent history reveals about how the industry's balance of power has shifted. Harry Phillips founded his own London auction house in 1796, a full three decades after Christie's own first sale, and built an early reputation partly on the same kind of celebrity-adjacent provenance this book's ninth chapter later shows still driving headline prices centuries afterward, auctioning items connected to Marie Antoinette's estate and to Napoleon Bonaparte's household among its notable early sales. Phillips changed ownership and identity repeatedly over the following two centuries, and its modern incarnation, substantially reshaped after LVMH's Bernard Arnault acquired and merged it with the dealers Simon de Pury and Daniela Luxembourg in 1999, deliberately staked out contemporary art, design, and photography as its core specialty rather than attempting to compete head-on with its two larger rivals across every category. The strategy has kept Phillips a real, if distinctly smaller, presence in the modern auction market: the house reported roughly $573 million in total sales in 2023, a figure that reads as substantial in absolute terms but trails Christie's own reported sales of roughly $2.9 billion the same year by a wide margin, underscoring how thoroughly Sotheby's and Christie's, even after Phillips's modern repositioning and even after the price-fixing scandal that briefly threatened both larger houses' reputations, have continued to dominate the very market Samuel Baker's 1744 book sale first helped institutionalize.

Both houses spent the twentieth century converting from single London institutions into genuinely global auction networks, a expansion that mattered enormously for the argument this book is building, because it is the first clear evidence that an auction house's real product was never really the room itself. Sotheby's opened a New York office in 1955 and merged with the American house Parke-Bernet in 1964, giving it a foothold in what would become, within a generation, the world's largest art market; Christie's followed with its own New York saleroom in 1977. Both houses expanded further still into Hong Kong, Geneva, Dubai, and eventually mainland China, chasing new pools of wealth as they emerged, and both discovered the same lesson repeatedly: a saleroom's value depended less on its physical address than on the depth and geographic reach of the bidder network it could reliably summon for any given sale, whether those bidders were sitting in the room, waiting on a telephone line staffed by a specialist relaying bids in real time, or, by the early 2000s, watching a live video feed and clicking a button from a laptop thousands of miles away. Both houses built genuine online bidding platforms over the following two decades — Christie's LIVE and Sotheby's own web and app-based bidding tools among them — explicitly to let the mechanism's oldest promise, that a rising public price reflects the true depth of competitive demand, keep holding even as the room itself dissolved into a distributed network of screens. That dissolution is worth flagging early, because it is the same dissolution this book traces, in a far more extreme form, when it reaches the servers running real-time digital ad auctions: the saleroom did not disappear so much as it kept shrinking, generation by generation, until almost nothing remained of it except the software enforcing its rules.

By the middle of the twentieth century, the English ascending auction that Baker and Christie had helped formalize was firmly established as the default format for high-value, uniquely identified goods: fine art, rare books, antiques, real estate, classic cars, and, eventually, entire companies sold through takeover bids. But it was never the only auction format in serious commercial use, and it was rarely the most efficient one for goods that needed to move in large volume, at speed, with minimal ceremony. A saleroom full of nervous, theatrically primed bidders taking nineteen minutes to settle on a single price works beautifully for a one-of-a-kind Leonardo. It works far less well when a wholesaler needs to sell forty thousand fresh-cut tulips before they wilt, or when a fish market needs to clear an entire morning's catch before it spoils, or when a government needs to allocate ninety-nine separate radio-spectrum licenses in a matter of weeks rather than years. Solving those problems required a genuinely different auction mechanism — one that runs in the opposite direction from Sotheby's rising bid, and one that, as the next chapter shows, a room full of Dutch flower traders had been quietly perfecting since long before anyone in London had heard of it.

It is worth being precise about what Baker, Leigh, Sotheby, and Christie actually contributed to that longer story, since the honest answer is narrower, and in some ways more interesting, than the popular founding myth. They did not invent competitive bidding, which the previous chapter traced back through Rome and, on Herodotus's contested account, further still. They did not even invent the scheduled, catalogued, professionally staffed auction house as a going commercial concern, an honor that belongs to the Dutch book trade a century and a half earlier. What they did was take a mechanism and an institutional template that already existed, attach it durably to the luxury end of the London art and antiques trade, and, crucially, keep the resulting business intact and recognizable across two hundred and eighty years of wars, recessions, changes in taste, and — in Taubman's case — outright criminal misconduct at the very top of the firm. Institutional durability of that kind is its own achievement, distinct from technical originality, and it is the specific achievement that let a mechanism first documented in ancient Rome and refined in seventeenth-century Leiden still be standing, recognizable gavel and all, in a Rockefeller Center saleroom on a November evening in 2017.
