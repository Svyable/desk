# The Chant

At approximately 7:02 p.m. on the evening of November 15, 2017, a Christie's auctioneer named Jussi Pylkkänen stood at a podium in Rockefeller Center and opened the bidding on lot 9B at $70 million. The painting behind him, a badly damaged, heavily restored panel showing Christ in Renaissance dress with two fingers raised in blessing, had been bought at a Louisiana estate sale in 2005 for less than $10,000, back when it was catalogued as a copy by a follower of Leonardo da Vinci. Specialists had spent the intervening years arguing about how much of it, if any, had been painted by Leonardo himself. Christie's had guaranteed the consignor roughly $100 million regardless of what happened in the room, which meant the house had already priced in the possibility that the bidding might go no further than that.

It went a great deal further than that. For nineteen minutes, bids arrived in increments of five and then ten million dollars, several of them relayed by Christie's specialists holding telephones to their ears, repeating numbers from bidders who did not want to be seen in the room. The price passed $200 million, then $300 million, then $400 million, each new number met with the kind of audible gasp that auction houses spend a great deal of effort trying to engineer and rarely get for free. At $400 million, two phone bidders were left. At $450 million, one of them stopped. Pylkkänen brought the hammer down at $450.3 million with buyer's premium included, and the room broke into applause of the sort usually reserved for a goal in stoppage time. *Salvator Mundi* — Savior of the World — became, in the space of those nineteen minutes, the most expensive painting ever sold at auction, more than double the previous record. A month later, Abu Dhabi's Department of Culture and Tourism confirmed it had been the buyer, on behalf of the Louvre Abu Dhabi, and the painting largely vanished from public view.

The episode has been picked apart for years since: by art historians disputing how much of the paint layer is actually Leonardo's hand, by journalists tracing the ownership chain back through a Russian billionaire and a Swiss art-storage facility, by economists using it as a case study in what happens when scarcity, celebrity, and a determined underbidder collide in a single room. Almost all of that analysis treats the sale as an anomaly — a freak convergence of a five-hundred-year-old panel, a disputed attribution, and two people with nine-figure budgets and something to prove. It was a freak convergence. It was also, mechanically, the same thing that happens dozens of times a second on computer servers most people have never heard of, selling something far less glamorous than a Leonardo: the right to put a banner ad in front of your eyes for the next four seconds.

That second kind of auction does not have an auctioneer, an applauding room, or a gavel. It has an ad exchange, a bid request containing a compressed profile of whoever just loaded a web page, and a few dozen automated bidders evaluating that profile and submitting silent numbers, all of it resolved and the winning ad rendered on your screen before the page has finished loading — commonly under 100 milliseconds from request to render, according to industry technical explainers of real-time bidding infrastructure. Nobody gasps. Nobody claps. The total dollar value moving through this kind of auction dwarfs the art market by orders of magnitude: real-time bidding platforms alone were estimated at roughly $16 billion to $33 billion in annual market size in 2025 depending on which research firm's methodology you use, and that figure counts only the technology layer, not the far larger pool of digital ad spending it allocates. More than 70 percent of digital display advertising is now delivered through real-time bidding, and programmatic buying generally is projected to account for the large majority of global digital display spending. Somewhere between the Rockefeller Center saleroom and the ad exchange server rack, the auction went from being a spectacle to being invisible — and, not entirely by coincidence, from being rare to being one of the most common transactions on Earth.

This is a book about that migration, and about the mechanism underneath it. An auction is not a genre of event, the way "wedding" or "trial" is a genre of event. It is a piece of engineering: a specific procedure for finding out what something is worth by asking a group of people to disclose, through their behavior, information they would often rather keep to themselves. Economists have a precise word for the family of procedures it belongs to — a mechanism — and a subfield, market design, devoted to studying which mechanisms produce good outcomes under which conditions. The Nobel committee has recognized this subfield twice in the last quarter-century: Roger Myerson, along with two colleagues, in 2007, for the broader theory of mechanism design; Alvin Roth and Lloyd Shapley in 2012, specifically for applying matching theory to real markets, from labor markets for doctors to a nationwide kidney-exchange program; and Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson in 2020, explicitly for improvements to auction theory and the invention of new auction formats, work that had already been used two decades earlier to design the auctions this book spends a full chapter on. That is three Nobel Memorial Prizes in Economic Sciences, spanning thirteen years, all substantially about the plumbing of how a price gets set when nobody knows in advance what the right price is. Plumbing rarely gets that kind of recognition. This plumbing has, because it turned out to be load-bearing for an unreasonable share of the modern economy.

Start with the everyday evidence, most of which does not look like an auction at all until you know what to look for. The interest rate the United States government pays to borrow money is set at auction, every week, at the Treasury market desk in New York and Washington, through a process that in 1991 nearly broke when a single bond-trading firm tried to corner it — a story this book returns to later, because the scandal is one of the clearest windows anyone has ever gotten into how badly an auction can go wrong when one bidder decides the rules are optional. The right to broadcast on a slice of the radio spectrum, the invisible medium that carries your phone calls, your GPS signal, and your Wi-Fi, is sold at auction by national governments; in Britain in 2000, one such auction raised the government £22.5 billion, an amount equal to roughly 2.5 percent of the country's entire economic output that year, extracted in a matter of weeks from five telecom companies competing for five licenses. The seat next to you on a full flight, priced differently for every passenger on the plane depending on when they bought and how the algorithm read demand that week, is the output of a pricing system with unmistakable auction DNA even though no bidder ever sees a paddle. The fare that doubles on your ride-hailing app on a rainy Friday night is not, strictly, an auction — this book will be careful about that distinction, because the difference matters — but it was built by people who understood auction theory, to solve the same underlying problem an auction solves: how do you allocate a scarce thing, in real time, to the people who want it most, without a human standing in the middle deciding by hand.

Even government programs most people would never describe as markets turn out, on inspection, to be auctions wearing a different name. When a new doctor in the United States is assigned to a hospital for residency training, the assignment comes out of an algorithm descended directly from the mathematics of two-sided matching — a cousin of auction theory, built by the same intellectual community, sometimes by the same individual economists — that has been running essentially unchanged in its core logic since the 1950s and now also runs the country's national kidney-exchange program, finding chains of willing but incompatible donor-recipient pairs and linking them into transplants that would otherwise never happen. When New York City or Boston lets a family rank their preferred public schools, the assignment that comes back is generated by an algorithm two economists redesigned in the early 2000s specifically because the old system was quietly punishing families who told the truth about their favorite school and rewarding families who gamed it — an argument this book will make in some detail, because it is one of the most persuasive real-world demonstrations that mechanism design is not academic decoration. It changes who gets what.

And then there is the newest branch of the tree, the one this book was, in a real sense, written to reach. In September 2025, Google published an open specification called the Agent Payments Protocol, designed to let a software agent — not a person clicking "buy," but a program acting on a person's stated intent — cryptographically prove to a merchant that a real human had actually authorized a specific purchase, with more than sixty companies including Mastercard, PayPal, American Express, and Coinbase signed on as launch partners. Weeks earlier, OpenAI and Stripe had released a complementary specification, the Agentic Commerce Protocol, standardizing how an AI agent completes a checkout on a merchant's behalf. By early 2026, Coinbase had launched a platform explicitly built for autonomous agents to transact directly with one another using stablecoins, settling machine-to-machine payments without a human in the loop for each one. None of this infrastructure was built to run auctions specifically. All of it was built because the fastest-growing category of buyer on the internet was starting to be software, and software needed a standardized way to prove it was allowed to spend money — which is, not coincidentally, close to the oldest problem an auction house has ever had to solve: how do you know the bidder in the room, or on the phone, or behind the paddle, is actually good for the money they are about to commit.

None of this is meant to suggest that auctions are secretly running everything, in the conspiratorial sense that phrase usually implies. Most prices in the economy are still posted prices — a number on a tag, take it or leave it — set by a seller using cost estimates, competitor research, and a fair amount of guesswork, the same way prices have been set for most of commercial history. Auctions are the exception, not the rule, and this book is not an argument that they should become the rule. It is an argument that the exception has been growing, quietly and for identifiable reasons, in exactly the places where a posted price struggles: where value is genuinely uncertain and different bidders know different pieces of it, where a resource is scarce enough that the seller cannot afford to guess wrong, where speed matters more than a human being's ability to negotiate in real time, and — increasingly — where the volume of individual transactions has become too large for any person to price one at a time. Auctions grow, in other words, exactly where computation gets cheap and human judgment gets expensive relative to it. That has been true, in a small way, since the moment the first computer touched a trading floor. It is becoming true in a much larger way now that the bidder itself, not just the pricing engine behind it, can be a piece of software with no need to sleep, blink, or feel the social pressure of an applauding room.

The history in the chapters ahead runs, roughly, in the order the mechanism itself developed complexity. It starts in the ancient world, with an auction Herodotus described happening in Babylon, where marriageable women were reportedly sold to the highest bidder in a ritual whose accuracy modern historians dispute but whose underlying logic — start with the person you value most, work down from there — turns out to recur, structurally, in every ascending auction that followed. It moves through the London saleroom in the 1740s and 1760s where Samuel Baker and James Christie built the houses that still carry their names, formalizing rules of conduct that nineteenth- and twentieth-century commodity and livestock markets would later borrow wholesale, chant and all. It arrives, a little past the book's midpoint, at a 1961 paper by an economist named William Vickrey that sat almost entirely uncited for a decade before quietly becoming one of the most influential documents in the history of applied economics, and at a Nobel Prize Vickrey received in October 1996 and lived only three days to enjoy. And it ends where the record currently stops: with a growing body of live infrastructure, built in the past two years, for a species of bidder that has never sat in a saleroom, never felt the endowment effect that makes a human overvalue whatever they are about to sell, and never once been tempted, the way human bidders reliably are, to keep raising a paddle purely because raising it feels like winning.

That last tendency deserves an early word, because it explains why an entire section of this book is about psychology rather than mechanism. An auction is, formally, just a set of rules for aggregating private information into a public price. In practice, it is also one of the most reliable machines ever built for making otherwise sensible people spend more than they meant to. Researchers studying the same category of common-value auction that undid Rockefeller Center bidders and oil-lease bidders alike have a name for the systematic pattern in which the winner of an auction for something whose value is uncertain, and roughly the same to everyone, tends to have overpaid relative to the item's actual worth, simply because winning is disproportionately likely when your private estimate happened to be the most optimistic one in the room. It is called the winner's curse, it was first documented rigorously by three petroleum engineers studying Gulf of Mexico oil-lease bidding in 1971, and it has since been found in art auctions, wine auctions, corporate takeover bids, and initial public offerings of stock — a genuinely general phenomenon, not a quirk of any one market, and one the book spends real time on because understanding it is close to a prerequisite for understanding why auction design is hard.

The chapters that follow try to hold two things in tension that popular writing about markets usually collapses into one. The first is that auctions, done well, are one of the more remarkable pieces of social technology humans have built: a way of letting strangers who trust each other not at all arrive at a number that reflects genuinely dispersed information, without any of them having to disclose what they privately know. The Nobel committee's own language about the 2020 prize to Milgrom and Wilson put it almost that plainly, crediting them with improving auction theory and inventing new auction formats that, in the committee's words, benefit sellers, buyers, and taxpayers around the world. The second is that the same technology, deployed carelessly, at the wrong speed, or by a seller more interested in theater than efficiency, produces some of the more embarrassing and occasionally predatory episodes in commercial history: a bidding-fee website that extracted more than fourteen hundred dollars from customers for a five-hundred-dollar game console using what researchers later identified as a textbook sunk-cost trap; a spectrum sale so lucrative for one government that it left the winning telecom companies groaning under debt for the better part of a decade; a rent-pricing algorithm that the U.S. Department of Justice alleged, in a 2024 lawsuit against the company RealPage, functioned as a mechanism for landlords to coordinate prices they could never have legally agreed to coordinate by hand. Both of these things are true about auctions, often about the very same auction format, and a book that only tells one half is not really explaining anything.

There is a reason to care about this history now, in a way that would have made a slimmer case ten years ago. The auction, throughout almost all of its five-thousand-year written record, has had a human being on both sides of it: a seller and a buyer, or an auctioneer standing between many buyers and one seller, all of them subject to fatigue, social pressure, limited attention, and the particular emotional charge of a room that wants them to keep bidding. That is no longer reliably true. The bidder in a real-time ad auction has not been a person, in any meaningful sense, for close to two decades; it has been a piece of software evaluating a profile and submitting a number, thousands of times a second, with no attention to fatigue at all. The infrastructure now emerging for AI agents to authorize and settle payments on a person's behalf extends that same shift — bidder as software, not just pricing engine as software — into categories of purchase that used to require a human to sit down and decide. Whether that turns out to be a quiet efficiency gain, a new vector for exactly the kind of collusion regulators are already suing over, or something stranger than either is the question the book's final chapters take seriously, without pretending to answer it more confidently than the evidence currently allows.

For now, go back to the room in Rockefeller Center, because it is worth sitting with one more time before it recedes into a history the rest of the book will treat mostly for its mechanics. Nineteen minutes. A gavel. Applause. A price four and a half times the pre-sale estimate, arrived at not because anyone calculated what *Salvator Mundi* was "worth" — nobody can calculate that, for an object with no comparable sale in the historical record and a disputed attribution to boot — but because two extraordinarily well-resourced bidders, each holding private information about how much the painting meant to them and how much they could afford to spend proving it, kept disclosing small pieces of that information through their behavior until only one of them was left standing. That is what an auction does. It does not discover a pre-existing true value sitting out there in the world, quietly waiting for a patient enough observer to come along and measure it correctly. It manufactures a price, in public, out of private conviction, under time pressure, in front of witnesses. The rest of this book is about what happens when you take that manufacturing process, strip out the room, the gavel, and eventually the human bidders altogether, and let it run underneath almost everything you buy, sell, borrow, or increasingly, simply hand off to a piece of software and trust to go handle competently on your behalf.
