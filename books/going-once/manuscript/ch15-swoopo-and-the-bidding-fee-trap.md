# Swoopo and the Bidding-Fee Trap

Swoopo, a German company that expanded aggressively into the American market in 2008, sold what looked, at first glance, like an ordinary online auction with an unusually cheerful, game-show-like interface: consumer electronics, gift cards, and the occasional car, offered up for bids that climbed in small increments the way an eBay listing's bids might climb, with a countdown clock ticking down toward a final sale. The resemblance to an ordinary auction stopped almost entirely at that surface level, because Swoopo's actual business model inverted the basic economics every previous auction in this book has relied on. In a conventional auction, whether run by Sotheby's, eBay, or Aalsmeer's flower clock, only the winning bidder pays anything at all; every losing bidder walks away having spent nothing beyond their own time and attention. Swoopo charged for bids themselves, not merely for winning. Registered users purchased "bid credits" in advance — in the American version of the site, roughly sixty cents apiece, typically sold in bundles of several dozen or several hundred — and each individual bid placed in a live auction consumed one credit, permanently, win or lose, while simultaneously nudging the item's displayed price upward by a small fixed increment, usually just a few cents, and resetting the countdown clock by several seconds to give other bidders a chance to respond. A bidder who placed a hundred bids across an auction and still lost had spent sixty dollars in nonrefundable bidding fees for absolutely nothing, receiving no item, no partial credit, and no refund of any kind.

The mathematics of this arrangement, once laid out plainly, explain why Swoopo could profit handsomely even on items it sold for a small fraction of their retail value, a feature the company's own marketing leaned into aggressively, advertising eye-catching final sale prices that made losing bidders feel, by comparison, as though they had narrowly missed an extraordinary bargain. Consider a five-hundred-dollar game console that a Swoopo auction eventually "sold" for two hundred forty dollars, a number the site's own promotional materials could honestly and quite accurately display, to any casual visitor, as a stunning eighty-percent discount off ordinary retail. That two-hundred-forty-dollar figure, at a few cents per bid increment, implies the auction absorbed something in the neighborhood of two thousand individual bids over its course — and Swoopo collected sixty cents in nonrefundable bidding-fee revenue for every single one of those roughly two thousand bids, regardless of who ultimately won, meaning the company's total revenue from that one auction, once bidding fees are added to the console's own final winning price, could run to well over a thousand dollars on a five-hundred-dollar item, a healthy profit extracted overwhelmingly from the roughly nineteen hundred ninety-nine losing bids rather than from the single winning one. Winning bidders, meanwhile, genuinely did receive a steep-looking discount on the item's face price, which meant Swoopo's most visible, most talked-about outcomes — the triumphant winners posting about their incredible bargain — were also its best possible advertising, a structural feature ensuring the site's most publicly visible stories were never the roughly nineteen hundred ninety-nine other participants who had each paid real money for nothing at all.

The psychological engine powering this arrangement, once economists began studying it seriously, turned out to be a close, almost textbook-precise cousin of a bias this book's seventh chapter already introduced in a milder form: the sunk-cost fallacy, the well-documented human tendency to let past, unrecoverable investment influence a decision that should, by any purely forward-looking economic logic, depend only on future costs and benefits. A Swoopo bidder who had already spent thirty dollars in nonrefundable bid credits chasing a two-hundred-dollar television faced, at each subsequent moment, a genuinely rational-sounding temptation to place just one more sixty-cent bid rather than abandon the thirty dollars already spent and walk away with literally nothing to show for it — even though, from a strictly forward-looking perspective, the thirty dollars already spent was gone regardless of what happened next, and the only question that should have mattered was whether one more sixty-cent bid, evaluated purely on its own future prospects, was worth placing. The economist Ned Augenblick's detailed academic analysis of the platform, titled plainly "The Sunk-Cost Fallacy in Penny Auctions," worked directly from real, transaction-level Swoopo bidding data rather than from a laboratory simulation, giving the paper an unusual degree of real-world grounding compared with much of the experimental behavioral-economics literature this book has drawn on elsewhere. Drawing on two large datasets covering roughly 166,000 individual penny auctions, Augenblick calculated that average profit margins across the format exceeded fifty percent, a figure that would be an extraordinary result in almost any conventional retail or auction business and that stood in stark contrast to the razor-thin margins competitive markets ordinarily grind prices down toward over time. To explain a margin that large surviving in what looked, on its surface, like a genuinely competitive marketplace with many bidders and no single dominant seller, Augenblick built the sunk-cost fallacy directly into his formal economic model of bidder behavior, generating specific, testable predictions about exactly how a bidder's probability of dropping out of a given auction should change as that same bidder's own accumulated, already-spent bidding fees grew larger. He then went on to confirm those very same predictions empirically and rigorously, using the full statistical weight of the transaction-level dataset: a bidder's likelihood of abandoning an auction fell significantly, and in a statistically robust way, as that bidder's own individual sunk costs climbed, with the measured effect running, across several different model specifications, at roughly half to nearly the full strength of the effect produced by the auction's genuine, forward-looking prize value itself — meaning bidders were, in effect, treating money they had already unrecoverably spent as though it were nearly as strong a reason to keep bidding as the value of the prize they were actually trying to win, precisely the logical error the sunk-cost fallacy predicts and precisely the error a purely rational, forward-looking bidder should never make. Augenblick found that Swoopo generated excessive profits, relative to any plausible competitive benchmark, across the sample periods he examined, and he was explicit in attributing the pattern specifically to the sunk-cost mechanism rather than to simpler explanations like bidder irrationality about probabilities or straightforward entertainment value — bidders, his analysis showed, behaved in ways that were difficult to reconcile with rational, forward-looking decision-making but were entirely consistent with a well-documented, previously studied cognitive bias operating exactly as laboratory research predicted it would, just deployed here against real money in a live commercial setting rather than a university subject pool. His 2015 paper argued that this sunk-cost dynamic, rather than simple irrationality or a failure to understand the rules, was the specific, identifiable mechanism explaining why bidders kept escalating auctions well past the point where any individual bid could plausibly be expected to pay off — a systematic, exploitable pattern built directly into human psychology that the auction's own design, whether by careful engineering or fortunate accident, was extraordinarily well suited to trigger and sustain, bid after sixty-cent bid, auction after auction.

The financial journalist Tim Harford, writing his long-running "Undercover Economist" column, examined Swoopo not long after the site's initial expansion and described it in terms that anticipated much of the academic analysis that would follow: a mechanism explicitly designed around what he called pure temptation, engineered specifically to exploit the gap between a bidder's cool, deliberative judgment before an auction begins and that same bidder's hot, in-the-moment psychology once real money and a visible, ticking clock enter the picture. Harford's column, alongside similar contemporaneous treatments in other outlets, helped popularize an understanding of Swoopo's mechanics well before Augenblick's formal academic paper gave the underlying dynamic rigorous statistical backing, an ordering worth noting because it recurs elsewhere in this book's history: journalists and consumer advocates, working from lived observation and reader complaints rather than from a dataset, frequently identify an auction-format's exploitative core years before economists arrive with the statistical tools to prove it rigorously, much as contemporaneous newspaper coverage of Europe's 3G spectrum auctions flagged the existential, panic-driven bidding this book's eleventh chapter later examined using formal auction theory, and much as the earliest, most skeptical eBay shill-bidding complaints preceded, by several years, the statistical detection methods this book's eighth chapter described academics eventually building to confirm what frustrated buyers had already suspected from lived experience alone.

Journalists and consumer advocates who examined the platform during its period of greatest popularity reached blunt, largely unflattering conclusions about what Swoopo actually was, once the game-show presentation was stripped away. One contemporaneous analysis, examining the site's mechanics for a general readership, described it as arguably one of the most effective methods yet devised for separating ordinary people from their money, citing the specific combination of features this chapter has already catalogued — pitting bidders directly against each other in a visible, competitive contest; pressuring them into rapid, low-deliberation decisions under a constantly resetting countdown clock; and structuring each individual bid to feel trivially small, a mere sixty cents, in a way that made the cumulative total easy to lose track of even as it climbed into the tens or hundreds of dollars. Consumer-protection regulators and legal commentators, examining the format from a different angle, focused less on the psychology and more on a narrower legal question with real regulatory teeth behind it: whether a bidding-fee auction, structurally, functioned as an illegal lottery rather than a legitimate auction, since a bidder's chance of winning bore, in the view of at least some legal experts who examined the format, an uncomfortably close resemblance to a paid chance at a prize rather than a genuine, value-reflecting competitive bid, a resemblance serious enough that several jurisdictions moved to regulate or restrict bidding-fee auction sites explicitly, treating them closer to gambling operations than to ordinary e-commerce, and requiring, in some cases, the same kind of licensing, disclosure, and consumer-protection obligations a licensed casino or a state lottery commission would otherwise be expected to satisfy before opening its doors to the public.

Swoopo's own commercial trajectory ultimately validated the skeptics more thoroughly than its early rapid growth had suggested it might. The scale the company reached before its collapse is worth stating plainly, because it undercuts any temptation to treat Swoopo as a marginal, easily dismissed internet curiosity rather than a genuinely large consumer business. By April 2009, Swoopo reported more than 1.2 million registered customers across the six countries where it operated auctions — Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, Austria, Canada, and Spain — and the company's German operations alone generated roughly €20 million, or about $28.3 million, in revenue in 2008, funded in part by venture capital including a $10 million round from the Silicon Valley firm August Capital in April 2009, the same year the underlying economics this chapter has described were already drawing sustained academic and journalistic scrutiny. This was not, in other words, a fly-by-night operation running out of someone's apartment; it was a professionally staffed, internationally expanding, venture-backed company that briefly looked, to sophisticated investors willing to put real capital behind it, like a genuinely disruptive new category of e-commerce rather than the psychological extraction machine this chapter has spent its length documenting. The company expanded aggressively across multiple countries through the late 2000s, riding a wave of media coverage that oscillated between fascination with its novel mechanics and mounting alarm at its exploitative potential, before its business began deteriorating as public understanding of the underlying economics spread, regulatory scrutiny intensified in several markets, and a wave of close imitators diluted whatever first-mover advantage the original site had enjoyed. By March 2011, Swoopo's website had gone dark, replaced by a brief notice citing unspecified technical issues that never resolved into a relaunch, and the company's underlying corporate entity was reported to have entered insolvency proceedings shortly afterward — an unusually swift collapse for a company that had, at its peak just two or three years earlier, been covered as a genuinely disruptive, venture-backed e-commerce innovation rather than as the cautionary tale it had become by the time it disappeared.

An American site called QuiBids, founded in Oklahoma in 2009 and structured around essentially the same bidding-fee mechanics Swoopo had pioneered, outlasted its German predecessor by years and drew a correspondingly larger and more sustained wave of regulatory and legal scrutiny within the United States specifically. The Federal Trade Commission issued a formal consumer alert in August 2011 warning shoppers that penny-auction sites functioned, in the agency's own characterization, closer to a lottery than to a genuine auction, cautioning that the advertised bargain prices reflected only the winning bidder's final cost and obscured the far larger sum every losing bidder had already spent on nonrefundable bids by the time the auction closed. QuiBids itself faced multiple federal class-action lawsuits in the years that followed, including litigation filed in its home state alleging the company had engaged in deceptive business practices by failing to disclose essential information about the true cost structure to prospective bidders, and separate suits elsewhere alleging the format constituted illegal gambling under state law and that the company had misappropriated news-website imagery and fabricated favorable customer testimonials in its own marketing materials. None of this litigation produced the kind of swift, decisive shutdown that overtook Swoopo in 2011; QuiBids continued operating for well over a decade after the FTC's initial warning, a durability that says less about the strength of its legal defenses than about how difficult American consumer-protection law and state gambling statutes, written for entirely different contexts, have proven to apply cleanly to a genuinely novel pricing mechanism that resembles an auction closely enough to claim the label while functioning, on the mathematics this chapter has laid out in detail, considerably more like a lottery with an unusually elaborate, auction-shaped façade built on top of it.

The format did not entirely vanish with Swoopo's own collapse, which is itself a meaningful data point about how durable a genuinely effective, if ethically dubious, psychological mechanism can prove to be even after its most prominent pioneer has failed. Numerous smaller bidding-fee auction sites continued operating in various countries for years afterward, some marketed with even less transparency about the underlying fee structure than Swoopo itself had used, prompting continued attention from consumer-protection regulators in multiple jurisdictions and periodic fresh rounds of the same illegal-lottery legal argument that had dogged Swoopo throughout its own run. The persistence of the format, despite Swoopo's own well-publicized failure and despite years of critical journalism explaining exactly how the sunk-cost mechanism worked, offers a useful, uncomfortable coda to this book's seventh chapter on auction psychology more broadly: understanding a cognitive bias intellectually, even reading a detailed magazine article explaining precisely how a specific website is engineered to exploit it, turns out to offer strikingly little practical protection against falling for it in the heat of an actual live bidding sequence, sixty cents at a time, with a countdown clock ticking and a screen showing just how close the current price sits to an unbelievable bargain that never quite arrives.

Swoopo's underlying mechanic — small, repeated, nonrefundable payments made in pursuit of an uncertain prize, sustained well past the point of obvious rational return by the accumulating weight of money already spent — has, in the years since the site's own collapse, reappeared in a considerably larger and more culturally normalized form than penny auctions ever achieved: the loot box, a randomized digital reward mechanism now embedded in a large share of the world's most popular video games, including many marketed directly to children. A player purchasing loot boxes is not, formally, bidding against other players the way a Swoopo customer was; there is no competing bidder and no final "winning" price to observe. But researchers studying loot-box spending have identified the identical psychological engine this chapter has spent its length describing, under the same name: the sunk-cost dynamic, in which a player who has already spent real money chasing a particular rare, randomized reward becomes progressively more reluctant to stop, precisely because stopping means accepting that the money already spent purchased nothing of the value originally sought, with academic studies published as recently as 2024 and 2025 drawing an explicit, empirically grounded link between loot-box spending patterns and the broader diagnostic criteria used to identify problem gambling. Regulators in several countries have begun treating loot boxes with a version of the same skepticism the FTC once directed at penny auctions, though enforcement and disclosure compliance, according to researchers tracking the global regulatory landscape, has remained inconsistent and, in a number of studied jurisdictions, poor even where formal probability-disclosure rules already exist on paper. The specific technology changes: an auction countdown clock in one era, a randomized in-game reward animation in the next. The underlying exploit, this book's seventh and fifteenth chapters together suggest, does not change nearly as much.

It is worth being precise, in closing this chapter, about exactly what distinguishes a bidding-fee auction from every other format this book has examined, because the distinction clarifies something important about where the ordinary, centuries-old logic of auction design actually breaks down. Every format examined earlier in this book — English, Dutch, sealed-bid, second-price, the generalized second-price auctions running search advertising — shares one structural feature regardless of how differently they otherwise behave: a losing bidder's cost of participation is, in the ordinary case, zero or close to it, which means a rational bidder can always safely withdraw the instant a rising price exceeds their true valuation, with nothing lost beyond the time already spent watching. Swoopo's bidding-fee structure broke that assumption at its foundation, converting *every single bid* into a small, immediate, nonrefundable cost regardless of the auction's ultimate outcome, which transformed the entire mechanism from a device for discovering how much a genuinely willing buyer would pay into a device for extracting small, repeated, psychologically underweighted payments from a very large number of people who would, individually, testify with complete sincerity that they were merely trying to win a good deal on a television.
