# The Four-Hundred-Million-Dollar Bid

The painting's journey to that November night in 2017 began, as far as the modern documented record goes, at a small regional auction house in New Orleans in 2005, where two art dealers, Alexander Parish and Robert Simon, bought it for less than $10,000 out of the estate of a Louisiana collector. It was catalogued at the time as the work of a follower of Leonardo da Vinci — a common and usually accurate attribution for the countless period copies and workshop variations of popular compositions that circulated widely in the centuries after a master's death, and an attribution that, if it had held, would have made the panel worth a few thousand dollars rather than hundreds of millions. Parish and Simon believed, or came to believe, that they had found something considerably more significant underneath centuries of overpainting, damage, and a crude restoration that had, among other indignities, given Christ a mustache he was never meant to have. They commissioned a careful conservation cleaning, consulted specialists, and began the long, genuinely uncertain process of building a case that the painting beneath the restoration was not a follower's copy but a work substantially by Leonardo's own hand.

That process illustrates something this book has not yet had occasion to examine closely: how a common-value auction handles an item whose underlying "common value" is not merely uncertain in the ordinary sense — nobody knows exactly how much oil sits under a lease tract, but everyone agrees oil is what they are estimating — but genuinely contested at the most basic definitional level, because different qualified experts, working from the same physical object and the same historical record, can and did reach different conclusions about who actually painted it. Attribution of an Old Master work is not a matter of simple chemical testing, the way Beltracchi's forged pigments could eventually be caught by a laboratory; it rests on connoisseurship, a scholarly discipline combining stylistic analysis, documentary research, and technical examination that produces expert judgment rather than binary proof, and reasonable, well-credentialed specialists can and frequently do disagree. Several leading Leonardo scholars did eventually endorse the *Salvator Mundi* attribution, following extensive research and an important 2011 exhibition at London's National Gallery that displayed it publicly as a Leonardo alongside works whose authorship was far less contested. But the endorsement was never unanimous. A meaningful number of other respected specialists in the field continued to argue that the painting was, at most, substantially a workshop production — completed with significant participation from Leonardo's own students or assistants working under his supervision, a common practice in Renaissance workshops — rather than a work painted predominantly by Leonardo himself, and some went further, arguing the surviving canvas retained too little of its original paint layer, after centuries of damage and restoration, for any attribution above the level of "circle of Leonardo" to be responsibly sustained at all.

The 2011 National Gallery exhibition deserves a closer look, because it functioned, whether the museum intended it to or not, as a pivotal moment in exactly the kind of information cascade this book's later chapters on prediction and financial markets return to repeatedly: a moment when an institution's willingness to publicly associate itself with a claim becomes, for outside observers who cannot independently evaluate the underlying evidence, a powerful substitute for actually evaluating that evidence themselves. The National Gallery is one of the world's most respected repositories of Renaissance painting, staffed by curators whose own professional reputations depend on getting attribution calls right, and its decision to display the *Salvator Mundi* in 2011 as part of a major loan exhibition on Leonardo's work as painter to the Milanese court, rather than declining the loan or displaying it with heavy attributional caveats, was read by much of the art world, and by the market that would go on to trade the painting, as close to an institutional endorsement. Critics of that reading have since pointed out, reasonably, that inclusion in a loan exhibition is not the same thing as a museum's own permanent acquisition or catalogue attribution, and that curatorial decisions about what to display are shaped by many considerations beyond a flat verdict on authenticity. The market, largely, did not draw that finer distinction. A painting that had entered the National Gallery's galleries under Leonardo's name left them, in the eyes of most subsequent buyers and journalists, considerably closer to settled fact than contested hypothesis, regardless of how carefully the museum's own published materials had actually hedged the claim.

That unresolved scholarly disagreement did not stop the painting from changing hands twice more at escalating prices before its record-setting Christie's sale, each transaction adding a further, semi-official layer of market credibility to the attribution regardless of where the underlying scholarly debate actually stood. A consortium of dealers bought it in 2013 for a reported $75 to $80 million; the Russian billionaire Dmitry Rybolovlev purchased it from that consortium later the same year for $127.5 million, in a private transaction that would itself later become the subject of extensive litigation between Rybolovlev and the dealer who brokered several of his major art purchases, over allegations of dramatically inflated markups across that dealer's entire relationship with the collector. Rybolovlev's subsequent lawsuits, filed in multiple jurisdictions, alleged that the dealer who had brokered the *Salvator Mundi* purchase and several dozen other major acquisitions had systematically misrepresented the prices he was actually paying sellers, pocketing enormous undisclosed markups — in the *Salvator Mundi*'s specific case, reportedly buying the painting from the 2013 consortium for a price tens of millions of dollars below what he then charged Rybolovlev for it, while representing himself to Rybolovlev as acting purely as a trusted agent rather than as an independent counterparty profiting from the spread. The litigation, which dragged on for years across courts in Switzerland, Monaco, Singapore, and the United States, never produced a single clean verdict on the full scope of the alleged fraud, but it did put on public record an uncomfortable fact this book's earlier chapters on auction-house theater have already prepared the reader to expect: even the private, dealer-brokered end of the art market, ostensibly built on personal trust between a collector and an advisor rather than on any competitive bidding mechanism at all, is not immune to exactly the kind of information asymmetry and quiet self-dealing that a well-run public auction is supposed to make considerably harder to sustain. Each of these private sales functioned, in the peculiar epistemics of the art market, less as a pure economic transaction than as an additional data point supporting the attribution's credibility: a painting that sophisticated, well-advised buyers keep paying tens and then hundreds of millions of dollars for starts to acquire a kind of market-conferred authenticity that operates almost independently of, and is considerably easier for an ordinary observer to verify than, the underlying scholarly dispute about brushwork and pigment. The restoration itself, carried out over roughly a decade by the conservator Dianne Modestini, is worth dwelling on for a moment, because it sits at the exact center of the authenticity debate in a way few outside observers fully appreciated while the auction-house marketing machine was running at full speed. Modestini has spoken openly about the condition she found: a face she described as grotesquely repainted, curls on one side of the head badly damaged by earlier, heavy-handed attempts to correct a warped panel, losses in the forehead and around one eye likely caused by worm tunneling through the centuries-old wood support, and a muddy, clumsily repainted background bearing little resemblance to what conservators believed the original composition would have looked like. To bring the panel back to something resembling a coherent, exhibitable painting, Modestini had to reconstruct substantial passages of flesh tone and detail by hand, working in a technique deliberately modeled on Leonardo's own layered glazing methods so that her additions would blend convincingly with whatever original paint survived underneath. The scale of that reconstruction became, in its own right, a point of genuine controversy within the art world: a joke that circulated among skeptical critics held that the *Salvator Mundi* was less a Renaissance painting than a contemporary one, on the grounds that roughly ninety percent of its visible surface had, in some sense, been painted within the previous decade rather than five hundred years earlier, and some conservators and historians argued publicly that Modestini's restoration had gone beyond repair into a kind of speculative reconstruction — critics used the pointed verb "Leonardo-ized" — that made the final public image considerably more confidently "Leonardo" in appearance than the fragmentary original evidence could actually support. None of this technical debate about brushwork percentages and restoration ethics made it into Christie's marketing campaign in any prominent way. The video of hushed, awestruck visitors said nothing about worm tunneling, or muddy overpaint, or the specific percentage of the visible image that had been reconstructed by a single conservator's own hand rather than inherited unbroken, intact, and untouched from the Renaissance across five centuries of wars, fires, careless previous owners, and at least one documented earlier attempt at repair that had done the panel more harm than good.

By the time Christie's took the consignment for its November 2017 evening sale, the house had a genuinely difficult marketing and pricing problem on its hands: how do you set expectations, in a printed catalogue and pre-sale estimate, for an object whose comparable-sales universe is essentially empty, since there was no meaningful precedent for what a rediscovered, if contested, Leonardo should fetch at public auction, the artist's other paintings being almost entirely locked away in museum collections that will plainly never sell them.

Christie's answer to that problem was a masterclass in the kind of auction-house theater this book's third chapter described in more general terms, deployed here at a scale the London saleroom of 1744 could never have imagined. Rather than categorize the painting within a traditional Old Masters sale, where its most natural scholarly peer group would have placed it and where buyer expectations would have been shaped by a long, well-understood history of Renaissance and Baroque prices, Christie's slotted *Salvator Mundi* into its postwar and contemporary art evening sale — the category commanding, by that point in the art market's history, the single highest ceiling prices and the deepest pool of extremely wealthy, competitively minded global buyers, collectors more accustomed to bidding nine figures for a Warhol or a Basquiat than for a five-hundred-year-old panel. The house produced a lavish, cinematic marketing campaign built around the painting, including a widely circulated promotional video showing wealthy visitors gazing at the work in hushed, quasi-religious awe, and staged the physical object on a pre-sale world tour through Christie's Hong Kong, London, San Francisco, and New York galleries, drawing enormous public queues and international press coverage that had almost nothing to do with the traditional, specialist Old Masters audience and everything to do with generating the kind of broad cultural momentum a contemporary-art blockbuster commands. Christie's had, in effect, decided that the painting's greatest asset was not really its disputed attribution at all, but its story — rediscovered masterpiece, near-miraculous survival, near-priceless religious subject, near-mythical creator — and it built a sale designed to let that story, rather than any narrow scholarly consensus, set the emotional register the room would be bidding inside.

The guarantee this book's opening chapter mentioned did real, quantifiable work inside that emotional register, and it is worth returning to with the fuller vocabulary this book has since developed. Christie's structured a financing arrangement, reported at the time to be in the neighborhood of $100 million, ensuring the consignor would receive that sum regardless of how the live bidding actually went — protecting the seller entirely from the risk of an unsold or disappointingly cheap lot, and simultaneously signaling to every bidder in the room, before a single hand was raised, that the house itself was sufficiently confident in the painting's drawing power to underwrite a nine-figure floor. That signal is not neutral information; it is itself a form of the anchoring this book's third chapter described pre-sale estimates as providing, except deployed at a scale and with a specificity — a guaranteed nine-figure floor, not merely a printed estimate range — that no ordinary lot in Sotheby's or Christie's history had ever carried. A bidder walking into that room already knew the painting would sell for at least nine figures no matter what happened; the only live question was how many additional figures a genuinely uncertain, thinly precedented common value, combined with two extraordinarily determined bidders, might add on top.

It is worth placing the eventual $450.3 million figure against the broader art market's own actual scale, because the sum is large enough to distort most people's intuitive sense of proportion even within a market already accustomed to extraordinary numbers. Annual industry surveys compiled by the economist Clare McAndrew for Art Basel and UBS have put total global art market sales — every gallery transaction, every dealer sale, and every public auction combined, across every country and every price tier from emerging local artists to blue-chip masterworks — at figures generally in the range of $55 to $68 billion in recent years, meaning a single painting's hammer price represented, in the year it sold, something on the order of one-tenth of one percent of the entire worldwide art trade's annual total. Public auction sales specifically, the segment *Salvator Mundi* belonged to rather than the larger private-dealer market, have typically made up a meaningfully smaller share of that global total than dealer sales, which makes the painting's single evening at Christie's an even larger outlier relative to its own specific market segment than the headline global figure alone would suggest. Numbers at this scale do real, if temporary, work reshaping an entire market's sense of what is possible: several art-market analysts writing in the sale's aftermath noted that comparable Old Master and Renaissance-adjacent works saw renewed collector interest and, in some cases, meaningfully higher subsequent estimates, evidence that a single, spectacular auction result can shift an entire category's baseline pricing expectations even when, as this chapter has shown at length, the underlying object's true value remains every bit as genuinely contested after the sale as it was before it ever began.

What actually happened over the following nineteen minutes has been reconstructed, argued over, and picked apart by journalists and art-market analysts more thoroughly than almost any other single auction lot in modern history, and the reconstructions converge on a picture that fits this book's earlier chapters almost too neatly to be coincidental. Bidding opened at $70 million and climbed, in increments that grew as the number grew, past the $100 million guarantee within the first few minutes — meaning the painting had already cleared its guaranteed floor and every subsequent bid represented pure incremental appetite from the room rather than the guarantor's protected downside. Several bidders participated in the early rounds, competing in increments large enough that outside observers described an audible sense of shock rippling through the room each time the number cleared another hundred-million-dollar threshold, a physical, collective reaction to a rising price of exactly the kind Sotheby's and Christie's had spent two and a half centuries learning to cultivate as a commercial asset in its own right. By the time the price passed $300 million, the field had narrowed to two determined telephone bidders, each relayed through a Christie's specialist rather than bidding directly, their identities never publicly confirmed by the house and reported, with varying degrees of confidence, by different journalists in the months that followed — one widely repeated account eventually linked the winning bid to a Saudi prince bidding, according to later reporting, on behalf of Abu Dhabi's Department of Culture and Tourism, which confirmed itself as the ultimate buyer, on behalf of the Louvre Abu Dhabi, roughly a month after the sale. At $450 million, one of the two remaining bidders stopped. Pylkkänen brought the hammer down at that figure; with Christie's buyer's premium included, the final recorded price came to $450.3 million, more than four times the painting's pre-sale low estimate and more than double the previous auction record for any work of art — a 2015 sale of a Picasso that had itself closed at roughly $179.4 million, a figure the *Salvator Mundi* sale eclipsed so decisively that no subsequent auction result has come remotely close to challenging it in the years since. A 2025 Sotheby's sale of a Gustav Klimt portrait passed $236 million, becoming a genuinely significant modern record in its own right and drawing considerable press attention as the second-highest price any artwork had ever fetched at auction, yet it still closed at barely half the *Salvator Mundi*'s own total, leaving Leonardo's disputed panel sitting, nearly a decade later, in a price category no other painting has approached. Even the identity of the guarantor who protected Christie's own downside on the sale was never officially confirmed by the house, in keeping with the confidentiality this book's third chapter described as standard practice across the modern guarantee system; one prominent art-market journalist later identified the guarantor as Pierre Chen, a Taiwanese electronics-manufacturing billionaire known in the trade as one of the auction world's most active and successful backers of exactly this kind of high-value guarantee, though Christie's itself never publicly verified the claim one way or the other. Whoever held the guarantee walked away, if the identification is accurate, with a considerable profit share on top of their original fee, since the room's actual bidding cleared the $100 million floor by a factor of more than four before the gavel ever fell — a reminder that even the financial backstop meant to protect a seller from catastrophe can itself become one of an auction's more lucrative, if deliberately obscured, positions to hold.

The aftermath has done at least as much to shape this book's argument as the sale itself, because it supplies a rare, unusually well-documented natural experiment in what happens to a common-value auction's outcome once the underlying valuation dispute keeps running in public long after the gavel has fallen. The painting largely disappeared from public view after its sale, reportedly stored rather than displayed at the Louvre Abu Dhabi as originally announced, its planned unveiling repeatedly postponed for reasons the museum has never fully explained; some of the scholars who had never accepted the full Leonardo attribution took the painting's prolonged public absence as tacit, if unstated, confirmation of their own skepticism, while defenders of the attribution pointed to ordinary logistical and conservation considerations as an equally plausible explanation requiring no scandal at all. Art historians and journalists have continued publishing competing accounts of the painting's technical examination, its restoration history, and the strength of the documentary evidence connecting it to Leonardo's own workshop, with no single, universally accepted resolution to the underlying attribution question emerging in the years since. The $450.3 million figure, whatever else it represents, was never really a market's confident verdict on the painting's authenticity. It was the price two extraordinarily wealthy bidders were willing to pay, on one particular November evening, under the specific emotional and informational conditions Christie's had spent months carefully engineering, for the right to own an object whose true nature nobody in that room, including the specialists who had spent years studying it, could state with full certainty. That is not a flaw unique to this one sale. It is, this book has been arguing since its opening pages, close to the honest definition of what any common-value auction actually manufactures: not a discovered truth waiting patiently to be found, but a price, arrived at under real pressure, by real people who each genuinely knew less than they needed to know and bid substantial sums of real money anyway.
