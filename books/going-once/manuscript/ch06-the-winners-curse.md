# The Winner's Curse

In the early 1970s, three practicing petroleum engineers working for Atlantic Richfield — Arco, one of the largest oil companies operating in the Gulf of Mexico — sat down to figure out why their employer, and seemingly every other major oil company bidding on offshore drilling leases, kept losing money on tracts they had won at auction. It was not an abstract question. The federal government's Outer Continental Shelf leasing program auctioned the right to drill specific undersea tracts through sealed first-price bidding, and Arco had been aggressive enough in one earlier sale that it won essentially everything it bid on, a result that sounds, on its face, like a triumph and turned out instead to put the company under real budgetary strain for the following two years, because a great many of the tracts it had won produced far less oil, relative to what Arco had paid for them, than its own geologists had projected before the sale. Edward Capen, Robert Clapp, and William Campbell published their explanation in the *Journal of Petroleum Technology* in June 1971, in a paper titled, with the flat understatement common to engineering journals, "Competitive Bidding in High-Risk Situations." The explanation they offered has since become one of the most widely cited and most consequential findings in the economics of auctions, under a name so evocative it has migrated well beyond economics into ordinary business vocabulary: the winner's curse.

The mechanism is easiest to see by first setting aside auctions entirely and considering a simpler thought experiment. Imagine a large glass jar full of coins, and suppose a hundred people are each asked to estimate, just by looking at the jar, how much money it contains. No individual estimate is likely to be exactly right; some people will guess too high, some too low, and the average of all hundred guesses, in a well-known finding from the study of collective judgment, will often land remarkably close to the true total, because individual errors in each direction tend to cancel out across a large enough crowd. Now change the setup slightly: instead of averaging every guess, award the jar to whoever guessed the *highest* number. That single change transforms an exercise in collective wisdom into a trap. The person who wins is not the person whose estimate was most accurate. It is the person whose estimate happened to be the most wildly optimistic, drawn from the extreme upper tail of the distribution of guesses rather than its well-calibrated center. If the average guess was accurate, the winning guess — by construction, the single highest number out of a hundred — was almost certainly too high, and the winner has just paid an inflated price for a jar of coins actually worth considerably less than what they bid.

Oil-lease auctions replicate this exact structure with a specific technical feature economists call a common-value setting. The amount of oil actually sitting beneath a given offshore tract is, in principle, one true number, the same for every bidder — nobody's geology is different because of who owns the lease — but no bidder knows that number with certainty in advance, and different oil companies' geological surveys, seismic data, and internal models produce different estimates of it, some too optimistic, some too pessimistic, clustered loosely around the true figure the way the hundred jar-guessers' estimates were. When several such companies submit sealed bids based on their own private estimates, the tract is awarded to whichever company happened to produce the single highest estimate — not the most accurate one, the highest one — and if the field of bidders is large enough and their individual estimates are honestly, independently derived rather than all suspiciously inflated together, the winning bid is, on average, systematically too high relative to the tract's true value. Capen, Clapp, and Campbell showed that a bidding company ignoring this dynamic — treating its own geological estimate as simply correct, and bidding some straightforward fraction of it without adjusting for the fact that *winning the auction is itself informative*, since winning means your estimate beat everyone else's — would predictably lose money over a large enough number of auctions, even though each individual bid, viewed in isolation and without the benefit of hindsight, might have looked perfectly reasonable at the time it was made. Their prescription, genuinely radical for its era among practicing petroleum engineers more comfortable with geology than game theory, was that a rational bidder needed to shade its bid down, sometimes substantially, specifically to compensate for the fact that winning would itself be evidence the bidder's estimate was too optimistic — an idea now taught, in more general and more mathematically formal terms, in essentially every serious graduate course on auction theory offered anywhere in the world today.

The winner's curse is, importantly, not a claim that bidders behave irrationally in some crude sense, and later theoretical work went to some lengths to make that distinction precise. A fully rational bidder who correctly reasons through the logic above — who explicitly accounts for the fact that winning conveys unfavorable information about their own estimate's accuracy, and adjusts their bid downward accordingly — should not fall prey to the curse at all, at least not systematically; the phenomenon Capen, Clapp, and Campbell documented was less "rational people are cursed to overpay" than "naive people who fail to reason through this specific piece of statistical logic reliably overpay, and a great many otherwise sophisticated bidders turn out to be naive in exactly this way." That distinction matters because it explains why the winner's curse has proven so durable a finding across decades of subsequent research, despite the fact that virtually every professional bidder in an affected market has, by now, had ample opportunity to learn about it: adjusting correctly for the curse requires a genuinely counterintuitive piece of conditional reasoning — treating the very fact of victory as bad news about your own judgment — that experimental economists have repeatedly found people struggle to internalize even after having the logic carefully explained to them, and even after losing money to the pattern themselves.

Twelve years after Capen, Clapp, and Campbell's paper, two researchers named Max Bazerman and William Samuelson decided to test whether the winner's curse was really a quirk of specialized industry expertise, confined to oil executives reasoning about geology they only partly understood, or something that would show up in an ordinary classroom the moment you handed people an auction to run. In 1983, they brought jars of coins and paper clips into a dozen microeconomics classes for MBA students at Boston University — a jar of eight hundred pennies here, a jar of a hundred sixty nickels there, jars of large and small paper clips valued at four cents and two cents apiece — with each jar's true contents worth exactly eight dollars, a figure the students were never told. Students first wrote down their own private estimate of each jar's value, then submitted a sealed bid to buy it, with the highest bidder winning the jar at the price they had bid. The results reproduced Capen, Clapp, and Campbell's oil-patch finding almost exactly, in a room full of graduate business students rather than petroleum engineers: the average *estimate* across all the students, for all the jars, came in at $5.13 — too low, but a reasonable, unbiased-looking guess in the aggregate, off by less than three dollars from the true value of eight. The average *winning bid*, by contrast, came in at $10.01, meaning the students who actually won a jar had, on average, paid $2.01 more than the jar was worth. The class as a whole had underestimated the jars' value. The individual winners, drawn from the optimistic tail of that same class, had overpaid for the privilege of being wrong in the most expensive direction. Bazerman and Samuelson's explanation for why bidders kept losing money even once informed of the phenomenon in advance — a version of the experiment they reran with subjects explicitly warned about the winner's curse beforehand — found that warning alone barely dented the effect, because knowing a trap exists in the abstract turns out to be a poor substitute for correctly discounting your own specific, personally felt certainty that your own estimate this time is the accurate one.

The finding did not stay confined to oil leases for long, and its spread across wildly different markets over the following half-century is the strongest evidence available that Capen, Clapp, and Campbell had identified something structural about common-value auctions generally, rather than a quirk specific to petroleum geology. Art and wine auctions have produced some of the most carefully studied evidence outside the oil patch: economists analyzing repeat-sale wine auction data have documented winner's-curse-consistent patterns in which winning bids for wines whose eventual quality was genuinely uncertain at the time of sale correlated with subsequent disappointment once bottles were actually opened and tasted, exactly the pattern the theory predicts when buyers are bidding on a shared, not-yet-fully-revealed underlying quality rather than a purely private, subjective taste. Corporate acquisitions supply another well-documented arena: when several potential acquirers compete to buy the same target company, each basing its bid on its own analysts' estimate of the target's future cash flows — a number that is, again, in principle one true figure rather than something that varies legitimately from bidder to bidder — the winning acquirer is, on average, the one whose analysts were most optimistic, and a substantial body of finance research has linked competitively won acquisitions to systematically worse post-acquisition returns than acquisitions completed without a bidding war, a pattern sometimes labeled, only half-jokingly, "the winner's curse in M&A."

Professional sports free agency turned out to be another rich, if unglamorous, testing ground, and the sports-economics literature that grew up around it makes the point with unusual clarity because the "auction" in question is informal — competing teams do not submit literal sealed bids to a league official — while the underlying structural logic remains identical. When a talented player becomes a free agent, every interested team's front office produces its own internal estimate of how much future value that player will deliver, based on scouting reports, statistical projections, and medical evaluations that vary in quality and optimism from team to team; the player then, in effect, signs with whichever team produced the single most optimistic estimate, since that team can offer the largest contract. Economists studying Major League Baseball free-agent signings across multiple decades have found a persistent pattern consistent with exactly this dynamic: teams systematically overpay for free agents relative to the statistical production those players go on to deliver, with the effect concentrated among players whose recent performance was hardest to evaluate cleanly, and researchers have specifically flagged the tendency of clubs to pay largely for a player's most recent, most visible seasons rather than for a more skeptical, aging-adjusted forecast of what those same players are actually likely to produce over the life of a multi-year contract. The pattern recurs across other leagues and other sports economists have studied, and it recurs for a reason this book keeps returning to: whenever the object being bid on has one genuine, shared future value that different bidders are independently, imperfectly trying to forecast, and the highest forecaster wins, the winner's curse is not a possibility to guard against. It is closer to a mathematical default the winning side has to actively work to avoid.

The 1988 battle for RJR Nabisco remains the textbook illustration of how an M&A bidding war can spiral through exactly this dynamic in full public view, chronicled in enough contemporaneous detail, including the bestselling account *Barbarians at the Gate*, that the escalation is unusually easy to trace step by step. RJR Nabisco's own chief executive, Ross Johnson, opened the contest in October 1988 with a management-led buyout offer of $75 a share, valuing the tobacco and food conglomerate at roughly $17 billion; once the company's board opted to solicit competing bids rather than accept quietly, the private-equity firm Kohlberg Kravis Roberts entered with $90 a share, and the two sides spent the following weeks trading escalating offers in full view of the financial press, with Johnson's group eventually reaching $112 a share and a late, aggressive entrant, First Boston, briefly floating a bid as high as $118. KKR's final, winning offer came in at $109 a share, valuing the deal at roughly $25 billion and making it, at the time, the largest leveraged buyout in history. The subsequent, extensively documented history of RJR Nabisco under KKR's ownership was not a happy one for the winning bidder: the acquired company struggled for years under the enormous debt load the leveraged buyout had placed on it, and KKR's own return on the deal, by most later retrospective accounts, fell well short of what the firm's own investment models had projected when the bidding was still climbing — a real-world instance of exactly the pattern this chapter's earlier discussion of common-value acquisitions predicts, in which the winning bid in a competitive takeover auction correlates with a bidder whose own valuation model happened to be the most optimistic in the room, rather than the most accurate — a pattern KKR's own investors would spend much of the following decade living with directly, as the debt-laden company worked its way through years of restructuring before the private-equity firm eventually exited the investment at a return well short of what its own models had projected back when the bidding war was still climbing toward $109 a share.

Initial public offerings of stock supply perhaps the most elegant and most consequential extension of the idea, developed by the finance economist Kevin Rock in a 1986 paper that recast the winner's curse as a problem not between competing bidders but between two different *kinds* of investor. Rock's model divides IPO investors into two groups: informed investors, who have done real research and can tell a promising offering from a mediocre one, and uninformed investors, who cannot make that distinction and simply apply for shares in every IPO that comes to market, informed ones and mediocre ones alike. Informed investors, precisely because they can tell the difference, concentrate their applications on the genuinely promising offerings and skip the mediocre ones. That selective behavior means an uninformed investor applying indiscriminately for every IPO ends up receiving a *disproportionately large allocation* of shares in exactly the offerings the informed investors avoided — the mediocre ones, where there was less competition for shares — and a disproportionately *small* allocation, rationed away by informed competition, in the genuinely promising ones. The uninformed investor is, in other words, cursed by the same structural logic as the oil-lease bidder: they tend to "win" more shares precisely in the deals they should least want to win. Rock's insight was that this dynamic explains a long-documented and, before his paper, poorly understood puzzle in finance: IPOs are, on average, priced below the level at which they begin trading once public, handing early investors a predictable first-day gain, and Rock argued that issuers and their underwriting banks had to price offerings this way specifically to keep uninformed investors in the market at all, compensating them, in effect, for a winner's curse they would otherwise systematically suffer. Subsequent empirical work, including studies of IPO rationing data from several national markets, has found real support for the pattern Rock's theory predicts, even as later researchers have identified additional forces — underwriter reputation concerns, deliberate promotional pricing strategies, straightforward market sentiment — that also help explain IPO underpricing and complicate any claim that the winner's curse is the whole story.

It is worth pausing on why the winner's curse specifically requires a common-value setting to operate, because the distinction clarifies which real-world auctions are genuinely vulnerable to it and which are not. The *Salvator Mundi* sale this book opened with was, in the economic sense, closer to a private-value auction: what the winning bidder was willing to pay reflected that bidder's own idiosyncratic desire to own the most expensive painting in the world, a desire that had little to do with any objectively "correct" underlying value the painting possessed and that other bidders did not need to share or estimate accurately for their own valuations to be perfectly sensible. A private-value auction of that kind is largely immune to the winner's curse, because there is no single hidden "true value" that a bidder's win reveals unfavorable information about; each bidder's valuation is genuinely their own, and winning does not imply anyone was wrong about anything. Oil leases, corporate acquisitions, and, as later chapters of this book will show, radio-spectrum licenses and search-advertising keywords sit much closer to the common-value end of the spectrum, because the underlying value being bid on — barrels of recoverable oil, a company's actual future cash flows, the advertising revenue a given search term will generate — is a real, shared quantity that every bidder is independently trying to estimate, with genuinely different degrees of accuracy, which is exactly the condition under which the curse takes hold. Most real auctions, sensibly enough, sit somewhere between these two poles rather than at either extreme, containing both a private element, reflecting each bidder's own idiosyncratic taste or strategic position, and a common element, reflecting some genuinely shared underlying value that everyone is estimating with imperfect and differing accuracy — which is precisely why sophisticated modern auction designers, as later chapters show in detail, have had to build bidding formats robust enough to handle both kinds of value simultaneously, rather than assuming away the harder, common-value half of the problem.

One company took Rock's diagnosis seriously enough to try engineering around it directly, in a move that gives this chapter an unusually concrete, real-world test of the underlying theory. When Google went public in August 2004, the company deliberately rejected the traditional IPO process this chapter has just described — investment banks setting a price, favored institutional clients receiving preferential allocations, and a predictable first-day price pop that Rock's model explains as compensation for the winner's-curse risk uninformed investors otherwise bear — in favor of a modified Dutch auction, letting any investor, large or small, submit a bid specifying both a price and a quantity of shares, with underwriters then setting a single clearing price at or above which all the shares would actually sell. Google's own founders framed the choice explicitly in terms of fairness and broad access, writing in the company's public offering documents that they wanted a process inclusive of small investors alongside the large institutions traditional IPOs typically favor, and the auction format was, in substantial part, a direct attempt to let the market itself set the price through open competitive bidding rather than leaving that decision to underwriters allocating scarce, underpriced shares to favored clients. The choice unsettled much of Wall Street at the time; several major investment banks reportedly expressed real discomfort with ceding pricing authority to an open auction rather than their own traditional bookbuilding process, and commentary on the offering in the years since has remained genuinely divided over how well the experiment actually worked, with some analysts crediting it for closing much of the traditional first-day underpricing gap and others noting that Google, and the small number of companies that later attempted similar auction-based offerings, remained a clear minority against an IPO industry that continued overwhelmingly favoring the traditional, underwriter-directed process Rock's original 1986 model had set out to explain in the first place.

There is a useful, if slightly deflating, coda to the oil-lease story that rarely makes it into the popular retellings of Capen, Clapp, and Campbell's discovery, and it is worth including because it keeps the finding honest. Later economists revisiting decades of actual Outer Continental Shelf lease results found the picture more mixed than the original paper's dramatic framing suggested: some leasing rounds showed clear evidence of aggregate industry overpayment consistent with an uncorrected winner's curse, while others showed oil companies, collectively, earning perfectly reasonable average returns once enough time had passed for firms to learn and adjust their bidding practices in response to earlier losses. That mixed record is not a refutation of the underlying theory. It is closer to confirmation of a second, related idea this book will keep encountering: markets often correct for known biases over time, as participants who lose money repeatedly either adapt their behavior or exit the market entirely, which means a well-documented psychological trap can be real, well-understood, mathematically provable, and still only partially visible in aggregate industry outcomes a few decades after the relevant lesson has had time to spread through an entire profession's institutional memory.

The most important practical lesson from half a century of winner's-curse research, and the one this book carries forward into its discussion of far more modern auctions, is not that common-value auctions are dangerous and should be avoided. It is that a well-designed common-value auction has to actively account for the curse, on both sides of the transaction, rather than pretending it does not exist. Sophisticated bidders learn, sometimes through expensive experience like Arco's, sometimes through formal training grounded directly in the Capen-Clapp-Campbell paper and its many academic descendants, to shade their bids in proportion to how many rivals are competing and how uncertain their own private estimate really is — the more bidders in the room, and the murkier the underlying value, the more aggressively a rational bidder should discount their own number before submitting it. Sophisticated auction *designers*, for their part, learned something equally important from the same body of research: that formats which let bidders observe some information about rivals' behavior during the auction, rather than bidding entirely blind in a single sealed round, can genuinely help bidders calibrate their estimates in real time and mitigate the curse's worst effects — one of several reasons, as the chapters ahead will show, that the economists eventually hired to design the century's largest and most consequential auction chose an ascending, information-revealing format over a sealed-bid one, even though sealed bidding might, at first glance, have looked like the simpler and more efficient choice.
