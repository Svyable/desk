# Selling the Air

For most of the twentieth century, the United States federal government gave away one of the nation's single most valuable natural resources for free, or close enough to it that the distinction barely mattered. Licenses to broadcast on specific slices of the radio spectrum — the finite range of electromagnetic frequencies that carries every radio signal, television broadcast, and eventually every wireless phone call and Wi-Fi connection through the air — were allocated by the Federal Communications Commission through a process called comparative hearings, commonly nicknamed "beauty contests." Applicants for a broadcast license submitted proposals describing their qualifications, their programming plans, and their commitment to serving the public interest, and FCC administrative law judges evaluated competing applications on paper, weighing factors that were notoriously vague, slow to resolve, and vulnerable to political influence and outright gaming. A determined applicant could tie up a license for years by filing competing applications purely to extract a payoff from a rival willing to pay for the delay to end, and cases genuinely took years, sometimes over a decade, to work through the FCC's administrative process before a winner was finally declared — years during which the spectrum in question typically sat unused, generating no revenue for the government and no service for the public it was nominally being allocated to serve. By the 1980s, Congress had grown frustrated enough with the comparative-hearing process's delays and perceived arbitrariness that it authorized the FCC to allocate some licenses instead by lottery, a reform that solved the speed problem and the gaming problem simultaneously but created an entirely new one: a lottery, by definition, allocates a scarce and valuable resource without any regard whatsoever to which applicant actually values it most or would use it most productively, handing some winners a license worth millions of dollars for no reason beyond the luck of a random drawing, a windfall several lottery winners promptly captured by reselling licenses they had never intended to use for broadcasting at all.

The economist Ronald Coase had, decades earlier, proposed the obvious alternative, in a 1959 paper arguing that the FCC should simply auction spectrum licenses to the highest bidder rather than allocating them through administrative fiat or chance — a proposal so far outside the regulatory conventional wisdom of its era that it was reportedly greeted with considerable skepticism by commissioners who could not readily imagine treating the public airwaves as an ordinary commodity to be sold like oil leases or wheat futures on an open exchange. It took roughly three and a half decades, a great deal of academic and legislative groundwork, and the looming commercial urgency of the new cellular telephone industry — a genuinely new use for spectrum, with genuinely deep-pocketed corporate bidders who badly wanted licenses and were tired of years-long beauty contests standing between them and a functioning national wireless network — before Congress finally authorized the FCC to conduct spectrum auctions, in legislation passed in 1993. The commission then faced an entirely unprecedented practical problem: nobody had ever designed and run an auction remotely like the one Congress had just ordered it to hold. The FCC was not proposing to sell a single item, the way Christie's sells one painting at a time, nor a large but essentially interchangeable stack of identical items, the way Aalsmeer sells identical bundles of tulips. It needed to sell dozens, eventually hundreds, of distinct but interrelated licenses simultaneously — different geographic regions, different frequency bands, licenses whose value to any given bidder depended intricately on which *other* licenses that same bidder also managed to win, since a wireless carrier's value from owning spectrum in Chicago depends partly on whether it also holds compatible spectrum in the surrounding suburbs and the cities its customers travel to.

The FCC turned to academic economists to solve that problem, in one of the more consequential examples in modern history of pure economic theory being translated, under real time pressure and with real money at stake, directly into government policy. Two rival design proposals emerged from Stanford economists working roughly in parallel: Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson developed one approach, while Preston McAfee, then at the University of Texas and working under contract for the cellular carrier AirTouch, developed an independently derived but strikingly similar alternative. Both converged on the same core mechanism, building directly on the theoretical foundations this book's earlier chapters traced back to Vickrey and forward through Myerson: a simultaneous multiple-round ascending auction, in which every license being offered is up for bid at the same time, in a series of successive rounds, rather than being auctioned one at a time in sequence. In each round, bidders could place new bids on any license they were interested in, prices rose only on licenses that had actually attracted a new competing bid, and the auction continued running, round after round, until an entire round passed with no new bids placed on any license in the whole auction — at which point every license was simultaneously and finally awarded to whoever held the standing high bid on it. The design's central innovation was letting all the auctions run together rather than one after another: a bidder chasing a Chicago license could watch, in real time, how the competing bidding for the surrounding Illinois suburban licenses was going, and adjust its own Chicago strategy accordingly, something a series of sequential single-license auctions, run one after another over weeks or months, could never have allowed.

The FCC, remarkably for a large federal regulatory agency facing a genuinely novel and untested proposal, agreed to run with the design essentially as the economists had sketched it, and the first simultaneous ascending spectrum auction in the world went live in July 1994, selling ten narrowband nationwide paging licenses over forty-seven rounds of bidding and raising $617 million — a result that, by the standards of the beauty-contest era it replaced, looked almost unbelievable, since those same licenses would previously have been allocated to whichever applicant wrote the most persuasive public-interest essay, generating no direct revenue for the Treasury at all. The real test came a few months later, in the FCC's broadband personal communications services auction beginning in December 1994, offering ninety-nine licenses covering the spectrum that would underpin the coming generation of digital cellular phone service across the country's major metropolitan markets. That auction ran for months, through round after round of careful, strategic bidding by every major telecommunications company in the country, and closed having raised approximately $7 billion for the U.S. Treasury — a sum that dwarfed even optimistic pre-auction projections and instantly transformed spectrum auctions from an academic curiosity into one of the most politically popular pieces of regulatory innovation in modern American government, a mechanism that simultaneously allocated a scarce public resource to the bidders who valued it most, did so far faster than the old comparative-hearing process ever had, and handed the federal government a multi-billion-dollar windfall in the process. Economists writing retrospective assessments of the early FCC auctions, including detailed analyses by Milgrom, Wilson, and their collaborators published within a few years of the first sales, generally judged the design a substantial success on its own stated terms: licenses were going to bidders who valued them highly enough to pay for them, the process ran in weeks or months rather than years, and the federal government was, for the first time, capturing a meaningful share of the enormous private value locked up in the public airwaves rather than giving that value away.

The design's theoretical elegance rested heavily on features this book's earlier chapters have already introduced, adapted to a genuinely harder problem than any single-item auction Vickrey or Myerson had originally analyzed. Because bidding proceeded gradually across many rounds rather than closing in a single sealed-bid instant, bidders could watch prices rise on the specific combination of licenses they cared about and update their own private valuations in something close to real time — a partial antidote, though never a complete one, to the winner's-curse dynamics the earlier chapter on oil leases described, since a bidder who saw rivals bidding unexpectedly aggressively on a given license could take that as a genuine signal that other well-informed parties saw more value in it than the bidder's own initial estimate had assumed, and adjust accordingly, rather than committing blind to a single irreversible sealed number. The design also had to solve problems no single-item English auction had ever needed to address at all: how to prevent a large, well-capitalized bidder from strategically threatening to drive up prices on licenses it did not actually want, purely to punish or exhaust a rival competing for a different license entirely; how to structure minimum bid increments large enough to keep the auction moving through its many rounds at a reasonable pace without so large that they distorted efficient price discovery; and how to give smaller, less capitalized bidders — regional carriers and minority-owned businesses Congress had specifically directed the FCC to ensure meaningful auction access for — a genuine chance to compete against telecommunications giants with vastly deeper pockets, which the FCC addressed through bidding credits and set-aside provisions layered on top of the core auction mechanism, provisions that generated their own, ongoing controversies about whether they achieved their intended goal or were themselves gamed by sophisticated bidders structuring joint ventures specifically to qualify for credits meant for genuinely disadvantaged businesses.

The FCC's own most consequential early misstep grew directly out of one of these small-business provisions, and the resulting saga occupied federal courts, including eventually the United States Supreme Court, for the better part of a decade. Two of the licensing blocks in the mid-1990s broadband PCS auctions, designated the C-block and F-block, were reserved specifically for small businesses and other qualifying "designated entities," with successful bidders permitted to pay for their winning licenses in installments over time rather than in a lump sum up front — a genuinely reasonable accommodation, in principle, for capital-constrained smaller companies that might otherwise be shut out of competing against telecommunications giants able to write a check immediately. A company called NextWave Personal Communications won sixty-three C-block licenses in the 1996 auction for a combined bid of $4.74 billion, a staggering sum for a company relying on the installment structure specifically because it could not otherwise finance a purchase of that scale, and NextWave, like several other C-block winners, soon found itself unable to keep up with its payment schedule as the broader telecommunications sector's financing conditions deteriorated in the years that followed. When the FCC moved to reclaim the licenses over the missed payments, NextWave filed for bankruptcy protection and fought the commission through years of litigation, eventually winning a Supreme Court ruling in 2003 that the FCC could not simply cancel a bankrupt company's spectrum licenses to collect a straightforward debt the way an ordinary creditor might. The episode left billions of dollars in spectrum tied up in legal limbo for years, sitting essentially unused by anyone while courts worked through the dispute — precisely the kind of protracted, unproductive delay the entire auction reform had been designed to eliminate in the first place, arriving instead through a well-intentioned financing accommodation nobody had adequately stress-tested against a genuine industry downturn.

The scale of what these auctions eventually accomplished, measured simply in dollars raised for public coffers, is worth stating plainly rather than left as an abstraction. Successive rounds of FCC spectrum auctions through the following decades raised many tens of billions of dollars in cumulative proceeds for the United States Treasury, a trajectory that kept climbing rather than plateauing as demand for wireless capacity grew: a 2014 sale of spectrum for advanced wireless services raised a then-record $44.9 billion, and the FCC's 2020-2021 C-band auction, offering mid-band spectrum specifically suited to the next generation of 5G networks, shattered that record entirely, closing with gross winning bids of roughly $81.1 billion across twenty-one winning bidders and 5,684 individual licenses — the largest gross proceeds any spectrum auction had ever produced anywhere, three decades after Ronald Coase's original 1959 proposal had been greeted with open skepticism by regulators who could not imagine treating the public airwaves as an ordinary commodity at all. funding that flowed toward deficit reduction and, in various legislative allocations over the years, toward specific public priorities including first-responder communications infrastructure. It is worth pausing, before moving on, to register just how large a leap the FCC's decision represented, measured against everything this book has covered up to this point. Every auction format examined so far — Sotheby's rising bid, Aalsmeer's falling clock, a sealed government procurement envelope — had been designed to sell one item, or a run of essentially identical items, at a time. The FCC needed a mechanism robust enough to let hundreds of distinct, interdependent licenses find their efficient allocation simultaneously, among bidders whose true valuations for any single license depended in complicated ways on which other licenses those same bidders also won, all resolved through a process transparent and defensible enough to survive inevitable legal and political challenge from the many billions of dollars in losing bids it would generate. That the mechanism worked as well as it did, on its first genuinely large-scale real-world test, and continued working well enough to survive three subsequent decades of iteration, expansion, and international replication, remains one of the strongest pieces of evidence available anywhere in applied economics that auction theory, for all its abstraction, is not merely an academic parlor game. It is engineering, tested against real money, real corporate strategy, and a real federal government with genuine political incentives to make the whole thing fail embarrassingly if the design had been badly flawed.

Not every government took the lesson, and the clearest evidence of what the auction reform actually replaced comes from a country that spent 2008 doing precisely what the FCC had abandoned fifteen years earlier. India's telecommunications ministry that year allocated a large batch of second-generation mobile spectrum licenses not through any competitive auction at all, but through a first-come-first-served administrative process reminiscent of the American beauty-contest era this chapter opened with, under a telecom minister who set prices well below what independent estimates suggested a genuinely competitive sale would have produced. India's own government auditor, the Comptroller and Auditor General, later estimated the resulting loss to the public exchequer at a figure so large — cited in different official estimates ranging from roughly 53,000 to nearly 140,000 crore rupees, tens of billions of dollars by any of the estimates' conversions — that the episode became known simply as the 2G scam, precipitating criminal fraud charges against the responsible minister and, in February 2012, a Supreme Court ruling that cancelled 122 licenses outright and explicitly directed the government to reallocate the underlying spectrum through auction rather than administrative discretion going forward. The Indian court's own language identified the core problem in terms this chapter's readers will find immediately familiar: a scarce natural resource allocated by administrative fiat rather than competitive bidding had been priced, predictably, far below its genuine market value, with the resulting windfall captured by favored applicants rather than by the public that nominally owned the resource in the first place — the exact failure mode Ronald Coase had warned against in his own original 1959 proposal, and the exact failure mode the FCC had spent 1993 and 1994 building a genuinely working, if imperfect, alternative to.

Other countries watched the American experiment closely and, over the following years, adopted variants of the same basic simultaneous ascending design for their own spectrum sales, turning what had been, in 1993, a purely theoretical proposal from a handful of Stanford economists into the standard global template for how a modern government allocates wireless spectrum — a genuinely rare example of an applied economic theory translating, within less than a decade of its first tentative real-world test, from an obscure academic paper into a dominant, largely unquestioned, and remarkably durable worldwide regulatory practice, adopted by telecommunications regulators on nearly every inhabited continent within roughly a decade of the FCC's own first tentative, entirely untested American attempt. Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson's role in that translation was, twenty-six years later, formally recognized by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, which awarded them the 2020 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences explicitly "for improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats," a citation that named the spectrum-auction work directly among the committee's stated reasons, alongside their broader theoretical contributions — completing, as this book's fifth chapter previewed at some length, a genuinely remarkable thirteen-year run of three separate Nobel prizes traceable back to Vickrey's original 1961 insight about what happens when you change one small rule governing who pays what in a sealed auction.

Milgrom's own involvement in spectrum auction design did not end with the 2020 Nobel Prize or even with the original 1990s FCC sales; he returned to lead the design of what became, in 2016 and 2017, the single most mechanically ambitious spectrum auction the FCC had yet attempted. The broadcast incentive auction solved a genuinely new problem the earlier sales had never faced: rather than simply selling previously unassigned spectrum to new bidders, the FCC needed to simultaneously buy spectrum back from existing television broadcasters willing to give up their licenses voluntarily, in a reverse auction determining how much to pay each broadcaster to relinquish its frequencies, and then resell that same reclaimed spectrum forward to wireless carriers hungry for mobile broadband capacity, with the entire two-sided process running as a single, integrated mechanism rather than as two separate sales. Milgrom led a team of prominent auction theorists, including the Stanford economists Jonathan Levin and Ilya Segal and the University of Maryland's Lawrence Ausubel, in designing the combined mechanism, which ran from March 2016 through its final close in March 2017 and ultimately repurposed 84 megahertz of broadcast spectrum for wireless use. The auction's own revealed numbers illustrated the underlying economics with striking clarity: wireless carriers bidding forward for the reclaimed spectrum revealed a collective willingness to pay of at least $19.3 billion, while the broadcasters being bought out in the reverse auction revealed a collective cost of relinquishing their licenses of no more than $10 billion — a gap of roughly $9 billion that the auction's design captured as genuine economic surplus, created simply by moving a scarce resource from a lower-value use to a higher-value one through competitive bidding on both sides simultaneously, rather than through any government mandate or administrative reallocation.

None of this success should be read as evidence that the simultaneous ascending auction is a flawless, universally applicable design, immune to the kind of failure modes this book has been cataloguing since its opening pages, and the very next chapter exists specifically to correct any such impression. The American spectrum auctions succeeded, in substantial part, because of conditions that do not automatically hold everywhere the same basic format gets applied: a Federal Communications Commission willing to iterate the design carefully across successive auctions, learning from early mistakes; a deep, genuinely competitive field of bidders with real, independently formed valuations rather than a handful of desperate incumbents each convinced that losing meant catastrophe; and, not least, simple good fortune in the specific economic moment the earliest, most closely studied American auctions happened to catch. A very similar design, applied a few years later by a different government, facing a different competitive landscape and a very different psychological climate among its bidders, would produce a result so different in character — and so damaging to the companies that won it — that it remains, a quarter-century later, one of the standard cautionary case studies taught alongside the American auctions' success in every serious graduate course on market design.
