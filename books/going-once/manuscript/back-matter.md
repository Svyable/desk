# Back Matter

## About the Book

*Going Once* is a history of the auction as a piece of social engineering: a deliberately designed procedure for turning private, self-interested information into a public price, under competitive pressure, in front of witnesses. The book traces that procedure from a contested account in Herodotus's *Histories*, through the London saleroom trade Samuel Baker and James Christie built into permanent institutions, through William Vickrey's 1961 proof that a single rule change could make honest bidding a dominant strategy, into the Federal Communications Commission's spectrum auctions, the psychology laboratory eBay accidentally became, the digital advertising exchanges that now run more auctions in a single day than Sotheby's has run in its entire history, and the earliest infrastructure for AI agents to bid, negotiate, and transact on a person's behalf without a human reviewing each individual decision.

The book tries throughout to hold two claims in tension rather than resolving them into a simpler, more marketable story. Auctions, well designed, are one of the more remarkable pieces of social technology humans have built, reliably extracting real information from people who have no independent reason to disclose it honestly. Auctions, carelessly designed or deliberately abused, have produced some of the more embarrassing and occasionally predatory episodes in commercial history, from chandelier bidding to Swoopo's sunk-cost machine to the algorithmic rent-pricing coordination the U.S. Department of Justice sued RealPage over in 2024. Both claims are true, often about the same auction format, and a book willing to tell only one half would not really be explaining the subject.

## Notes and Sources

Every chapter of this manuscript was researched against primary and authoritative secondary sources: academic papers in economics and finance journals, government and regulatory records (including U.S. Department of Justice filings, Federal Communications Commission and Federal Reserve historical materials, and European Union and Belgian data-protection rulings), company disclosures and press releases, and contemporaneous journalism from outlets with a documented record of factual accuracy. The research trail accompanying this manuscript records, for each significant factual claim, the specific source consulted, the date it was accessed, and notes on its limitations, uncertainty, or counterevidence.

The sourcing discipline applied throughout is deliberately conservative. A disputed attribution, such as the ongoing scholarly disagreement over how much of the *Salvator Mundi* was painted by Leonardo da Vinci himself, is presented as disputed rather than resolved in either direction. A contested ancient account, such as Herodotus's description of a Babylonian marriage auction, is presented alongside the historiographical skepticism serious Assyriologists have raised about its literal accuracy. A pending or settled legal matter, such as the RealPage antitrust litigation, is described in terms of what the government alleged and what was actually resolved, rather than treated as a proven, final verdict on every disputed underlying fact. No dialogue, inner thought, or invented biographical detail appears anywhere in this manuscript; every direct quotation is drawn from a documented, cited source, and no quotation has been invented or paraphrased into quotation marks.

Several figures in this draft are time-sensitive by nature — advertising-industry market-size estimates, current corporate revenue figures, and the status of ongoing litigation and regulatory proceedings chief among them — and should be rechecked before any future release. The chapter on emerging AI-agent commerce infrastructure describes a genuinely fast-moving and still-consolidating technology landscape as of this manuscript's drafting date; readers encountering this book at a later date should treat its description of AP2, ACP, and x402 as a snapshot of an early, formative period rather than a permanent settlement of how agentic commerce ultimately came to work.

## Acknowledgments

This book depends on the economists who did the patient, often decades-unrewarded theoretical work this manuscript tries to make legible to a general reader: William Vickrey, Roger Myerson, David Gale, Lloyd Shapley, Alvin Roth, Paul Milgrom, Robert Wilson, and the many collaborators and critics who tested, extended, and occasionally overturned pieces of their work along the way. It depends equally on the journalists, court reporters, and regulators who documented the auction's failures as carefully as economists documented its theory, without whose record this book's less flattering chapters could not have been written responsibly.

Any remaining errors of fact or interpretation belong to the author.

## About the Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes about technology, markets, institutions, and human judgment. His work combines quantitative research, strategic consulting, and a practical interest in how people make decisions under uncertainty.
