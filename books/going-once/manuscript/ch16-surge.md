# Surge

Open a ride-hailing app during a sudden downpour, right after a major concert lets out, or in the chaotic hour after a subway line goes down, and the fare displayed before you confirm a trip may be double, triple, or on rare occasions several times the price the same route would have cost an hour earlier. Uber calls this surge pricing; Lyft calls its own version Prime Time; both describe, in their public materials, essentially the same underlying mechanism, continuously comparing the number of riders currently requesting trips within a small geographic zone against the number of available drivers currently nearby, and automatically raising the displayed price whenever that ratio tips far enough toward more riders than drivers, in the hope that a higher price will simultaneously discourage some riders from requesting a trip right now and encourage some nearby drivers to switch on the app and start accepting rides. It is worth being precise about the distinction this book's earlier chapters have already prepared the reader to draw, because it is a distinction Uber's own critics and defenders have frequently blurred in public debate: surge pricing is not, in the formal sense this book has been using throughout, an auction. No rider submits a bid; no competing rider's willingness to pay is directly revealed and compared against another's in anything resembling a live bidding process. It is a dynamically adjusted posted price, recalculated by an algorithm rather than negotiated bid by bid, closer in its formal mechanics to a retailer marking up a popular item during a shortage than to a Sotheby's ascending sale or an eBay auction. But it was built, consciously and explicitly, by people who understood auction theory, to solve precisely the allocation problem an auction exists to solve: how do you assign a genuinely scarce resource — in this specific case, the finite number of drivers currently willing to be on the road in one particular neighborhood at one particular moment — to the people who want it most, in real time, without a human being standing in the middle making that call by hand.

The economic logic underneath surge pricing traces back to an idea considerably older than ride-hailing apps themselves, one economists sometimes describe as market clearing through price rather than through queuing: absent any price adjustment mechanism at all, a sudden spike in demand for rides during a thunderstorm does not simply vanish or distribute itself evenly; it instead produces exactly what anyone who has tried to hail a cab in the rain already knows from lived experience, a shortage in which far more people want a ride than there are cars available, and the scarce rides get allocated not to whoever values them most but to whoever happens to be standing in the right spot, glances at their phone at the right moment, or simply gets lucky. Economists studying ride-hailing markets have specifically documented this failure mode under the alternative of a genuinely fixed, non-surging price: a 2020 analysis of Uber and Lyft's own trip data found that markets constrained to hold prices artificially flat during periods of high demand produced measurably worse outcomes on exactly the dimension that matters most to a rider stranded in bad weather — persistent, anomalous shortages in which a meaningful share of ride requests simply went unfulfilled, riders opening the app, seeing no available cars, and getting no ride at any price, rather than getting a ride at an elevated but still available price. The taxi system Uber's surge-pricing model eventually displaced in New York City had, ironically, been built around a very different scarce-resource allocation mechanism this book has already examined in detail — a government-issued license, the medallion, required to legally operate a yellow cab, with a fixed total supply the city had kept deliberately capped for decades. Rather than staying a purely regulatory artifact, medallions themselves became a tradable, sought-after asset, and their price climbed relentlessly for years, from roughly $200,000 in 2002 to more than $1 million by 2014, driven not primarily by genuine demand for more cabs but, according to a detailed New York Times investigation into the episode, substantially by brokers, fleet owners, and specialty lenders who marketed medallions to working drivers as an appreciating investment nearly as safe as real estate, extending increasingly aggressive loans to finance purchases at ever-higher prices. Roughly fifteen hundred people bought medallions between 2010 and 2014 alone, near the very top of the bubble, often borrowing heavily against future income that assumed the asset's price would keep climbing indefinitely. It did not: the medallion market collapsed beginning in late 2014, coinciding with Uber's rapid local expansion, and prices fell from over $1 million to roughly $170,000 by 2018 and as low as $335,000 by mid-2019 even after some partial recovery, leaving thousands of individual drivers, many of them immigrants who had borrowed their life savings and more against a medallion's promised value, underwater on debt secured by an asset now worth a small fraction of what they had paid. Nearly a thousand medallion owners filed for bankruptcy in the years following the crash, and several drivers' deaths were publicly linked by advocates and journalists to the resulting financial desperation. The episode sits at an uncomfortable angle to this book's broader argument: a fixed-supply license, artificially scarce by design and never intended to function as a freely tradable financial asset at all, was nonetheless allowed to become one, and the resulting bubble and collapse produced a very different, and in important ways more devastating, kind of winner's curse than any this book's sixth chapter examined — one visited not on sophisticated oil companies or private-equity funds well positioned to absorb a bad bet, but on individual working drivers who had trusted an ordinary municipal license to behave like the safe, appreciating asset it had never actually been designed to be.

Uber's own defense of the mechanism, articulated consistently since the company's early years, rests on precisely this logic: a price allowed to rise temporarily does two things simultaneously that a queue or a lottery cannot — it rations existing scarce supply toward the riders who value a trip most urgently in that specific moment, whether from bad weather, an early flight, or a genuine emergency, while simultaneously pulling additional drivers onto the road, out of their own homes or away from other activities, specifically because the elevated fare makes driving right now, in this specific neighborhood, worth their while in a way it was not fifteen minutes earlier.

The public reaction to this logic has been considerably less favorable than its academic defenders might hope, and the gap between economic efficiency and felt fairness surge pricing regularly exposes is itself a genuinely useful lens on why auctions and auction-adjacent mechanisms provoke the discomfort this book's earlier chapters have already documented in other contexts. A surging fare feels, to many riders experiencing it in the moment, less like an efficient allocation mechanism quietly solving a real supply-demand imbalance and more like exploitation — a company charging more precisely because a customer is desperate, stranded, or has no realistic alternative, extracting value from vulnerability rather than genuinely creating any new value in exchange for the higher price. The clearest single episode illustrating that gap remains a December 2014 hostage crisis at a café in central Sydney, Australia, during which an armed gunman held customers and staff hostage for roughly sixteen hours in an incident that dominated national and international news coverage throughout the day. As panicked residents in the surrounding central business district tried to leave the area, Uber's algorithm detected exactly the demand spike it was designed to detect and responded exactly as designed, pushing fares in the affected zone to several times their normal level — reported at the time as a minimum fare of roughly one hundred Australian dollars for trips that would ordinarily have cost a small fraction of that, a multiple driven by nothing more sinister than genuinely panicked people trying to get away from genuine danger, registering in the algorithm's model as simple, undifferentiated demand. The public reaction was immediate and overwhelmingly hostile, with widespread social media criticism accusing the company of profiteering from a terrifying emergency rather than genuinely allocating scarce rides to whoever needed them most. Uber initially defended the pricing as an automatic, unmanipulated response to real conditions before reversing course within hours, first making trips out of the area free and then formally apologizing days later, acknowledging publicly that keeping surge pricing active during the crisis had been the wrong decision regardless of what the underlying supply-and-demand mathematics technically justified. The company subsequently built explicit emergency-override policies into its pricing systems, capping or suspending algorithmic surge pricing during officially declared disasters and emergencies in several markets — a direct, lasting institutional concession that efficient price discovery, however well-grounded in genuine economic logic, is not always the value a society wants a scarce-resource allocation mechanism to optimize for first, particularly in the middle of a crisis nobody chose to be caught inside of.

That gap between an economist's efficiency argument and an ordinary rider's fairness intuition has produced real, recurring public controversy: high-profile incidents of extreme multiplier surge pricing during emergencies, including reports of dramatically inflated fares during weather emergencies and, in one particularly widely covered controversy, during a period of civil unrest, drew sustained public criticism and, in several cases, direct company responses capping or suspending surge pricing during the specific emergency in question, effectively overriding the algorithm's own real-time signal in favor of a public-relations and, in the company's own stated reasoning, an ethical judgment that a pure market-clearing price was not the right mechanism to run during a genuine crisis, whatever its ordinary economic merits during routine Friday-night demand spikes.

Airline revenue management offers a useful comparison point, because it solves a structurally similar problem — allocating a fixed, perishable amount of capacity, in an airline's case a finite number of seats on a specific flight departing at a specific time — through mechanics that are simultaneously more elaborate and, from an ordinary traveler's perspective, considerably more opaque than Uber's comparatively transparent, visibly displayed surge multiplier. Airlines have, since the deregulation of American air travel in the late 1970s, built increasingly sophisticated systems that continuously adjust the price of a given seat based on how many seats remain unsold, how far in advance of departure the booking is being made, historical demand patterns for that specific route and time, and a running, continuously updated forecast of how many more bookings the flight is likely to receive before departure — a system whose underlying goal, precisely matching Uber's own stated logic for surge pricing, is to avoid both of the two failure modes a fixed, non-adjusting price would otherwise guarantee: selling out too early at too low a price, leaving genuine last-minute demand from business travelers willing to pay considerably more unmet, or failing to sell out at all, leaving seats that generate zero revenue once the plane actually departs, since an empty seat's revenue-generating potential expires permanently and irreversibly the moment the aircraft door closes. Airlines' dynamic pricing rarely if ever presents itself to a passenger as a visible auction or a labeled surge multiplier the way Uber's app does; a traveler shopping for a fare simply sees a single number that has already been computed, invisibly, by the same underlying real-time supply-and-demand logic, a choice about presentation that generated far less public controversy for decades than Uber's more transparent, explicitly labeled approach, even though the two systems are solving essentially the same allocation problem through closely related mechanics.

That relative quiet began to break down as airlines' pricing algorithms grew sophisticated enough to move from dynamic pricing, adjusting a single fare based on aggregate remaining seat inventory and time to departure, toward something regulators and consumer advocates began calling personalized or surveillance pricing instead — a fare computed not merely from how many seats remain unsold, but from data about the specific individual shopping for the ticket, including browsing history, device type, and other behavioral signals a carrier's systems could infer about that particular traveler's likely willingness to pay. One major American carrier's public exploration of AI-driven personalized fare pricing drew sharp criticism from consumer advocates and at least one U.S. senator, who argued the practice amounted to charging different passengers different prices for the identical seat based on how much the algorithm calculated each individual could be induced to pay, a considerably harder practice to defend on efficiency grounds than Uber's transparent, uniformly applied surge multiplier, since every rider in a given surge zone at least faces the same posted price regardless of what the app happens to know about their individual browsing habits. The Federal Trade Commission's own 2024 surveillance-pricing inquiry, discussed in more detail in this book's next chapter, specifically named airlines among the industries facing formal information requests about exactly this kind of individualized, behavior-driven fare-setting, treating the practice as a distinct and potentially more troubling category than the aggregate, inventory-driven dynamic pricing airlines had used relatively uncontroversially for nearly half a century.

A related but distinct controversy has grown up around the opposite side of the same gig-economy pricing algorithms this chapter has been describing: not what a rider pays, but what a driver or delivery worker actually earns for accepting a given trip or delivery, an amount increasingly set by the same kind of opaque, continuously adjusting algorithm rather than by any transparent, published rate card. A 2025 Human Rights Watch investigation examining seven major gig-work platforms, including Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, and Instacart, found that most relied on what researchers and affected workers have taken to calling "black box" pay algorithms — systems that determine a worker's compensation for a specific job using proprietary, undisclosed formulas incorporating granular, individualized data about that specific worker, meaning two drivers completing what looks like an identical trip at an identical time can be offered meaningfully different pay with neither ever learning why. Instacart workers interviewed for the same research described a shift away from an older, more transparent pay structure toward one offering no breakdown of how a given payment was calculated at all, with some workers reporting effective pay reductions of thirty to forty percent under the new, opaque system; DoorDash separately settled claims with New York State for close to $17 million over allegations that the company had used customers' tips to subsidize its own guaranteed base pay to workers rather than passing tips through as a genuine addition on top of it, a practice regulators characterized as a deceptive blending of two revenue streams workers had every reason to believe were kept separate. The parallel to this book's earlier chapters on shill bidding and provenance fraud is not exact — no one is accused of faking a bid or forging a signature — but the underlying structural problem is a close cousin of the same information asymmetry this book has traced since ancient Rome: a pricing mechanism's legitimacy depends on the people subject to it being able to trust that the numbers it produces reflect what the mechanism's own designers claim they reflect, and an algorithm opaque enough that even the workers whose livelihoods depend on it cannot reconstruct how their own pay was calculated is, functionally, asking for exactly that kind of trust without offering any of the verifiability a transparent surge multiplier, whatever its other faults, at least makes visible on a rider's own screen.

New York City's congestion pricing program, launched in January 2025 after decades of political debate and legal challenges, illustrates a related but conceptually distinct application of dynamic, demand-responsive pricing to a genuinely public resource, and it is worth distinguishing carefully from both Uber's surge pricing and an ordinary auction, because congestion pricing is neither. It is a government-imposed toll, roughly nine dollars for most passenger vehicles entering Manhattan south of 60th Street during peak hours, set through a regulatory and legislative process rather than through any competitive bidding mechanism or continuously adjusting algorithm, with the explicit, publicly stated policy goal of reducing traffic congestion in one of the most gridlocked urban cores in North America while generating dedicated revenue for the city's public transit system. The toll functions economically in a way genuinely analogous to Uber's surge multiplier — both use price as a rationing device to manage demand for scarce, congestible capacity, whether that capacity is street space or a driver's availability — but it lacks the continuous, algorithmically adjusted, real-time responsiveness that defines true surge pricing, operating instead on a fixed, publicly announced schedule that varies by time of day and vehicle type rather than fluctuating minute to minute in response to live measured congestion. The comparison is nonetheless instructive for this book's broader argument: whether a scarce resource is priced through a live competitive auction, an algorithmically adjusted surge multiplier, or a fixed government toll schedule, societies facing genuine scarcity keep reaching for some version of the same underlying tool — letting price, rather than a queue, a lottery, or an administrator's discretion, do at least some of the work of deciding who gets access to something that cannot be made available to everyone who wants it, at the moment they all want it most.

The distinction between transparent surge pricing and opaque personalized pricing turns out to track, closely enough to be more than coincidental, a distinction this book has drawn before: an ascending auction, whatever its many well-documented psychological flaws, at least lets every bidder present see the very same rising number, updating in real time, in front of the same room or the same shared screen, while a shill bid or a fabricated provenance document works specifically because the deception stays hidden from the people it is meant to influence. A visible surge multiplier is closer, structurally, to the first category — every rider in a given zone sees the same posted price, however much that price stings, and can at least evaluate whether the multiplier looks proportionate to conditions they can independently observe, like heavy rain or a stadium letting out. A personalized fare computed from an individual traveler's own browsing history and inferred willingness to pay is closer to the second, since no passenger has any way of knowing, while comparing fares, whether the number in front of them reflects genuine scarcity or simply the algorithm's own private assessment of how much that specific shopper could be persuaded to accept without walking away.

None of the mechanisms this chapter has described are, formally, auctions in the sense this book has spent most of its length examining, and it is worth ending on that distinction rather than blurring it, because the difference matters for what comes next. A true auction, however automated, still involves bidders revealing genuine private information about their own willingness to pay, through the specific numbers they submit; a surge multiplier or a congestion toll instead posts a single price, computed by an algorithm or set by a regulator, that every affected rider or driver either accepts or declines, with no opportunity to reveal a more nuanced individual valuation through an actual competing bid. That distinction, transparent single price versus opaque coordinated pricing, will matter directly in this book's next chapter, which examines a genuinely different and considerably more troubling phenomenon: not an algorithm posting a single transparent price everyone can see and choose to accept or decline, but multiple, formally independent companies each running their own private pricing algorithm, fed by data those companies have agreed to pool, in a way federal prosecutors have alleged amounts to coordinated price-fixing dressed up in the reassuring, seemingly neutral, technocratic-sounding language of automated software supposedly optimizing a market for everyone's mutual, if entirely coincidental, benefit.
