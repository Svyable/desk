# RAW INTELLIGENCE — 1995 AIR operational-verdict audit

Bounded source audit for the book's central distinction between statistical anomaly and intelligence utility. This note does not revise manuscript prose.

## Why this matters

Chapter 1 currently uses CIA's later public summary to make the book's cleanest point: an apparent departure from randomness can coexist with failure as an intelligence capability. The originating 1995 management record is stronger because it preserves the operational recommendation and the reason for it in contemporary bureaucratic language.

## Primary record recovered

CIA record `CIA-RDP96-00791R000100150004-8`, *Star Gate Program — Proposed Management Strategy*, approved for release 18 April 2003:

https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000100150004-8.pdf

The memorandum says the American Institutes for Research review had been completed and that CIA's proposed management strategy would be based closely on it. Its key recommendations were to terminate remote-viewing operations within the Intelligence Community, terminate remote-viewing R&D within the Intelligence Community, and conduct a comprehensive declassification review for transfer of program files to the National Archives.

Most important for this book, the memorandum reports AIR's operational conclusion after interviews with multiple current users: remote-viewing information was too vague and ambiguous to provide information of sufficient quality and accuracy for actionable intelligence.

That is more precise than `the government decided it didn't work.` The failure criterion was not metaphysical. It was operational quality.

## The scientific verdict was deliberately less tidy

The same memorandum reports AIR's technical conclusion that it remained unclear whether the existence of the paranormal phenomenon called remote viewing had been demonstrated, and that the laboratory studies did not establish the source or origin of the claimed phenomenon.

This matters because the book should not let CIA's later popular-history phrasing — apparent departures from randomness but no useful intelligence — become a consensus scientific verdict. The 1995 record preserved disagreement.

Ray Hyman's 28 August 1995 evaluation, preserved as `CIA-RDP96-00789R003200290001-5`, explicitly judged the anomalous-cognition conclusion premature without adequate independent replication:

https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00789R003200290001-5.pdf

A separate CIA briefing record summarizes Jessica Utts's opposing conclusion that psychic functioning was well established and that statistical results were far beyond chance expectations:

https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200080001-8.pdf

The disagreement therefore should be represented as disagreement, not averaged into a synthetic verdict neither evaluator actually held.

## Strongest counterargument to the book's current framing

The phrase `statistical anomaly and failed spy system` is commercially sharp, but `statistical anomaly` can sound more settled than the 1995 review permits. AIR did not conclude that a genuine anomaly had been established and merely failed deployment. One evaluator argued strongly for an effect; another argued the scientific case was premature; the management record said the existence of the phenomenon remained unclear.

The book can keep its central possibility — a weak real effect could be scientifically interesting yet operationally useless — but it should label that as the hypothesis being tested against the archive, not the government's established finding.

The operational half is firmer. The 1995 management recommendation ties termination to vague/ambiguous output that did not meet actionable-intelligence requirements. That gives the book a historically grounded threshold against which celebrated hits should be tested.

## Editorial consequence

When Chapter 1 is revised, prefer the contemporary 1995 management record over the later CIA retrospective for the institutional decision. Let the prose separate three propositions:

1. Utts argued that the laboratory evidence established psychic functioning.
2. Hyman argued that this conclusion was premature without adequate independent replication.
3. AIR/CIA's operational decision did not require resolving that dispute: the output was judged insufficiently reliable and specific for actionable intelligence.

That third proposition is the book's strongest bridge from controversy to something measurable. It also gives later chapters a demanding question for every famous case: not `is there a correspondence?`, but `would the information, prospectively and without target revelation, have changed the correct operational decision?`

## Publication holds

- Do not write that AIR or CIA concluded `psi was real`.
- Do not write that AIR proved the laboratory effect was chance or artifact.
- Do not convert Utts's position into the government's consensus position.
- Do not convert Hyman's replication objection into proof that every experiment was defective.
- Recover the complete AIR final report with page-level citations before quoting its conclusions in finished manuscript prose; this audit uses the contemporary CIA management memorandum's report of those conclusions plus the preserved evaluator materials.

## Why this improves the book

The archive supplies a cleaner narrative reversal than a generic believer-versus-skeptic debate. In 1995 the government could leave the ontology unresolved and still make a practical decision. A spy service does not need to settle consciousness to reject a collection method whose output cannot reliably tell an analyst what to do next. That is the translation problem in institutional form.
