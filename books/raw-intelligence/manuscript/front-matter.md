# RAW INTELLIGENCE

## The psychic program that produced a statistical anomaly and failed as a spy system

### An epistemic contract with the reader

There are at least four different books that can be written from the same stack of STARGATE documents.

One says the United States government proved psychic spying and then buried it.

One says credulous officials wasted public money on nonsense.

One says the laboratory statistics settled the question and only prejudice kept science from admitting it.

One says the statistics are garbage, the participants were fools, and there is nothing here worth another minute.

I don't trust any of those books enough to write one.

This book begins from an uglier possibility. The U.S. government may have encountered a body of experimental results interesting enough to resist easy dismissal and still have been correct to conclude that the resulting practice was not useful intelligence. That possibility is uncomfortable because our culture likes verdicts more than conditional probabilities. Either the psychic sees the submarine or he doesn't. Either the CIA believed or it didn't. Either science proved psi or science debunked it.

The archive does not behave that way.

Some declassified session reports explicitly call the viewer's impressions **raw intelligence data** and warn that the material has not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation, or collation. That sentence is more important than any sketch of a crane, gantry, building, submarine, hostage, antenna, mountain, or mysterious cylinder. It tells us what the institution thought it had in its hands before interpretation began.

Raw.

That word is the book.

### Four shelves

Every consequential claim in these pages belongs on one of four shelves.

**Shelf One: documented fact.** A program existed. A memo was written. A session occurred. A target was assigned. A government office requested information. A review panel reached a stated conclusion. These claims can be tied to records.

**Shelf Two: credible but incomplete evidence.** A participant remembers an operational success. An evaluator reports an above-chance aggregate. A correspondence looks impressive but the complete target pool, scoring history, cueing path, or contemporaneous analytical record is not available. These claims deserve attention and restraint at the same time.

**Shelf Three: disputed allegation.** Remote viewers obtained information by a paranormal channel. A particular celebrated case could not have been produced by leakage, inference, flexible matching, selective reporting, or chance. A government secretly retained the capability after the public program ended. These propositions require evidence beyond the existence of the program itself.

**Shelf Four: synthesis.** This is where I am allowed to think. The book will introduce concepts such as translation loss and specificity tax. They are not discoveries hiding in CIA files. They are analytical tools, and they survive only if they help discriminate cases better than the alternatives.

The shelves do not bleed into each other because the story would be more exciting if they did.

### What declassification does not mean

A CIA stamp does not certify the contents of the page as true.

This should be obvious. It is somehow not obvious.

Intelligence archives contain reports, rumors, hypotheses, adversary claims, raw collection, failed experiments, internal disagreements, contractor work, threat estimates, speculative memoranda, administrative debris and things somebody once thought might matter. The fact that an agency possessed a document establishes custody. Sometimes it establishes interest. Sometimes funding. Sometimes operational use. It does not automatically establish the document's theory of reality.

This distinction becomes especially important with remote viewing because the surviving files are visually persuasive. A transcript stamped SECRET feels different from the same words in a parapsychology journal. The classification marking supplies atmosphere. It can also supply counterfeit epistemic weight.

We will not borrow it.

### What statistical significance does not mean

Suppose, for the moment, that an experiment really does produce a small above-chance effect under conditions good enough to deserve further investigation.

That does not give an intelligence officer a psychic sensor.

A military sensor has to do more than beat chance in aggregate. It must provide information at the right time, about the right target, with enough specificity to discriminate among live alternatives, at an error rate the user understands, under conditions that can be repeated and audited. It must survive adversarial use. It must tell you when it is probably wrong. Ideally, another channel can corroborate it.

A weak laboratory anomaly and a useful collection system live under different loss functions.

That gap is where this book lives.

### The strongest countercase stays in the room

The strongest skeptical explanation is not that every participant was dishonest or stupid. It is that small methodological vulnerabilities, flexible judging, sensory or informational leakage, optional analytical choices, selective memory, publication effects and ordinary coincidence can produce an apparently persistent anomaly—especially when the underlying signal is weak.

The strongest pro-anomaly explanation is not that every vivid operational story is true. It is that some controlled datasets may contain a small information-bearing effect that conventional models have not adequately explained, while the effect remains too unstable for dependable operational exploitation.

Both possibilities remain live until evidence kills one.

### Defeat conditions

This book's central thesis fails in either direction.

It fails if the best controlled evidence collapses under modern independent preregistered replication, leaving no residual anomaly worth translating into operations.

It also fails if a blinded, independently replicated protocol can produce target-specific information with calibrated confidence at a rate that creates repeatable decision advantage. At that point `statistical anomaly but operational failure` becomes history, not diagnosis.

The test should be allowed to embarrass the book.

Otherwise it is not a test.
