# 1 — Raw Intelligence

The warning is sitting near the top of the page.

It is bureaucratic. That helps.

No incense. No bent spoons. No silver-haired mystic describing Jupiter from a recliner. Just the language of a government office trying to keep one kind of information from becoming another kind by accident.

A remote-viewing session has been conducted. The viewer has impressions. Those impressions are being transmitted onward. And then comes the sentence that ought to be printed over the door of every museum exhibit, podcast studio and congressional hearing room where this history gets retold:

The impressions are **raw intelligence data**.

They have not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation or collation.

Interpretation and use are the requestor's problem.

The exact wording varies across surviving GRILL FLAME reports, but the architecture is remarkably clear. Somebody inside the system understood that a description produced by a remote viewer was not yet knowledge.

This is where I want to begin, because almost everybody else begins at the wrong end.

They begin with the hit.

A shape resembles a crane. A sketch resembles a gantry. A viewer says `water` and the target is near water. Somebody describes a large building and there is a large building. A story survives forty years because the correspondence is strange enough to be repeated at dinner.

Or they begin with the money.

The CIA funded it. The Army used it. DIA kept it alive. Therefore the government knew something.

Or they begin with the ending.

In 1995 the program was reviewed and the government did not continue it. Therefore nothing happened.

All three beginnings are lazy.

The interesting object is the pipe between them.

What enters the pipe? What transformations occur inside it? What comes out the other side? At which stage can a real effect disappear? At which stage can a false effect be manufactured? What does a decision-maker actually need from a phenomenon before `interesting` becomes `useful`?

That is the remote-viewing problem.

It is also a much larger problem in science.

## The report that refuses to certify itself

Consider a surviving 1981 GRILL FLAME session report. It is not a retrospective memoir. It is not a documentary made after the mythology hardened. It is a classified operational artifact later released into the public record.

The document tells the reader what kind of thing it is.

A session was conducted in response to a request for information. The viewer's impressions follow. They are raw. They have not been analyzed, evaluated or collated. Responsibility for interpretation lies downstream.

That distinction is not cosmetic.

Imagine a conventional intelligence collector reports that three trucks entered a fenced compound at 02:10. The collector may be accurate about the trucks and wrong about what they mean. They could carry missiles. They could carry food. They could be decoys. They could be maintenance vehicles. Collection is not analysis.

Remote viewing added a more radical uncertainty one step earlier: nobody had established what the collection mechanism was, whether there was a collection mechanism, or how often the apparent information content exceeded chance and ordinary leakage under operational conditions.

So the chain was not:

`target → sensor → analyst → decision`.

It was closer to:

`target → unknown process? → human impressions → transcript → matching/judging → analyst → decision`.

Every arrow is a place where information can be gained, lost, distorted or invented.

The question `Did remote viewing work?` crushes that entire chain into one verb.

We are going to stop doing that.

## Tehran

The hostage crisis in Iran gives the archive its cruelest setting.

On 4 November 1979, Iranian students seized the U.S. Embassy in Tehran. Americans were held for 444 days. The crisis consumed the Carter administration, generated an enormous intelligence demand and ended one rescue attempt in fire and wreckage at Desert One.

Remote viewers were tasked against the problem.

Declassified GRILL FLAME records describe sessions whose purpose was to provide information relevant to the hostages and embassy compound. Some asked viewers to locate or describe people. Some moved the imagined observation forward in time. The language can be startling because the administrative frame is so ordinary. A national crisis is happening. An unconventional collection method is being asked to contribute.

That fact is documented.

It does not tell us whether the method contributed useful truth.

This is the first discipline the book will enforce until it becomes annoying.

**Use is not validation.**

Institutions try things under pressure. Intelligence organizations are especially supposed to investigate possibilities that would be strategically catastrophic if an adversary possessed them first. A program can therefore be evidence of uncertainty rather than evidence of belief.

And the Cold War manufactured uncertainty at industrial scale.

If Soviet researchers were investigating psychotronics, bioinformation, telepathy or anomalous cognition, U.S. analysts faced an ugly asymmetry. Dismissing the field could be rational if it was nonsense. Dismissing it could be catastrophic if it was not. Investigating it was comparatively cheap.

This logic can fund a bad idea for years.

It can also keep a weak real effect alive long enough to understand it.

The funding history alone cannot tell us which occurred.

## A miss in the archive

There is another kind of document I like more than the famous hits.

A 1978 training report says the target images produced by the viewer showed **absolutely no target correlation**.

There it is.

Not paranormal ambiguity. Not a believer's reconstruction. A miss, written down.

Another 1982 training report says post-session analysis found **very little correlation**.

These pages matter because they reveal a property of the archive that popular retellings erase. Remote viewing was not a vending machine that dispensed uncanny sketches whenever the government inserted a target coordinate. The records include failure.

That should make skeptics less theatrical and believers more careful.

The presence of misses does not disprove an above-chance aggregate effect. If a medical treatment works 55 percent of the time against a 50 percent baseline, failure is expected. But the operational consequences are completely different from a treatment that works 99.9 percent of the time.

This is the first appearance of what I will call the **specificity tax**.

A statistical effect can survive noise that an operation cannot.

Suppose a viewer in a forced-choice experiment identifies the correct target often enough to produce a small but robust departure from chance. That is scientifically interesting if the protocol is clean and the effect replicates independently.

Now give an intelligence officer a blank sheet of paper and ask where a hostage is.

`Large structure. Metal. Water nearby. Open area. Something vertical.`

What does the officer do?

There is no chance baseline printed in the margin. There is no target pool of four photographs. There may be dozens of compounds compatible with the description. If the viewer supplies one strikingly correct detail and twelve wrong ones, the analyst must know which is which before acting. If several viewers disagree, somebody must combine them. If feedback arrives after the session, the human mind becomes a ruthless correspondence engine.

The scientific question may be whether information crossed a boundary it should not have crossed.

The intelligence question is whether the information changes the right decision.

Those are not synonyms.

## The strange 1995 split

By 1995 the government had inherited a long-running argument.

The American Institutes for Research was asked to evaluate the remote-viewing program. The resulting debate is often flattened into whichever sentence the reteller prefers.

One side emphasized statistical evidence it regarded as difficult to explain by chance and argued that anomalous cognition had been demonstrated in the laboratory.

The skeptical evaluation emphasized the absence of adequate independent replication and the gap between laboratory claims and useful operations.

CIA's later public summary states the institutional conclusion in a form almost too perfect for this book: enough accurate experiences existed to appear to defy randomness, but the phenomenon was too unreliable, inconsistent and sporadic to be useful for intelligence purposes. CIA did not restore the program.

Read that again without trying to make it vote for your tribe.

`More than randomness` and `not useful` can occupy the same sentence.

That is the crack I want to widen.

The crack is not unique to psi. Medicine lives there. Machine learning lives there. Behavioral science lives there. Financial prediction lives there. A model can beat a benchmark and lose money after costs. A biomarker can correlate with disease and fail as a screening test. A classifier can achieve impressive average accuracy and become dangerous in the rare cases that matter most.

Effect size is not deployment.

Remote viewing makes the distinction grotesquely visible because the proposed mechanism is so extraordinary.

## What would count as replication?

In one of the 1995 evaluation documents, psychologist Ray Hyman made a point that deserves to survive the personalities around it. A revolutionary claim should not be treated as established merely because one research lineage reports repeatable results. Independent laboratories matter.

That requirement is not hostility to discovery. It is one of the technologies by which science prevents a local laboratory culture from becoming a law of nature.

The remote-viewing literature immediately makes the question difficult.

What counts as independent?

A different building? Different experimenters? Different viewers? A protocol designed without the originating group? A target pool nobody connected to the original researchers has seen? Independent analysis? Preregistration? Data deposited before anyone knows the outcome?

And what exactly must replicate?

A mean score above chance?

A particular viewer's performance?

A physiological correlate?

A usable description of a novel target?

An operational decision advantage?

These are different claims with different evidentiary burdens.

The ganzfeld literature later reproduced the same fight in miniature. Milton and Wiseman's 1999 meta-analysis of thirty studies from seven laboratories failed to reproduce the earlier main effect. Storm and Ertel answered in 2001 with a larger aggregate and argued that the broader database did show a replicable effect.

That argument is not a footnote. It is the terrain.

If your phenomenon appears or disappears depending on inclusion rules, study quality definitions, time windows, statistical models, laboratory lineages or analytical choices, the correct response is not to choose the meta-analysis whose conclusion you enjoy.

The correct response is to design the next experiment so that fewer choices remain after the data arrive.

## The viewer is not the only instrument

Remote viewing is usually narrated as though the viewer is the experimental apparatus.

That is incomplete.

The target selector is part of the apparatus.

The person who cues the viewer is part of the apparatus.

The room is part of the apparatus.

The transcript is part of the apparatus.

The target pool is part of the apparatus.

The judge is part of the apparatus.

The scoring rule is part of the apparatus.

The feedback procedure is part of the apparatus.

The analyst who decides which nouns matter is part of the apparatus.

The archivist is part of the apparatus.

And forty years later, the storyteller is definitely part of the apparatus.

This is why anecdotes age so badly. Once the target is known, correspondence becomes cheap. Human beings are excellent at finding it. We tolerate synonyms. We forgive scale errors. We rotate sketches. We privilege unusual matches. We forget generic descriptors. We remember the session that looks impossible and misplace the folder containing the session that described nothing at all.

None of this proves that every hit is manufactured.

It proves that **retrospective elasticity** is an alternative mechanism that must be measured.

A good protocol makes the match before the story can.

## The wrong argument about mechanism

There is a temptation on both sides to demand a mechanism too early.

Believers reach for quantum mechanics because quantum mechanics is strange and remote viewing is strange. This is not an argument. A vocabulary can be technically sophisticated and causally empty.

Skeptics sometimes make the mirror error: because no plausible mechanism is known, the effect cannot exist.

That is too strong. Phenomena can precede mechanisms. Biology was full of reliable regularities before molecular explanation caught up.

The right sequence is less satisfying.

First establish whether there is a stable effect.

Then characterize its boundary conditions.

Then see whether known mechanisms can produce it.

Then earn a new mechanism if the residue demands one.

Remote viewing repeatedly tried to skip from unusual result to ontology. That is how you end up discussing nonlocal mind before you have finished arguing about judging procedures.

The mechanism question is fascinating.

It is not first.

## Decision Augmentation

One of the more interesting later attempts to rethink the phenomenon was Decision Augmentation Theory, associated with Edwin May, Jessica Utts and S. James P. Spottiswoode. Rather than assuming anomalous cognition worked like an invisible camera transmitting target information into a viewer's mind, the model explored whether the anomaly might appear through decision processes.

I am not asking you to accept the model.

I like what the model does to the room.

It moves the mystery.

That is useful because remote viewing acquired much of its cultural power from an implicit picture: there is a distant object, the psychic somehow sees it, and the sketch is a degraded photograph delivered through consciousness. Once that picture hardens, every experiment gets interpreted as evidence for psychic perception even when the data may support much less specific claims.

A model that relocates the anomaly into selection or decision reminds us that **phenomenology does not identify mechanism**.

The viewer may feel as though she is seeing.

That does not prove seeing is what happened.

The same caution applies to ordinary cognition. Confidence is not a readout of accuracy. Vividness is not a readout of source. The mind does not hand us a chain-of-custody form with each experience.

## Build the sensor

Here is the bargain I want to make with the strongest version of the psi claim.

You do not have to tell me how it works yet.

Build me the sensor.

Not literally a box. Build the behavioral equivalent.

Pre-register the protocol. Use independent laboratories. Lock the target-generation process. Make cue leakage structurally difficult rather than merely prohibited. Define scoring before data collection. Publish all sessions. Include negative controls. Rotate skeptical and sympathetic teams through the same pipeline. Let an adversarial collaboration agree in advance what result changes whose mind.

Then move beyond statistical detection.

Give the system a task with a real decision consequence.

Not `which of four pictures was selected?` but a bounded problem where the information must discriminate among live alternatives before feedback exists. Calibrate confidence. Penalize false specifics. Measure how much the output improves decisions over a baseline analyst with the same conventional information.

If the effect survives that, we have something much more interesting than a CIA curiosity.

If it dies, we have learned where it was living.

Maybe in leakage.

Maybe in judging.

Maybe in a small laboratory anomaly that cannot survive operational translation.

Maybe in the stories.

The point is to force the possibilities apart.

## The archive is not the answer

There are thousands of pages.

That is part of the problem.

Volume creates gravity. A giant archive feels like evidence proportional to its mass. The program ran for years. There are classified stamps, code names, contractors, session transcripts, evaluation reports, diagrams, memoranda, budgets, personalities, disagreements and enough oddity to keep a person awake for a month.

But bureaucracy generates paper around uncertainty too.

The existence of a large documentary residue proves that the United States government spent real time and real money trying to answer a question.

It does not answer the question for us.

That may be the most important lesson in the whole affair.

The state can investigate a phenomenon without validating it.

A laboratory can report an anomaly without producing a capability.

A capability can be interesting without being useful.

A vivid hit can coexist with a disastrous base rate.

A skeptical critique can identify real weaknesses without explaining every result.

And an unresolved residue can remain unresolved without becoming magic.

On the first page of the operational record, the bureaucracy had already given us the correct adjective.

Raw.

The mistake was thinking time would cook it for us.
