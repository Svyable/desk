# Copies Without Cloning

The phrase “the environment copies information” should immediately create a problem for anyone who remembers the no-cloning theorem.

Quantum mechanics does not allow an arbitrary unknown quantum state to be duplicated perfectly by a universal machine. This is not a defect in current hardware. It is a theorem. If I hand you a single qubit in an unknown state, there is no physical operation that will reliably produce two independent perfect copies of that state for every possible input.

So what exactly is proliferating in quantum Darwinism?

Not the whole state.

That answer is the hinge on which the classical world turns.

Imagine a qubit in a superposition of two pointer states, conventionally written as zero and one. If an environmental qubit interacts in a controlled way, it can become correlated with which pointer state is present. Another environmental qubit can do the same. Soon the environment contains many records of the distinction zero versus one. But those records do not each contain an independent copy of the original arbitrary superposition. The phase relation that made the original state quantum is not locally replicated into each fragment.

The environment can spread the answer to one question while withholding the full object.

This is how classical information escapes the no-cloning prohibition. Orthogonal states can be distinguished and their labels copied. A classical bit can be duplicated because its alternatives are perfectly distinguishable. What cannot be universally cloned is an arbitrary unknown quantum state containing amplitude and phase information across nonorthogonal possibilities.

The public world therefore emerges through a form of selective broadcasting.

Only some observables can leave many independent records. The interaction chooses a preferred set. Those values become shareable in a way the full quantum state cannot.

There is something almost bureaucratic about this. Quantum mechanics will issue unlimited certified copies of a narrow document while refusing to release the complete file.

The certified document says: the pointer is here.

It does not say: here is the complete coherent state from which every incompatible observable can be reconstructed.

The distinction is easy to miss because classical life trains us to think copying is neutral. If I duplicate a digital file, I expect the copy to contain everything in the source file. If I photocopy a page, I accept some loss in color or resolution but regard it as technical imperfection. Quantum theory imposes a deeper limit. There is no universal channel that takes arbitrary unknown states and sends complete perfect descendants into many independent systems.

Nature's broadcasting has to choose what kind of information becomes public.

This links quantum Darwinism to older results about broadcasting quantum states. The no-broadcasting theorem generalizes the no-cloning intuition: arbitrary noncommuting quantum states cannot be broadcast so that multiple subsystems each retain the original state as their local reduced state. Commuting states—the states that can be represented as classical probability distributions over a common basis—are the broadcastable ones.

That boundary is exactly where objectivity would like to live.

If many observers are to inspect separate environmental fragments and all recover the same property without disturbing one another's evidence, the relevant information has to behave classically. It needs to be shareable.

This offers a wonderfully strict answer to a loose philosophical demand. What makes a fact public? One condition is that information about it can be broadcast into many disjoint places without violating quantum mechanics.

Classical information is the shareable residue.

That does not mean all classical descriptions are equally natural. The environment selects the observable through dynamics. Nor does it mean every commuting family will actually be redundantly recorded. Broadcasting requires interactions that create the records. But once the Darwinistic mechanism works, the information that proliferates has an effectively classical structure.

The full quantum information remains jealous.

This jealousy is not merely philosophical. Quantum cryptography, quantum communication, and error correction all rely on the fact that quantum information cannot be passively copied and inspected the way classical bits can. Eavesdropping can disturb states. Unknown qubits cannot be backed up by simple duplication. Quantum teleportation moves a state using entanglement and classical communication but destroys the original in the process. The theory is full of reminders that “information” is not one homogeneous substance.

Classical information is promiscuous. Quantum information has custody rules.

The environment turns one into the other only selectively.

There is a common way of talking about measurement in which the apparatus “reads” the quantum state. That is too generous. A measurement reads an observable. Different measurements reveal different aspects, and incompatible observables cannot all be assigned the same preexisting classical story under ordinary quantum assumptions. The apparatus is designed to correlate a particular system property with a stable pointer.

Quantum Darwinism extends that correlation outward.

The pointer state is copied into environmental records, not as a clone of the system but as a chain of correlations. The photon does not become the dust grain. It becomes a photon whose state depends on where the dust grain is. Another photon carries another such dependence. The records can differ physically while agreeing informationally.

This is a better model of ordinary evidence than duplication anyway.

A footprint is not a copy of a shoe. A shadow is not a copy of the body. A bank transaction record is not a copy of the purchase. A spectrum is not a copy of a star. Evidence consists of transformed correlations. What matters is whether the transformation preserves enough structure to infer the variable of interest.

Nature rarely sends replicas. It sends consequences.

The classical world is assembled from those consequences.

This gives redundancy a richer meaning. If ten environmental fragments all carry evidence about the same system observable, their physical states need not match. They can be different witnesses whose testimony has a common cause. In realistic scattering, different photons depart in different directions and frequencies. Their detailed states vary. Yet each may constrain the object's position.

Redundancy is about recoverable information, not identical media.

That makes quantum Darwinism less like Xerox and more like forensic convergence.

A detective may have a security image, a timestamp, a tire mark, and a witness statement. These are not copies of one another. They become redundant with respect to a proposition if each independently supports it. The quantum case is cleaner because the correlations can be mathematically quantified. The human case is messier because causal independence and reliability must be established rather than assumed.

Still, the distinction between copying a representation and independently recording a source will matter later when we reach civilization.

For now the physical limit is the important one.

Why should classical information be broadcastable at all?

Because orthogonal alternatives can be distinguished without forcing the ambiguity that plagues nonorthogonal quantum states. If the pointer states are mutually distinguishable and stable under interaction, the environment can become correlated with their labels repeatedly. Observers can then measure environmental fragments in ways that reveal the label without needing direct access to the system.

The price is that complementary information does not become equally public.

There is no free lunch where every possible basis gets its own equally redundant environmental newspaper. If that happened, observers could combine the newspapers to reconstruct and clone arbitrary quantum states. Quantum mechanics forbids the arrangement.

So publicity is exclusive.

A system cannot generally make every incompatible property objective at once.

This is one of the places where the everyday classical world looks richer than it is. A baseball seems to have position, velocity, spin, color, temperature, and an indefinite catalog of other properties simultaneously. But these macroscopic quantities are coarse-grained collective variables with uncertainties enormous compared with quantum limits. At that scale, approximate joint classical descriptions work beautifully. The inaccessible quantum incompatibilities are buried far below what the environment and our instruments resolve.

Classical abundance is built on quantum coarse graining.

The environment does not need to encode an object's position to Planck-scale precision for the position to be objective to us. It needs to encode enough to distinguish the macroscopic alternatives relevant to observers. Redundancy depends on a tolerated information deficit, often represented by a parameter: how close to the full classical information must a fragment get before we count it as a sufficient record?

This makes objectivity quantitative rather than absolute.

A property can be objective to one resolution and not another.

Everyone in a stadium may agree that the ball landed in the left half of the field while disagreeing about the centimeter. A photon fragment may reveal a dust grain's coarse location without carrying microscopic details. The public world is layered by scale.

That observation keeps us from turning pointer states into Platonic labels. The environment records distinctions with finite precision, finite energy, finite time, and finite channel capacity. Redundancy is always about some information task.

What is the observer trying to infer?

How accurate must the inference be?

How large a fragment can they intercept?

How independent are the fragments?

How noisy was the environment before the interaction?

Once these questions enter, objectivity stops being a binary word and becomes a resource.

The classical property is not simply “there.” It is available with a certain redundancy, at a certain fidelity, over a certain spatial and temporal range.

This is closer to lived reality than philosophers sometimes admit. The location of the Moon is objective to extraordinary precision because enormous observational resources and stable dynamics support it. The exact temperature of a turbulent cubic millimeter of air is less cheaply public. The fact that someone whispered a sentence in an empty stairwell may have almost no redundant record. Objectivity in practice has gradients even before quantum theory enters.

Quantum Darwinism provides a physical lower floor beneath those gradients.

The environment can only broadcast what quantum structure allows and what the interaction selects.

This also clarifies why the word “information” must be used carefully. Information is always information in correlations. A photon by itself does not carry a little label saying DUST GRAIN AT X. Its state is correlated with the system under a model of the interaction. To extract the information, an observer needs an appropriate measurement and usually background knowledge about the channel.

A random bit string can encode a novel if you know the key. The same string is noise if you do not.

Physical records are no different in principle. The environment can contain correlations that no realistic observer knows how to decode. Darwinistic objectivity requires not merely that information exists somewhere in the global state but that it is locally accessible from fragments.

Accessible is doing a great deal of work.

A full quantum state can contain entanglement correlations that only a joint global measurement would reveal. Local observers cannot exploit them independently. Classical information is special because it can be read from separate fragments.

This motivates later refinements such as strong quantum Darwinism and spectrum broadcast structures. A mutual-information plateau can sometimes hide quantum correlations that do not satisfy the intuitive demand for independently accessible classical records. Researchers therefore asked for stricter mathematical conditions: can multiple observers really each obtain the same classical information without disturbing the system or one another?

The answer leads to broadcast structures in which the system's preferred classical states are correlated with mutually distinguishable conditional states in separate environmental fragments.

The name is ugly. The idea is beautiful.

The world becomes public not because quantum mechanics relaxes its rules but because the only information that can proliferate in the required way is already on the classical side of the boundary.

Objectivity is what can survive copying without being cloned.

That distinction also gives us a warning for human systems.

Digital civilization has made copying almost costless. We duplicate representations at rates no physical newspaper era could imagine. A video can exist on ten million devices. A sentence can be indexed, summarized, embedded, translated, and generated again. This abundance feels like evidence because our deepest physical experience associates redundancy with independently accessible reality.

But digital copies can be descendants of one source rather than independent environmental records.

The brain has no native reason to distinguish those genealogies. In the natural world, seeing the same mountain from three positions usually means receiving three sets of photons that separately interacted with the mountain. On a network, seeing the same claim on three screens may mean receiving three renderings of one database row.

We have built copying machinery that can counterfeit the surface appearance of physical objectivity.

That does not mean online information is false. It means redundancy has lost its old evidentiary innocence.

The no-cloning theorem protects quantum information from a universal copying machine. Classical civilization has no corresponding theorem protecting us from excessive copies.

We need provenance instead.

But before we climb into that human problem, the physics has another lesson. If copies of classical information can be spread widely, how many copies are enough to make a world feel objective?

That is the problem of redundancy.
