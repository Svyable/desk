# Objectivity Has a Bandwidth

The public world is not infinitely detailed.

This seems obvious when said about eyesight. A person cannot resolve the atomic lattice of a chair from across the room. It becomes less obvious when said about objectivity itself.

Quantum Darwinism does not make every detail of a system redundantly available. Environmental fragments have finite capacity. Scattering has finite resolution. Noise consumes channel space. Observers tolerate information deficits. Pointer states are often coarse-grained packets rather than mathematical points.

Objectivity has a bandwidth.

The phrase is not standard terminology. I mean something practical: only a limited amount and kind of information about a system can become locally, redundantly, and reliably available through a given environment on a given timescale.

The cup has a public position to millimeter precision long before it has a public microstate.

The Moon has a public orbit at useful precision without every grain of regolith broadcasting its coordinate to Earth.

A person's body has a public outline while most molecular events inside it remain hidden.

Publicity is layered.

The scale at which a property becomes objective depends on the distinguishability of the records. If two possible system states produce nearly identical environmental fragments, local observers cannot reliably tell them apart. Increase the separation or improve the detector and the alternatives become distinguishable. What counted as one coarse state splits into two public states.

Better instruments widen the bandwidth.

This is one way to understand the history of science. We repeatedly turn formerly private differences into public records.

The naked eye sees one star. A telescope resolves a binary. Spectroscopy distinguishes compositions invisible to imaging. A microscope resolves cells, then organelles, then molecules under different techniques. Particle detectors convert ephemeral interactions into tracks. Sequencers turn molecular order into strings that can be copied globally.

Science is an expansion of the objectivity bandwidth available to humans.

But every expansion leaves a frontier.

An electron microscope has limits. A sequencing platform has error modes. A telescope has diffraction, noise, and finite photons. A measurement that extracts one observable can disturb another. The quantum state cannot be broadcast whole.

There is no final instrument that makes everything equally public.

This is a physically grounded reason to distrust fantasies of complete legibility.

Governments have them. Companies have them. Engineers have them. Technologists periodically convince themselves that enough sensors and storage will turn a city, organization, market, body, or person into a fully observable system.

The dashboard will contain reality.

It never does.

Even in classical engineering, observability is a technical property. A system can have internal states that cannot be reconstructed from the available outputs. Control theory knows this explicitly. Add sensors and some hidden variables become estimable. Others remain weakly constrained. Models determine what the outputs mean.

Quantum mechanics adds a deeper ceiling: not all information is simultaneously broadcastable at all.

The universe is not obligated to be a dashboard.

This matters for the human role because our civilization increasingly creates its own environmental record layer.

A smartphone contains accelerometers, microphones, cameras, radios, clocks, location services, and logs. Cars are sensor platforms. Buildings monitor occupancy and energy. Satellites image Earth. Payment systems record transactions. Networks log clicks. Hospitals produce streams of measurements. Public spaces collect video. Scientific instruments generate petabytes.

We have never had more records.

It is tempting to say we therefore have more reality.

We have more measurements of selected variables.

That is not the same thing.

The selection function has politics.

A sensor exists because someone funded it. A variable enters a database because someone defined a schema. A category becomes official because an institution needed it. A platform logs behavior because it serves a product or business model. What does not fit the system can become administratively invisible.

Physical environments select observables through interaction Hamiltonians. Human environments select observables through design and power.

The mechanisms are different. The structural consequence is similar enough to be useful: embedded observers experience what the environment makes legible.

A city full of traffic sensors can become very objective about vehicle flow and remain vague about why people cannot afford housing.

A hospital can have exquisite billing records and incomplete measures of patient suffering.

A school can track test performance to the decimal and know little about curiosity.

A social platform can measure engagement continuously and infer satisfaction poorly.

The bandwidth is not neutral.

This is where the frontier-physics thesis stops being a curiosity and becomes an uncomfortable mirror.

Nature taught us that objectivity is selective. We built institutions as though selection were merely a technical limitation to be eliminated by more data.

More data often means more of the selected observable.

A million position measurements do not reveal the lost quantum phase.

A million clicks do not reveal every dimension of human welfare.

Redundancy can deepen a blind spot.

This does not make measurement suspect. The opposite. It makes measurement precious enough to define carefully.

A good scientific variable is powerful because its limits are known. Temperature has a domain. GDP has a definition. Blood pressure has a protocol. A detector has a calibration curve. A telescope publishes sensitivity. The responsible use of objective variables requires remembering what they do not encode.

The danger appears when a public variable becomes ontologically arrogant.

“What we can measure is what matters.”

Quantum mechanics has already disproved the general principle.

The global quantum state contains information not locally present in the environmental records. The classical layer is a selected interface.

Human systems should take the hint.

There is another bandwidth constraint: attention.

Even if the environment contains enormous redundant information, an observer can sample only a fraction. Human senses have limited throughput. Working memory is limited. Time is limited. Institutions allocate attention through filters.

A public fact can exist and remain unseen.

The physical environment may broadcast it abundantly in a channel we do not monitor. A nearby device can emit radio interference no human notices until an antenna listens. A disease can leave biomarkers no routine test measures. A galaxy can blaze in X-rays while looking unremarkable in visible light.

Objectivity is not visibility to a particular observer.

It is availability under a channel.

This difference matters in debates about what science “knows.” A phenomenon can be physically recordable before any human instrument accesses the record. After instrumentation, it can become part of public science.

The universe had X-rays before Röntgen.

The environment had gravitational waves before LIGO.

The distinction between existence and human publicness should be obvious and is repeatedly forgotten.

Our technology changes the set of environmental fragments we can sample.

It also changes what fragments exist.

A telescope does not merely receive; its detector creates durable classical records. A weather station takes a local physical variable and rebroadcasts it digitally. A satellite turns reflected radiation into maps accessible to millions. A genomic sequencer converts chemical structure into symbolic redundancy.

Humans increase objectivity bandwidth by transduction and rebroadcast.

This is a real power.

It is not unlimited and it is not always benign.

Surveillance is one form of deliberate objectivization. A behavior that once left only ephemeral local traces can become a permanent database event. A face crossing a plaza becomes a timestamped image. A purchase becomes a durable transaction record. A location becomes a history.

We convert fleeting states into public—or at least institutionally accessible—pointer-like records.

The ethical question is not whether information exists in principle. It is who creates the redundant record, who can access it, how long it persists, and what inferences attach to it.

Quantum Darwinism does not contain an ethics of surveillance. It does expose how physically consequential the act of making a state public can be.

Publicity is not passive.

In the quantum setting, uncontrolled environmental monitoring destroys coherence between alternatives. In human life, monitoring changes behavior through entirely different psychological and institutional mechanisms. The analogy should not be literal. But both remind us that making information available can alter the system.

Observation architectures are interventions.

This is obvious to anyone who has worked under metrics. Once a measurement becomes consequential, people adapt. Goodhart's law and related phenomena describe how proxies degrade when optimized. The environmental record ceases to be passive evidence and becomes part of the causal dynamics.

Nature's pointer states are selected by coupling. Human pointer states can be selected by incentives.

A worker becomes a productivity score. A student becomes a test score. A driver becomes an insurance risk category. The measure is copied through systems and begins to shape decisions that alter the person being measured.

The public variable feeds back.

This feedback has no exact quantum-Darwinist equivalent, but the broader lesson about bandwidth remains: what becomes objective in a system can dominate what the system becomes.

A society should therefore care deeply about the variables it makes redundantly public.

The most legible features acquire power.

There is a natural counterargument. Without shared metrics, coordination becomes arbitrary. Courts need records. Markets need prices. Medicine needs measurements. Science needs reproducible observables. Administration without legibility can hide abuse as easily as legibility can cause it.

Correct.

The answer is not to retreat into private ineffability.

It is to build plural channels and preserve the distinction between variable and system.

Science offers a model. One instrument is rarely the final authority. Measurements have uncertainty. Methods are compared. New channels are built. Anomalies can justify widening the observable set. A theory predicts variables not yet measured and motivates instruments to find them.

A healthy knowledge system treats the objectivity bandwidth as expandable and incomplete.

An unhealthy one mistakes current observability for the boundary of reality.

This may be the deepest human lesson available from quantum Darwinism without abusing it.

The classical world itself is a narrow broadcast carved out of a richer quantum state.

We are creatures of that broadcast.

Our first senses evolved inside its bandwidth. Our instruments widened it. Our institutions built secondary broadcasts on top.

Now our machine systems ingest the secondary broadcast and call it data.

The risk is recursion.

If models learn mostly from what previous systems made legible, they may become very good at predicting the world that gets copied and increasingly blind to the world that does not.

A feedback loop of public information can narrow attention even while data volume explodes.

This is not model collapse in the technical synthetic-data sense, though that is a neighboring concern. It is an epistemic selection effect. The measurable, archived, searchable, standardized parts of reality become overrepresented. Rare, local, embodied, unrecorded, or poorly digitized phenomena become underrepresented.

Civilization's objectivity bandwidth has a shape.

We should know what it filters out.

The universe does not owe us full access. Our institutions should at least admit the same.
