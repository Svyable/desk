# Redundancy Makes a World

A single witness can be wrong.

Physics does not need that sentence, but humans do. We are so accustomed to using redundancy as a test of reality that the habit is almost invisible. A noise in the night becomes more credible when the dog reacts. A weather forecast becomes easier to trust when radar, satellite images, and the air outside converge. A laboratory result becomes stronger when another apparatus reproduces it. The common structure is multiple routes to the same conclusion.

Quantum Darwinism puts redundancy inside the physical account of classical objectivity.

Suppose an environment contains a hundred subsystems that have interacted with one system. If you need ninety-nine of them to infer the system's pointer state, the information is not very public. Two observers cannot each take a small independent sample and learn the answer. If, on the other hand, any one of twenty disjoint fragments is sufficient, the same classical information exists in many accessible copies.

That multiplicity is quantified by redundancy.

The exact definitions vary across the literature, but the intuition is stable. Choose a tolerated information deficit. Ask how large a fragment of the environment an observer needs to recover all but that small deficit of the system's classical information. If a tiny fragment is enough, many such fragments can exist. The redundancy is roughly the number of disjoint sufficient fragments available.

A high redundancy means the system has many independent publicity channels.

This is why the partial information plot matters. Imagine the horizontal axis as the fraction of the environment intercepted and the vertical axis as information about the system. In a non-Darwinistic case, the curve might climb gradually; you need more and more of the environment to learn more and more. In a strongly Darwinistic case, the curve jumps quickly to a plateau. A small fragment already tells you nearly everything classical. Gathering more fragments mostly gives repeated confirmation. Only near the end, when almost the whole environment is captured, can additional global quantum information appear.

The plateau is boredom made rigorous.

Once you know where the object is, the next million photons mostly tell you where the object is again.

Boredom is exactly what an objective world requires. If every additional observer discovered a new incompatible description, coordination would be impossible. Classical reality is stable enough that most observations are redundant rather than revelatory.

There is a lovely inversion here. In science, redundancy can look inefficient. Why measure the same quantity again if we already know it? In the physical emergence of objectivity, inefficiency is the feature. Nature overproduces certain records so thoroughly that no privileged channel is needed.

The cup is not objective because there is one perfect photon. It is objective because there are too many mediocre ones.

A macroscopic world's robustness comes partly from this excess.

Destroy one record and others remain. Close one eye and the cup persists. Block one scattering direction and light leaves another way. The environmental archive is fault tolerant.

This resembles error correction without being the same mechanism. A classical repetition code protects a bit by storing multiple copies. Environmental redundancy protects access to a classical property because many fragments carry correlated information. The system's state may continue evolving, the fragments may disperse, and no engineer arranged a decoding circuit. Yet an embedded observer benefits from the same broad principle: repeated information is harder to lose.

This is one reason the environment can generate an objective world even though no observer controls it.

The public fact is distributed.

Distributed systems engineers know the advantages and hazards of this architecture. Replication improves availability but raises consistency problems. Multiple copies can lag, conflict, or preserve stale state. Quantum Darwinism's clean models often avoid such headaches because environmental records are created by a common interaction over short timescales and the pointer variable is robust. Real physical environments can be messier. Records propagate at finite speed. The system can change between scattering events. Fragments can interact. Some channels carry old information.

Even classical objectivity has latency.

Look at the Sun and you see a record roughly eight minutes old. Look at Andromeda and you receive photons that left about two and a half million years ago. The environment broadcasts state, but broadcast is not instantaneous. “The star is there” is shorthand for “the radiation reaching us is consistent with a source that occupied that state when the radiation departed, under the model we have for propagation.”

Ordinary life collapses these distinctions because nearby light travel times are tiny. Cosmology cannot afford the convenience.

Redundancy therefore lives inside a causal structure.

A system's records spread outward. Different regions of the environment contain information about different moments. An observer's world is a mosaic assembled from retarded signals.

This is not a flaw in objectivity. It is what objectivity can mean in a relativistic universe.

The fact that many observers can agree does not require a single cosmic present stamped across space. It requires compatible records under the causal relations available to them.

Quantum Darwinism is usually developed without making relativity its central concern, but the two subjects meet naturally in this emphasis on records. Physics never gives us God's live dashboard. It gives local interactions and signals.

The classical world is locally syndicated.

That word—local—also prevents an easy exaggeration. A property can be highly redundant in one environment and inaccessible elsewhere. A small illuminated object on Earth may scatter enough photons to make its position locally objective, but those photons spread and dilute. Far away, the record becomes too weak. The environment does not necessarily contain useful copies everywhere.

Objectivity has a spatial footprint.

It also has a temporal one. Records decay. Photons travel on, are absorbed, or become mixed with other radiation. Air molecules collide again. A footprint erodes. A magnetic bit flips. An environmental fragment that once carried distinguishable information may cease to be locally useful after further interactions.

Publicity is produced, propagated, and eventually degraded.

The redundancy at a given moment is therefore not a permanent property of the system alone. It belongs to the joint dynamics of system and environment.

This is where human record-making departs sharply from passive environmental scattering.

We build devices whose purpose is to freeze a transient redundancy into a durable one. A camera gathers photons that would otherwise disappear into walls and turns them into persistent electrical or chemical state. A seismograph converts ground motion into a trace. A telescope records photons from a source that may no longer exist. An archive copies the trace again. Civilization creates persistence across timescales the original natural records would not survive.

A species that makes archives is manipulating redundancy through time.

That may be our most defensible “role” in the universe: not to observe reality into existence, but to extend the lifetime of certain records.

The claim is contingent. Plenty of information persists without us. Fossils, ice cores, sediment layers, isotopic ratios, and planetary surfaces are natural archives. DNA is a record shaped by evolutionary processes. Light crossing interstellar space can survive millions or billions of years. Humans did not invent memory.

We did invent libraries that know they are libraries.

We can decide that a record is rare and deliberately replicate it. We can preserve a measurement because future observers will not be able to repeat the event. We can compare independent records and mark their provenance. We can build redundancy strategically rather than merely suffer it.

This capacity matters because natural redundancy is uneven.

A solar eclipse repeats in related forms; a particular supernova does not. A species can go extinct before anyone sequences it. A particle collision at a collider lasts a fraction of a second; the detector and data system create the durable public object scientists actually analyze. A spacecraft flyby may be humanity's only close look at a body for decades. In each case, scientific institutions convert ephemeral interaction into persistent shared evidence.

Modern physics is full of one-time events made repeatable at the level of records.

LIGO does not replay a black-hole merger. It stores the strain data. Astronomers do not ask a gamma-ray burst to occur again on command. They coordinate telescopes, issue alerts, and preserve photons as calibrated measurements. High-energy experiments cannot rerun the exact microscopic history of one collision; they produce enormous redundant datasets of comparable events and retain detector records.

Science works because objectivity can attach to records of unrepeatable events, not only to repeatable events themselves.

That observation broadens the Darwinistic intuition. Public reality does not require the source to remain available. Enough stable, independent, interpretable records can support agreement after the source has vanished.

But now a problem appears.

Redundancy can persist after truth has changed.

A printed map may be obsolete while thousands of copies remain. A database replica can faithfully preserve a wrong entry. A medical guideline can continue circulating after new evidence weakens it. A scientific citation can be copied from paper to paper without anyone rereading the original experiment. Human-built redundancy has memory, and memory can become inertia.

The physical environment is not immune to stale information either. Light from a dead star keeps traveling. Fossils describe organisms that no longer live. But human systems add a layer of interpretation. We may mistake a durable record of a past state for evidence of a present one.

Objectivity needs timestamps.

This is so mundane that metaphysics rarely mentions it and engineering cannot survive without it.

A record answers not merely “what?” but “when?” A high-redundancy fact with a missing time coordinate can be dangerously ambiguous.

Quantum Darwinism's formal models typically know the evolution parameter. Real observers have to reconstruct it.

The same goes for source identity. If many fragments are truly independent consequences of one system, redundancy is evidentiary. If they are copies of one fragment, the apparent redundancy overstates the number of contacts with the system.

This difference is negligible for photons scattering independently from a macroscopic object under a clean model. It becomes central in human information networks.

A thousand articles can cite one study.

A thousand accounts can repost one photograph.

A thousand models can train on descendants of the same dataset.

The count of copies is not the count of witnesses.

Physical objectivity taught us to trust repeated access because natural redundant records often originate from repeated interactions with the source. Digital systems detach the two quantities. They can create enormous representational redundancy at near-zero cost without increasing empirical redundancy at all.

We now live in a world where the shape of a Darwinistic plateau can be simulated socially.

Take a small sample of your information environment and you already encounter the same claim. Take another sample and the claim appears again. The repetition feels like objective saturation. Yet all fragments may descend from one assertion.

This is not an argument that quantum Darwinism explains misinformation. It emphatically does not. It is an argument that our epistemic intuitions evolved in a physical environment where redundancy had a different genealogy.

The mismatch is worth studying.

In the natural classical world, repeated signals usually cost repeated interactions, energy, matter, and causal contact. In a networked symbolic world, repeated signals can cost a database query.

The brain still counts encounters.

A public fact in the physical sense is supported by enough disjoint fragments that observers can learn it independently. A public claim in the social sense may be supported by enough disjoint screens that observers think they learned it independently.

The difference is provenance.

This book will return to that later. For now, the physics itself has one more twist. High mutual information between a system and many environmental fragments can look like redundancy, but not all information is equally accessible. Some of it may remain quantum. Some fragments may be correlated with one another in ways that undermine their independence. A plateau can sometimes flatter us.

Researchers noticed.

The next generation of objectivity frameworks asked for something stronger than “many fragments contain information.” They asked whether observers can independently extract the same classical information without disturbance.

That demand leads toward spectrum broadcast structures and strong quantum Darwinism.

Before that machinery, though, it is worth appreciating what redundancy has already done.

A world does not become common because every observer touches the same object.

It becomes common because the object leaves enough consequences that nobody has to.
