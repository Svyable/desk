# The Plateau

A graph can change the kind of question a field knows how to ask.

For quantum Darwinism, one of those graphs is almost childishly simple. Put the fraction of the environment on the horizontal axis. Put the information that fraction contains about the system on the vertical axis. Start with nothing and collect more of the environment.

What shape does the curve make?

If the environment merely swallowed quantum coherence into one inseparable mess, the information might remain inaccessible until an observer gathered most of the surroundings. If instead the environment redundantly records a preferred classical observable, the curve rises rapidly. A small fragment gives nearly all the information ordinary observers care about. Then the curve goes flat.

That flat region is the plateau.

It is one of the central signatures of quantum Darwinism because it turns the fuzzy notion of “many copies” into an information-theoretic pattern.

Imagine a system with one bit of classical information: pointer left or pointer right. If a tiny fraction of the environment already gives you almost one bit about the answer, then adding another similarly sized fraction tells you very little new. The second fragment repeats the information. So does the third. The plateau persists across a large range of fragment sizes.

Only when the observer captures nearly the entire environment can the mutual information rise again toward what is available in the complete pure system-environment state. That final rise can contain information tied to quantum correlations inaccessible in ordinary local fragments.

The graph therefore separates two worlds in one picture.

The plateau is the public world: cheap, redundant, locally accessible.

The final rise is the expensive remainder: global, correlation-heavy, quantum.

This is elegant enough to create trouble.

Elegant criteria attract overinterpretation because a clean graph looks like a solved concept. If the mutual information has a plateau, have we derived objectivity? Not necessarily.

Mutual information counts correlations. It does not, by itself, tell us that those correlations are all classical or independently accessible in the way ordinary objectivity demands. Quantum correlations can contribute. Environmental fragments may share information in ways that prevent multiple observers from simply reading the same property without disturbance.

The plateau can be real and still not be the whole story.

This became one motivation for stronger formulations of Darwinistic objectivity.

The original intuition is operational: many observers should be able to intercept separate fragments, learn the same state of the system, and agree without appreciably disturbing the system or one another's records. That is stricter than saying the fragments have mutual information with the system.

A locked safe can be correlated with a secret without making the secret public.

Accessibility matters.

Quantum information theory has tools for distinguishing total correlations from classical information that can actually be extracted by local measurements. Quantum discord, Holevo information, conditional mutual information, and related quantities enter because the architecture of correlations matters, not only their magnitude.

This is where the subject becomes technical enough that popular summaries usually retreat to pictures of branching trees.

It is worth staying.

Suppose the system and a fragment are entangled. Their mutual information can be large, but an observer measuring the fragment may not be able to extract all that information as a classical record of a preferred system observable. Some of the correlation exists only in the joint quantum state. If another observer measures another fragment, their actions may not fit the independence condition we want.

A classical world needs information that can be shared without a conference call among all the witnesses.

Strong quantum Darwinism was developed to capture more of this requirement. The rough demand is that a fragment contain essentially all the system information in an accessible classical form, with the relevant quantum discord suppressed. Spectrum broadcast structures approach the same issue through the state itself: the system's preferred classical alternatives are correlated with distinguishable states in multiple environmental fragments so observers can identify the alternative independently.

Different roads, same dissatisfaction.

The plateau was necessary evidence of redundancy. It was not always sufficient evidence of objectivity.

This is science behaving well. A seductive criterion gets examined, edge cases appear, the definition tightens.

The history should make us cautious about philosophical conclusions built from one diagnostic. Quantum foundations has a long tradition of words—measurement, information, observer, reality—being asked to support more weight than their formal definitions carry.

A graph of mutual information does not contain a metaphysics.

What it does contain is a powerful clue about scale.

If the plateau begins after a tiny environmental fraction, then ordinary observers can remain radically ignorant of the total system-environment state while still agreeing about the system's classical properties. Classical consensus requires surprisingly little data.

That is why the environment can support so many simultaneous observers.

Nobody needs the whole light field around the cup. Each observer can throw almost all of it away.

The redundancy is the inverse of the needed fraction, roughly speaking. If one percent of the environment suffices, perhaps on the order of a hundred disjoint fragments can independently convey the same classical information. If one millionth suffices, the redundancy can be enormous.

For everyday illuminated macroscopic systems, theoretical estimates can indeed become enormous because scattering events are so numerous.

The scale difference is the important fact, not any single headline number.

Classical information can become available from microscopic samples of a macroscopic environment.

This helps explain why observers do not fight over evidence physically. I can see the mountain without stealing the photons you needed. The mountain sends enough.

Scarcity returns when the record is weak.

Astronomers compete for telescope time because distant sources deliver limited photons into a detector. Particle physicists build huge accelerators because rare events require enormous data. Biologists preserve scarce specimens because the environment did not generate unlimited accessible copies. Historians argue over one surviving letter because symbolic redundancy collapsed over time.

Objectivity is easiest when the plateau starts early.

That principle travels surprisingly well if we resist pretending the equations travel with it.

In an information-rich domain, a small independent sample may reproduce the central fact. In an information-poor domain, every additional fragment matters. Human institutions often confuse the two regimes.

A viral claim creates an early apparent plateau: sample one corner of the network and you see it; sample another and you see it again. But the fragments may not be independent. They can be descendants of one source. The plateau measures saturation of distribution, not redundancy of evidence.

This difference gives provenance a mathematical flavor even without importing quantum formulas.

What we care about is not the number of copies but the number of conditionally independent paths back to the event.

If ten articles all cite one press release, there may be one evidentiary fragment in ten representational packages. If ten laboratories independently measure the same physical constant with different apparatuses and calibrations, the redundancy is much stronger.

Science tries to manufacture the second kind.

Replication is expensive because independent contact with reality is expensive.

Copying is cheap.

This cost difference may be one of the most important facts about modern knowledge systems. The natural physical environment spends energy and interactions to create redundancy. Digital networks can duplicate a claim at negligible marginal cost. Our information landscape can therefore produce arbitrarily broad plateaus unsupported by new empirical contact.

Consensus becomes easier to counterfeit than it was in the ancestral world.

Again, quantum Darwinism does not explain social belief. But it provides a useful contrast class: in the physical model, redundancy is created because many environmental subsystems independently become correlated with the system. In the social counterfeit, many subsystems become correlated with one another.

Common cause versus copying chain.

The difference can be invisible at the surface.

This is why source tracing matters more as networks become denser. A good epistemic system should discount descendant copies and reward independent contact. Citation networks, provenance metadata, experimental replication, data lineage, and audit trails are attempts to recover that structure.

They are ways of asking whether the plateau is real.

There is another lesson in the shape of the quantum curve.

The plateau does not reach complete information.

It reaches the information that has become classically accessible. The inaccessible remainder survives in global correlations. An observer who stops at the plateau knows enough to navigate the classical world and not enough to reconstruct the quantum whole.

This is a profound warning against equating agreement with completeness.

A billion observers can agree about the pointer state and still share the same blindness to complementary information.

Consensus can be objectively correct and radically partial.

This matters in ordinary science too. A mature field may converge on a robust variable while leaving deeper descriptions unresolved. Everyone agrees on the boiling point under specified conditions without having solved quantum many-body dynamics of liquid water from first principles. Engineers can agree on a material's stress limits without possessing a complete microscopic history of every defect. Objectivity often attaches to coarse variables long before theory is complete.

The plateau is where reliable action lives.

The remaining information may be real and scientifically important without being necessary for ordinary prediction.

This offers a defense of effective theories. Physics does not fail when it describes the stable public variables at one scale rather than the full microscopic state. The world itself privileges some compressed descriptions through robustness and accessibility.

That does not make every coarse description equally good. The environment's records constrain which ones are stable.

The plateau has a basis.

If we ask the wrong observable, the redundancy may disappear. The environment does not redundantly encode arbitrary superpositions of pointer states. The accessible information is selective.

A world can therefore be information-rich and question-poor.

It will answer the same few questions from many fragments and remain silent about others.

This is perhaps the most important conceptual move in the book.

We usually treat observation as a neutral act applied to an already furnished world. Quantum Darwinism suggests that the world available to local observers has been preformatted by interaction. Some observables arrive with enormous redundancy. Other features require heroic global control.

The public universe has defaults.

Human beings live inside those defaults and then build symbolic systems that create new ones. Search engines decide which records become easy to encounter. Scientific databases decide which variables are standardized. Governments decide which events become official statistics. Sensors decide what is logged. Cameras decide what enters the archive. Machine-learning systems learn from whatever survived these selection processes.

A civilization has partial-information plots too, though no single curve captures them.

For some facts, tiny samples of the public record are enough. The date of a national election is redundantly stored. For others, crucial information exists in one neglected archive or one uninstrumented community. Visibility is not the same as importance.

The danger begins when we confuse early plateau with total reality.

What is easy to know becomes what we think there is to know.

The quantum case tells us this confusion would be a category error. The plateau deliberately excludes the global quantum remainder. Its power comes from compression, not completeness.

A classical observer is competent because they ignore almost everything.

A civilization may need the same humility.

We should value redundant public facts while remembering the hidden remainder: unmeasured variables, rare records, minority observations, inaccessible correlations, destroyed provenance, transient events, private experience, and phenomena our sensors were never built to notice.

The world that gets copied is not necessarily the whole world.

The plateau is proof.
