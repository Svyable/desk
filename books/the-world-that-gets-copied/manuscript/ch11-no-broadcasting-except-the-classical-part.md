# No Broadcasting, Except the Classical Part

There is a wonderfully severe theorem hiding beneath the softness of the word “objective.”

If many independent observers are to carry the same information, quantum mechanics limits what that information can be.

The no-broadcasting theorem says, roughly, that a general collection of quantum states cannot be distributed into multiple subsystems so that each subsystem separately retains the original state. Broadcasting is possible for commuting families—the states that behave like classical probability distributions in a shared basis. Noncommuting quantum information refuses the arrangement.

Objectivity therefore has a compatibility condition built into the theory.

The public part has to become classical enough to be shareable.

This is not merely the no-cloning theorem with a new label. Cloning asks for independent exact copies, usually pure states. Broadcasting allows correlations among the recipients and is formulated for mixed states as well. The impossibility still reaches beyond pure-state cloning. Arbitrary quantum information cannot be made locally available to many parties while preserving what each party would need to regard their share as the original state.

The restriction is fundamental.

Classical information is different because a known set of mutually distinguishable alternatives can be copied into many records. A bit can control a fleet of target bits. A pointer value can correlate with many environmental fragments. Everyone receives the label.

No one receives the full quantum secret.

This creates a hard boundary between “many observers know the same thing” and “many observers possess the system.”

In the classical world we casually blur those. A photograph is treated as a copy of a scene. A database replica is treated as another instance of the same information. A printed score is treated as the score. Because classical information can be proliferated cheaply, we forget that copying is a special physical privilege.

Quantum theory revokes the privilege for most states.

A quantum state can contain information that is operationally meaningful and still cannot be released into the environment in arbitrarily many readable copies.

This private quantum remainder is one reason objectivity cannot mean complete information.

To make the full state objective in the ordinary sense would require many observers to independently obtain enough information to reconstruct it without collectively destroying the conditions of the state. Quantum mechanics blocks that in general. The most that can become redundantly public is a restricted classical sector.

This is where spectrum broadcast structures enter with unusual force.

Instead of looking only at information curves, researchers including Jarosław Korbicz and the Horodeckis asked what the joint system-environment state must look like if objectivity really holds. The resulting structure has an intuitive form. The system is in a classical mixture of preferred states. For each system alternative, separate environmental fragments occupy conditional states that are distinguishable from those associated with other alternatives. An observer can measure their fragment, identify the system's classical label, and leave other fragments available for other observers.

The environment contains many distinguishable echoes of one classical variable.

The echoes need not be identical. They need to be locally readable and correlated with the same system alternative.

This is stronger than a loose claim that the system and environment share a lot of information. It builds nondisturbing accessibility into the state structure.

Objectivity becomes a special geometry of correlations.

The phrase “spectrum broadcast structure” is not destined for airport bookstores. The idea deserves a wider audience anyway because it clarifies something philosophers have argued over for centuries: public facts are not facts every observer contains in full. They are facts encoded so observers can independently recover the same label from their local evidence.

The world is shareable because the records commute.

That is too compressed, but it points in the right direction.

Commutativity is what allows a classical probability distribution to exist over alternatives without measurement order creating the same trouble it does for incompatible quantum observables. When the environment broadcasts a pointer observable, it has effectively selected a common classical language.

Observers can disagree about other questions and still agree in that language.

This is more subtle than saying decoherence “makes things classical.” Decoherence suppresses interference in a preferred basis. Spectrum broadcast structure asks whether the preferred information has also been deposited into multiple locally distinguishable records.

Classicality plus publicity.

The distinction matters because a decohered system can lack redundant records and a correlated environment can lack the right classical structure. Objectivity is the intersection.

There is an experimental consequence. To fully verify such a structure in an engineered system, one would ideally reconstruct enough of the system-environment state to test both the classical correlation and the distinguishability of conditional fragment states. Full tomography becomes expensive as the number of environmental components grows. This is one reason researchers have proposed witnesses of non-objectivity and more scalable diagnostics.

The foundations problem collides with the exponential cost of quantum characterization.

There is poetry in that. To prove that a world is objectively classical, the experimentalist may need access to precisely the quantum correlations ordinary observers never see.

The classical appearance is cheap. Certifying its quantum origin is expensive.

Experiments in 2019 and subsequent work have begun to demonstrate pieces of this architecture using photonic simulators and solid-state spin environments. They show redundant information emerging under controlled interactions. But laboratory demonstrations are necessarily small, engineered, and far from a derivation of the classical universe in all its messiness.

The distinction between demonstration and completion matters.

A six-photon experiment can test the mechanism without proving that every chair in the universe owes its objectivity to one unique formalism. An NV center coupled to nuclear spins can show redundant environmental records without resolving every interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Good experiments isolate a claim.

The no-broadcasting boundary makes the isolated claim especially interesting: whatever proliferates into many independent records has shed the features that made arbitrary quantum states unbroadcastable.

The environment is a classicalization machine in the informational sense.

Not because it changes the fundamental laws. Because it selects which information becomes local and repeatable.

This suggests a sharp way to think about measurement.

Measurement is not simply extraction. It is the construction of a broadcastable record.

The microscopic system may begin with incompatible possibilities. The apparatus couples a chosen observable to a pointer. Decoherence stabilizes pointer alternatives. Amplification produces many correlated degrees of freedom. The environment carries copies of the pointer information outward. By the time a human reads the display, the result is already massively redundant.

A successful measurement is an information phase transition from private quantum structure to public classical record.

That phrase is mine, not a technical theorem. It captures the direction without pretending there is a literal thermodynamic phase boundary.

What matters is the change in copyability.

Before measurement, the relevant quantum information cannot be freely distributed. After measurement, a classical outcome can be repeated on screens, stored in memory, printed, spoken, and inspected by many observers.

The apparatus does more than tell us what happened. It changes the social life of the information.

This is why laboratory records become so mundane after extraordinary quantum events. A Bell test ends in bit strings. A superconducting qubit readout becomes voltages. A photon count becomes an integer. The quantum resource is consumed or transformed to produce classical evidence.

Science happens on the broadcast side.

The paper does not contain the entangled photons. It contains public consequences.

There is a deep limit here for dreams of total archival capture. If reality contains nonclassical information that cannot be broadcast into independent records, then no classical archive can preserve the universe in full. An archive can preserve measurement outcomes, settings, reconstructed states, classical descriptions, perhaps quantum states if maintained in quantum memory under controlled conditions—but it cannot turn arbitrary quantum reality into unlimited classical copies without loss.

The record is always a projection.

This may sound obvious, but human culture increasingly behaves as though sufficiently large storage can eliminate selectivity. Log everything. Record every interaction. Mirror every dataset. Train on every document. Build a digital twin.

Storage volume does not repeal the structure of measurement.

Even before practical constraints, the act of turning physical events into classical records chooses observables, resolutions, thresholds, and timing. The archive contains what the sensors were built to broadcast.

A perfect classical log of the wrong variables is still blind.

Quantum theory gives this blindness a foundational example. The environment makes certain pointer information public precisely by not making the full quantum state public.

The world's legibility is purchased with omission.

This is one reason the phrase “data is reality” should make a physicist uncomfortable. Data are stable classical records produced through interactions. Their power comes from reproducibility and shareability, not completeness.

The same is true of a photograph, census, genome sequence, telescope image, economic indicator, and clinical chart.

Public records are selected survivals.

The no-broadcasting theorem prevents us from pretending otherwise at the quantum floor.

There is also a hopeful side.

Because classical information can be broadcast, knowledge can become communal. A measurement result need not remain trapped in one observer. Once it is encoded in stable classical degrees of freedom, it can proliferate essentially without quantum restriction. Culture is physically possible because records can be copied.

Books exist on the permissive side of no-broadcasting.

This sentence exists because countless electrons, magnetic domains, photons, and storage cells can instantiate the same classical pattern. You can read it without erasing my copy. A library can lend a book and keep another. A scientific archive can mirror data across continents.

Civilization depends on the cheap promiscuity of classical information.

The quantum world underneath is not so generous.

That contrast suggests a more grounded way to talk about the human role. We are not privileged observers who complete quantum mechanics. We are organisms built out of broadcast-friendly macroscopic states who discovered how to engineer additional broadcast layers. We take already classicalized records and copy them across time and space.

Our distinctive power is not observation. The environment had that covered.

Our power is deliberate rebroadcast.

We can choose which public facts deserve more descendants.

We can also choose badly.

A lie, once represented classically, is just as copyable as a truth. Quantum mechanics does not attach a moral checksum to bits. The no-broadcasting theorem protects quantum states, not societies.

So the same physical freedom that permits science and libraries permits spam, propaganda, forged images, recursive model output, and rumor cascades.

Classical copyability is civilization's substrate and its vulnerability.

The environment solves objectivity by correlating many fragments with a source. Human networks can skip the source and correlate fragments with one another.

The theorem does not care.

We have to.

Before reaching that second-order broadcast world, the physics deserves one more close look. What does an objective joint state actually look like, and why did researchers need a stronger concept than the original Darwinistic plateau?

The answer is where the classical part stops being a metaphor and becomes a structure.
