# Reconstruction Is an Argument

A replica is persuasive in a way a footnote is not.

Turn a crank and the pointers move. The Moon changes phase. A dial advances. Gears turn without collision. The mechanism feels solved because motion is such a powerful form of evidence.

But a reconstruction is still an argument.

Every replica of the Antikythera Mechanism contains choices. Some parts reproduce surviving dimensions and tooth counts closely. Some restore missing plates. Some choose one interpretation among competing gear trains. Some build the front planetary display according to a particular model. Materials, tolerances, bearing methods, pointer arrangements, case construction, and scale can vary.

The strongest reconstructions advertise those choices rather than hiding them.

Experimental reconstruction is indispensable because mechanical proposals need contact with reality. A gear train that works on paper may bind when plates flex. Arbors can collide. A pointer may require clearance the diagram forgot. Tolerances can accumulate. Physical construction exposes design problems that mathematical fit alone misses.

Yet there are two very different questions a replica can answer.

Could this design work?

Was this the design?

The first can often be tested by building it. The second requires the artifact.

Antikythera research has benefited enormously from people willing to build competing models, because the act of construction reveals where hypotheses are mechanically extravagant or elegant. Michael Wright's reconstructions, later UCL models, and other physical and digital efforts are part of the evidentiary history. Their disagreements show which regions of the mechanism remain underconstrained.

This is healthy science. A damaged object should generate families of models until evidence eliminates them.

Public presentation makes that harder because museums and documentaries need something visible. A half-transparent confidence map is less satisfying than a gleaming complete device. The viewer rarely sees which gear is original, which is inferred strongly, and which exists because the reconstruction needs an answer where the artifact gives none.

A book can do what a display case struggles to do: keep several states of knowledge alive at once.

The reconstruction chapter should therefore operate like an engineering drawing with epistemic tolerances. Some dimensions are measured. Some are bounded. Some are estimated. Some are selected.

This matters especially for extraordinary-history claims. A complete modern replica can be cited by fringe writers as though its entire architecture had been recovered from the wreck. The visual authority of the finished machine then launders speculation into fact.

The antidote is not to stop reconstructing. It is to label the joints.

There is a larger principle here. Historical reconstruction is unavoidable. We do it with buildings, languages, genomes, climates, battles, extinct animals, and lost technologies. The question is not whether inference enters the picture. It is whether the path from surviving evidence to restored whole remains visible.

Antikythera deserves reconstructions that move. It also deserves reconstructions whose uncertainty moves with them.
