# Bronze That Refused to Be Sculpture

A bronze statue can lose an arm and still be legible. A machine can lose a washer and become a riddle.

That asymmetry shaped the early life of the Antikythera fragments. The surviving material is not a handsome instrument waiting to be dusted off. It is a set of corroded fragments, warped and mineralized, with plates laminated together, inscriptions faint or hidden, teeth incomplete, axes displaced, and large portions simply gone. The object we call the Antikythera Mechanism is partly an artifact and partly a forensic reconstruction of an artifact.

That distinction is easy to flatten in photographs of modern replicas. A replica has clean brass, confident pointers, symmetrical dial plates, labels that can be read, and gears whose tooth counts are the numbers chosen by the rebuilder. The original survives as evidence of a much harsher kind. A gear with most of its circumference present may support a tooth count strongly. A broken plate with a line of letters may narrow a function. A missing gear cannot be counted at all.

The mechanism's modern history therefore resembles the history of a cold case more than the history of a preserved clock. Every new instrument changes what can be asked of the same physical fragments. Early observers could see obvious gears. Radiographs revealed gear trains beneath surfaces. Later microfocus X-ray computed tomography made it possible to examine internal structure and hidden inscriptions in three dimensions. Polynomial texture mapping and related imaging methods helped make faint surface lettering more legible. The machine did not change; the evidentiary aperture did.

This is one reason Antikythera is dangerous territory for both skeptics and enthusiasts. The skeptic can look at a fragmentary object and underestimate what careful inference can establish. The enthusiast can look at a reconstruction and forget how much of it is inference.

The right posture is less emotionally satisfying. Some portions of the mechanism are constrained tightly. Others are not. Researchers have strong evidence for several rear-dial functions, for eclipse prediction, for calendrical cycles, and for a mechanical representation of lunar anomaly. The front, especially the missing planetary display and its gearing, carries more reconstruction burden. A model can be elegant, fit inscriptions, fit available space, use plausible ancient astronomical period relations, and still remain a model rather than a recovered assembly.

This is not a weakness unique to Antikythera. Archaeology runs on constrained reconstruction. Roofs vanish while postholes remain. Wooden superstructures rot while stone footings survive. Texts are copied through medieval manuscripts long after originals disappear. Ship timbers preserve in one sediment and vanish in another. Every historical argument has a preservation filter whether the historian foregrounds it or not.

Antikythera simply makes the filter impossible to ignore because the object is mechanically specific. Gear teeth are integers. Shafts must occupy space. Two solid gears cannot pass through each other. A proposed train must physically connect input to output. Astronomy supplies period relationships; surviving inscriptions supply words and numbers; the fragments supply dimensions and tooth counts. The reconstruction problem is hemmed in from several directions at once.

That is why the object is more useful than a mystery. Mysteries are allowed to remain atmospheric. Machines are rude. Eventually the teeth have to mesh.
