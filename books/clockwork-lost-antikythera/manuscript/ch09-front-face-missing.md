# Front Face, Missing

The most seductive part of the Antikythera Mechanism is the part that is mostly gone.

The rear dials survive well enough to make strong claims about calendrical and eclipse functions. The front is harder. Inscriptions indicate that the mechanism represented the motions of the Sun, Moon, and the five planets known in antiquity. Surviving components and text constrain the design. But much of the gearing that would have produced a complete planetary display does not survive.

This is where reconstruction becomes intellectually dangerous because the missing part is exactly the part a modern audience most wants to see. A compact mechanical cosmos with planetary pointers is irresistible. Once rendered beautifully in brass or animation, it acquires the psychological weight of an artifact.

The 2021 Scientific Reports model by Tony Freeth and colleagues is serious work precisely because it does not pretend the front survived. It builds a reconstruction from inscriptions, known astronomical period relations, surviving physical constraints, available space, and a set of explicit reconstruction principles. The authors also state where direct gearing evidence is missing.

That should be the model for how the public talks about the machine: confidence attached to components, not sprayed over the whole reconstruction.

A proposed gear train can be good for several different reasons. It may fit the surviving case depth. It may use tooth counts compatible with astronomical periods named in inscriptions. It may route outputs through plausible shafts. It may reproduce known Greek cosmological ordering. It may use mechanical devices already evidenced elsewhere in the mechanism. Each success increases plausibility. None causes lost bronze to reappear.

Competing reconstructions are therefore not an embarrassment. They are a consequence of missing evidence. Michael Wright, Price, Freeth and collaborators, and other researchers have proposed different arrangements over decades as imaging and interpretation changed. The history of those models is itself a map of what the artifact constrains.

A weak mystery book would treat disagreement as proof that experts know nothing. A weak triumphalist book would treat the newest model as the final machine. The useful position is between them. Experts can know a great deal while still disagreeing where evidence runs out.

The front face also illuminates a general problem in reverse engineering. Outputs do not uniquely determine internal architecture. If you know what a device must display, there may be several mechanisms that produce the same motion. Engineering inference becomes stronger when dimensions, surviving bearings, tooth counts, inscriptions, wear patterns, and manufacturing conventions eliminate alternatives.

This is one reason chain-of-custody language belongs in archaeology too. Not because the mechanism is a criminal exhibit, but because each inference needs a traceable path from material to conclusion. What is observed? What is measured? What is restored? What is supplied by an ancient text? What is imported from a known astronomical model? What is chosen because it solves a packing problem elegantly?

When those layers are separated, the front of Antikythera becomes more impressive, not less. We can watch modern researchers reason into an absence without pretending the absence has vanished.

The missing face is not a hole in the story to be patched. It is one of the story's main instruments.
