# Teeth Inside Corrosion

A gear tooth is a tiny piece of historical evidence with an unusually bad personality. It wants a whole number.

That is helpful. Much of ancient history arrives as language, and language bends. A gear ratio is less accommodating. If one wheel has a particular number of teeth and drives another, the ratio constrains the motion. Add enough surviving wheels and the machine begins to disclose what kinds of periodic relationships it was built to carry.

The Antikythera Mechanism survives with roughly thirty recognizable gears, depending on how one counts fragments and components. Some tooth counts are secure enough to anchor reconstructions. Others have been debated because corrosion destroys edges and makes direct counting difficult. Early work by Derek de Solla Price and Charalambos Karakalos used radiography to look through the concreted mass. Later microfocus CT greatly improved the view.

The important thing is not merely that gears exist. Gears had long histories before Antikythera. The shock is the density of mathematical intention packed into them. The mechanism does not use gearing simply to transfer rotation from a crank to a pointer. Ratios embody astronomical cycles. A turn of an input can advance a calendar, a lunar phase indication, an eclipse-prediction scheme, and other displays at different rates.

Once that is understood, the machine stops looking like an isolated feat of metalworking and becomes a materialized table of relationships. Numbers that could live in astronomical texts or computational procedures have been assigned teeth and axes.

This is also where some of the most seductive overstatement begins. If a gear train represents a sophisticated cycle, it is tempting to attribute all of the underlying astronomy to the instrument maker. That need not be true. Technologies often combine knowledge produced elsewhere. A person capable of cutting a gear accurately does not have to discover the celestial period the gear represents. A mathematician does not have to know how to file a bronze tooth. A workshop can embody a theory inherited from generations of observers.

Antikythera therefore points toward a network of competence rather than a solitary genius. Somebody had to know astronomical period relations. Somebody had to design the train. Somebody had to lay out circles, divide them, mark teeth, cut plates, drill bearings, write inscriptions, assemble the device, test interference, and make corrections. One person might have done several of those things. We do not know.

The machine's compactness makes the social problem sharper. Precision accumulates. Small errors in tooth spacing, arbor placement, plate flatness, and clearance can turn a beautiful diagram into a box that jams. Ancient craftsmanship is often discussed as though skilled hands were magic. They were not. Repeatable skill implies tools, habits, tolerances, inspection, training, and a material culture in which errors can be noticed before expensive work is ruined.

We do not possess that workshop. We possess its consequence.

There is a useful humility in counting teeth. It forces the argument down from civilizational adjectives to things somebody actually had to make.
