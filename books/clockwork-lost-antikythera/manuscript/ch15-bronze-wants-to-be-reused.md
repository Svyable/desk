# Bronze Wants to Be Reused

A technological tradition can disappear without anyone suppressing it.

Bronze is enough.

The metal in the Antikythera Mechanism was valuable before it became archaeological evidence. A broken astronomical calculator would not necessarily be treated as a sacred archive of human ingenuity. It could be treated as bronze. Plates could be cut down. Gears could become scrap. Metal could be melted and recast into tools, vessels, fittings, weapons, statues, coins, or parts of other machines.

This simple economic fact should sit beside every claim about the uniqueness of the mechanism.

Uniqueness in the surviving archaeological record has several possible meanings. The object may truly have been unique. It may have been one of a handful. It may have belonged to a narrow specialist tradition. Comparable devices may have existed but been recycled. They may have been lost in contexts that destroyed them. They may survive as fragments that have not been recognized. Different explanations can coexist.

Survivorship bias is often invoked too casually, as if saying “most things don't survive” permits us to populate antiquity with anything we want. It does not. Missing evidence is not positive evidence for a preferred technology.

The right use of survivorship bias is narrower. It tells us how strongly absence should count against a hypothesis. If an object is made of material with high recycling value, contains perishable components, and is unlikely to enter burials or other protected deposits, then failure to find many examples is weaker evidence of rarity than it would be for something routinely made of durable stone and intentionally deposited by the thousands.

Antikythera itself calibrates that logic because the artifact exists. We know at least one sophisticated geared calculator was made. Therefore the prior claim that such a thing could not have existed in the Hellenistic world is dead. What remains open is frequency.

This distinction generalizes far beyond one machine. Histories of wood construction, textiles, organic chemistry, oral technique, ephemeral architecture, and software all suffer from asymmetric preservation. Civilizations are remembered disproportionately through what endures, not necessarily through what mattered most while they were alive.

Metal recycling makes the bias especially vicious because technological artifacts are often made from exactly the materials later societies want most.

There is also a selection effect within museums. Early excavators and collectors were more likely to recognize inscriptions, sculpture, coins, and complete prestige objects than anonymous mechanical fragments. A gear encased in corrosion can be discarded, miscataloged, or separated from related pieces before anyone understands what it is. Recognition is part of preservation.

This does not mean another Antikythera lies unnoticed in every storeroom. It means the probability is not zero, and the record cannot be interpreted as though recovery were automatic.

Lost-civilization stories exploit missing evidence by filling it with maximal fantasy. A more rigorous missing-technology history does the opposite. It asks how much can vanish through ordinary incentives.

Bronze wants to be reused. Wood wants to rot. Workshops want to keep moving. Practical knowledge often wants to live in hands rather than books.

Two thousand years later, historians want a clean archive.

Nature did not sign that contract.
