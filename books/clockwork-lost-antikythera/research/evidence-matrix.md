# Evidence Matrix

| Claim | Class | Current support | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| A complex geared astronomical mechanism existed in the Hellenistic period | 1 | Surviving Antikythera fragments; museum artifact; radiography/CT; inscriptions | Does not establish prevalence |
| Rear mechanism encoded the 19-year Metonic cycle | 1 | Surviving spiral dial, gearing, scale divisions, inscriptions; 2006/2008 Nature analyses | Exact user practices remain partly inferential |
| Lower rear dial encoded Saros eclipse recurrence | 1 | Surviving dial/glyphs/gearing; inscription studies | Not equivalent to modern local eclipse ephemerides |
| Device represented lunar anomaly mechanically | 1 | Surviving epicyclic pin-and-slot gearing and reconstruction anchored in fragment evidence | The underlying ancient physical theory was not modern celestial mechanics |
| Device tracked games/festival cycle | 1 | Inscribed Games dial; 2008 analysis | Does not by itself identify primary use or patron |
| Front displayed Sun, Moon and five known planets | 1–2 | 2016 front-cover inscription strongly supports planetary display; much implementation is missing | Full gear architecture not directly preserved |
| 2021 UCL front Cosmos is the exact original | 2/3 | Strongly constrained reconstruction model | Significant front gearing lacks direct survival; alternatives must remain discussable |
| Calendar ring had ~354/355 holes | 2 | 2020 measurements; 2024 Woan/Bayley statistical reanalysis | Model-dependent inference from incomplete ring; preserve active debate |
| Mechanism was made by Archimedes | 3 | Ancient literary tradition credits Archimedes with mechanical spheres; possible intellectual lineage | No direct artifact attribution |
| Mechanism was made by Hipparchus | 3 | Lunar theory/context sometimes linked to Hipparchan astronomy | No signature or direct maker evidence |
| Hellenistic world had an industry of Antikythera-like machines | 3 | One artifact plus literary references to related mechanical displays | No production denominator, workshop archive, or second comparable surviving mechanism |
| Bronze recycling helps explain sparse survival | 4 with strong general plausibility | Economic/material logic; comparative archaeological preservation behavior | Must be supported with ancient recycling/metal-economy scholarship before quantitative claims |
| Antikythera is a useful case of survivorship bias | 4 | One demonstrated capability against sparse comparative record | Cannot be used to infer arbitrary lost technologies |
| Non-human or lost-supercivilization origin is required | 3, rejected | No evidentiary necessity; known Hellenistic astronomy, mathematics, metallurgy and mechanics provide a human explanatory environment | Extraordinary-origin claim would require independent positive evidence |

## Contradictions to preserve

- A model can fit inscriptions and astronomical periods while still lack direct surviving gears.
- High manufacturing precision can coexist with very low production volume.
- Literary references can make mechanical astronomical devices culturally plausible without proving construction details.
- Sparse archaeological survival can result from rarity, recycling, bad preservation, recognition failure, or combinations of all four.
- Calling the mechanism an analogue computer can clarify its computational role while misleading readers into imagining a modern computer industry.

## Disinformation / incentive audit

Antikythera is not primarily an intelligence/disinformation subject, but it has strong narrative incentives. Museums and media benefit from “world's first computer” clarity; replica makers benefit from complete visual systems; fringe publishers benefit from treating uncertainty as evidence of suppression; national and civilizational storytelling can reward claims of priority; academic reconstruction teams have normal incentives to defend models they have invested in. None of these incentives invalidates evidence. They are reasons to separate artifact observations from interpretive completion.
