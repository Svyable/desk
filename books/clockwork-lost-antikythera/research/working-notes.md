# Working Notes

## Chronology

- **Hellenistic period, broadly second–first centuries BCE:** mechanism manufactured. Precise dating and workshop attribution remain debated and should be reported as ranges tied to paleography, astronomical parameters, wreck chronology, and artifact analysis rather than one false-exact year.
- **First century BCE:** ship carrying the mechanism sinks off Antikythera. Wreck chronology must be rebuilt from current numismatic, ceramic, and excavation literature.
- **1900–1901:** sponge divers discover the wreck and recover major cargo with support of Greek authorities and naval vessels.
- **1902:** Valerios Stais recognizes a gear in the corroded mechanism material, transforming interpretation of the object.
- **1905–1906:** Albert Rehm studies the mechanism and develops important early calendar/astronomical insights; recover notebooks/photographs through Bayerische Staatsbibliothek references used in modern scholarship.
- **1950s–1974:** Derek de Solla Price develops a major reconstruction programme; Charalambos Karakalos radiographs the fragments; *Gears from the Greeks* published in 1974.
- **Late twentieth century:** Michael Wright and others refine mechanical interpretations and use improved radiography / reconstruction.
- **2005:** microfocus X-ray CT and advanced surface imaging campaign creates transformational dataset.
- **2006:** Nature paper decodes rear gearing and inscriptions, including major lunar/mechanical results.
- **2008:** Nature paper establishes/strengthens interpretation of calendars, Games dial and eclipse prediction.
- **2016:** major Almagest special issue publishes surviving inscriptions systematically.
- **2021:** Scientific Reports publishes UCL front-Cosmos reconstruction model; correction later that year.
- **2024:** Woan and Bayley publish improved statistical estimate of calendar-ring hole count, favoring ~354–355 under their model.
- **2021–2025:** renewed underwater excavation programme at the wreck; 2024–2025 work includes important hull recovery and additional context.

## Strongest competing explanation

The core survivorship synthesis may be overstated. Antikythera could be close to genuinely singular: an expensive experimental masterpiece made by an unusually capable specialist or tiny lineage, with its scarcity reflecting historical scarcity more than recycling or preservation. A rare, elite prototype can still draw on broad astronomical knowledge. The final book must test that possibility rather than arguing automatically for a larger lost tradition.

## Defeat conditions

The “missing technology problem” should be narrowed if future comparative research shows that similar bronze technical instruments preserve relatively well and are genuinely absent despite intensive recovery; if manufacturing analysis indicates the mechanism could plausibly have been produced as a one-off without transferable specialist practice; or if stronger dating/workshop evidence ties the device to a uniquely documented exceptional commission. The thesis survives in narrower form even then: one artifact would still demonstrate capability while failing to establish prevalence.
