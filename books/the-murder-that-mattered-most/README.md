*250 Years of America, One Consequential Killing at a Time.*

The premise is deliberately arguable. For each calendar decade from the 1770s through the 2020s, the book chooses the killing whose consequences traveled furthest through American life. The standard is not fame, cruelty, body count, or how often a case appears on television. It is consequence: a presidency altered, a law rewritten, a movement accelerated, a community reorganized, a federal power expanded or weakened, a migration set in motion, or a national memory permanently changed.

That means some obvious true-crime cases lose. It also means several chapters are not conventional murder cases at all. They include assassinations, lynchings, massacres, wartime killings, a disappearance widely believed to have ended in murder, and a killing followed by an acquittal. The manuscript states those legal distinctions rather than laundering them through the market-facing title.

Each chapter is therefore both a story and a verdict. It reconstructs what happened, follows the institutional afterlife, considers the strongest rival cases from the decade, and asks the question history books often skip: if this death had not happened, what else would plausibly have changed?
