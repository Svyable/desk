# After the Verdicts

The calendar was always artificial.

Nobody in 1799 knew that the next killing belonged to a new decade. People do not experience history in ten-year containers. Wars cross them. Movements cross them. Court cases cross them. A murder committed in the last month of one decade can produce consequences that belong almost entirely to the next.

The calendar is useful anyway.

It forces selection.

Without that constraint, a book like this becomes a familiar anthology of famous American violence: assassinations, serial killers, lynchings, mass shootings, unsolved cases, and courtroom spectacles arranged until the pile feels comprehensive. Comprehensiveness would make the project safer and less interesting. Nobody has to defend a list that includes everything.

One verdict per decade creates an argument.

The argument is not that one person’s life was worth more than another’s.

Historical importance is not human value.

That distinction is the most important qualification in the book.

A child killed in a case that changed no law did not matter less to the people who loved her than a president whose death changed a government. A Black man lynched without national press did not have a smaller claim on justice than Emmett Till. A Chinese miner whose name disappeared from popular memory was not less human than Alexander Hamilton. The victims who never became symbols did not fail some test of significance.

What differs is the afterlife.

Some deaths encounter institutions capable of carrying them.

A newspaper.

A church.

A political party.

A family willing to speak publicly.

A movement with organizers already in place.

A federal agency looking for jurisdiction.

A president who needs a legislative argument.

A photograph that travels farther than the killer expected.

A court case that turns local violence into constitutional doctrine.

A camera connected to a network.

Those systems determine which killings become history in the public sense and which remain primarily family or local history.

That inequality of memory is one of the book’s actual subjects.

Boston’s five dead became part of the national founding story because revolutionary printers, engravers, lawyers, and political organizations knew how to use the event. More than ninety Christian Lenape people killed at Gnadenhutten never acquired comparable place in schoolbook memory because the expanding republic had little reason to center an atrocity committed by Americans against Native people.

The difference does not tell us which event was worse.

It tells us who controlled the archive.

That pattern repeats.

The friends of Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and Will Stewart included Ida B. Wells, who possessed a newspaper and the mind to turn lynching from isolated horror into documented system. The murder of Emmett Till encountered Mamie Till-Mobley, Black newspapers, migration networks between Mississippi and Chicago, and a civil-rights movement already building capacity. Vincent Chin’s death encountered communities capable of turning a local sentence into a pan-Asian American civil-rights campaign. Trayvon Martin’s death encountered social media and organizers who created a phrase that would still be waiting when George Floyd was murdered seven years later.

The murder matters.

The surviving network decides how far it travels.

That observation creates an uncomfortable bias in the project. Organized communities leave larger consequences than isolated people. Famous families command attention ordinary families do not. Presidents automatically produce constitutional consequence because the office has a succession rule. Wealth and political office create records. Marginalized communities often have to build their own archives before the rest of the country notices they exist.

A ranking based on consequence therefore risks rewarding power twice: first when society values a person or institution highly, and again when the death produces a large recorded reaction.

There is no way to eliminate that bias completely.

The better response is to make it visible.

That is why several chapters choose events whose victims were not nationally powerful at the time. Gnadenhutten, Rock Springs, the People’s Grocery lynching, the Osage murders, Moore’s Ford, Vincent Chin. Their consequence often appears not through the office a victim occupied but through what the violence revealed about the institutions around them.

The murders become stress tests.

What can federal law reach?

What happens when a local jury will not convict?

How does a government behave when a private mob claims jurisdiction?

What does citizenship mean when a person can work, pay taxes, serve in the military, or own valuable property and still be treated as foreign, incompetent, or unprotected?

What does due process mean when public opinion has already rendered a verdict?

What does police accountability mean when a citizen’s video reaches the public before an official report?

These questions make the cases consequential because the answers become institutional.

The book’s recurring question eventually became clearer to me than the original premise:

What institution learned from this murder, and what did it learn wrong?

Institutions do learn.

They learn through law, budgets, architecture, training, databases, doctrine, insurance, hiring, jurisdiction, ritual, and memory.

After McKinley’s assassination, presidential protection became systematic and permanent. After Lindbergh, federal kidnapping jurisdiction expanded. After Columbine, police active-shooter tactics and school threat-assessment systems changed. After September 11, the United States created a new cabinet department, reorganized intelligence, federalized airport screening, expanded surveillance, and entered wars.

The learning is not automatically wise.

Security can overfit the last attack.

A school can rehearse one scenario so often that it creates fear without reducing the next risk. An intelligence agency can respond to information-sharing failure by collecting so much information that signal becomes harder to find. A government can answer terrorism with authorities whose civil-liberties costs persist after the emergency. A legislature can create a criminal jurisdiction after a famous victim while leaving ordinary victims invisible.

The fact that murder changes institutions does not prove the institutional change was justified.

It proves the event altered the pressure under which choices were made.

That distinction is essential after terrorism.

The September 11 hijackers created the crisis. Americans created the Department of Homeland Security, the PATRIOT Act, the wars, the watchlists, the airport procedures, the surveillance programs, and the later reforms to those programs. Treating every post-9/11 decision as something “9/11 caused” would erase political responsibility.

Violence narrows choices psychologically.

It does not abolish choice.

The same is true in smaller cases. Fort Mims produced a demand for retaliation, but Andrew Jackson made decisions about how to fight the Creek War and how much land to demand afterward. Emmett Till’s killers produced a body; Mamie Till-Mobley made a decision about how the public would see it. George Floyd’s murder created a crisis; cities, states, police departments, activists, legislators, prosecutors, corporations, voters, and later administrations made conflicting decisions about what reform required.

Consequences are made by responders too.

That is why the book resists the idea that a killer “changed America” as though he possessed historical genius.

Most killers do not control what happens next.

John Wilkes Booth wanted vengeance for the defeated Confederacy. He helped turn Lincoln into a martyr and installed Andrew Johnson, whose Reconstruction politics were not something Booth could program. The mob that killed Elijah Lovejoy wanted to stop a press. It gave abolitionists a free-speech martyr. The men who killed Thomas Moss and his partners wanted to remove local Black economic rivals. They helped propel Ida B. Wells into an international anti-lynching campaign. Leo Frank’s lynchers wanted to restore a death sentence. Their act became one of the enduring American warnings about antisemitism and mob rule.

Violence is often strategically stupid after it succeeds tactically.

The target is dead.

The idea becomes larger.

Not always.

Sometimes terror works exactly as intended.

Colfax is the warning against romanticizing martyrdom. White supremacist violence killed Black citizens and helped make political participation physically dangerous. Federal prosecution produced *United States v. Cruikshank*, which narrowed federal reach over private violence. Reconstruction retreated. The killers did not lose simply because later historians condemned them.

Rock Springs is another reminder. Chinese miners were murdered and hundreds driven out. Federal troops could restore order and facilitate return, but local prosecution failed. Exclusionary politics continued.

Moore’s Ford remained unsolved despite a large FBI investigation. Time protected the killers better than any legal defense could have.

Historical consequence includes successful intimidation.

That is a darker proposition than the comforting idea that injustice always creates its own reform movement.

Sometimes a murder closes a future.

Hamilton never wrote the arguments he might have written after 1804. Lincoln never made the Reconstruction decisions historians assign to his counterfactual presidency. Martin Luther King Jr. never developed the Poor People’s Campaign, antiwar politics, and economic-justice agenda through the next decade. Harvey Milk never discovered what a long openly gay political career could look like. Vincent Chin never married the woman he was celebrating with days before his death.

We can follow the consequences of their absence.

We cannot recover the missing acts.

That is why counterfactual sections in these chapters stay deliberately modest.

A dead person is the easiest politician to make agree with us.

Kennedy would have ended Vietnam.

Lincoln would have perfected Reconstruction.

Robert Kennedy would have won the presidency.

Hamilton would have saved the Federalists.

Those sentences satisfy the desire for a clean hinge. They are not evidence.

The responsible counterfactual asks a smaller question: does changing the person plausibly change the range, timing, or probability of later decisions?

Usually the answer is yes.

The size of the change is the uncertain part.

The decade verdicts therefore rely more heavily on observed consequences than imagined ones. Johnson actually became president. Roosevelt actually became president. Feinstein actually became mayor. The Anti-Masonic Party actually formed. The federal kidnapping statute actually passed. The FBI actually investigated the Osage conspiracy. Federal and state institutions actually changed after George Floyd.

Where the argument depends more on counterfactual possibility, the chapter says so.

The 1790s remain the weakest verdict. The Harpes are culturally and institutionally revealing, but no single murder of that decade offers the kind of clean national consequence found elsewhere. That may change in a later edition if better scholarship produces a stronger candidate.

The 1930s are borderline for a different reason. The Lindbergh case mattered enormously, but much of the statutory consequence flowed from kidnapping rather than the later murder. Huey Long’s assassination is a cleaner homicide with a larger political counterfactual and a less certain observed afterlife. The book chooses Lindbergh because the public case was inseparable and the federalization of jurisdiction was permanent. A reader can reasonably choose Long.

The 1960s are not weak at all.

They are overcrowded.

Kennedy wins here only under an institutional-breadth standard. King can win under movement consequence. Robert Kennedy can win under counterfactual political consequence. Malcolm X can win under intellectual afterlife. Medgar Evers can win under the history of civil-rights prosecution.

The inability to produce an uncontestable winner is the point.

A ranking is most useful when disagreement clarifies what we value as consequence.

The 2010s require another kind of precision. George Zimmerman was acquitted in the killing of Trayvon Martin. The chapter does not reverse that verdict by prose. It chooses the fatal shooting because the acquittal helped create Black Lives Matter and because that movement became one of the decade’s defining political infrastructures.

Law and history answer different questions.

A criminal court asks whether the state proved a defendant guilty under a defined statute and burden of proof.

History asks what happened afterward.

Confusing those questions produces bad legal history and bad movement history at once.

The 2020s require temporal humility.

George Floyd is a provisional winner as of September 6, 2026.

Four years remain in the decade. Even the consequences already described are still moving. Federal policing policy changed dramatically between administrations. Minneapolis remains subject to a state court-enforceable agreement even after the federal government moved in 2025 to dismiss the proposed federal consent-decree case. Corporate and university responses to the 2020 protests have expanded, contracted, or been renamed under legal and political pressure. Crime trends, police staffing, reform politics, and public opinion continue to change.

A later edition should revisit the verdict.

That is not a defect in the project.

It is the honest condition of writing about a decade before it ends.

The murders also reveal changes in evidence across 250 years.

In 1770, the Boston Massacre became famous partly through an engraving whose political simplifications mattered more than evidentiary precision. At Gnadenhutten, the record was shaped by frontier witnesses and institutional neglect. William Morgan’s body was never conclusively identified, allowing political certainty to outrun criminal proof. By the Lindbergh era, handwriting, currency tracking, and wood analysis had become public forensic drama.

Dallas gave America the Zapruder film.

Columbine produced school surveillance footage, emergency calls, journals, videos, digital traces, and a media archive later attackers could study.

George Floyd’s murder was recorded deliberately by a teenager holding a smartphone connected to a distribution network.

The history of consequential murder is partly a history of who can make evidence.

The state once held enormous control over the first official account of a violent encounter. Citizen cameras have weakened that monopoly.

That is good for accountability.

It creates a new problem of viral judgment.

A video can expose conduct before an institution suppresses or sanitizes it. It can also circulate before investigators recover context and before a defendant receives due process. Public evidence is not automatically complete evidence.

The answer is not to distrust cameras.

It is to understand what they can and cannot prove.

That principle applies backward too.

A newspaper report is not neutral because it is old. A court verdict is not the entire historical truth because it is official. A later memoir is not accurate because the witness sounded certain. A government report can be rigorous and still reflect institutional limits. A movement source can accurately describe its own origin while using legal language more broadly than a historian should.

Every chapter therefore has a research problem beneath the narrative problem.

Some source records are better than others.

The Harpes accumulated folklore. William Morgan accumulated conspiracy. Kennedy accumulated an industry. Leo Frank accumulated more than a century of adversarial memory. Modern cases accumulate social-media evidence at a rate earlier historians could not have imagined.

The proper response to abundance and scarcity is the same.

State what is known.

State what is disputed.

Do not invent the connective tissue.

This book also began with a title problem that never entirely disappears.

“Murder” is legally narrower than the book’s range. Fort Mims occurred in war. The Boston soldiers were largely acquitted, with two convicted of manslaughter rather than murder. William Morgan disappeared and is presumed to have been killed, but no body produced a murder conviction. Trayvon Martin’s shooter was acquitted. September 11 was mass murder and terrorism rather than an ordinary criminal prosecution. Some massacres involved combat followed by killing of captives or civilians.

Why keep the title?

Because the title names the public category being examined: intentional wrongful killing and the American systems built around it.

The chapters carry the legal precision the title cannot.

Changing the title to *The Most Consequential Homicidal Events, Assassinations, Lynching Murders, Massacres, Presumed Murders, Terrorist Mass Killings, and Fatal Shootings of Each Decade* would be more exact and less honest about how people actually discuss these histories.

Precision belongs in the argument, not in making the cover unreadable.

The project also has a danger common to true crime even though it is not meant as true crime.

Killers are narratively efficient.

One killer connects many victims. He has motive, movement, preparation, trial, and biography. Victims often share nothing except the person who killed them. That structure gives the murderer more pages automatically.

The book tries to resist that gravity by following survivors and institutions instead.

The Boston chapter becomes partly about Adams, Attucks’s later memory, and revolutionary propaganda. Gnadenhutten becomes about Native-American relations and the young republic’s frontier authority. The People’s Grocery becomes about Wells. Till becomes about his mother and movement infrastructure. Chin becomes about coalition. Floyd becomes about a witness, organizers, courts, governments, and reform.

The murderer begins the event.

He does not deserve to own the history.

That may be the strongest reason to organize the book by consequence rather than notoriety.

Notoriety rewards the killer’s singularity.

Consequence rewards what other people did afterward.

The distinction is small enough to fit in one sentence and large enough to change the genre.

The most important murders are not necessarily the murders with the most interesting murderers.

They are the deaths after which institutions, movements, laws, cities, parties, families, and national stories could no longer behave quite the same way.

This is also why a murder can be historically important even when reform fails.

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act did not become law in the form its supporters sought. That failure is consequence because it reveals the boundary of national consensus. Federal consent-decree policy later reversed. That reversal is consequence too. A movement that changes the agenda but not every statute still changes politics.

Similarly, the anti-lynching campaign spent decades losing federal legislative fights before Congress finally enacted a federal anti-lynching statute in 2022. Ida B. Wells’s importance is not diminished because the law took more than a century to arrive.

Institutional time is often cruelly slow.

Murder is fast.

The gap between them is where much of this book lives.

The killer acts in seconds or minutes.

A conviction may take years.

A movement may take decades.

A constitutional doctrine may survive a century.

An archive may open after everyone involved is dead.

A monument may be erected and removed by different generations.

A family may carry a name long before the nation learns it.

That difference in speed produces another form of injustice. The immediate event belongs to the victim. The historical meaning is continually rewritten by people who arrive later.

That includes me.

A book that chooses “the most important” killing of any decade is an act of interpretation disguised as a clean category unless it admits the choices underneath it.

The research notes therefore preserve the runners-up and the evidence that could change a verdict. They are not leftovers. They are the mechanism that keeps the book revisable.

The ideal outcome is not that every reader agrees with all twenty-six choices.

It is that disagreement gets more precise.

If you choose Garfield over Rock Springs for the 1880s, you are probably weighting direct statutory reform over immigration, labor, racial violence, and diplomatic consequence. If you choose Wilmington over the People’s Grocery in the 1890s, you are weighting the direct destruction of multiracial government over the creation of Wells’s national anti-lynching campaign. If you choose King over Kennedy in the 1960s, you are weighting movement leadership and moral afterlife more heavily than presidential succession and state power. If you choose Oklahoma City over Columbine, you are weighting domestic terrorism and federal security more heavily than the reorganization of school life.

Those are arguments worth having.

They reveal a theory of history.

Mine, after these twenty-six chapters, is fairly simple.

A consequential killing changes the probability distribution of what institutions do next.

Sometimes it does that by removing a person.

Sometimes by creating a symbol.

Sometimes by revealing a gap in law.

Sometimes by producing fear large enough to authorize new power.

Sometimes by creating evidence around which people can finally organize.

Sometimes by teaching killers that violence works.

Sometimes by teaching the state that an old procedure does not.

The consequences become important when they persist beyond the immediate grief.

That does not redeem the death.

There is a habit in historical writing of ending tragedy with progress. Someone was killed, therefore a law passed. Someone was lynched, therefore a movement grew. Someone was assassinated, therefore the nation became wiser.

That shape is emotionally satisfying and morally dangerous.

It suggests history needed the body.

It did not.

Every reform in this book could have been discovered without the murder that made it politically urgent. The United States did not need a kidnapped and murdered child to learn that interstate crime required coordination. It did not need Chinese miners murdered to learn that exclusion and racial labor politics were dangerous. It did not need Emmett Till killed to know Jim Crow was violent. It did not need September 11 to discover weaknesses in aviation and intelligence. It did not need George Floyd’s murder to know police use of force deserved scrutiny.

Institutions could have learned earlier.

They usually did not.

That is the indictment hidden inside a history of consequential murder.

The death matters because systems waited until afterward.

So the final question is not which murder mattered most.

It is why murder had to matter at all.
