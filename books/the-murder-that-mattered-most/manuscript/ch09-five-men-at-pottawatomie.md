# Five Men at Pottawatomie

John Brown did not become violent at Harpers Ferry.

That is one of the myths produced by the dramatic shape of his life. The raid in Virginia in 1859 is so theatrical—a federal armory, hostages, U.S. Marines, Robert E. Lee, a treason trial, an execution—that everything before it can look like preparation.

Kansas was not preparation.

Kansas was where Brown killed.

On the night of May 24 and into the early hours of May 25, 1856, Brown and a small group that included several of his sons moved along Pottawatomie Creek in Kansas Territory. They went to the homes of proslavery settlers. Five men were taken outside and killed: James Doyle and two of his sons, William and Drury; Allen Wilkinson; and William Sherman.

The killings were intentional and retaliatory.

They were also political.

That combination makes them the most consequential murders of the 1850s.

The decade offers almost unfair competition. It includes the violence of slavery every day. It includes the killing of abolitionist editor Elijah Lovejoy’s ideological successors in local conflicts. It includes the beating of Senator Charles Sumner nearly to death on the Senate floor, though he survived. It includes the Sack of Lawrence, border warfare in Kansas, murders by both proslavery and antislavery factions, and the Harpers Ferry raid itself, in which people died on both sides.

The Pottawatomie killings matter most because they mark the moment when Brown’s antislavery conviction became organized lethal action against named political enemies, and because that action helped intensify a territorial conflict that Americans increasingly understood as a preview of civil war.

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 had created the conditions.

Congress organized Kansas and Nebraska territories while allowing settlers to determine the status of slavery through popular sovereignty, effectively repealing the Missouri Compromise restriction that had barred slavery from much of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36°30′. Senator Stephen Douglas promoted the measure as a democratic solution to sectional conflict.

Instead it moved the conflict onto the ground.

Proslavery and antislavery settlers rushed into Kansas. Missourians crossed the border to influence elections. Rival governments claimed legitimacy. Political identity became geographic competition: control the territory by settling it, voting in it, organizing it, and, increasingly, fighting for it.

“Bleeding Kansas” was not metaphorical.

By May 1856, proslavery forces had attacked Lawrence, an antislavery center, destroying newspaper presses and damaging buildings. News also arrived of Representative Preston Brooks’s assault on Senator Sumner in Washington after Sumner delivered his antislavery “Crime Against Kansas” speech and insulted Brooks’s relative, Senator Andrew Butler.

Brown interpreted events through a moral universe in which slavery was war whether the federal government admitted it or not.

He had spent years opposing slavery. His family had ties to abolitionism and the Underground Railroad. He was deeply religious and read the struggle in providential terms. He also came to believe that peaceful antislavery politics was inadequate against an institution maintained by violence.

At Pottawatomie, that belief crossed into killing.

The five victims were associated with the proslavery side, but they were not killed in a battlefield engagement. Brown’s party selected homes, removed men, and killed them. Later defenders sometimes tried to describe the act as preventive self-defense against men who threatened Brown’s family or antislavery settlers. Threats and fear were real in the region. The evidentiary record does not turn the episode into an ordinary act of immediate defense.

Brown had decided retaliation was necessary.

That distinction matters because his later martyrdom can make earlier violence look cleaner than it was.

Brown is unusually vulnerable to retrospective moral laundering. His cause—the destruction of slavery—was just. His willingness to die for it was extraordinary. His final statements after conviction in Virginia were powerful. Frederick Douglass, although he refused to join the Harpers Ferry raid, later praised Brown’s commitment. During the Civil War, Union soldiers marched to “John Brown’s Body.”

A righteous cause does not make every tactic righteous.

The Pottawatomie murders force the question his legend would prefer to postpone: when does resistance to an unjust system become permission to kill people selected as representatives of it?

That question mattered in 1856 because the normal political process had already been compromised.

Popular sovereignty promised ballots. Kansas received fraudulent voting, rival constitutions, intimidation, armed associations, and competing claims to government. Congress could not settle the slavery question because each compromise reopened it in a new territory. Courts would soon deepen the crisis with *Dred Scott v. Sandford* in 1857, denying Black citizenship and limiting congressional power over slavery in territories.

When political institutions repeatedly fail, people begin treating force as the remaining form of decision.

Brown arrived at that conclusion earlier and more completely than most white abolitionists.

Pottawatomie helped make his conclusion contagious.

The killings intensified fear and retaliatory violence in Kansas. Brown participated in later armed clashes and became a national figure in antislavery circles. He left Kansas not as an obscure farmer but as a militant with a reputation.

He then planned something larger.

The Harpers Ferry raid of October 1859 sought to seize weapons from the federal armory and spark an uprising of enslaved people. Brown expected that escaped enslaved people could be armed and organized into a force operating through the mountains, destabilizing slavery.

The plan failed quickly.

Local militia, civilians, and federal forces surrounded Brown’s party. U.S. Marines under the command of Colonel Robert E. Lee stormed the engine house. Brown was captured. Several raiders and others were killed. Virginia tried Brown for treason against the Commonwealth, murder, and conspiracy to incite slave insurrection. He was convicted and hanged on December 2, 1859.

Harpers Ferry is the larger event.

Why not choose it as the decade’s murder?

Because Harpers Ferry is better understood as an armed insurrection and political raid than a murder case. The deaths mattered, but the event’s consequence came from the attempt itself and Brown’s trial and execution.

Pottawatomie is where murder is the act.

It is also where Brown’s later history becomes morally legible.

Without Pottawatomie, one can imagine Brown as a man who moved from antislavery activism to a desperate attempt to liberate enslaved people. With Pottawatomie, the line is sharper. He had already accepted killing political opponents as a tool.

That acceptance was one of the signs that the country’s argument over slavery was leaving the realm of compromise.

The Civil War did not happen because John Brown killed five men.

The conflict over slavery was embedded in the Constitution, the economy, territorial expansion, party systems, racial ideology, and the balance of political power. The Mexican Cession had reopened the territorial question. The Compromise of 1850 had postponed rather than solved it. The Fugitive Slave Act radicalized northern resistance. The Kansas-Nebraska Act destroyed older party alignments. The Republican Party rose rapidly. *Dred Scott* worsened sectional distrust. Abraham Lincoln and Stephen Douglas debated slavery’s expansion. Southern leaders increasingly treated a Republican presidential victory as intolerable.

Brown was one accelerant among many.

His importance lies in demonstrating that violence could no longer be contained as an accidental breakdown of politics.

It was becoming politics.

The Pottawatomie murders also changed how each side imagined the other.

Proslavery Americans could point to Brown as proof that abolitionism concealed murderous fanaticism. Slaveholders had long warned that antislavery agitation would produce insurrection. Brown seemed to validate the fear in human form.

Abolitionists divided.

Some condemned the murders. Some defended Brown. Some avoided the details. After Harpers Ferry and Brown’s execution, admiration for his courage sometimes eclipsed the earlier killings. Southerners watched northern memorialization with alarm.

If people in free states sang for a man who had tried to arm enslaved people and who had already killed proslavery settlers, what did that say about the future of slavery inside the Union?

The perception mattered whether the North was actually united behind Brown or not.

Political crises feed on worst-case interpretations.

Abolitionists could interpret slaveholders through the brutality of slavery and the attack on Sumner. Slaveholders could interpret abolitionists through Brown. Each side could select the other’s most violent representative as its truest one.

The center then becomes harder to trust.

This is why Pottawatomie belongs to the history of polarization rather than merely biography.

Polarization is not just disagreement becoming intense. It is the process by which people stop believing ordinary institutions can safely contain the other side.

If the opposing coalition is not merely wrong but an existential threat, procedural defeat becomes dangerous. Losing an election can look like surrender. Compromise can look like collaboration. Violence can begin to look preventive.

Brown had already reached that psychological territory.

The country followed.

The Kansas setting also matters because national institutions had intentionally outsourced a moral conflict to local majorities.

Popular sovereignty sounded neutral: let settlers decide.

But when the issue is whether one human being may legally own another, procedural neutrality does not eliminate moral stakes. It relocates them. And when both sides understand migration itself as a political weapon, the voter population becomes part of the contest.

Northern emigrant aid societies supported antislavery settlement. Proslavery Missourians crossed into Kansas. Elections were disputed. Legislatures competed. Armed groups formed.

The territory became a political laboratory in which sovereignty was claimed through settlement before it was stabilized through law.

Brown’s killings were one experiment in that laboratory.

They showed what happens when a citizen decides the state’s failure authorizes personal judgment over life and death.

His defenders could answer that the slave system had made such personal judgment unavoidable. Enslavers already exercised lethal coercion with legal protection. People escaping slavery faced capture. Free Black people could be kidnapped. Federal law compelled assistance to slaveholders through the Fugitive Slave Act. Violence was not introduced to an otherwise peaceful system by John Brown.

That argument is historically important.

It is not a complete defense of Pottawatomie.

The fact that an unjust institution uses violence does not automatically identify which individual may be killed in resistance to it.

Brown selected men as part of a political faction. The killings therefore raised the problem revolutionary movements always face: how far can collective guilt extend?

The Civil War would soon answer on a scale beyond anything Brown could produce personally.

The rival case most likely to displace Pottawatomie is the killing of Charles Dow in Kansas in 1855, often described as one of the first murders of the territorial conflict. Dow, a Free-State settler, was shot by proslavery neighbor Franklin Coleman during a land dispute entangled with politics. The killing helped trigger the Wakarusa War and showed how private conflict could become sectional confrontation.

Dow’s murder is historically important precisely because it reveals how quickly local violence acquired national labels.

Pottawatomie wins because Brown’s later trajectory gave the killings a much larger afterlife.

The other rival is the 1856 assault on Sumner, which was nearly lethal and arguably more consequential than any single Kansas murder. Brooks beat Sumner with a cane at his Senate desk until the cane broke. Sumner survived but was incapacitated for years. Northerners treated the attack as evidence of the “Slave Power”; southerners sent Brooks replacement canes.

Had Sumner died, he might be this chapter.

He did not.

The fact that a nonfatal assault can rival five murders in historical consequence is another reminder that this project measures afterlife, not body count.

If Brown had never gone to Pottawatomie, would Harpers Ferry have happened?

Possibly.

His hatred of slavery and interest in armed resistance predated Kansas. He had imagined forms of militant action earlier. But Kansas gave him experience, reputation, allies, fundraising credibility among certain abolitionists, and a personal proof that he was willing to kill.

The raid in Virginia is harder to imagine without the man Kansas made visible.

If Harpers Ferry had not happened, would the Civil War have happened?

Yes, probably, though timing and political reactions could differ. No responsible account of sectional conflict makes Brown indispensable to disunion.

His consequence is not necessity.

It is acceleration and revelation.

He forced Americans to see one possible endpoint of the slavery conflict before armies made it official.

Pottawatomie is where that endpoint first appeared in his hands.

The murders mattered because they helped transform Brown from activist into militant, intensified Bleeding Kansas, supplied proslavery Americans with a durable image of abolitionist violence, and became part of the biography that made Harpers Ferry and Brown’s execution nationally explosive.

The victims did not die in a battle.

They died because Brown’s group had decided political affiliation and perceived threat justified taking them from their homes and killing them.

That fact should survive every hymn written afterward.

So should the fact that the institution Brown opposed was itself maintained by law, coercion, and violence against millions of enslaved people.

The moral difficulty is not a reason to look away.

It is why the chapter matters.

By 1860, the country would elect a Republican president committed to preventing slavery’s expansion. Southern states would begin seceding. In April 1861, artillery would open on Fort Sumter.

Four years later, the Civil War would be ending when an actor named John Wilkes Booth entered a theater in Washington.

The next killing would not foreshadow national rupture.

It would alter the peace.
