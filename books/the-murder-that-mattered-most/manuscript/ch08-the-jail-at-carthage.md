# The Jail at Carthage

Joseph Smith went to Carthage because a government promised custody would be safer than resistance.

That promise failed.

On June 27, 1844, a mob attacked the jail in Carthage, Illinois, where Smith, the founder of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, was being held with his brother Hyrum and several companions. Hyrum was shot and killed. Joseph was shot while trying to escape through an upper-story window and fell outside. The mob dispersed after the killings.

Smith was thirty-eight.

His death did not end the religious movement he founded.

It changed where that movement would live, who would lead it, how it would understand itself, and ultimately the political geography of the American West.

That is enough to make the Carthage murders the most consequential killings of the 1840s.

The obvious difficulty is that Joseph Smith was not merely a religious dissenter persecuted for belief. By 1844 he was also mayor of Nauvoo, commander of a large local militia, a presidential candidate, and the central figure in a rapidly expanding theocratic civic experiment that alarmed neighbors. His church had been driven from earlier settlements amid conflicts in Ohio and Missouri. In Missouri, violence had escalated into armed confrontation, and Governor Lilburn Boggs issued an infamous 1838 order directing that Mormons be “exterminated or driven from the state” if necessary for public peace.

The refugees moved to Illinois and built Nauvoo.

The city grew quickly on the Mississippi River. Illinois lawmakers granted a broad charter. Smith accumulated religious, political, judicial, and military authority. Members of the church often voted as a bloc, which made them attractive to politicians and threatening to surrounding communities. Smith introduced plural marriage to a select circle while publicly denying accusations of polygamy. Former allies became critics.

The final crisis began with a newspaper.

The *Nauvoo Expositor*, published by dissident former church members, produced a single issue in June 1844 criticizing Smith’s religious and political conduct, including plural marriage and concentration of power. The Nauvoo city council declared the paper a public nuisance. Smith, as mayor, ordered its press destroyed.

The act was disastrous.

A man who had experienced persecution and mob violence now used municipal authority to destroy a hostile press.

Opponents treated the destruction as proof that Nauvoo was beyond ordinary law. Smith and others were charged with riot. Tensions escalated. The Illinois governor, Thomas Ford, demanded that Smith submit to legal process in Carthage. Smith initially fled across the Mississippi, then returned and surrendered.

He was jailed on a treason charge.

Governor Ford promised protection.

The protection failed.

This is one reason Carthage belongs beside Lovejoy in the chronology. The two Illinois killings make an uncomfortable pair.

In 1837 a mob killed an editor while destroying an abolitionist press the local state could not protect.

In 1844 a religious leader used local government to destroy a dissenting press, was taken into state custody, and then killed by a mob the state could not protect him from.

The principle is not that Lovejoy and Smith were morally equivalent actors. They were not. It is that freedom of expression, mob violence, municipal power, and weak state enforcement were colliding repeatedly in a republic expanding faster than its institutions could settle deep social conflict.

Smith’s killing mattered because the mob solved none of the conflict that produced it.

It removed the leader and made the movement harder to contain.

A less durable church might have fractured into disappearance. Mormonism fractured, but it survived.

Several men made leadership claims after Smith’s death. Sidney Rigdon argued for a guardianship role. Brigham Young, president of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles, emerged as the leader of the largest body of Latter-day Saints. Other groups followed James Strang, Joseph Smith III, and additional claimants over time. The succession was not a minor administrative dispute. Smith had combined prophetic charisma, institutional office, revelation, and personal authority in ways no successor could simply inherit by assumption.

The murder therefore forced religious institutionalization.

A movement built around a founder had to discover how to continue without him.

That problem recurs across religions, companies, political movements, and states. Founders often solve ambiguity personally. Their presence is a governance mechanism. People accept decisions because the founder made them. Remove the founder and every unresolved question becomes constitutional.

Who has authority?

What is the source of legitimacy?

Which documents govern?

Can revelation continue?

Does bloodline matter?

Does office matter?

Do councils matter?

Smith’s death opened all of those questions at once.

The largest answer traveled west.

Hostility toward the Mormon community did not end after Carthage. Violence and political pressure continued. By 1846, the major Latter-day Saint exodus from Nauvoo began under Brigham Young. Thousands crossed Iowa and eventually moved toward the Great Basin, then Mexican territory and soon part of the United States after the Mexican-American War.

In 1847, the first major Mormon pioneer company entered the Salt Lake Valley.

The migration changed the settlement history of the interior West.

Latter-day Saints founded or helped establish communities across what became Utah and surrounding regions. Their church built irrigation systems, farms, towns, commercial networks, migration routes, and a distinctive political order. The proposed State of Deseret claimed an enormous region. Congress instead created Utah Territory in 1850 as part of the Compromise of 1850, with Brigham Young as territorial governor.

A killing in an Illinois jail had helped send a religious society into the continental interior.

That is consequence you can draw on a map.

The move west did not place the Saints outside federal power for long. The Mexican Cession brought the Great Basin into the United States. Conflict followed over plural marriage, territorial governance, judicial authority, Native relations, land, and the boundary between church and state. In 1857–58, the Utah War brought federal troops toward the territory amid a confrontation between President James Buchanan’s administration and the Mormon leadership.

The church’s later abandonment of officially sanctioned plural marriage and Utah’s eventual statehood in 1896 belong to another era, but the distinct political culture of Utah cannot be separated from the migration triggered by the crisis after Smith’s death.

Carthage did not *cause* Utah in a simple sense.

Smith himself had considered western refuge before his murder. Conflict around Nauvoo was already severe. The church had a history of forced movement from New York to Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois. Migration was part of its institutional experience.

But Smith’s death removed the figure around whom Nauvoo had been organized and made long-term coexistence in Illinois much less plausible.

The murder accelerated departure and changed leadership at the same time.

That double consequence is unusual.

The event also hardened a theology of martyrdom.

Religious movements do not remember the killing of founders as ordinary biography. Death can become evidence of truth, sacrifice, persecution, or sacred continuity. In Latter-day Saint memory, Joseph and Hyrum Smith became martyrs. The jail at Carthage became a site of pilgrimage and interpretation.

The martyr story gave later generations a framework for understanding conflict with outsiders.

That framework could sustain solidarity. It could also intensify suspicion.

A community repeatedly driven from settlements and then deprived of its founder by a mob has historical reasons to expect hostility. Persecution memory can become institutional glue.

This does not mean every later conflict was simply persecution. Mormon communities exercised power too. In Utah, church leadership dominated political life. Native peoples experienced displacement and violence as settlement expanded. Dissenters inside the Mormon world faced pressures. The 1857 Mountain Meadows Massacre, in which a wagon train of emigrants was killed by a local Mormon militia with Native participation, remains one of the darkest episodes in territorial history.

Victimhood does not prevent a community from exercising coercion when it gains power.

Carthage matters partly because it helps explain how intensely Latter-day Saints valued autonomy after reaching the Great Basin.

The state that failed to protect Smith in Illinois did not automatically look trustworthy when it later sent judges, officials, or troops to Utah.

This is another recurring consequence of political killing: damaged legitimacy travels.

A government can regain control of a street and still lose confidence for a generation.

The legal aftermath of Carthage deepened that loss.

Men were charged and tried for the murders. They were acquitted.

The verdict did not prove no mob had killed the Smith brothers. The attack was obvious. It showed that identifying and convicting individual participants inside a politically sympathetic community was difficult.

This pattern will become painfully familiar in later lynching chapters.

A killing can be public and still legally unresolved.

Witnesses may fear retaliation. Juries may share the attitudes of defendants. Local officials may have conflicts. Collective action disperses responsibility. The mob’s strength lies partly in numbers: everyone acts, so no one person seems to own the whole act.

Carthage was a nineteenth-century demonstration of that protection.

The strongest rival murder of the 1840s is hard to choose because the decade contains war on a national scale.

The U.S.-Mexican War killed thousands and transferred an enormous territory to the United States. Violence against Native people continued across expanding settlements. Enslaved people were killed within a system that often failed to recognize them as victims in the same way white citizens were recognized. The California Gold Rush began at the decade’s end and would accelerate lethal violence against Native Californians in the 1850s.

One individual homicide struggles to compete with those structures.

The Smith murders stand out because they produced a clearly traceable institutional migration.

If Joseph Smith had lived, would the Latter-day Saints have remained in Illinois?

Probably not indefinitely.

Hostility was too intense. Political conflict around Nauvoo had grown too severe. Smith was already considering alternatives. His ambitions and the city’s autonomy made compromise difficult.

But survival could have changed the destination, timing, leadership, and form of migration. Smith might have led the church elsewhere. He might have been imprisoned, politically defeated, or killed later. Brigham Young might never have acquired the same authority. The succession branches could have developed differently.

Counterfactual certainty is impossible.

What is certain is that the murder closed every future in which Smith personally negotiated the next stage.

It handed the movement to successors.

That makes Carthage a founder-deletion event like Hamilton’s death, but the outcomes were almost opposite.

Hamilton’s institutions were designed to outlive him and did so with little formal disruption.

Smith’s movement had to improvise succession because his personal authority had been central.

The resulting institutional split produced separate Latter Day Saint traditions, while the largest moved west under Young.

The murder also changed American religious history by making the church’s survival itself a test.

New religious movements often disappear after charismatic founders die. Mormonism did not. It became a global religion whose membership would eventually number in the millions. Its temples, missionary system, genealogical work, universities, welfare institutions, investment assets, political influence, and cultural reach all belong downstream from the successful solution to the 1844 succession crisis.

No mob in Carthage could have intended that.

The killers wanted to end a local threat.

They helped create a migration that made the movement harder to suppress.

Violence again produced the opposite of its intended political effect.

This is the lesson shared with Lovejoy. Destroy the press and the dead editor becomes a free-speech martyr. Kill the prophet and the community carries his memory across the plains.

The consequence of murder often depends less on the killer’s purpose than on the organizational capacity of survivors.

That capacity is the real protagonist of this chapter.

The Latter-day Saints had records, offices, councils, shared belief, family networks, economic cooperation, leadership structures, and experience moving together. They could survive decapitation because the movement was already more than one man, even though one man had dominated it.

Communities without that infrastructure can be destroyed by far less violence.

The history of consequential killing therefore contains a bias toward organized survivors. The deaths that “change history” are often deaths around which someone has the capacity to mobilize.

Carthage proves the point.

The mob killed Joseph and Hyrum Smith.

It did not kill the church.

Instead the murders reordered it, hardened its persecution memory, accelerated its departure from Illinois, and helped redirect American settlement toward the Great Basin.

That is the verdict for the 1840s.

The next decade returns to a frontier dispute in which murder did not drive a movement away from contested land. It helped turn contested land into civil war.

In May 1856, John Brown and a small group pulled five proslavery men from their homes along Pottawatomie Creek in Kansas and killed them.

Brown would become one of the most polarizing figures in American history.

Pottawatomie is where the moral arithmetic of his violence became impossible to ignore.
