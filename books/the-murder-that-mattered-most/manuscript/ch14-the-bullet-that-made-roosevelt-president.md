# The Bullet That Made Roosevelt President

Theodore Roosevelt did not expect to become president in September 1901.

That was part of the reason powerful Republicans had been comfortable making him vice president.

Roosevelt was popular, energetic, reform-minded, unpredictable, and difficult for New York’s Republican machine to control. The vice presidency offered a solution. It honored him, used his popularity on William McKinley’s reelection ticket, and moved him away from the governorship where he could create trouble for party bosses.

Then Leon Czolgosz shot McKinley.

On September 6, 1901, the president was greeting visitors at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York. Czolgosz, an anarchist who had wrapped a handgun in a handkerchief, approached in the receiving line and fired twice. McKinley initially appeared to improve after surgery. Infection and gangrene followed. He died on September 14.

Roosevelt took the oath of office that day.

At forty-two, he became the youngest person ever to assume the presidency.

The killing is the most consequential murder of the 1900s because it accelerated a transition already forming inside American government: from a restrained nineteenth-century presidency toward a more visible, activist, national executive.

Roosevelt did not invent that transition.

The office had been expanding under earlier presidents. Abraham Lincoln exercised extraordinary wartime powers. Grover Cleveland intervened in labor conflict and used the veto aggressively. McKinley himself presided over the Spanish-American War, acquisition of overseas territories, annexation of Hawaii, and the beginning of a more assertive international role. Industrialization, national markets, railroads, corporations, urbanization, mass newspapers, and overseas expansion were already making the federal government more important.

But presidents are not interchangeable vessels for structural change.

Roosevelt had a theory of the office.

He believed the president could act for the public interest unless the Constitution or law specifically prohibited action. That “stewardship” conception differed from narrower views that required explicit constitutional or statutory authorization. It fit his temperament: investigate, negotiate, threaten, publicize, intervene.

McKinley’s death placed that theory inside the executive branch years earlier than voters had chosen it directly.

This is what presidential assassination does that other murders cannot.

It converts a hypothetical future into immediate authority.

Had McKinley lived, Roosevelt might have sought the presidency in 1904. He might have won. Party leaders might have blocked him. Another candidate might have emerged. McKinley might have shaped succession. None of those possibilities survived September 14.

Roosevelt already had the office.

He used it.

His administration intervened in the 1902 anthracite coal strike, bringing mine owners and workers into federal mediation rather than reflexively using federal power only to suppress labor. The episode did not make Roosevelt a labor radical. It made the presidency a visible broker in an industrial conflict with national consequences.

He pursued antitrust enforcement, most famously against the Northern Securities Company, and turned the phrase “trust busting” into part of presidential politics. He supported the Hepburn Act strengthening federal regulation of railroad rates. He backed consumer-protection legislation after public attention to food and drug safety. He used executive authority to conserve vast areas of public land and expand national forests, monuments, and wildlife protection.

He also projected American power abroad.

The Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine asserted a broad U.S. role in the Western Hemisphere. His administration supported Panama’s separation from Colombia and secured rights to build the Panama Canal. He mediated the end of the Russo-Japanese War and won the Nobel Peace Prize. He sent the Great White Fleet around the world as a demonstration of naval power.

This was not a modest presidency.

The bullet in Buffalo did not create Roosevelt’s ideas.

It gave them timing.

The timing matters because American institutions were struggling to catch up with industrial scale.

Corporations operated across state lines. Railroads coordinated national markets. Meatpacking, oil, steel, finance, and communications concentrated power in firms larger than many governments. Cities grew rapidly. Immigration transformed urban populations. Journalists exposed corruption and dangerous conditions. Reformers pushed civil service, sanitation, labor standards, women’s suffrage, and political reform.

A state-by-state regulatory model was increasingly inadequate to national industrial systems.

Roosevelt’s presidency became one answer: national executive power as counterweight to national private power.

That answer would influence successors even when they rejected his personality.

William Howard Taft pursued more antitrust cases in formal terms. Woodrow Wilson expanded federal economic regulation through the Federal Reserve Act and Federal Trade Commission. Franklin Roosevelt later transformed executive power on a scale Theodore Roosevelt never approached. The modern presidency did not descend in a straight line from one man, but Theodore Roosevelt helped make activist executive leadership electorally and culturally normal.

McKinley’s assassination moved that normalization forward.

The event also changed presidential security.

McKinley was the third American president assassinated in thirty-six years: Lincoln in 1865, Garfield in 1881, McKinley in 1901. Three successful assassinations made the vulnerability of the office impossible to treat as isolated misfortune.

The Secret Service had been created in 1865 within the Treasury Department to combat counterfeiting. It had performed occasional protective duties before 1901, but after McKinley’s assassination Congress increasingly directed the Service to protect the president, establishing the basis of the permanent protective mission.

That institutional consequence reaches into every modern presidential appearance.

Motorcades, advance teams, secure perimeters, magnetometers, armored vehicles, counter-sniper teams, communications systems, and restricted access are descendants of a problem the country took decades to accept: elected leaders could not remain physically accessible under nineteenth-century norms without unacceptable risk.

The tradeoff is democratic.

A president surrounded by protection is safer and less ordinary.

Citizens can see the office at a rally while remaining far from the person. Spontaneous contact becomes a managed event. Protection creates distance around a leader whose constitutional legitimacy is supposed to come from the public.

McKinley was shot while performing accessibility.

He was greeting people one by one.

The ritual was democratic theater, and Czolgosz used the theater’s openness as the attack surface.

Modern security tries to preserve the appearance of access while removing most of its vulnerability.

That is an institutional afterlife of murder.

Czolgosz’s political identity also mattered to the era.

He identified with anarchist ideas and had heard Emma Goldman speak, though established anarchists did not necessarily know or trust him. After the assassination, public and governmental hostility toward anarchists intensified. Immigration law in the early twentieth century would include ideological exclusions aimed at anarchists, and the broader history of American anti-radicalism would grow through the Red Scares, wartime repression, and deportation campaigns.

It would be too much to attribute that trajectory to McKinley’s murder.

Anarchist violence was already an international phenomenon. European monarchs and leaders had been assassinated. The 1886 Haymarket affair had linked anarchism to bombing in American public memory. Labor conflict and radical politics were already sources of fear.

Czolgosz gave the fear a presidential body.

That made anti-anarchist politics easier to nationalize.

The trial moved quickly. Czolgosz was convicted of murder and executed in the electric chair in October 1901, less than two months after the assassination.

The speed contrasted with the uncertainty surrounding earlier cases in this book.

There was no mystery about the shooter. No mob protected him. No jurisdictional puzzle prevented prosecution. The state could punish the murderer.

Punishment did nothing to reverse succession.

This is another reason presidential assassination is uniquely consequential. Criminal justice operates after constitutional consequence has already happened.

Execute the assassin and the new president remains president.

The strongest rival killing of the 1900s is probably the 1906 murder of architect Stanford White by Harry Kendall Thaw at Madison Square Garden, a case that became one of the era’s great media spectacles. Thaw shot White in public over White’s past relationship with Evelyn Nesbit, Thaw’s wife. The trial became an early “trial of the century,” combining wealth, sex, celebrity, psychiatric testimony, and mass circulation newspapers.

Its cultural consequence was significant.

Its governmental consequence was minor beside McKinley.

Another rival is the violence of the 1908 Springfield race riot in Illinois, where Black residents were killed and neighborhoods attacked. The riot was one of the events that helped prompt white and Black reformers to create the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People in 1909. If the selection focused on movement formation, Springfield would be an exceptionally strong candidate.

That case deserves special respect because the NAACP would become one of the most consequential civil-rights institutions in American history.

McKinley still wins because his death simultaneously changed the presidency, executive ideology, national policy, and protective institutions.

The counterfactual is unusually concrete.

McKinley had begun his second term only months earlier. If he had lived through it, Roosevelt would have remained vice president until 1905 unless another event intervened. Four years is a long time in politics.

The coal strike would have occurred under McKinley.

Would he have intervened in the same way?

The Northern Securities case might have proceeded differently. Conservation policy would have had a different executive champion. Panama and canal diplomacy might have developed differently. Roosevelt’s public relationship with investigative journalists and reformers might have remained outside the White House.

Even if Roosevelt won in 1904, his presidency would have started with a fresh electoral mandate rather than succession, and he would have had four fewer years in which to shape national politics.

The murder changed time.

That is often what consequential killing does.

It does not create forces from nothing. It moves them forward, removes a veto player, installs a successor, hardens an argument, or gives an existing movement the event around which to organize.

McKinley’s assassination accelerated Roosevelt.

Roosevelt then accelerated the presidency.

The line is not perfect. It is strong enough to defend.

The murder also helped change the Republican Party’s internal balance.

McKinley was a skilled party politician closely associated with industrial growth, protective tariffs, and the business-aligned Republican order built by figures including Mark Hanna. Roosevelt shared many Republican commitments but presented himself as willing to discipline corporate abuses in order to preserve capitalism from its own excesses.

He did not seek class war.

He sought legitimacy.

A government that appeared captured by concentrated wealth would eventually provoke more radical alternatives. Regulation could therefore be conservative in the literal sense: preserve the system by reforming it.

This became one of Progressivism’s central political logics.

Roosevelt’s accidental presidency gave it a uniquely visible champion.

There is a tendency to make this story flattering because Roosevelt was charismatic and because later environmental and regulatory achievements are easy to praise.

The consequences were not uniformly progressive.

Roosevelt held racial views common to white elites of his era and his administration’s record included grave failures, including the dishonorable discharge of Black soldiers after the 1906 Brownsville affair based on disputed allegations. His imperial vision treated U.S. power abroad with confidence that could become coercion. The Panama episode involved intervention in another country’s territorial politics. Conservation policies sometimes treated lands as empty landscapes while limiting Native use and presence.

An energetic presidency can do more good.

It can also do more harm.

That is the institutional lesson, not a defect in it.

Power is capacity before it is virtue.

McKinley’s assassination mattered because it placed a president unusually eager to use capacity into office at a moment when national problems were demanding more of the federal government.

The security response added another layer of state capacity around the person of the president.

One murder therefore expanded executive power in two senses: what the president could do and what government would do to keep the president alive.

That is difficult for any 1900s rival to match.

The next decade’s murder would not elevate a politician.

It would expose the relationship between mob violence, antisemitism, elite complicity, and the rebirth of one of America’s most notorious white supremacist organizations.

In August 1915, a mob removed Leo Frank from a Georgia prison farm and lynched him.

The men involved did not wear hoods to hide from their community.

Some were pillars of it.
