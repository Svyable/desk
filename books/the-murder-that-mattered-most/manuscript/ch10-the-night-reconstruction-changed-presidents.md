# The Night Reconstruction Changed Presidents

Abraham Lincoln was killed at the moment victory had made the next problem unavoidable.

The Civil War was ending. Robert E. Lee had surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia at Appomattox on April 9, 1865. Other Confederate forces remained in the field, but the military result was no longer seriously in doubt. Washington celebrated. The president who had spent four years managing armies, generals, emancipation, conscription, elections, border states, foreign pressure, and an opposition party now had to answer a question war could postpone but peace could not.

What did reunion mean?

On the evening of April 14, John Wilkes Booth entered Ford’s Theatre and shot Lincoln in the back of the head. Lincoln died the next morning.

Andrew Johnson became president.

No other killing in this book changes the identity of national government so directly at such a consequential moment.

That is why Lincoln’s assassination is the most important murder of the 1860s, and perhaps the easiest selection in all twenty-six decades.

The difficulty is not deciding whether it mattered.

The difficulty is separating what changed because Lincoln died from what later Americans wish he would have changed had he lived.

Lincoln has one advantage over every counterfactual president: he never had to disappoint the future.

He did not have to design a complete Reconstruction policy and watch Congress resist it. He did not have to decide how aggressively to protect Black political participation against southern white violence over several years. He did not have to manage land redistribution, labor contracts, state readmission, pardons, military occupation, voting rights, or the status of former Confederate leaders through the long, ugly politics of peace. He did not have to run for another term or leave office in 1869 with compromises attached to his name.

Death froze him at victory.

Johnson inherited everything unfinished.

The difference between the two men mattered immediately.

Johnson was a Tennessee Democrat and slaveholder who had remained loyal to the Union when his state seceded. Lincoln chose him as his 1864 running mate on the National Union ticket to broaden the coalition for war and reunification. Johnson hated the planter elite that had led secession and spoke harshly about traitors. That did not make him an advocate of racial equality.

His Reconstruction policy moved quickly to restore southern state governments under lenient terms. He issued pardons widely. Former Confederates and old elites returned to political influence. Southern legislatures enacted Black Codes restricting the freedom of formerly enslaved people. Violence against Black communities and white Unionists escalated.

Congress reacted.

The confrontation between Johnson and congressional Republicans became one of the defining constitutional struggles of the nineteenth century.

Congress renewed and expanded the Freedmen’s Bureau over Johnson’s veto. It passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866 over his veto, the first major federal civil-rights law. The Fourteenth Amendment constitutionalized national citizenship, equal protection, and due process principles. Congressional Reconstruction imposed new conditions for southern readmission and used military districts. The conflict eventually produced Johnson’s impeachment by the House after he violated the Tenure of Office Act in his attempt to remove Secretary of War Edwin Stanton. The Senate fell one vote short of conviction.

Would Lincoln have fought Congress in the same way?

Probably not.

Would he have supported every Radical Republican demand?

Also probably not.

That middle ground is where responsible counterfactual history has to remain.

Lincoln had advocated a relatively lenient path for restoring loyal governments in the South. His “ten percent plan” offered recognition when a relatively small portion of voters took loyalty oaths and accepted emancipation. Congressional Republicans objected and passed the Wade-Davis Bill with stricter requirements; Lincoln pocket-vetoed it.

At the same time, Lincoln’s thinking on Black citizenship was changing. In his final public speech on April 11, 1865, he endorsed limited Black suffrage in Louisiana, especially for educated Black men and Black veterans. Booth heard or learned of the speech and was enraged by the prospect of Black citizenship.

Three days later he shot Lincoln.

That connection gives the assassination an unmistakable racial politics.

Booth was not merely a Confederate sympathizer angry that his side lost. He was a white supremacist who opposed emancipation and Black political rights. His conspiracy originally contemplated kidnapping Lincoln. By April, with Confederate defeat near, the plan became assassination. Booth’s accomplices also targeted Vice President Johnson and Secretary of State William Seward in an attempt to decapitate national leadership. Seward was attacked and severely wounded in his home. The conspirator assigned to kill Johnson lost his nerve.

The plot therefore aimed at government, not just one man.

Only one of its central assassinations succeeded.

That success was enough.

Presidential succession in 1865 was simple in constitutional form: the vice president became president. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment did not exist, but the Constitution and precedent from John Tyler’s succession after William Henry Harrison’s death had established that the vice president assumed the office rather than merely its powers.

The institutional transition worked.

The political transition was catastrophic.

That contrast is essential. America did not experience a succession crisis in the sense of competing claimants to the presidency. It experienced a policy crisis because legitimate succession installed a man with a radically different relationship to the central questions of Reconstruction.

Institutions can work procedurally and still produce enormous substantive consequences.

Lincoln’s murder is therefore not a story about fragility of the constitutional office.

It is a story about the power of the office.

If changing one life changes federal policy for millions, the institution is strong enough to matter.

The assassination also altered the emotional environment of Reconstruction.

Before April 14, Confederate surrender could be interpreted through victory, relief, grief, exhaustion, and the difficult work of reunion. After the assassination, defeat was attached to murder.

Northern anger intensified. Booth’s conspiracy made treason feel alive after the armies surrendered. Lincoln’s funeral became a national ritual of mourning as his body traveled by train through northern cities toward Springfield, Illinois. Crowds gathered along the route. The president became a martyr to Union and emancipation before political memory had time to complicate him.

Martyrdom changed the usable Lincoln.

The real Lincoln had been a politician who compromised, delayed, maneuvered, doubted generals, balanced factions, worried about elections, and changed positions over time. The dead Lincoln could become principle without friction.

That transformation benefited multiple political traditions. Republicans could invoke him as savior of the Union and emancipator. Later reconciliationists could emphasize his language of “malice toward none.” Civil-rights advocates could emphasize emancipation and his late movement toward Black suffrage. Opponents of federal intervention could also imagine a lenient Lincoln who would have rejected Radical Reconstruction.

The assassination created a president everyone could finish according to preference.

Johnson did not have that luxury.

He had to make decisions.

The comparison has often produced a simple narrative: Lincoln would have reconstructed the South wisely; Johnson ruined it. The evidence is not that cooperative.

Reconstruction faced structural obstacles no president could remove by personal skill. Four million people had emerged from slavery into freedom without land redistribution on a scale sufficient to create broad economic independence. Former enslavers retained property and local influence. White supremacist violence would become organized through groups including the Ku Klux Klan. Southern economies were devastated. Northern commitment to sustained federal intervention had limits. Courts would later narrow parts of Reconstruction’s legal revolution. Economic panic in 1873 and political fatigue would weaken Republican resolve.

Lincoln alive does not make those conditions disappear.

But leadership could change the path through them.

Johnson used presidential power repeatedly against congressional efforts to protect freedpeople and redefine citizenship. His vetoes forced Congress to assemble supermajorities. His speeches deepened political conflict. His lenient pardons facilitated the return of former Confederate leaders. His opposition helped radicalize congressional Republicans, producing a harsher institutional confrontation than Lincoln’s political skill might have produced.

The assassination did not predetermine Reconstruction’s failure.

It changed the person making presidential choices during its foundational year.

That alone is enormous.

The killing also produced the first presidential assassination in American history and permanently altered security around the office, though the modern Secret Service protective system developed gradually and more fully after later assassinations. Lincoln had been accessible by standards that now seem astonishing. He attended public events, traveled with limited protection, and moved through a capital filled with veterans, office seekers, spies, and former enemies.

Booth knew the theater world and could enter Ford’s Theatre without passing through anything resembling modern protective screening.

Presidential openness survived the assassination for decades because the United States resisted turning elected leaders into guarded monarchs.

That resistance would weaken after James Garfield was shot in 1881 and William McKinley in 1901.

Lincoln began the argument.

How much security can democracy place around a leader before the leader becomes physically separate from the public?

The question remains unresolved because the risks are real in both directions.

The legal response to the conspiracy was severe.

Booth fled and was killed by pursuing troops in Virginia. Eight accused conspirators were tried by military commission. Four, including Mary Surratt, were executed. Others received prison sentences. The use of a military tribunal for civilians after the war became controversial, especially as the Supreme Court’s 1866 decision in *Ex parte Milligan* limited military trials where civilian courts were open.

The assassination therefore touched not only Reconstruction but the postwar boundary between emergency power and ordinary law.

War creates exceptional institutions.

Peace has to decide when they end.

Lincoln’s death delayed emotional transition from one to the other.

The strongest rival killing of the 1860s is not close in national consequence but is powerful in civil-rights history: the 1863 New York City draft riots included the lynching and murder of Black New Yorkers during days of racial and class violence. Black homes and institutions were attacked, including the Colored Orphan Asylum, which children escaped before the building was burned. The violence accelerated the departure of Black residents from parts of the city and revealed northern racism during a war increasingly tied to emancipation.

Another rival is the 1864 Sand Creek Massacre, where U.S. volunteer cavalry attacked a Cheyenne and Arapaho village in Colorado Territory, killing a large number of people, many of them women and children. Congressional investigations condemned the attack. Its consequences for Plains conflict and Native-American relations were profound.

If this book measured atrocity or number of victims, Lincoln would not automatically win.

He wins because presidential assassination transformed the national decision-making structure during the most consequential domestic transition since the founding.

What would falsify the choice?

Evidence that Johnson’s Reconstruction policies were essentially the same as Lincoln’s likely policies would weaken it. So would evidence that congressional action would have overridden Lincoln just as completely, leaving the trajectory unchanged. Historians disagree on the counterfactual, and the uncertainty should remain.

But the burden of proof runs the other way too.

Two presidents with different political coalitions, temperaments, racial views, congressional relationships, and levels of public legitimacy are unlikely to produce identical outcomes at a moment of extraordinary policy freedom.

Lincoln’s political capital in April 1865 was immense. He had won reelection, preserved the Union, and was associated with victory. Johnson inherited the office but not the same relationship to the electorate or party.

The assassin changed more than a nameplate.

He changed which political capital entered Reconstruction.

Booth imagined his act as vengeance and perhaps as a final service to the Confederate cause. In the short term, he made reunion harder and martyrized the leader of the Union he hated. In the long term, he installed a southern Unionist whose resistance to congressional Reconstruction helped produce a constitutional struggle that nearly removed a second president.

Killers rarely control consequences.

Booth is one of the clearest examples.

The conspiracy sought to decapitate the government. The government survived. The target became larger in death. Emancipation became attached to martyrdom. Yet the policy vacuum created by the assassination was real and immediate.

That combination makes Lincoln’s death a near-perfect case for this book’s method.

The murder changed a person, a presidency, a constitutional struggle, a political memory, and the environment in which freedom would be defined for millions of former slaves.

It also created questions no counterfactual can answer.

Would Lincoln have broken with Radical Republicans or led them? Would he have protected Black suffrage more aggressively after seeing white southern resistance? Would his talent for managing factions have preserved northern commitment? Would leniency toward former Confederates have produced a different but equally damaging retreat?

We do not know.

We know who did make the decisions.

Andrew Johnson.

That fact began at Ford’s Theatre.

The next decade asks what happened when federal power tried to protect the freedom Lincoln’s war had made possible—and when murderers learned that constitutional rights were only as strong as the government able to enforce them.

In Colfax, Louisiana, on Easter Sunday 1873, white supremacist forces attacked Black citizens defending a courthouse.

The bodies would become a Supreme Court problem.
