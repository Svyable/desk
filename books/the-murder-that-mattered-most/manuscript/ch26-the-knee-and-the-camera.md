# The Knee and the Camera

The last verdict in this book is provisional.

The 2020s are not over.

That sentence is not a disclaimer tucked into the back matter. It belongs at the top of the chapter because the method of the book depends on afterlife, and afterlife requires time. A murder in 2028 could change the decade more than anything that happened in 2020. A case whose consequences now look permanent could shrink. A reform could succeed, fail, be repealed, or become the foundation for something larger.

History has not closed the file.

As of this edition, George Floyd’s murder remains the strongest verdict.

On May 25, 2020, Minneapolis police officers responded to a call about a suspected counterfeit twenty-dollar bill. Floyd, a forty-six-year-old Black man, was handcuffed and restrained on the street. Officer Derek Chauvin pressed his knees onto Floyd’s neck and back for more than nine minutes while Floyd was prone, handcuffed, and eventually unresponsive.

Bystanders watched.

One of them, seventeen-year-old Darnella Frazier, recorded the encounter on her phone.

The video changed the scale of the murder.

Chauvin was convicted in Minnesota state court of second-degree unintentional murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter. He later pleaded guilty to a federal civil-rights charge for willfully depriving Floyd of his constitutional rights. Other officers at the scene were convicted of federal civil-rights violations or entered guilty pleas in state cases.

The legal system therefore produced something many earlier chapters did not: a murder conviction against an agent of the state for killing a Black man in custody.

The historical consequence went far beyond the verdict.

Floyd’s murder triggered protests across the United States and around the world. The phrase Black Lives Matter, created after George Zimmerman’s acquittal in the killing of Trayvon Martin, became a global organizing language. City councils, legislatures, police departments, corporations, universities, sports leagues, unions, prosecutors, and federal agencies were forced to respond to questions about policing, race, use of force, protest, monuments, budgets, and institutional discrimination.

The murder mattered because three infrastructures were already waiting around it.

The first was movement infrastructure.

Black Lives Matter had spent seven years connecting deaths into a political argument. Ferguson had produced national networks. Activists had organized around Eric Garner, Tamir Rice, Freddie Gray, Sandra Bland, Philando Castile, Alton Sterling, Breonna Taylor, and many other cases. Police departments had already faced consent decrees, body-camera campaigns, civilian-oversight demands, and use-of-force reform.

George Floyd’s name did not create the issue.

It entered an issue with a vocabulary.

The second infrastructure was technological.

Almost everyone carried a camera connected to a distribution network.

This had changed the relationship between police and public evidence gradually. The beating of Rodney King in 1991 had been recorded by a citizen with a camcorder. Oscar Grant’s fatal shooting by a transit police officer in Oakland in 2009 was recorded by multiple passengers on phones. Philando Castile’s girlfriend livestreamed the aftermath of his 2016 shooting during a traffic stop.

By 2020, the camera and the network were one device.

Frazier did not need a television station to decide the video was news.

The public did not need to wait for an official press conference to understand the physical sequence.

That changed institutional time.

Police departments traditionally possess the first official narrative after use of force. Officers write reports. Supervisors review. Investigators gather evidence. Prosecutors make decisions. Press officers summarize.

Video can reverse that order.

Millions may see the event before the institution has finished writing its first account.

That does not make video self-interpreting. Camera angle, missing context, events before recording, audio quality, and legal standards still matter. Images can mislead as well as reveal.

Floyd’s video was unusually powerful because the central conduct continued for minutes in public while bystanders pleaded with officers to stop.

Duration became evidence.

A split-second use-of-force case often turns on what an officer reasonably perceived in a compressed moment.

Floyd’s murder unfolded slowly enough for viewers to watch repeated opportunities for someone to change course.

That is why the conduct of the other officers became part of the federal criminal case.

Civil-rights law did not ask only who applied force.

It asked about duties to intervene and provide medical aid.

The third infrastructure was pandemic disruption.

The United States in May 2020 was living through COVID-19 lockdowns, mass unemployment, fear, political conflict, closed schools, disrupted work, and unusual public attention to government authority and unequal vulnerability. Black Americans were experiencing disproportionate illness and death from the pandemic for reasons tied to occupation, health access, housing, underlying inequality, and exposure.

Millions of people had fewer ordinary routines competing for attention.

The murder landed in a country already asking whose lives institutions protected.

That context does not explain away the protests.

It helps explain their scale and timing.

Consequential events require receptive conditions.

The Boston Massacre needed revolutionary printers. Ida B. Wells needed a press. Emmett Till’s murder entered a Black media network and an organized civil-rights movement. Trayvon Martin’s death entered social media and movement organizing. George Floyd’s murder entered all of those accumulated systems at once.

The result was extraordinary mobilization.

Protests occurred in large cities, suburbs, small towns, and communities with very different racial demographics. Some were peaceful marches. Some included civil disobedience. Some devolved into looting, arson, property destruction, or violent clashes. Police responses ranged from facilitation to aggressive crowd control, arrests, chemical agents, impact munitions, and use of force against demonstrators and journalists.

The variation matters.

A national movement is not one event repeated in every city.

Local institutions produce local outcomes.

The phrase “the George Floyd protests” therefore covers millions of individual decisions: march, kneel, chant, donate, organize, break a window, protect a store, arrest a protester, fire tear gas, change a policy, remove a monument, paint a street, introduce a bill, reject a bill.

Historical consequence accumulated from all of them.

The most visible policy debate centered on policing.

“Defund the police” emerged as a slogan with multiple meanings. Some activists meant shifting portions of police budgets toward housing, mental-health response, youth services, violence prevention, and other social programs. Some advocated abolishing conventional policing and replacing it with different community-safety institutions. Others used the phrase more loosely to demand accountability and reduced police scope.

Opponents heard abandonment of public safety.

The ambiguity became politically consequential.

Support for police reform did not translate automatically into support for reducing police departments. Rising homicide in many cities during 2020 and 2021, disorder concerns, police staffing problems, and political backlash changed the reform environment. Some cities that had promised major restructuring later restored funding or changed course.

This is why the chapter cannot end in the summer of 2020.

Movements produce counter-movements.

Reform produces implementation.

Implementation produces friction.

Floyd’s murder moved policing policy, but not in one direction.

At the federal level, the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act passed the House in 2021 but did not become law. Negotiations over qualified immunity, federal standards, police misconduct, chokeholds, no-knock warrants, data collection, and accountability failed to produce a bipartisan statute.

Versions of the legislation have continued to be introduced, including again later in the decade.

The failure is part of the consequence.

A murder can transform national debate without generating the comprehensive federal law its supporters seek.

The contrast with the Lindbergh kidnapping is striking.

In 1932, Congress federalized kidnapping within months.

In the polarized twenty-first century, even a globally visible murder with mass public mobilization did not create equivalent national legislative consensus on policing.

Policy moved more substantially through states, cities, courts, prosecutors, collective bargaining, administrative rules, and litigation.

Some jurisdictions restricted neck restraints or chokeholds. Some changed use-of-force standards, duty-to-intervene rules, body-camera requirements, decertification systems, misconduct databases, or public-record laws. Some created or expanded civilian alternatives for behavioral-health calls. Some reduced low-level enforcement. Others emphasized recruitment, retention, enforcement, and public-order restoration.

The result is not one post-Floyd policing model.

It is a national experiment.

Minneapolis became the most direct institutional test.

The Minnesota Department of Human Rights opened an investigation one week after Floyd’s murder and later found probable cause that the city and police department engaged in a pattern or practice of race discrimination in violation of state law. A state court approved a court-enforceable agreement in 2023 requiring reforms and independent monitoring.

The U.S. Justice Department opened a separate federal pattern-or-practice investigation after Chauvin’s conviction. In 2023, it reported reasonable cause to believe the Minneapolis Police Department used excessive force, unlawfully discriminated against Black and Native American people, violated First Amendment rights, and discriminated against people with behavioral-health disabilities. The department and city negotiated a federal consent decree filed in January 2025.

Then national politics changed.

In May 2025, the Justice Department under a new administration moved to dismiss the federal case and retract the prior department’s findings, arguing the proposed consent decree was unjustified and overly intrusive. A federal judge dismissed the case. Minneapolis said it would continue implementing reforms, while the separate Minnesota state court agreement remained binding.

This reversal belongs in the chapter because it prevents a false ending.

Federal civil-rights enforcement is not an automatic institutional consequence.

It is governed by elections.

One administration can investigate a police department, negotiate a decree, and describe systemic violations. Another can reject that framework and withdraw federal enforcement. State law can continue where federal policy changes.

The post-Floyd reform story is therefore also a federalism story.

Colfax asked whether federal power could protect Black citizens when local institutions failed.

Minneapolis asks who controls police reform when federal and local governments disagree about the diagnosis.

The architecture changes.

The question survives.

The murder also changed corporate and cultural institutions.

Companies issued racial-equity statements and made funding commitments. Universities reviewed names, monuments, curricula, hiring, and campus policing. Sports leagues allowed or promoted protest. Newsrooms changed style rules and coverage priorities. Publishers, museums, foundations, and nonprofits expanded work around race and representation.

Some changes endured.

Some faded.

Some became targets of political backlash against diversity, equity, and inclusion programs. By the middle of the decade, corporations and institutions were retreating from or renaming some initiatives amid legal, political, and market pressure.

This is consequence in motion.

A murder can create an institutional wave whose recession is part of the history.

The Floyd protests also changed monuments and public memory.

Confederate statues and monuments to colonial or racist figures were removed, relocated, vandalized, or newly contested in cities across the country. Supporters argued public honor should not celebrate white supremacy or conquest. Opponents argued removal erased history, surrendered public space to mobs, or applied present moral standards simplistically to the past.

The argument was not really about stone.

It was about who has authority over civic memory.

The same question has appeared throughout this book.

Boston revolutionaries renamed King Street. Communities built monuments at massacre sites. Libraries took Harvey Milk’s name. Emmett Till became a national monument. September 11 created memorial architecture from absence.

A public monument is a verdict made physical.

George Floyd’s murder changed which verdicts Americans were willing to leave standing.

The economic afterlife included philanthropy and consumer behavior, but also insurance losses, business closures, municipal costs, and neighborhood conflict after unrest. Any history that describes the summer only as peaceful democratic awakening or only as riots is incomplete.

Both occurred.

A movement can contain people committed to nonviolence and people exploiting disorder. Police can protect protest and violate protest rights in the same national episode. Local businesses can support racial justice and suffer damage during unrest.

Complexity is not false equivalence.

It is refusing to assign one action to millions of people.

Floyd’s family also became public actors. Civil litigation produced a large settlement with Minneapolis. Family members testified before Congress, spoke internationally, and created charitable and memorial initiatives. Their role again demonstrates how families become infrastructure after consequential killing.

The burden is repetitive in American history.

Mothers, fathers, siblings, and children inherit cameras, microphones, court dates, foundations, anniversaries, and policy meetings because someone else killed a person they loved.

Public history then calls them advocates.

They were family first.

The strongest rival murder of the 2020s so far is the Uvalde school massacre of 2022, where a gunman killed nineteen children and two teachers at Robb Elementary School in Texas. The law-enforcement response became a national scandal because officers waited to confront the attacker despite post-Columbine active-shooter doctrine. Uvalde has had major consequences for school safety, police command, training, and gun politics.

Another rival is the 2022 racist mass murder at a Buffalo supermarket, where a white supremacist targeted Black shoppers and workers and livestreamed part of the attack. The case demonstrated the intersection of online radicalization, domestic terrorism, and platform amplification.

The decade has also seen mass killings in Monterey Park, Lewiston, Nashville, and other communities, as well as political and ideological violence whose full historical consequence is not yet measurable.

The chapter cannot know what comes next.

That uncertainty is part of the form.

Why does Floyd remain the provisional winner?

Because the murder connected a criminal conviction, a preexisting national movement, global protest, state and federal civil-rights investigations, local police restructuring, national legislation attempts, corporate and cultural changes, monument politics, and a continuing partisan argument over the role of police and federal oversight.

The breadth is unmatched so far.

The camera is central to that breadth.

Without Darnella Frazier’s video, police would still have had body-camera footage and witnesses. Investigators might still have charged officers. The case might still have become nationally known.

The public would not have encountered it in the same way.

Frazier’s recording removed institutional monopoly over the first version of events.

That is a historical shift larger than one case.

Citizen video can create accountability before formal investigation.

It can also create viral judgment before formal investigation.

Both are true.

The institutional challenge is to preserve due process while refusing to treat official narrative as automatically superior to public evidence.

This is where the book returns to Dallas.

Abraham Zapruder accidentally filmed a presidential assassination in 1963. His camera created evidence outside the state, but the film moved through editors, investigators, and controlled public release.

Frazier deliberately filmed state force in 2020 and distributed the evidence into a network built for immediate replication.

The distance between those two cameras is the distance between two media systems.

The murders mattered differently because information moved differently.

That is the final recurring lesson.

A killing is never historically consequential by itself.

Someone has to survive it.

Someone has to record it, remember it, prosecute it, organize around it, legislate after it, retreat from the legislation, build a monument, remove a monument, create a department, abolish a doctrine, write a report, hide a file, release a file, teach a child, or decide that this death belongs in the same sentence as another death from decades before.

Consequence is social work performed after violence.

George Floyd’s murder became enormous because the social machinery was ready.

The law convicted the officer.

The movement used the name.

Institutions fought over what the name required.

That fight is still happening.

Which is why this is not the end of the history.

It is only where the calendar catches us.
