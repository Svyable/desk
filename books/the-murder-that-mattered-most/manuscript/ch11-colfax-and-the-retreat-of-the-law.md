# Colfax and the Retreat of the Law

The courthouse at Colfax was not valuable because of the building.

It was valuable because of what a courthouse meant in Reconstruction Louisiana.

Who controlled the parish government? Who counted votes? Who held office? Who could serve on juries? Who could enforce contracts? Who could call a militia? Who could decide that Black citizenship existed in practice rather than only in amendments ratified in Washington?

Those questions brought armed men to the Grant Parish courthouse in April 1873.

Louisiana’s election of 1872 had produced rival claims to state and local offices amid fraud, intimidation, factional conflict, and federal intervention. In Grant Parish, Black Republicans occupied the courthouse at Colfax to defend officeholders they regarded as legitimately elected. White opponents organized armed forces to remove them.

On Easter Sunday, April 13, the confrontation became massacre.

White supremacist attackers overwhelmed the Black defenders. Estimates differ because the dead were not counted with the precision expected of a modern disaster investigation. National Park Service accounts commonly place the number of Black victims above one hundred, with many killed after they had surrendered. White deaths were few by comparison.

The bodies did not remain local.

Federal prosecutors used the Enforcement Acts, Reconstruction legislation intended to protect citizens from conspiracies that deprived them of constitutional rights. Members of the white mob were indicted. A handful were convicted in federal court.

Then the case traveled upward.

In *United States v. Cruikshank* in 1876, the Supreme Court overturned convictions and sharply limited the federal government’s ability to use the Fourteenth Amendment and Enforcement Acts against private violence.

That is why Colfax is the most consequential killing of the 1870s.

The massacre was not merely an episode of racial terror.

It became constitutional doctrine.

The distinction matters because Reconstruction was an experiment in whether the federal government could convert the end of slavery into enforceable citizenship.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery except as punishment for crime. The Fourteenth established national citizenship and prohibited states from denying due process or equal protection. The Fifteenth prohibited denial of the vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Congress enacted civil-rights and enforcement legislation. Federal troops and officials supported reconstructed state governments. Black men voted, held office, served in legislatures, organized politically, created institutions, and participated in governance at levels impossible before the war.

White supremacist resistance understood that political transformation clearly.

Organizations including the Ku Klux Klan used violence to suppress Black voting and Republican organization. Murders, beatings, threats, arson, and economic coercion were not random eruptions of prejudice. They were tools for changing election outcomes and restoring white control.

Colfax was politics with guns.

That phrase risks making the massacre sound like symmetrical combat between parties.

It was not.

Black citizens had armed themselves and occupied the courthouse because they expected attack. There was fighting. But once white forces gained the advantage, large numbers of Black men were killed, including prisoners and men attempting to surrender. The scale of post-surrender killing is what turns a contested armed confrontation into massacre.

The political objective was equally clear: control of parish government.

This is what makes Colfax different from a lynching carried out solely as punishment or racial spectacle. The killers were enforcing a rival theory of sovereignty.

If Black Republicans could hold the courthouse, Reconstruction government existed.

If white armed groups could kill them and take it, another government existed on the ground regardless of what federal law said.

Sovereignty is often taught as a constitutional abstraction.

At Colfax it was who remained alive in the building.

Federal prosecution attempted to answer private force with national law.

The Enforcement Acts had been passed during President Ulysses S. Grant’s administration in response to Ku Klux Klan terrorism and other efforts to suppress Black political rights. Federal officials had successfully prosecuted Klan members in parts of the South. Congress and the executive branch were claiming that individual rights created by Reconstruction amendments required national enforcement when states failed or participated in abuse.

The Colfax prosecutions tested how far that claim could go.

The indictments were broad and legally messy. Prosecutors charged defendants with conspiring to deprive citizens of rights including assembly and bearing arms. The Supreme Court’s response in *Cruikshank* was devastating to federal reach.

Chief Justice Morrison Waite’s opinion held, among other things, that the First and Second Amendments constrained the federal government rather than private individuals or the states, and that the Fourteenth Amendment prohibited state action rather than purely private conduct. The indictments did not adequately allege racial motivation under the Fifteenth Amendment or connect deprivations to state action in the manner the Court required.

The legal reasoning was technical.

The practical consequence was simple.

Private white terrorism became harder for the federal government to punish under Reconstruction civil-rights laws.

The Court did not say murder was legal. State courts could prosecute murder. Louisiana law could punish killing.

That answer was almost meaningless where local institutions were unwilling or unable to convict white attackers for murdering Black citizens.

A right remitted to hostile local enforcement can become a right without remedy.

Colfax therefore reveals the difference between formal federalism and lived federalism.

The constitutional question “Which level of government has authority?” can sound like a neutral allocation problem. When one level refuses protection, allocation decides whether a person has protection at all.

This is why the massacre’s legal afterlife matters more than its notoriety.

*Cruikshank* did not single-handedly end Reconstruction. The retreat had many causes: northern fatigue, economic crisis after the Panic of 1873, Democratic electoral gains, organized paramilitary violence in the South, contested elections, judicial decisions, political bargaining, and the eventual withdrawal of federal troops from key southern statehouses after the election crisis of 1876.

But the decision narrowed one of the tools available to answer terror.

The timing was terrible.

White supremacist organizations were learning to operate not only as secret night riders but as overt paramilitary forces. In Louisiana, the White League would emerge as a powerful organization using violence to overthrow Republican control. In Mississippi, armed Democratic campaigns would suppress Black voting during the 1875 election. Terror became increasingly coordinated with ordinary party politics.

The federal government needed stronger capacity and sustained political will.

It lost both.

Colfax became a model of impunity.

The word *model* does not mean attackers elsewhere copied a manual. It means the outcome communicated information. White political actors could observe that overwhelming violence might succeed locally and that federal prosecution faced constitutional limits. Black citizens could observe that rights written into amendments did not guarantee protection against organized attack.

Political behavior changes when expectations change.

If voting may get you killed and killers expect acquittal, turnout is not a simple measure of preference.

If officeholding exposes your family to violence, candidate recruitment changes.

If federal troops may leave, alliances change.

Terror works by changing the calculation before violence is necessary again.

That is why counting only the people murdered at Colfax understates the event.

The dead were the immediate victims. The electorate was the larger target.

This was the logic of Reconstruction violence throughout the South.

The political rights of Black Americans were being attacked through individual bodies.

The Supreme Court’s doctrine of state action would remain a central feature of Fourteenth Amendment jurisprudence, though later constitutional law and federal statutes developed additional routes for civil-rights enforcement. The long twentieth-century civil-rights revolution would rely on federal power again: criminal civil-rights prosecutions, voting-rights legislation, school desegregation orders, federal courts, Department of Justice litigation, and national enforcement against state and local resistance.

Colfax sits near the beginning of the period when that federal commitment receded.

It helps explain why the legal gains of Reconstruction could coexist with nearly a century of Jim Crow.

The amendments survived.

Enforcement weakened.

This distinction is essential because Americans sometimes speak of constitutional amendments as though ratification ends the historical problem.

The Fourteenth Amendment existed at Colfax.

The Fifteenth existed.

Congress had enacted enforcement laws.

Black men held office.

And more than one hundred Black citizens could still be killed in a struggle over political power.

Law is not self-executing.

That is the chapter’s central fact.

The strongest rival killing of the 1870s is the 1876 Hamburg Massacre in South Carolina, where a confrontation involving a Black militia company and white armed men led to the killing of Black men, including prisoners. Hamburg became part of the violent campaign that helped Democrats regain control of South Carolina.

Another rival is the 1874 Coushatta Massacre in Louisiana, where members of the White League forced Republican officeholders to resign and then killed several white Republicans and Black witnesses or supporters. The event directly targeted Reconstruction government.

Either could represent the decade.

Colfax wins because its murders produced a Supreme Court case whose doctrine translated local terror into national legal limitation.

That combination is unusually consequential.

The legal case also demonstrates how poor charging can create large precedent.

The indictments in *Cruikshank* were not models of precision. The Court criticized them for failing to identify the specific rights and racial grounds necessary to sustain the federal convictions. It is tempting to blame prosecutors for losing a case with enormous consequences.

That is too easy.

Reconstruction prosecutors were trying to fit unprecedented political violence into new constitutional provisions whose scope had not been settled. Congress had written ambitious laws. Courts were deciding what those laws meant. The entire national civil-rights enforcement system was experimental.

The attackers benefited from the experiment’s weaknesses.

This is another recurring pattern in consequential murder.

New forms of political violence often exploit gaps between old legal categories and new social realities.

The Harpes moved faster than frontier information systems. Anti-Masonic suspicion grew because institutions could not resolve Morgan’s disappearance credibly. Colfax paramilitaries exploited the gap between constitutional rights and federal jurisdiction over private violence. Later terrorists would exploit aviation systems designed for hijacking rather than suicide attacks. Digital video would expose police conduct faster than internal review systems could define the public narrative.

Institutions usually learn after someone pays the cost.

Sometimes they learn in the wrong direction.

*Cruikshank* was learning as limitation.

The Supreme Court was concerned with federalism, the text of the amendments, and the reach of national power. Those are legitimate constitutional questions. But constitutional interpretation has distributional consequences. Narrow federal authority meant reliance on state institutions that had often failed Black citizens.

Doctrine does not become neutral because it is written abstractly.

Who receives protection after the doctrine matters.

If Colfax had not happened, would *Cruikshank* have disappeared from constitutional history?

Perhaps the specific case would, but similar questions were already heading toward the Court. The 1870s produced a broader judicial narrowing of Reconstruction measures, including the Slaughter-House Cases in 1873 and later civil-rights decisions. Federalism and the state-action requirement would still have developed.

That is the strongest argument against Colfax’s singular importance.

The answer is that the massacre supplied the facts around which the limitation became unforgettable.

Abstract doctrine can be reargued.

Doctrine attached to bodies has a different moral weight.

The Court’s report did not dwell on the massacre in the way modern histories do. Legal opinions often strip away the human scene to isolate the question presented. That professional compression is useful for adjudication and dangerous for memory.

Read only the doctrine and you can forget what the federal government had been trying to prosecute.

Read only the massacre and you can miss why the killers’ impunity mattered nationally.

The two belong together.

The courthouse struggle also complicates the language of “Reconstruction failure.”

Black citizens at Colfax were not passive people waiting for federal salvation. They organized, voted, held office, armed themselves, and defended the local government they believed legitimate. Reconstruction was not a project done *to* the South by northern officials. It was also a period in which formerly enslaved people and their allies built political institutions under extraordinary threat.

The massacre killed participants in that project.

Later histories that describe the end of Reconstruction as northern “weariness” can make the retreat sound like a change in mood. For Black southerners, withdrawal meant exposure to people who had already demonstrated what they would do when federal protection weakened.

Colfax made the warning explicit in 1873.

The country proceeded anyway.

By the end of the decade, federal troops no longer sustained Republican governments in the remaining contested southern states. Redeemer Democrats consolidated control. Black voting continued in some places but under growing suppression. Segregation and disfranchisement would become more systematic in the decades ahead.

The Reconstruction amendments remained in the Constitution like dormant machinery.

Future generations would reactivate them.

That future is one reason Colfax matters now. The massacre is not only evidence of nineteenth-century racism. It is evidence about the architecture of rights.

Who enforces them when local majorities are hostile?

When does private violence become a constitutional problem?

How much power should the federal government possess to protect citizens from other citizens?

What happens when courts fear national overreach and local institutions practice underreach?

Those are not dead questions.

They are federalism.

The men killed at Colfax did not die to create a Supreme Court case. They were defending a courthouse and the political order it represented.

The attackers did not need to write constitutional doctrine. They created the facts from which doctrine emerged.

The consequence traveled farther than either group could see.

That is the verdict for the 1870s.

Colfax mattered most because murder became a mechanism for reversing democratic participation and because the federal prosecution of that murder produced a landmark limitation on national civil-rights enforcement at the precise moment such enforcement was most needed.

The courthouse remained.

The federal remedy shrank.

The next decade moves west to a mining town in Wyoming Territory, where white miners killed Chinese workers, burned homes, expelled hundreds of people, and discovered another route to impunity.

This time the conflict was not over a ballot.

It was over labor.
