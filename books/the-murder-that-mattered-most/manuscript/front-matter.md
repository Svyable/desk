# The Murder That Mattered Most

*250 Years of America, One Consequential Killing at a Time*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

A murder can be famous without being important.

That distinction is the reason for this book.

America has no shortage of killings that became spectacles. Some produced lurid trials, bestselling books, tabloid industries, documentaries, conspiracy cultures, tourism, museums, and decades of retelling. A few of those cases changed the country. Many did not. Their cultural afterlife became larger than their historical consequence.

The opposite happens too. A killing can sit far from the center of popular memory and still redirect institutions. A mob can kill people whose names most Americans never learn, and the prosecution that follows can narrow federal power for generations. The murder of three men can transform a schoolteacher and newspaper editor into the most formidable anti-lynching journalist in the country. A disappearance near the edge of the Erie Canal can help create a political party. The killing of a young draftsman outside Detroit can reorganize a collection of ethnic communities into a national civil-rights coalition.

History does not assign significance according to fame.

It assigns significance through consequences.

So this book asks an unreasonable question twenty-six times: what was the most important killing of each American decade?

The answer cannot be objective in the way an election result or a death certificate can be objective. There is no Bureau of Historical Consequence producing a final table. A presidential assassination and a lynching do not come with compatible units. Neither does the murder that produces a statute, the murder that radicalizes a movement, the massacre that accelerates a war, or the death that becomes the image through which millions of people understand an injustice that already existed.

The only honest way to make the choice is to make the standard explicit.

In these pages, *important* does not mean most gruesome. It does not mean most victims. It does not mean most mysterious. It does not mean the case with the most podcasts, the most famous defendant, or the most expensive investigation. It means the killing whose effects moved furthest beyond the people immediately involved.

Did it change who governed? Did it change what government could do? Did it create, fracture, or accelerate a movement? Did it change migration, policing, civil rights, federal jurisdiction, public safety, war, political organization, or national memory? Did later actors explicitly organize around the death? Did institutions form because of it? Did law respond? Did the country begin telling itself a different story afterward?

Those are the questions behind the verdicts.

The word *murder* in the title needs equal care.

This is not a book that silently converts every disputed death into a legal conclusion. Some chapters concern killings that resulted in murder convictions. Some concern assassinations, lynchings, massacres, or wartime violence. One concerns a disappearance widely believed to have ended in murder even though a body was never recovered. One concerns a killing after which the defendant was acquitted. The Boston Massacre produced acquittals and manslaughter convictions rather than murder convictions. The slaughter at Fort Mims occurred in war. George Zimmerman was acquitted of second-degree murder and manslaughter in the death of Trayvon Martin. Those distinctions are not technical clutter. They are part of the history.

The title uses murder in the older, public sense of a wrongful intentional killing because that is the question people actually argue about when they argue about the deaths that changed America. The chapters use the narrower language whenever the law requires it.

This matters for another reason. A book organized around killing can easily become a book organized around killers. That is not the intention here.

Killers often receive more narrative attention than the people they killed because investigation naturally follows the actor. The murderer has motives to reconstruct, movements to trace, weapons to identify, statements to analyze, trials to narrate. The victim can become the still point around which the killer’s biography expands.

I have tried to resist that gravity.

The historical question is what happened after a life was taken. That usually means following the people who survived: a mother who insisted the public see what had been done to her son; a journalist whose friends were lynched; a religious community forced into a succession crisis and migration; Black citizens who tried to hold political power during Reconstruction; Chinese miners driven from a town; Asian American organizers who discovered that a local sentencing decision could bind previously separate communities together; students, parents, teachers, activists, legislators, police departments, presidents, courts, and movements that inherited the consequences.

That choice also changes the emotional register of the book. There is violence here because violence happened. There is no reason to decorate it. The details included are the details needed to understand the event, the evidence, the public reaction, or the institutional response. Suffering does not become more serious when prose becomes more graphic.

The first chapter begins before the United States formally existed. In 1770, British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd and five civilians died. The legal proceedings mattered. The propaganda mattered more. The episode became one of the visual and political memories from which revolutionaries built an argument about what British rule meant. Starting there gives the book roughly the span promised in its subtitle and, more importantly, begins with a problem that never goes away: the difference between what happened, what a court could prove, what politics made of it, and what later generations remembered.

From there the path is not a march toward moral improvement.

The 1780s bring us to Gnadenhutten, where Pennsylvania militiamen killed more than ninety Christian Lenape men, women, and children. The 1870s bring us to Colfax, Louisiana, where white supremacists killed Black citizens in a struggle over Reconstruction government, and the federal prosecutions became part of a Supreme Court case that weakened the reach of federal civil-rights enforcement. The 1880s bring us to Rock Springs, Wyoming, where white miners killed Chinese miners and expelled hundreds more. The 1940s bring us to Moore’s Ford in Georgia, where two Black couples were murdered and a large federal investigation failed to produce convictions.

These events do not fit the comforting version of American crime in which law is the opposite of violence. Sometimes law punishes killing. Sometimes law proves unable to reach it. Sometimes legal procedure protects a defendant from public rage, as John Adams’s defense of British soldiers in Boston reminds us. Sometimes a legal verdict becomes the event that mobilizes people, as the acquittal in the Trayvon Martin case did. Sometimes a prosecution succeeds and still leaves the country arguing about the institution that made the killing possible.

Courts matter in this book, but verdicts are not the same thing as history.

The selection process therefore follows consequences across time. A case does not win its decade because people cared intensely for a week. It wins because something downstream became different and stayed different.

That rule produces choices readers will dispute.

The 1960s alone make the premise almost cruel. Medgar Evers was murdered in 1963. President John F. Kennedy was assassinated months later. Malcolm X was murdered in 1965. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in 1968. Robert F. Kennedy was assassinated that same year. Each changed American life. I chose Kennedy for the decade because the immediate transfer of presidential power, the creation of the Warren Commission, the enduring crisis of public trust around the assassination, and the consequences of a Johnson presidency reach across unusually many institutions. Someone else could choose King and build an excellent case.

That disagreement is not a flaw in the project. It is the project.

The interesting part is not the ranking. The interesting part is the argument the ranking forces us to make.

What do we count as consequence? Are changes in federal power more important than changes in movement consciousness? Is a statute more important than a migration? Does a death that becomes a symbol matter because the symbol changes behavior, or are we mistaking memory for causation? When an event accelerates a movement already underway, how much credit can the event receive? When a killing opens a path that another event might have opened anyway, are we looking at cause or timing?

Every chapter has a shadow chapter made of the cases that lost.

Sometimes those runners-up appear explicitly. Sometimes they remain in the research notes because crowding every decade with a list would turn the book into exactly the anthology I wanted to avoid. The selection briefs record the strongest alternatives and the evidence that could overturn a choice. If better evidence shows that a claimed consequence was overstated, the verdict should change.

That is especially important because murder invites myth.

Famous cases accumulate things people “know” that never happened, details compressed by movies, quotations repeated without sources, motives simplified after the fact, conspiracies that survive because mystery is more satisfying than bureaucracy, and moral lessons polished until the people involved stop resembling people. This book does not need those additions. The documented record is strange, painful, contingent, and consequential enough.

There is one more boundary worth drawing.

This is a history of America through selected killings. It is not a claim that violence is the best way to understand America. A country is built far more often by people working, organizing, teaching, caring, bargaining, voting, inventing, maintaining, migrating, raising families, serving institutions, resisting institutions, and changing their minds than by people killing one another.

But a killing can behave like a stress test.

It exposes what a society values, what it fears, who receives protection, who does not, which powers government suddenly discovers, which powers courts take away, which communities can turn grief into organization, and which stories a country chooses to carry forward.

Twenty-six deaths, or groups of deaths, cannot explain 250 years.

They can reveal pressure points.

That is enough for a book.

We begin on King Street, Boston, on a cold March night in 1770, before anyone at the scene knew they were standing inside the opening chapter of another country’s memory.
