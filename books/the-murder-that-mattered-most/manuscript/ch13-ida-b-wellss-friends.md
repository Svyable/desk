# Ida B. Wells’s Friends

Thomas Moss owned a grocery store.

That ordinary fact is the beginning of one of the most consequential transformations in American journalism.

Moss was a Black letter carrier and businessman in Memphis. With Calvin McDowell and Will Stewart, he helped operate the People’s Grocery in a neighborhood called the Curve. The store competed with a white-owned grocery nearby. Business rivalry, racial hostility, and local confrontation escalated in March 1892. After an armed clash in which several white men were wounded, authorities arrested Moss, McDowell, Stewart, and other Black men.

The three proprietors were taken from jail by a white mob.

They were lynched.

The murder of Moss, McDowell, and Stewart is the most consequential killing of the 1890s because one of their friends refused to accept the explanation white America usually gave itself for lynching.

Her name was Ida B. Wells.

Wells was already a journalist and teacher. She co-owned and wrote for the *Memphis Free Speech and Headlight*. She had sued a railroad after being forcibly removed from a first-class car. She was politically active, sharp, and willing to challenge both white supremacy and Black leadership she considered too cautious.

The People’s Grocery murders changed the scale of her work.

Moss was not an abstraction to her. He was a friend. His daughter was Wells’s godchild. The killing forced a question that would become her method: if lynching was supposedly a response to Black criminality, what crime explained the murder of successful businessmen whose economic competition had angered white neighbors?

The answer was not crime.

It was power.

Wells began investigating lynchings systematically. She collected newspaper reports, compared accusations with evidence, examined the recurring claim that Black men were lynched for sexually assaulting white women, and showed how often the public rationale concealed consensual relationships, economic competition, political conflict, fabricated allegations, or offenses that would never have produced mob execution for white defendants.

She did something more dangerous than condemn brutality.

She challenged the story that made brutality legitimate.

That distinction is why her work mattered.

Plenty of Americans could deplore mob excess while accepting the premise that lynching responded to an exceptional threat. If the victim had been accused of rape or murder, some white observers treated legal procedure as a technical inconvenience. The mob might be regrettable, but the target was assumed guilty.

Wells attacked that assumption with data and cases.

She understood that a lie repeated across separate newspapers can become a social fact. Each lynching was local. The justification was national. Black male sexuality was portrayed as predatory; white womanhood as under siege; mob violence as defensive.

The People’s Grocery killings did not fit the script.

Moss, McDowell, and Stewart were businessmen.

Their store challenged a local white economic monopoly. Their conflict showed that Black independence itself could be treated as provocation.

Wells drew the implication openly: lynching was a tool for maintaining racial hierarchy, not merely punishment for alleged sexual crime.

That claim endangered her.

While Wells was away from Memphis in 1892, a white mob destroyed the office of the *Free Speech*. Threats made returning unsafe. She continued her anti-lynching campaign from New York and later Chicago.

The murder had displaced the journalist it created.

That migration widened her audience.

Wells published *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* in 1892 and *A Red Record* in 1895. She traveled to Britain, where her speaking tours embarrassed American officials and reformers by making lynching an international issue. She challenged prominent white journalists, ministers, politicians, and reformers who minimized racial violence.

She also challenged Black accommodation.

Her argument was not built around patient moral appeal alone. She advocated economic resistance, armed self-defense under some circumstances, migration away from places where Black lives and property were unprotected, and organized pressure.

After the Memphis lynching, she urged Black residents to leave the city and to withhold patronage from systems that denied them protection.

Thousands did leave.

This is consequence moving through exit rather than law.

A community can punish a city by taking labor, consumption, talent, and population elsewhere.

The idea would recur throughout Black American history. The Great Migration was driven by many factors—industrial jobs, agricultural crisis, family networks, World War I labor demand, education, voting rights, and escape from southern racial violence. Lynching and the failure of law were among the pressures making migration rational.

Wells recognized that logic early.

If a government will not protect you, leaving can be political action.

The People’s Grocery case also belongs to the history of Black entrepreneurship.

Economic independence has never been separate from civil rights.

A Black-owned business creates income, property, jobs, credit, civic standing, and a place where community can organize. Under a racial order designed to enforce dependence, those gains can appear threatening.

The massacre in Tulsa in 1921 would later demonstrate this at catastrophic scale. White violence destroyed a prosperous Black district after a different triggering accusation. Other towns saw expulsions and attacks when Black political or economic power grew.

People’s Grocery was smaller.

The mechanism was already present.

The three men were not killed because they were powerless.

Their relative success helped place them in conflict.

This is why Wells’s investigation expanded beyond the moral horror of lynching to its material incentives.

White supremacy was not merely hatred.

It allocated land, jobs, customers, votes, offices, schools, legal standing, and safety.

Violence protected those allocations.

That is a harder system to dismantle than prejudice because people can benefit from it without needing to feel constant personal hatred.

Wells’s work threatened beneficiaries by naming the system.

The national context of the 1890s made her timing crucial.

Reconstruction had ended. Southern states were building Jim Crow through segregation, disfranchisement, poll taxes, literacy tests, constitutional conventions, and violence. The Supreme Court upheld racial segregation in *Plessy v. Ferguson* in 1896. Federal protection of Black voting rights had weakened. Congress repeatedly failed to enact federal anti-lynching legislation.

Lynching became a form of racial governance inside that retreat.

Wells kept count.

Counting can be radical when a society prefers anecdotes.

One lynching can be explained away as local passion.

Hundreds reveal a pattern.

Wells used statistics compiled from press reports not because nineteenth-century newspapers were perfectly reliable but because white newspapers were themselves documenting the violence. She turned the record of the system against the story the system told.

That method makes her a predecessor to modern data journalism and civil-rights documentation.

The NAACP would later track lynching. The Tuskegee Institute maintained records. Federal investigators, civil-rights organizations, sociologists, and journalists would build datasets around racial violence. In the twenty-first century, databases of police killings and hate crimes serve a similar political purpose: converting incidents into patterns that institutions can no longer dismiss as isolated.

Wells did this when the tools were paper, scissors, correspondence, rail travel, and nerve.

Her consequence therefore lies partly in method.

Moss’s murder gave the method urgency.

The personal relationship matters because it changed the distance between Wells and the subject. Before 1892, she had written against injustice. After the lynching, she devoted a major portion of her life to exposing mob violence.

Biographical turning points are dangerous to overstate. People rarely become entirely new because of one event. Wells already possessed the skills and commitments necessary for the work. The murder did not manufacture her courage.

It focused it.

This is a recurring pattern in consequential killing.

An event finds a person already prepared to respond.

The Boston Massacre found printers and organizers. Lovejoy’s death found abolitionists able to create a martyr. Emmett Till’s murder would find a mother willing to force the public to see what had been done to her child and a Black press capable of carrying the image. George Floyd’s murder would find networks of activists, smartphones, social media, and a public already debating police violence.

The killing matters because of the infrastructure waiting around it.

Wells was infrastructure in human form.

She had a press, a readership, political judgment, professional skill, and personal independence.

The mob destroyed the press.

It did not destroy those capacities.

Her work also complicated the American women’s rights movement.

Wells was a suffragist and later clashed with white suffrage leaders over race and segregation. At the 1913 suffrage parade in Washington, organizers asked Black women to march separately; Wells refused to accept that exclusion and joined the Illinois delegation.

Her politics did not fit inside one reform category.

Anti-lynching, women’s suffrage, Black political participation, economic autonomy, journalism, and urban reform overlapped.

This breadth is another downstream effect of the People’s Grocery murders. The journalist produced by the crisis did not remain confined to one issue.

Wells later helped found organizations including the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, though her relationship with the NAACP leadership was complicated and she did not remain central to the organization. She helped create Black women’s clubs and civic institutions in Chicago. She continued investigating and speaking about racial violence.

The murder therefore altered the institutional history of American civil rights through one person’s expanded career.

The strongest rival killing of the 1890s is the 1898 Wilmington massacre in North Carolina.

That event may be the decade’s strongest rival by a wide margin. White supremacists overthrew a legitimately elected biracial local government, killed Black residents, destroyed a Black newspaper, and forced political leaders from the city. It is often described as the only successful coup d’état in United States history.

If the selection rule were “most consequential episode of political murder,” Wilmington could win.

Its direct destruction of multiracial democracy and its role in the broader disfranchisement campaign are enormous.

Why choose the People’s Grocery killings instead?

Because this book is trying, when possible, to follow identifiable murders into a national afterlife rather than select the largest massacre every time. Moss, McDowell, and Stewart’s deaths can be traced directly into Wells’s anti-lynching campaign, which reshaped national and international understanding of lynching for decades.

The choice is close enough that the chapter should admit it.

Another rival is the 1892 Homestead Strike violence in Pennsylvania, where steelworkers and Pinkerton agents died in an armed confrontation at Andrew Carnegie’s Homestead plant. The battle changed labor politics and weakened the union. Again, it is a violent labor conflict rather than a straightforward murder case.

The 1890s were not short of blood.

The People’s Grocery case wins because three murders produced an intellectual weapon.

Wells’s central contribution was not simply saying lynching was wrong.

It was demonstrating that the stated rationale for lynching was often false.

That is a more destabilizing form of criticism.

A society can tolerate reformers who condemn excess while leaving the system’s logic intact. It has more trouble with someone who shows the logic is fraudulent.

Wells did that with names, dates, accusations, outcomes, and comparisons.

She also understood the press as a battleground rather than a neutral observer.

White newspapers often repeated allegations that rationalized lynching. Black newspapers challenged them. The contest was over which account became public truth before any court examined evidence.

The mob acts fast.

Fact-checking is slow.

That asymmetry still shapes public violence.

Wells responded by creating a counter-record durable enough to survive the first headline.

If Moss, McDowell, and Stewart had not been murdered, Wells might still have become a major journalist and reformer. Her earlier writing makes that plausible. The anti-lynching movement would still have existed. Black communities did not need one Memphis case to understand the danger.

But her own testimony about the turning point is strong, and the trajectory of her work after 1892 is unmistakable.

The murders supplied a case that demolished the sexual-crime justification and revealed the economic function of racial terror in one blow.

That is the counterfactual weight.

The killers removed three Black businessmen from a Memphis neighborhood.

They helped send Ida B. Wells into the world.

That outcome was not justice. It was consequence.

The distinction matters because historical narratives sometimes redeem atrocities by emphasizing what good eventually followed. Nothing Wells achieved makes the murders worthwhile. A movement created by a death does not retroactively purchase the death.

History is not a moral balance sheet.

The point is that survivors build with what violence leaves them.

Wells built an archive.

The archive changed the argument.

The argument outlived the men who killed her friends and the newspapers that defended the racial order around them.

That is the verdict for the 1890s.

The next decade opens with another assassination of a president.

William McKinley would die in 1901 after being shot at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo.

The bullet placed Theodore Roosevelt in the White House.

This time presidential succession would not change Reconstruction.

It would change the scale of the modern presidency.
