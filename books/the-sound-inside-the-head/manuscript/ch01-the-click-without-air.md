# The Click Without Air

The easiest way into this subject is to remove Havana from it.

Forget diplomats. Forget Russia. Forget intelligence agencies, embassies, lawsuits, congressional hearings, leaked briefings, and the phrase “directed energy weapon.” Put a human being in front of a pulsed radio-frequency source under controlled conditions and ask a narrower question: does the person hear anything?

The answer, under the right pulse conditions, is yes.

That fact is so strange that it has spent sixty years attracting explanations larger than itself. It does not need them. The phenomenon is interesting enough on its own.

The subjective sound produced by pulsed radio-frequency exposure has been described as a click, buzz, chirp, knock, or hiss depending on the pulse train and modulation. It does not arrive through the air in the ordinary way. A nearby microphone does not have to detect a corresponding acoustic wave in the room. The sensation can seem to originate inside or just behind the head. Deafness does not remove every version of the effect, though the state of the auditory apparatus matters. Early work found that pulse structure and peak power were more important than average power.

That last point matters because average power is what people reach for when they want to make the effect sound impossible.

Microwave exposure is often discussed as heat: put enough energy into tissue and temperature rises. But the auditory effect is not ordinary bulk heating. A sufficiently short pulse can deposit a tiny amount of energy rapidly enough that the resulting thermal expansion launches a pressure transient before the heat diffuses. The temperature change at hearing threshold can be microscopic. The pressure transient does the useful work.

The head becomes part of the apparatus.

That sentence is easy to abuse. It does not mean the brain demodulates radio as though it were a crystal set. It means soft tissue absorbs a pulse, expands by a minuscule amount, and launches a mechanical wave. The cochlea then receives an acoustic event generated inside the body. The effect belongs to thermoacoustics more than telepathy.

The distinction is not semantic. It changes what follows.

If the mechanism were direct neural reception of electromagnetic information, almost any science-fiction extension would feel nearby. If the mechanism is thermoelastic conversion, then the problem acquires hard constraints: absorbed energy per pulse, pulse width, head geometry, frequency-dependent absorption, pressure amplitude, repetition rate, beam geometry, and tissue heating. Engineering becomes unavoidable.

By the 1970s, Arthur Guy, Chung-Kwang Chou, James Lin and others had moved the phenomenon away from anecdote. Animal experiments recorded auditory responses. Physical models predicted pressure waves. Work on guinea pigs showed threshold behavior consistent with thermal-expansion theory. Lin compared candidate mechanisms and found thermal expansion capable of dominating radiation pressure and other proposed effects. Later reviews described the microwave auditory effect as one of the best-established biological responses to pulsed microwave energy with a known mechanism.

There is a temptation to stop there and say: therefore Havana.

That “therefore” is the entire problem of this book.

A demonstrated sensory effect establishes that pulsed RF can create a sound-like perception without an ordinary external sound source. It does not establish that the same exposures produce vestibular injury, headache, cognitive dysfunction, or persistent neurological symptoms. It does not establish that any particular person was exposed. It does not identify a transmitter. It does not identify an operator. It does not identify a country.

It gives us one verified rung on a ladder.

In anomalous subjects, one real rung is dangerous. People climb it in both directions. Believers use it to pull every stronger claim upward. Skeptics use the weakness of the stronger claims to kick the real rung away.

The effect survives both maneuvers.

That makes it unusually useful.

There are many stories in fringe science where the first empirical step is already doubtful. Here the first step is not. Human beings can perceive properly pulsed radio-frequency energy as sound. The mechanism has been studied in humans, animals, models, and physical materials. The phenomenon does not need Havana syndrome to be real, and Havana syndrome does not need the phenomenon to deserve medical care.

That separation gives us a clean starting point.

It also gives us a warning about language. “Microwave hearing” sounds like reception of a broadcast. “Frey effect” sounds like an eponymous curiosity. “Radio-frequency auditory effect” sounds clinical. “Thermoelastic pressure transient” sounds like something an engineer can calculate. They point toward the same observed family of effects but carry different imaginative cargo.

The cargo matters when a mystery becomes national security.

Once a diplomat says a sound arrived from a direction and pain followed it, an old laboratory effect becomes culturally available as an explanation. That availability is not evidence of causation. It is evidence that the mechanism is not impossible in principle.

The phrase “not impossible” is weaker than people want.

It is also stronger than nothing.

The entire subject lives in that uncomfortable interval.
