# The Intelligence Community Says No

On March 1, 2023, the U.S. intelligence community published the assessment that many people had been waiting for and almost no one was prepared to read carefully.

Most agencies judged it very unlikely that a foreign adversary was responsible for the anomalous health incidents reported by U.S. personnel.

That sentence was immediately treated as the government closing the case.

The assessment was more specific.

It addressed whether one or more foreign actors bore responsibility, deliberately or unintentionally, for the incidents reported since 2016. Analysts pursued three broad lines of inquiry: intelligence linking an adversary to the events; feasibility and existence of a mechanism that could produce the reported symptoms; and medical analysis of whether affected personnel shared a common physical injury.

The conclusions were negative across the campaign hypothesis.

Investigators did not find intelligence showing adversary leaders had authorized a worldwide attack program. They did not identify a consistent operational pattern linking the reports. In some places where the United States had strong collection coverage, no adversary activity associated with the incidents emerged. Reported events occurred across many countries and circumstances, making a coherent clandestine campaign increasingly difficult to reconcile with the absence of intercepted planning, procurement, training, communications, or other residue.

Medical heterogeneity also weighed against one common cause.

Many reports could be explained by preexisting conditions, conventional illness, or environmental factors. The administrative category had grown far beyond the distinctive early Havana presentations. The more reports entered the pool, the harder it became to sustain the idea that every case represented the same technology.

None of that means the investigation proved a universal negative.

Intelligence assessments do not prove impossibility. They state judgments at defined confidence levels based on available collection.

This distinction is usually obvious when the subject is conventional. An assessment that a state probably lacks an operational missile does not prove no engineer has ever tested a component. An assessment that no invasion is planned does not prove no officer has drafted a contingency. Yet in the Havana debate, “very unlikely a foreign adversary is responsible” was often translated into “directed energy is impossible.”

That is not what the document said.

The 2023 assessment itself existed alongside the 2022 expert panel, which had found pulsed electromagnetic energy physically plausible for a selected subset. The U.S. government therefore held two ideas at once: a mechanism could be plausible, and an adversary campaign could still be very unlikely.

This is not bureaucratic incoherence.

It is what happens when different questions receive different evidence.

The mechanism panel asks whether an exposure could produce a pattern.

The intelligence assessment asks whether someone did it.

Suppose investigators discover that a particular poison could produce a diplomat’s symptoms. That raises the plausibility of poisoning. It does not identify a poisoner. If surveillance then shows no procurement, access, motive, or operational trace, the poisoning hypothesis can decline without the toxicology changing.

The same logic applies here.

The strongest public argument in the 2023 assessment was not medical.

It was operational absence.

A sustained global campaign against hundreds of U.S. personnel would be a substantial intelligence operation. It would require technology, operators, target acquisition, movement or emplacement, doctrine, command authority, and repeated execution in countries with different security environments. Over years, some combination of signals intelligence, human intelligence, captured equipment, defectors, procurement records, or allied reporting should begin to appear.

According to the assessment, it did not.

Absence of evidence is not always evidence of absence.

Scale changes that equation.

The larger and longer a proposed operation becomes, the more consequential a persistent null becomes. A single covert event can leave little trace. Hundreds of events across dozens of countries are a different proposition.

This is one reason the “subset” question became more important after 2023.

If the global campaign hypothesis weakens, the remaining space is not necessarily zero. It is smaller.

Could a handful of early cases have involved a device while later reports had other causes?

Could a foreign service have tested a system intermittently rather than run a campaign?

Could an exposure have been incidental to surveillance rather than intended as injury?

Could the mechanism be external but not foreign-state-directed?

Could the most compelling cases still have ordinary explanations not yet identified?

Those questions survive the global null because they require less operational residue.

They also become harder to prove.

A small-subset hypothesis can become unfalsifiable if it is allowed to shrink every time evidence fails. That is the danger. If every explained case is discarded and every unexplained case is retained as proof of a hidden attack, the hypothesis protects itself by definition.

A serious residual hypothesis needs prospective defeat conditions.

What would lower belief?

High-quality synchronized sensor data showing no relevant RF, acoustic, ultrasonic, or environmental anomaly during future core events would matter.

A robust medical account that explains the distinctive acute phenomenology through known disorders would matter.

Repeated failure to find any adversary technical program despite improved collection would matter.

Independent engineering work showing all plausible covert RF pathways require impossible or conspicuous exposures would matter.

Conversely, what would raise belief?

A captured device with measured output matching predicted effects would matter.

Intercepted technical documentation tied to an adversary program would matter.

Event-time RF recordings with spatial and waveform characteristics matching a plausible biological mechanism would matter.

A mechanism-specific biomarker appearing after monitored exposure would matter.

Operator testimony corroborated by technical records would matter.

The 2023 assessment did not provide any of those decisive positive findings.

It did provide a large and uncomfortable negative one: after years of intelligence work, the United States had not found a foreign campaign commensurate with the broad public story.

That result deserves weight.

It does not deserve powers it does not have.
