# From Bioeffect to Weapon

A weapon is a logistics system wrapped around a physical effect.

That definition is less exciting than the usual one and much harder to satisfy.

A laboratory phenomenon becomes a weapon only after someone can generate it reliably, deliver it at useful range, aim it under field conditions, power it, cool it, control its dose, produce the intended effect often enough to matter, avoid unacceptable collateral effects, train operators, move or conceal the hardware, and decide when to use it. The mechanism is the beginning of the program, not the end.

Microwave hearing has always tempted people to skip those steps.

The attraction is obvious. The effect is invisible. It can be directional. The sensation can appear to originate inside the head. Average power can be modest because short high-peak pulses do the work. In the imagination, all that turns quickly into a perfect covert weapon.

The imagination does not carry the antenna.

A fielded system needs an aperture. It needs a waveform generator and amplifier. It needs a power supply sized for peak demand. It needs the target to be inside the useful beam. It must tolerate reflections, multipath, windows, walls, metal, body orientation, movement, and other people. If the desired endpoint is more severe than a click, the exposure has to rise or exploit a different interaction, which creates a new set of engineering and biological constraints.

That middle layer is where historical claims deserve scrutiny.

One real U.S. effort is often invoked in this context: a small-business research project known as MEDUSA, “Mob Excess Deterrent Using Silent Audio,” pursued in the early 2000s for the Marine Corps. Contemporary descriptions and later reporting indicate that a prototype effort explored whether the microwave auditory effect could be used for crowd or personnel deterrence. The existence of such work establishes military interest in weaponizing the phenomenon. It does not establish successful incapacitation, operational deployment, or an injury mechanism matching anomalous health incidents.

The distinction is exactly the same one we applied to Soviet research.

A contract is a document showing that someone paid to test an idea.

It is not a certificate that the idea worked.

Patents deserve the same treatment. U.S. patents assigned to the Air Force describe methods and apparatus for communicating speech using the radio-frequency hearing effect. Those filings are interesting because they show that engineers considered intelligible modulation of the effect. Patent issuance is not efficacy testing. The patent office does not certify that a covert long-range device was built, fielded, or safe.

In controversial technology, patent records have become a peculiar species of counterfeit proof. They look official. They contain diagrams. They use precise language. They carry government assignees. But patents are claims to intellectual property, not independent replications.

Still, they should not be ignored.

They narrow what is plausible to have been contemplated. They can expose design lineages. They can identify inventors, assignees, dates, and technical assumptions. If a later allegation resembles a patent, that resemblance is a lead for historical research, not confirmation of an event.

The same hierarchy applies to directed-energy programs more broadly. The United States has fielded real high-power microwave systems for electronics effects and real millimeter-wave systems for crowd control. The Active Denial System, for example, uses millimeter-wave energy to heat the skin superficially and create intense discomfort. Its existence proves that directed radio-frequency energy can be engineered into an antipersonnel system.

It does not prove that an entirely different covert system can create the neurological pattern associated with anomalous health incidents through walls without obvious thermal injury.

Technology categories are not mechanisms.

A laser pointer and a surgical laser are both lasers. A microwave oven and a radar are both microwave systems. A known directed-energy weapon does not validate every proposed directed-energy effect.

This is where the phrase “energy weapon” becomes almost useless.

Everything from a flashlight to a rifle bullet transfers energy. The national-security phrase collects lasers, particle beams, high-power microwaves, acoustic systems, millimeter-wave heating, and speculative devices into one conceptual box. Once the box exists, evidence for any one member can be used rhetorically to support another.

The proper unit is the exposure waveform and the biological endpoint.

What frequency range?

What pulse width?

What peak field or power density?

What repetition rate?

What antenna gain?

What distance?

What tissue absorbs the energy?

What pressure or temperature transient results?

What cellular or organ-level mechanism follows?

What dose-response relationship has been observed?

What signs would distinguish that mechanism from ordinary disease or stress?

The Havana story spent years without public answers to most of those questions.

That is not evidence the answers do not exist inside classified programs. It is evidence that the public cannot scientifically evaluate the full weapon hypothesis.

In 2021, Foster, Garrett, and Ziskin tried to make part of the calculation public. Their analysis accepted the microwave auditory effect but challenged the idea that the classic thermoelastic mechanism could scale into a practical injurious weapon of the kind imagined in some Havana accounts. They compared pressure levels and energy requirements, arguing that harmful acoustic-equivalent stresses would entail exposures with other obvious consequences.

Their paper should not be treated as the last word. It is valuable because it does something the debate badly needs: it states a mechanism, calculates consequences, and creates assumptions that can be attacked.

The 2022 Intelligence Community Experts Panel reached a more permissive conclusion. It judged pulsed electromagnetic energy plausible for the core characteristics of a subset of cases and stated that sources could be concealable with moderate power requirements. That conclusion is more difficult to interrogate because the public summary redacted much of the technical basis.

The two records create a question rather than an answer.

Did the panel consider a different mechanism from classic microwave hearing?

Did it have measurements from cases that materially altered the calculation?

Did it model unusual antenna configurations, pulse structures, or near-field coupling unavailable in open literature?

Did it merely use a broader definition of plausibility than Foster and colleagues used for weaponization?

Without the missing technical material, public readers cannot know.

That uncertainty is often filled with allegiance.

People decide which institution they trust.

But trust is not a substitute for an experiment.

A weapon claim earns strength when multiple independent traces converge: hardware, emissions, injuries with mechanism-specific biomarkers, captured components, intercepted procurement, operator testimony, technical manuals, adversary communications, reproducible field signatures, or consistent dose geometry.

A mature covert program may hide some of those traces.

It is unlikely to erase all of them forever.

This becomes important later, when we ask not whether a microwave weapon can be imagined but whether a decade of investigation has produced the kind of residue a real deployment should leave.

The effect is real.

The weapon is a separate engineering proposition.

The operation is a separate historical proposition.

Three doors.

The same key does not open all of them.
