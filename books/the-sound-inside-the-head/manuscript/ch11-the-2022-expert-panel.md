# The 2022 Expert Panel

The intelligence community’s expert panel had something the National Academies did not: access to classified information.

That sentence is frequently used as a trump card.

It should instead be treated as an evidentiary complication.

ODNI and CIA convened the panel in 2021 to examine potential causal mechanisms for anomalous health incidents. The panel included specialists in medicine, psychiatry, biology, acoustics, environmental science, ionizing radiation, and radiofrequency effects. According to the declassified February 2022 release, the experts received dozens of briefings, reviewed more than a thousand classified documents, examined incident and trend information, and met affected individuals who shared medical records and experiences.

The panel was not asked to decide who was responsible.

That boundary was explicit.

Its first finding was that the signs and symptoms of AHIs were genuine and compelling. Its second was that a subset could not easily be explained by known environmental or medical conditions and could be due to external stimuli. Its third was the one that made headlines: pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly in the radio-frequency range, plausibly explained the core characteristics, although information gaps remained.

Ultrasound was also judged plausible, but mainly in close-access scenarios. Psychosocial factors alone did not explain the panel’s core subset, though they could contribute to some other incidents or to longer-term symptoms. Ionizing radiation, chemical and biological agents, infrasound, ordinary audible sound, long-distance ultrasound, and bulk heating from electromagnetic energy were judged implausible for the core characteristics absent other synergistic factors.

This was not a vague endorsement of “energy weapons.”

It was a comparative mechanism assessment.

The panel defined core characteristics tightly: acute onset of sound or pressure, often in one ear or on one side of the head; nearly simultaneous symptoms such as vertigo, loss of balance, or ear pain; a strong sense of locality or directionality; and no known environmental or medical explanation.

The narrower the phenotype, the stronger the panel’s confidence that ordinary explanations were insufficient.

But narrowing the phenotype creates a methodological problem that deserves more attention than it has received.

If the selection criteria are partly the same features a directed external stimulus is expected to produce, then the selected sample becomes enriched for compatibility with that hypothesis. That is not circular if the next step tests independent predictions. It becomes circular if phenomenological fit is treated as independent confirmation.

The panel did try to move beyond phenomenology. Its public summary said sources existed that could generate the required stimulus, could be concealable, and could have moderate power requirements. It said pulsed electromagnetic energy could propagate with low loss through air over tens to hundreds of meters and, with some loss, through many building materials using specialized antennas and techniques.

Those are engineering claims.

Most of the engineering basis was not public.

That is where the assessment became difficult to evaluate scientifically.

If the panel had access to classified measurements showing a particular waveform can produce vestibular disruption at a known field strength, that would materially change the public debate. If it had access only to intelligence reporting that an adversary had studied such devices, that would change the intelligence prior without validating the biological mechanism. If it had incident-specific RF measurements, those would be especially important. If it had none of those things and was instead integrating open literature with plausible classified device concepts, the conclusion would be weaker than many readers assume.

The declassified summary does not let us distinguish those possibilities.

This is not an accusation of bad faith.

It is the unavoidable consequence of a scientific conclusion whose premises are partly classified.

The normal logic of science is inspectable inheritance. A later researcher can read the methods, test the assumptions, reproduce the exposure, challenge the statistics, and discover where a result fails. A classified technical judgment has a different inheritance. Cleared successors can inspect it. The public cannot. Independent outsiders are left arguing over fragments.

The result is a strange hybrid authority.

The panel has more information than the public.

The public has less ability to evaluate the panel than it would a normal scientific paper.

That makes humility mandatory in both directions.

It is unreasonable to pretend the panel saw nothing important simply because the supporting material is classified.

It is equally unreasonable to treat classification as proof that the hidden material is decisive.

There is another reason the 2022 panel matters: it separated mechanism from campaign.

The same government was already moving toward the judgment that U.S. adversaries were not conducting a sustained worldwide operation responsible for hundreds of reported incidents. At first glance, this looked contradictory. How could a plausible external energy mechanism coexist with no foreign campaign?

Easily.

Most reports could have ordinary causes while a small subset remains externally caused.

A device could exist without having been used.

A foreign actor could have tested a capability only rarely.

An accidental or surveillance-related exposure could occur without an organized attack campaign.

A plausible mechanism could turn out not to explain any real case.

The causal and attribution questions are only loosely coupled until event-specific evidence joins them.

The panel’s seven recommendations reveal what it thought was missing. It called for better data, biomarkers, detectors, communications, clinical measurements, biological-effects work, and research devices.

That list is revealing.

A field with decisive event evidence would not need to begin by asking for detectors.

The panel was saying, in effect: the hypothesis is plausible enough to instrument for properly next time.

That is a much more useful conclusion than “the weapon exists.”

It also exposes a painful counterfactual.

Had calibrated RF, acoustic, ultrasonic, and environmental sensors been deployed early in Havana and later locations, the mystery might have been smaller. A synchronized record of an exposure and acute symptoms would have been transformational. A long run of high-quality null sensor data during reported events would have been transformational too.

Instead, years of investigation produced medical files without corresponding exposure files.

The body was measured after the fact.

The room usually was not.

The 2022 panel did not solve that problem.

It finally named it.
