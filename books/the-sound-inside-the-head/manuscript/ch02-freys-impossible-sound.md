# Frey's Impossible Sound

Allan H. Frey did not begin with a weapon.

He began with a complaint that should have sounded ridiculous.

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, people working near radar transmitters sometimes described clicking or buzzing sensations associated with the equipment. Radar operators had folklore. Folklore is not evidence, but it is often where an investigator first notices a repeatable nuisance. Frey, a biophysicist, decided to see whether the reports could be made to behave like an experiment.

His 1962 paper, “Human auditory system response to modulated electromagnetic energy,” is worth reading because so much later mythology has been poured into it. The paper did not claim mental transmission. It did not report voices planted into the mind. It did not describe invisible torture. It described auditory perception induced by pulsed electromagnetic energy, investigated across carrier frequencies, modulation conditions, distance, and power.

Frey reported that both normal-hearing and deaf subjects could perceive something under some conditions. He described the effect over distances of hundreds of feet from an antenna. He emphasized peak power density rather than average power. He compared the subjective sound with ordinary acoustic stimulation. The closest match occurred when an acoustic amplifier was driven by the same modulator used for the RF transmitter.

This was odd physics wearing the clothes of physiology.

It immediately raised a mechanism question. If the ear canal was not receiving an ordinary sound wave from the transmitter, where did the auditory event enter the system? Frey explored possibilities that later work would narrow. The early literature contains speculation about direct neural interaction because the phenomenon was new and because the experimental toolbox was primitive by current standards.

That uncertainty is important historically. It is also dangerous retrospectively. Later writers sometimes lift early mechanism speculation out of sequence and present it as though the field never advanced beyond it.

It did.

By the 1970s, a series of experiments by Guy, Chou, Lin, Galambos, and others increasingly supported thermoelastic generation of pressure waves. Chou and Guy measured microwave-induced auditory responses in guinea pigs and found the threshold dependence on pulse duration matched thermal-expansion predictions. Guy and colleagues demonstrated related effects in physical materials. Lin calculated expected acoustic frequencies and pressure amplitudes from head geometry and energy deposition.

The mystery changed shape.

The question was no longer whether a radio-frequency pulse could somehow become sound. The answer was yes. The question was how much energy per pulse was needed, how the resulting pressure wave propagated, and what happened at intensities above the hearing threshold.

The last question is where the modern controversy begins.

Hearing something is not the same as being injured by it.

At threshold, the calculated temperature rise can be extraordinarily small. A 2003 review by John Elder summarized the mechanism as rapid thermal expansion creating sound transients detected through bone conduction and noted that microwave-induced sound at hearing threshold was a biological effect without an accompanying health effect. That is an important boundary. A biological effect is simply a measurable interaction with biology. It is not automatically harmful.

Sunlight is a biological effect. So is caffeine.

The word “bioeffect” acquired darker meanings in Cold War literature because it sat between physics and physiology, a place where military interest, occupational safety, and adversary research overlapped. Soviet and Eastern European work sometimes reported effects at exposure levels or under conditions Western laboratories did not reproduce cleanly. Translation quality varied. Dosimetry varied. Experimental controls varied. Intelligence analysts had to decide whether poor reproducibility reflected bad science, different equipment, different endpoints, or hidden capability.

That is a miserable problem for an intelligence service.

If an adversary claims a weapon based on an effect your own scientists consider weak, you cannot simply laugh. Nor can you promote the adversary’s literature to truth because it is frightening. You have to investigate under conditions where the cost of a false negative and the cost of a false positive are both high.

Frey’s work became part of that landscape.

Decades later, after American officials in Havana described directional sounds and pressure followed by symptoms, the old papers were rediscovered by journalists and commentators. The rediscovery created a strange inversion. A real but limited sensory phenomenon became more famous for what it might imply than for what it actually demonstrated.

Frey’s result did not say that pulsed microwaves can secretly scramble cognition from across a street.

It said something narrower and stranger: under appropriate conditions, RF pulses can be converted by the head into an acoustic event the subject hears.

That is enough to force seriousness.

It is not enough to close the case.
