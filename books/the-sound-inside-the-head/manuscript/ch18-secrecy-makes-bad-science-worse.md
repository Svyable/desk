# Secrecy Makes Bad Science Worse

There are subjects in which secrecy protects the experiment.

This is not one of them.

A double-blind drug trial can hide allocation from participants and investigators in order to reduce bias. Classified weapons testing hides details from adversaries. Those are different kinds of secrecy. The first makes evidence stronger by controlling information. The second often makes public evidence weaker by removing information.

Anomalous health incidents sit at the collision point.

If the suspected mechanism involves a foreign intelligence capability, the best evidence may be classified: intercepted communications, captured hardware, clandestine reporting, telemetry, surveillance coverage, foreign procurement, technical exploitation, or information from human sources. Public scientists do not get to inspect most of it. They see a declassified conclusion.

If the suspected mechanism also involves biology, that is a problem.

Biology is noisy.

Small effects need replication. Exposure parameters need precision. Confounders multiply. Clinical outcomes vary. Mechanisms that look plausible in a classified briefing can disappear under independent replication.

The intelligence system and the scientific system optimize for different failure modes.

Intelligence protects sources and methods because disclosure can destroy future collection or endanger people. Science exposes methods because concealment prevents correction. Intelligence analysts can rely on a source whose identity must remain secret. Scientists normally cannot ask the world to accept an unreplicable claim because the decisive instrument is classified.

The Havana case tried to do both at once.

The result was predictable.

Government panels knew more than they could say.

Outside researchers could calculate only what was public.

Patients knew what they experienced but not the cause.

Journalists received fragments from people with unequal access and unknown motives.

Members of Congress saw classified briefings and then made public statements that could not be independently checked.

Political leaders changed, bringing new assumptions about which institutions had behaved honestly.

Everyone inherited a different dataset.

This environment rewards a specific kind of argument: “If you knew what I know, you would agree with me.”

That sentence is poison to public epistemology.

Sometimes it is true.

There are genuine classified facts.

But the structure is indistinguishable from bluff to anyone without clearance.

It also creates a market for insinuation. A former official can say a program is “consistent with” a foreign capability without naming evidence. A journalist can report that sources have seen extraordinary technical information. A skeptic can dismiss the entire story as unverified because the strongest claims remain hidden. None of those moves resolves the underlying question.

Secrecy creates another problem: selective declassification.

Governments rarely release a random sample of what they know. They release material for policy, legal, diplomatic, bureaucratic, or political reasons. A declassified assessment is therefore not simply the visible tip of a neutral iceberg. It is a chosen slice.

The 2022 expert panel released a summary saying pulsed electromagnetic energy was plausible while withholding much of the technical basis.

The 2023 intelligence assessment released a broad judgment against adversary responsibility while the underlying collection remained classified.

The 2025 update disclosed that two components had shifted but did not disclose enough new reporting to let outsiders reproduce that shift.

The 2026 recall of the assessments attacked the integrity of the analytic process without immediately replacing the old public record with a full new evidence package.

Each release changed the narrative more than the inspectable dataset.

This is how a false evidentiary ecology can form.

A claim can be simultaneously repeated by officials, journalists, lawyers, patients, and researchers while all of them ultimately depend on the same hidden source. Apparent independent confirmation may be circular.

The opposite can happen too.

Several agencies may appear to agree because they share one analytic framework or one flawed dataset. Institutional plurality does not guarantee evidentiary independence.

The only defense is provenance.

For every important claim, ask where it first enters the public record.

Did a physician observe it?

Did a patient report it?

Did a journalist hear it from an official?

Did an official read it in an intelligence report sourced to the same journalist?

Did a congressional statement rely on the intelligence report?

Did a later academic paper cite the congressional statement?

Without that genealogy, citation count can become an illusion.

This matters especially for exotic technology claims.

The public record around microwave weapons includes genuine peer-reviewed physiology, military research contracts, patents, press reports, declassified panel judgments, alleged foreign programs, and speculative extrapolations. They are often stacked together as though each independently proves the same thing.

They do not.

The physiology proves a sensory effect.

The contracts prove research interest.

The patents prove claimed designs were filed.

The panel judgments prove qualified experts considered certain mechanisms plausible.

The press reports prove sources made claims to journalists.

None alone proves deployment in Havana.

A mature hidden program should eventually generate cross-domain residue. That is the principle this book borrows from documented secret programs elsewhere in intelligence history. Real programs need people, hardware, maintenance, budgets, testing, and mistakes. Compartmentation can delay convergence. It cannot abolish physical reality.

That gives the public a way to reason without classified access.

Do not ask whether secrecy could hide the program.

Of course it could, for a time.

Ask what the program would have to do to exist.

Then look for those consequences.

If a foreign state built a compact pulsed-energy system, what industries and institutes could support it?

What components are unusual?

What test ranges or medical studies would be necessary?

What safety problems would operators encounter?

What would counterintelligence services see if they knew where to look?

What would an acquired device reveal?

Secrecy should change the search strategy.

It should not change the standard of reality.
