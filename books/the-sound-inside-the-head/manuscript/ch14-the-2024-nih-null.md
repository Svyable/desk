# The 2024 NIH Null

In March 2024, the National Institutes of Health published the kind of result that should have made the Havana debate more precise.

Instead it made it louder.

NIH researchers had spent years evaluating more than eighty U.S. government employees and adult family members who reported anomalous health incidents. The work included advanced MRI, clinical examinations, auditory and vestibular testing, neuropsychological measures, and blood biomarkers. Controls included healthy people with comparable work assignments.

The major finding was negative.

The researchers did not identify a consistent pattern of MRI-detectable brain injury that distinguished the AHI participants from controls. Most clinical, research, and biomarker measures also did not differ significantly. Participants reported more fatigue, post-traumatic stress, and depression, but the broad biological signature many readers expected was absent.

The result immediately entered two incompatible stories.

One said the government had finally admitted there was no injury.

The other said NIH had examined people too late, used the wrong tools, diluted the original Havana cases with heterogeneous later reports, or produced a politically convenient null.

The paper itself supported neither pole.

The imaging team made an important statement: failing to find persistent MRI differences does not exclude an adverse event at the time of the reported incident. Some participants were scanned relatively soon after symptoms, but on average the first MRI occurred around eighty days after onset. A transient physiological disturbance could have resolved. A mechanism could produce symptoms without the kind of structural or functional difference the chosen MRI methods can detect. The study’s sample size also limits detection of small subgroup effects.

Those caveats are real.

They cannot be used as an all-purpose solvent for null results.

A hypothesis that predicts persistent brain injury should become less likely when a careful study fails to find persistent brain injury. The correct update depends on what the proposed mechanism predicted before the result was known.

This is where the Havana literature has often lacked discipline.

Before a null, advocates sometimes point to brain injury as the expected consequence.

After a null, the injury becomes transient, microscopic, heterogeneous, or invisible to available tools.

Any of those revisions may be correct.

They need new predictions.

If the injury is transient, which biomarkers should be collected within hours?

If it is microscopic, which blood, CSF, electrophysiological, or imaging measures should detect it?

If only a narrow subset is affected, what preregistered criteria define the subset without selecting on the outcome?

If no lasting tissue injury is expected, what mechanism sustains months or years of symptoms?

The NIH study was strongest not because it disproved an attack but because it forced these questions.

It also complicated the earlier Penn imaging findings.

The two research programs were not simple replications. They included different cohorts, time windows, controls, acquisition methods, and analytic approaches. One cannot subtract the NIH result from the Penn result and declare the answer zero. Nor can one declare the later study irrelevant because the earlier one reported differences.

The correct comparison is methodological.

What did each study define as a case?

How were controls selected?

How many imaging outcomes were tested?

Which findings were prespecified?

How robust were they across analytic pipelines?

Did the observed differences correlate with symptom severity or timing?

Could the effect be reproduced in an independent cohort?

The NIH investigators emphasized reproducibility within their own methods. They used multiple measurements and models and did not find a stable distinguishing pattern. That is strong evidence against a large, common, persistent MRI signature across the studied AHI population.

It is weaker evidence against a rare transient effect in a small early subset.

This hierarchy should be kept intact.

The study also raises a difficult issue about what the category had become by 2024.

The first Havana cohort was small and unusually specific. Over time, reports were collected from many locations and circumstances. Administrative vigilance increased. Personnel were trained to recognize possible AHIs. That is sensible occupational health policy, but it changes the sample.

The denominator grows.

Ordinary migraines, vestibular disorders, panic episodes, infections, noise exposures, medication effects, and unrelated neurological events inevitably enter a broad surveillance category unless the case definition is highly specific.

This is not mass hysteria.

It is base rates.

If thousands of people are told to report a common set of symptoms, a large fraction of reports will have ordinary causes even if a rare exotic cause also exists. The more successful the surveillance system becomes at capturing possible cases, the lower the average specificity of the category can become.

The NIH null may therefore be telling us something about the category as much as the cause.

“Havana syndrome” may never have been one syndrome.

The government’s later preference for “anomalous health incidents” quietly acknowledges this. An incident is not a diagnosis. It is an event requiring investigation.

That language is better.

It is still vulnerable to reification.

Once an acronym exists, people begin treating it as a disease entity.

The NIH result pushes in the opposite direction. It says there is no single obvious durable biological fingerprint across the people studied.

That does not erase their symptoms.

It erases one easy story about them.

Good null results do that.

They do not end mysteries.

They take away explanations that were too comfortable.
