# Havana, 2016

The first public story was not about microwaves.

It was about sound.

American personnel in Havana began reporting strange episodes in late 2016. The early accounts varied, but several recurring features appeared: an abrupt sound or pressure sensation, sometimes perceived as highly localized; ear pain; dizziness; disequilibrium; headache; cognitive difficulty; sleep problems; visual complaints; and a lingering sense that something had happened in a particular place at a particular moment. Some people moved a few feet and felt the sensation stop. Others described no sound at all. Family members were sometimes involved. The episodes did not present as one clean syndrome.

The U.S. government’s response was slow, fragmented, and initially uncertain enough that the institutional history became part of the medical problem.

The Government Accountability Office later documented that the State Department office responsible for deciding whether to convene an Accountability Review Board learned about the Cuba incidents from media reports months after they began. That fact is bureaucratic, not etiological, but it matters. A mysterious exposure problem is unusually sensitive to early evidence. Rooms change. Equipment is moved. memories harden. buildings are cleaned. people talk to one another. If an external source existed, the best chance to measure it may have been at the beginning.

The early investigative frame also shifted.

“Sonic attack” became the phrase because sound was salient in many accounts. The label turned perception into mechanism. Once the public heard “sonic,” every later hypothesis had to explain a sound source in the room.

But a perceived sound need not originate as airborne acoustic energy.

That was one reason the microwave auditory literature entered the discussion.

Another reason was the directionality. Some early reports described an effect confined to a small spatial region or experienced from a specific direction. A directional radio-frequency source seemed to offer a way to combine an internal sound-like sensation with location dependence. Yet even that fit was imperfect. Classic microwave hearing is usually described as clicks, buzzes, or chirps. Some Havana recordings circulating publicly were later linked to crickets. The reported symptom constellation extended well beyond hearing.

The case did what ambiguous cases do: it accumulated candidate mechanisms faster than it accumulated discriminating measurements.

Infectious disease was considered. Toxic exposure was considered. Pesticides were considered. Infrasound and ultrasound were considered. Mass psychogenic illness or functional neurological mechanisms were proposed by some outside researchers and rejected as sufficient explanations for the most distinctive cases by later government expert panels. Pulsed radio-frequency energy moved from fringe-sounding suggestion to formal consideration.

Meanwhile, patients needed care.

This creates a moral complication that often gets mishandled. A physician does not need to know the geopolitical cause of an illness to treat symptoms. A government does not need to prove an attack before providing care to employees who became disabled while serving abroad. Yet compensation, security response, and public language can imply causal conclusions even when the science remains open.

Congress eventually passed the HAVANA Act to provide financial support to qualifying personnel. That was a policy judgment about care and service, not a scientific adjudication of mechanism.

The distinction is important because opponents of the weapon hypothesis sometimes treated compensation as political validation of an unproven cause, while supporters treated institutional recognition of patients as recognition of an attack.

Both readings ask a care system to settle a forensic question it was not designed to settle.

The first medical papers added another layer.

Teams evaluating personnel from Cuba reported neurological, vestibular, cognitive, and other abnormalities. Some early work used language associated with concussion or acquired brain injury despite the absence of conventional head trauma. Imaging studies reported group-level differences. Those findings received enormous attention because they appeared to convert subjective reports into visible injury.

But group-level neuroimaging is difficult evidence in a small, selected cohort.

Brains differ naturally. Scanner protocols matter. multiple comparisons matter. control groups matter. Timing matters. Prior conditions matter. A statistically significant group difference does not automatically identify the cause, date, or clinical meaning of that difference. Later NIH work with a different cohort and a broad battery of tests did not find consistent MRI-detectable brain injury or most biomarker differences.

The early and later studies cannot be cleanly treated as direct replications because they did not necessarily study identical people at identical times with identical methods.

Again the case produces two temptations.

One is to say the early imaging proved injury and the later null must therefore reflect delay or institutional retreat.

The other is to say the later NIH study disproved the early patients’ experiences.

Neither follows.

A transient injury may leave no persistent MRI signature. An early reported imaging difference may also be nonspecific, overfit, or unrelated to the incident. Symptoms can persist after a physiological insult is no longer measurable by the available tools. Symptoms can also persist through mechanisms that do not involve structural brain injury.

The event, the mechanism, and the current clinical state are separate variables.

By the time the United States reduced embassy staffing in Havana, the original question had already fragmented.

What happened to the first cluster?

Why did similar reports later appear in China, Europe, Russia, the United States, and elsewhere?

Were the later reports the same phenomenon?

Did publicity change who recognized or reported symptoms?

Was there one cause, several causes, or no common cause?

Was the first cluster different from the administrative category that grew around it?

And if a foreign actor was responsible for some subset, what was the objective: injury, harassment, surveillance, accidental exposure, or something else?

Those questions are still often compressed into “Do you believe Havana syndrome?”

It is a bad question.

Syndromes are not religions.

The right question is which observations survive when the label is removed.
