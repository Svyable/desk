# The First Brain-Injury Papers

In February 2018, the Havana story acquired a medical vocabulary more consequential than “sonic attack.”

A University of Pennsylvania team published a case series in *JAMA* describing twenty-one U.S. government personnel who had reported directional audible or sensory phenomena in Cuba. The patients had been referred for multidisciplinary evaluation after initial screening. The paper described persistent cognitive, vestibular, visual, auditory, sleep, and headache complaints, along with examination findings. The authors wrote that the group appeared to have sustained injury to widespread brain networks despite no history of blunt head trauma.

The phrase landed hard.

Brain injury turned an unexplained exposure into something that sounded anatomically settled.

The data were more complicated.

The study was a retrospective case series, not a blinded exposure experiment. Twenty-one people completed the multidisciplinary evaluation, on average 203 days after the reported exposure. Persistent symptoms were common: the paper reported cognitive symptoms in seventeen, balance symptoms in fifteen, visual dysfunction in eighteen, auditory symptoms in fifteen, sleep impairment in eighteen, and headaches in sixteen. The cohort was selected because these individuals had been identified as having a similar exposure history and clinical findings.

There was no known source.

There was no measured dose.

There was no unexposed embassy cohort evaluated through an identical referral pipeline at the same time.

That does not make the observations worthless. It defines what the paper can do.

It can describe a clinical cohort.

It cannot identify the causal agent.

The paper itself acknowledged the source was undetermined. Yet public discussion often promoted the clinical description into forensic evidence. The logic ran backward: concussion-like symptoms imply a concussion-like physical insult; no blunt trauma occurred; therefore an invisible external energy source must have produced equivalent injury.

That is an attractive inference.

It is not the only one.

Many neurological and vestibular conditions can produce symptoms overlapping with post-concussive syndromes. Functional neurological disorders can produce real disabling symptoms without structural injury. Migraine can produce dizziness, sensory sensitivity, cognitive fog, and visual disturbances. Inner-ear disorders can destabilize balance and trigger secondary cognitive fatigue. Sleep disruption amplifies almost everything. Anxiety and hypervigilance can worsen symptoms without creating them from nothing.

To say that is not to diagnose the Havana cohort with any of those conditions.

It is to explain why symptom resemblance does not identify mechanism.

The 2018 paper drew immediate criticism from neurologists and others who questioned case definition, testing, interpretation, and the concussion analogy. Some argued that the described abnormalities were nonspecific and that normal variation or overly permissive thresholds could make ordinary performance look pathological. The authors defended their methodology and emphasized the unusual cluster and consistent history.

This was medicine doing what medicine does under uncertainty: arguing about whether a pattern is a syndrome before the cause is known.

The next major paper made the dispute more visual.

In July 2019, a Penn group published advanced neuroimaging analyses of forty government personnel with possible exposure to “directional phenomena” and neurological symptoms, compared with forty-eight demographically similar healthy controls. The study reported group-level differences in whole-brain white-matter volume, regional gray and white matter volumes, cerebellar microstructural measures, and functional connectivity in auditory and visuospatial subnetworks.

MRI has enormous rhetorical power.

A colored brain map feels like a photograph of causation.

It is not.

The authors themselves said the clinical importance of the differences was uncertain. JAMA’s accompanying editorial made the same point. Group-level neuroimaging can identify statistical differences without telling us whether those differences are pathological, preexisting, caused by the reported events, or meaningful for an individual patient.

The control problem was central. A perfect comparison group would have been U.S. personnel with similar occupations, overseas assignments, stress exposures, demographics, and medical screening, evaluated through the same pipeline before any incident and then followed prospectively. That group did not exist.

Nor could it easily have existed.

The event was not a planned experiment.

This is why occupational and intelligence medicine is so vulnerable to hindsight. Baseline data are rarely collected at the granularity needed for a novel exposure. When something strange happens, investigators suddenly want pre-event MRIs, vestibular tests, neuropsychological batteries, blood biomarkers, and environmental measurements that no one had reason to gather.

The absence of baseline converts every later measurement into ambiguity.

If a white-matter metric differs from controls, did the event cause it?

Was it present before the event?

Did selection into a demanding government career correlate with it?

Did age, sleep, medications, migraine history, prior head injury, or scanner differences contribute?

Did the statistical pipeline uncover a real group signature that is simply not specific to injury?

Those questions do not refute the imaging findings.

They prevent them from becoming a timestamp.

The 2019 paper also demonstrates why the phrase “objective evidence” can mislead. Imaging is objective in the sense that a scanner produced data. Interpretation still depends on models, preprocessing, region definitions, correction for multiple comparisons, control selection, and biological meaning. An objective measurement can support an uncertain inference.

That nuance disappeared in two opposite narratives.

In the attack narrative, the scans became proof of brain damage from an external weapon.

In the psychogenic narrative, methodological criticism became proof there was no injury at all.

Neither was licensed by the paper.

The strongest reading was narrower: a selected symptomatic cohort of U.S. personnel who reported unusual directional phenomena showed a constellation of clinical findings, and a larger overlapping cohort showed certain group-level neuroimaging differences of uncertain clinical significance. The cause remained unknown.

That is not a satisfying sentence.

It is the one the evidence earned.

The story would become harder when later investigators looked for the same kind of durable biological signature and mostly did not find it.

A field that had already learned to argue from absence would then have to learn what a null result can and cannot erase.
