# The Device Question

By 2026, the device question had moved from hypothetical engineering to a more uncomfortable category: reported hardware that the public could not inspect.

That is progress only in the loosest sense.

A machine exists or it does not. That sounds binary. But the evidentiary value of a machine depends on what is known about it.

Who built it?

What waveform does it produce?

At what frequencies?

What peak and average power?

What pulse width and repetition rate?

What antenna geometry?

What effective radiated power?

What does the field look like at ten meters, fifty meters, behind glass, behind masonry, through foliage, in a reflective room?

What happens to a biological target at those exposures?

What happens to instruments nearby?

Does the system have any plausible connection to a reported event?

Without those answers, “device” is a noun carrying more certainty than the evidence.

The reported U.S. acquisition of a foreign-made pulsed-radio-frequency device is therefore fascinating and insufficient.

The House Homeland Security Committee’s January 2026 letter treated the reported purchase as serious enough to demand records about cost, procurement, testing, and purpose. The reported price—described as in the eight figures—suggests the government considered the object valuable. Price is evidence of willingness to pay, not efficacy.

The reported presence of Russian-origin components is even more dangerous rhetorically.

Components travel.

Commercial parts cross borders. A Russian component does not establish Russian design, Russian assembly, Russian government sponsorship, or use by Russian intelligence. Conversely, a device assembled elsewhere can embody Russian technical work without carrying Russian labels. Provenance must be reconstructed like any other supply chain.

The public needs a bill of materials, serial numbers, manufacturing marks, firmware, RF components, antenna design, sourcing records, and technical testing before origin becomes more than inference.

The Norway story raises a different problem.

According to *The Washington Post*, a government scientist built a pulsed-energy device from classified information and tested it on himself. If accurate, the episode could be one of the most important experimental facts in the entire AHI story because it would provide a bridge between device engineering and acute human effects.

It could also be almost useless scientifically.

A one-person self-experiment is not a controlled trial.

Expectation matters, especially when the subject is also the builder. Dose reconstruction matters. Prior medical history matters. Blinding matters. Sham exposure matters. Outcome definition matters. Timing matters. Independent replication matters. The ethics are obvious.

The reported scientist was skeptical beforehand, which reduces one simple expectation-bias story but does not solve the design problem.

The most valuable part of the episode, if it occurred as reported, is not that the researcher felt ill.

It is that the apparatus should still exist.

Hardware can be measured.

A device turns a vague mechanism into numbers.

If its output is characterized fully, independent laboratories can ask whether the exposure produces thermoacoustic pressure, vestibular effects, transient blood-brain barrier changes, neural stimulation, heating, or something else. Animal models can establish dose-response curves under controlled conditions. Phantoms can map internal fields. Sensors can be designed around the actual waveform instead of a guessed one.

This is how the mystery could become smaller.

The obvious obstacle is classification.

If the device embodies stolen or sensitive foreign technology, publishing its parameters may reveal collection sources, adversary capability, or countermeasure vulnerabilities. The national-security reasons for secrecy could be legitimate.

The scientific cost would also be real.

A causal claim that depends on a classified exposure regime cannot be independently validated by the broader biomedical community. Cleared laboratories could do the work, but the public would still receive conclusions without methods. The result would reproduce the exact problem that has plagued the case since 2016.

There is a middle path.

Parameters can be abstracted.

Investigators can publish normalized pulse energy, frequency bands, exposure geometries, pressure-equivalent outputs, specific absorption rates, and biological results without revealing every circuit detail. Independent red teams can be cleared. Multiple laboratories can receive functionally equivalent exposure sources. Pre-registered protocols can be released before results even if the exact source hardware remains classified. Raw biological data can be de-identified and published.

Classification need not mean methodological darkness.

The device question also invites a historical comparison.

Real secret technologies tend to leave maintenance residue.

They need testing facilities, calibration, operators, replacement parts, procurement, doctrine, safety procedures, failure reports, transport cases, power systems, and budgets. A covert device can be hidden during operations while still creating an institutional footprint somewhere else.

This does not mean the public will see the footprint in real time.

It means investigators should look for it.

If a foreign service has deployed a pulsed-energy system for a decade, what training pipeline supports it? What technical institute developed the emitter? What military or intelligence unit owns the mission? What procurement covers high-power RF components? What accidents occurred during development? What safety limits were written for operators? What countermeasures do insiders discuss? What allied services know?

A recovered device would make those searches much sharper.

It would also create a test for the “technological surprise” argument.

For years, advocates of the attack hypothesis have said the United States may underestimate foreign work because relevant research continued abroad while Western bioelectromagnetics narrowed or lost funding. That is possible. A captured device can convert the argument from sociology to engineering.

If the hardware contains techniques genuinely absent from open literature, that matters.

If it turns out to be a conventional high-power RF source with no demonstrated injury pathway, that matters too.

If it is optimized for electronics rather than people, that may redirect the entire story.

If it cannot reproduce any core symptom under controlled conditions, belief should fall.

The worst possible outcome is the one most compatible with bureaucracy: the device remains classified, testing remains opaque, fragments leak selectively, political factions cite different pieces, and no public experiment is allowed to settle anything.

That would turn hardware into another rumor.

A machine is supposed to make a mystery more concrete.

We should demand that it does.
