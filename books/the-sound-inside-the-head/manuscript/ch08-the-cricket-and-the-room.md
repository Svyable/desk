# The Cricket and the Room

One of the strangest turns in the Havana story came from an insect.

Recordings of the noises associated publicly with some incidents circulated in the press. Acoustic researchers later argued that a widely discussed recording closely resembled the call of the Indies short-tailed cricket, *Anurogryllus celerinictus*. The match was not merely “it sounds like a bug.” The pulse repetition structure and spectral characteristics were consistent with the insect, with indoor reflections potentially altering the sound.

The cricket result mattered.

It also proved less than either side wanted.

If a recording associated with the Havana story was a cricket, then that recording was poor evidence of a novel acoustic weapon. It did not follow that every affected person heard a cricket. It did not follow that every person who heard a cricket had no other exposure. It did not follow that the reported symptoms were imaginary. It did not follow that no external energy mechanism was possible.

The insect solved an audio file.

The public debate wanted it to solve a geopolitical mystery.

This mismatch is a recurring pattern in anomalous investigations. A mundane explanation for one artifact is promoted into a total explanation because the artifact had been culturally central. The same thing happens in reverse when one unexplained artifact is promoted into proof that the mundane explanations have failed.

The room itself complicates everything.

Acoustic localization indoors is fragile. Reflections create standing waves and comb filtering. High-frequency insect calls can produce strange spatial patterns. A person moving a short distance can encounter large changes in amplitude. Mechanical devices can couple through walls, floors, pipes, or furniture. Ultrasound can generate audible byproducts through nonlinear interactions. Air conditioners, security systems, power supplies, transformers, motors, and electronics can produce narrowband or intermittent sound. Human perception under stress is not a neutral instrument.

That does not make witnesses unreliable in a moral sense.

It makes environments complicated.

A person can accurately report that a sensation changed when she moved three feet and still be wrong about whether the cause was a beam. The observation is data. The inference is another layer.

Directionality is especially seductive because it feels diagnostic.

If something seems to come from the left, an external source seems obvious. But ordinary hearing is built to infer direction from tiny differences in arrival time and intensity between the ears. Bone conduction can scramble those cues. Interior reflections can create false apparent directions. A localized electromagnetic field could also create genuinely directional exposure. The phenomenology alone does not choose among them.

The 2022 Intelligence Community Experts Panel treated strong locality or directionality as one of the core characteristics of its priority subset. That was reasonable if the goal was to study the hardest-to-explain cases. It also means directionality became part of the selection function.

This is where environmental forensics should have become central.

A room is an instrument if investigators treat it that way early enough.

Measure the radio-frequency spectrum over time.

Map field strength spatially.

Record acoustic and ultrasonic channels with calibrated microphones.

Monitor power quality.

Inspect for mechanical sources.

Characterize windows, walls, and conductive structures.

Record timing with enough precision to compare symptoms to sensor events.

Preserve devices before replacing them.

Repeat measurements under controlled changes.

In practice, diplomatic environments are hostile to this ideal. Embassies are already full of classified emitters, shielding, counter-surveillance systems, secure rooms, communications equipment, and legitimate reasons not to publish a spectral inventory. Foreign services operate nearby. Sensitive collection capabilities may reveal themselves if defensive sensors are described publicly. Evidence preservation competes with operational security.

Secrecy again makes the experiment worse.

The cricket episode also illustrates a deeper psychological problem: explanations arrive at different scales.

“Cricket” is vivid and concrete.

“Multifactorial illness with heterogeneous environmental, medical, and perhaps external causes” is not.

“Russian microwave weapon” is vivid and concrete too.

Humans prefer explanations with an object in them.

That preference is dangerous in a case where the category may contain different events. Some reports could have ordinary acoustic sources. Some could involve migraine, vestibular disorders, infections, medications, anxiety, functional neurological symptoms, chemical exposures, or other conditions. A small number could still involve an external engineered stimulus. The more heterogeneous the pool, the more misleading any single mascot becomes.

The cricket became the mascot for skepticism.

The microwave auditory effect became the mascot for attack.

Neither mascot is the dataset.

There is another lesson in the room.

If an attacker wanted to create symptoms without detection, a system that also produced a distinctive sound would be an odd design choice unless the sound was incidental to the physical mechanism. Microwave hearing provides exactly that possibility: the sound can be generated inside the target as a side effect of pulsed energy.

That is one reason the mechanism remains interesting.

But an interesting fit is not a fingerprint.

A fingerprint would connect event-specific measurements to predicted exposure. If the reported direction points toward a wall, what field gradient should exist? If moving two meters stops the effect, what antenna geometry produces that boundary at the proposed frequency and range? If multiple people in a room are affected differently, do their locations and orientations match the model? If the source is outside the building, what attenuation and reflections follow from the wall construction? If no RF burst is recorded, was the sensor capable of seeing the hypothesized waveform?

These are tedious questions.

They are how a weapon hypothesis becomes science.

The cricket did not end the story.

It showed how badly the story needed instruments.
