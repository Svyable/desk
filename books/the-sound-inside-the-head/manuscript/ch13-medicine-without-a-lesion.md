# Medicine Without a Lesion

The hardest sentence in this story may be the one physicians already know.

A patient can be very sick without giving the investigator a satisfying picture.

Medicine is full of conditions that are disabling before they are legible to imaging, and conditions that remain disabling even when imaging never becomes diagnostic. Migraine does not need a tumor-shaped lesion. Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness does not require a torn vestibular nerve. Functional neurological disorder can produce severe symptoms without malingering and without a structural lesion that explains them in the old mechanical sense. Post-infectious syndromes can outlast the infectious agent. Pain can persist after tissue has healed.

The body is not obligated to leave a forensic souvenir.

This matters because anomalous health incidents were repeatedly discussed as though one question could settle all the others: is there brain damage?

If yes, an attack.

If no, imagination.

That binary was clinically crude and politically useful.

The people who reported AHIs did not merely ask for an etiological theory. They needed treatment, workplace accommodation, rehabilitation, compensation, and a government willing to take their symptoms seriously. Those needs existed whether the cause turned out to be a microwave device, vestibular disease, toxic exposure, migraine, functional disorder, infection, or several different things grouped under one administrative label.

The government struggled with that separation.

The 2024 Government Accountability Office report on Defense Department care is revealing precisely because it is almost boring. GAO did not solve the mechanism. It documented access problems. By January 2024, 334 AHI patients had qualified for care through the military health system, but as of May only 33 had been entered into the DOD registry. Consent procedures, interagency agreements, coordination, and implementation delays limited the system’s ability to create the dataset investigators said they needed.

This is the kind of failure that never appears in weapon diagrams.

If the syndrome is scientifically uncertain, a registry is not clerical housekeeping. It is infrastructure for learning. Standardized intake, timing, symptom definitions, exposure history, imaging protocols, vestibular testing, blood samples, follow-up intervals, and consent rules determine whether later researchers can compare cases at all.

A mystery becomes less solvable every time the metadata differ.

The registry problem also exposes the tension between care and research.

Patients are not specimens.

They may reasonably distrust an institution that first doubted them and later asks for extensive data. They may fear career consequences, privacy loss, classification complications, or having their symptoms reinterpreted as psychological. Researchers, meanwhile, need consistent information. Agencies need consent. Intelligence services need to protect sources and methods. Physicians need enough history to treat the person in front of them.

No one system optimizes all of those goals.

The phrase “psychogenic” made the situation worse.

In public argument, it was often heard as “fake.” That is not what modern neurology means by functional symptoms or stress-mediated amplification. The nervous system can generate real symptoms through altered processing rather than a visible structural lesion. Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness is a particularly relevant example because it can follow a vestibular event and then continue through maladaptive sensory weighting and heightened visual dependence. The National Academies explicitly considered PPPD as a possible secondary reinforcing mechanism in some affected people.

That model allows an external event and a functional chronic phase to coexist.

It also allows an ordinary vestibular event and the same chronic phase to coexist.

The diagnosis does not solve the cause of the trigger.

This is the kind of layered medicine that public controversy flattens.

A patient can have had an acute exposure and later develop symptoms maintained by a different mechanism.

A patient can have no exotic exposure and still have the same chronic symptoms.

A patient can have depression or PTSD after becoming chronically ill without those conditions having caused the original event.

A patient can have normal imaging and remain disabled.

A patient can have an imaging abnormality unrelated to the event.

Once those possibilities are admitted, “objective versus subjective” stops being useful.

Symptoms are subjective by definition: they are experienced by a person. Signs and biomarkers can be objective measurements. Neither category guarantees causality.

The intelligence problem pushes in the opposite direction. Security investigations want discriminators. If a specific external energy exposure occurred, analysts want a marker that distinguishes it from migraine, panic, viral illness, or ordinary vestibular disease. A syndrome defined by common symptoms is a terrible forensic instrument.

That is why biomarkers became a recurring recommendation.

But a biomarker must be specific enough to matter.

A marker of nonspecific neuronal stress is not a transmitter fingerprint. Elevated inflammatory markers do not identify a country. Vestibular dysfunction does not tell you whether the trigger was physical, infectious, toxic, or functional. Even if a study found a consistent injury marker, attribution would still require a separate chain.

The dream biomarker would connect physics to biology.

Imagine a monitored exposure producing a characteristic, reproducible molecular or physiological signature with a dose-response curve. If future patients showed that signature shortly after an incident, investigators would have something much stronger than symptom similarity. If the signature decayed predictably, timing would matter. If different waveforms produced different signatures, mechanism could become discriminable.

We do not have that in the public record.

Instead, medicine is being asked to operate in a causally impoverished environment.

The humane response is not to wait for certainty.

It is to decouple care from attribution while improving the data needed for attribution.

That sounds administratively modest.

In this case it is almost radical.

People should not have to prove a foreign weapon hit them before receiving treatment for disabling symptoms.

Investigators should not have to accept a foreign-weapon theory merely because the patients deserve treatment.

Compassion and skepticism are not opposites.

They become opposites only when institutions make causation the price of care.
