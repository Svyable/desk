# The National Academies Bet

The most consequential public scientific judgment in the Havana case arrived in December 2020, four years after the first reports and after the story had accumulated enough politics to distort almost any neutral reading.

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine had been asked by the State Department to assess the illnesses reported by government employees and families at overseas embassies and to evaluate plausible mechanisms. The committee was not asked to identify an attacker. It was not given a recovered device. It did not claim to solve every reported case.

Its headline conclusion nevertheless changed the debate.

Among the mechanisms it considered, directed, pulsed radio-frequency energy appeared to be the most plausible explanation for the cases with distinctive acute, directional or location-specific early manifestations.

That sentence has since been quoted as if the National Academies proved a microwave attack.

The report said something narrower.

The committee tried to separate the acute phase from the chronic phase. Some personnel described an abrupt onset: pressure or sound, pain, vestibular disturbance, dizziness, and other symptoms associated with a particular place or direction. Chronic complaints were broader and less specific. The committee considered whether the persistent phase might include secondary mechanisms, including persistent postural-perceptual dizziness, a functional vestibular disorder that is real without requiring a continuing external exposure.

This was a sophisticated move.

It allowed one event to have more than one mechanism over time.

A person can experience an acute physical insult and then develop a chronic disorder maintained by altered sensory integration, avoidance, anxiety, or compensation. Conversely, a chronic symptom pattern can arise without the proposed acute insult. Treating all symptoms as though they must come from one continuing mechanism is medically crude.

The committee also kept heterogeneity visible. It explicitly warned that people with only chronic nonspecific symptoms might not share the same cause as those who reported a sudden distinctive onset. That caveat should have prevented “Havana syndrome” from hardening into a single etiological object.

It did not.

Why did the committee favor pulsed RF?

Part of the answer was phenomenological fit. The unusual combination of abrupt onset, location specificity, directionality, and sound or pressure seemed compatible with known effects of pulsed radio-frequency exposure. The microwave auditory literature offered a demonstrated route by which RF pulses could create sound-like perceptions without an ordinary acoustic source. Other reported symptoms had appeared in portions of the broader RF bioeffects literature, though with more uncertainty.

Part of the answer was comparative elimination. The committee considered chemical exposures, infectious disease, psychological and social factors, and other mechanisms. It did not find those alternatives sufficient to explain the distinctive acute pattern across the cases it emphasized. It did not rule out psychological contributions or secondary functional disorders. It did not claim chemicals or infection could explain no cases. It ranked mechanisms for a selected clinical pattern.

That is an important limitation.

“Most plausible among those considered” is not the same statement as “demonstrated.”

Plausibility is relative to the candidate set and the available information. If every candidate has serious gaps, the least-bad fit can win without being well established.

The committee itself faced severe data limitations. It did not have a perfect common dataset across all patients. People had been evaluated at different sites with different protocols. Some information arrived through presentations, records, and interviews rather than a prospective unified study. The early Cuba cases had already aged by the time the committee worked. Environmental measurements from the moment of exposure were unavailable.

The committee’s confidence therefore rested partly on coherence rather than direct capture.

Coherence can be scientifically valuable. A mechanism that explains several otherwise disconnected observations deserves attention. It becomes dangerous when coherence substitutes for a missing discriminating measurement.

A pulsed RF source should leave possible traces.

A transient field might be recorded if appropriate sensors are present.

A beam should obey geometry.

A proposed waveform should predict thresholds and spatial patterns.

A biological mechanism should predict acute markers or dose-response relationships.

Repeated operations should create intelligence residue: procurement, testing, operator training, platform movement, communications, or eventually defectors and documents.

The 2020 report did not supply that chain.

It was not tasked to.

This is why calling it an “attack report” is misleading. The committee assessed illness and mechanisms. Attribution belongs elsewhere.

The report also became a strange authority token in later arguments. Supporters of the RF hypothesis cited the National Academies name. Skeptics responded with engineers who disputed whether the classic microwave auditory mechanism could create injury at practical field doses. Those engineers were often treated as contradicting the report.

But the disagreement was never fully joined in public.

The National Academies conclusion referred broadly to directed pulsed RF energy. The engineering critiques often modeled specific thermoelastic auditory pathways and pressure levels. If the committee contemplated additional cellular or neural mechanisms, those needed explicit dose-response evidence. If it did not, the engineering challenge became more serious.

The public record left that seam exposed.

Then another expert body entered it.

In 2021, the intelligence community convened a panel with specialists in acoustics, medicine, psychiatry, RF engineering, biology, ionizing radiation, and environmental science. According to the declassified account, the panel received more than a thousand classified documents as well as briefings and medical records. In February 2022, ODNI and CIA released its conclusions.

The panel again found pulsed electromagnetic energy plausible.

This time, however, the government was also separately telling the public that a global adversary campaign was unlikely.

The mechanism and the actor began to diverge officially.

That divergence is one of the most important facts in the entire case.

A government can believe a mechanism is physically plausible while judging that the proposed adversary probably did not use it.

Once that is understood, the Havana debate stops being a referendum on belief and becomes a set of experiments that have not all returned the same answer.
