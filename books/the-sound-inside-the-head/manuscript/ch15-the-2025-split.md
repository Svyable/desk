# The 2025 Split

The January 2025 intelligence assessment did not reverse the government’s position.

It fractured it.

Most of the intelligence community continued to judge it very unlikely that a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported anomalous health incidents. Five components stayed with the basic 2023 conclusion. Two components, however, shifted. With low confidence, they judged there was roughly an even chance that a foreign actor had developed or deployed a novel weapon or prototype device capable of harming a small subset of U.S. personnel.

That is an extraordinary sentence if read slowly.

It is also much weaker than the headlines it can generate.

“Roughly even chance” is not confirmation.

“Low confidence” means the information is limited, questionable, or difficult to corroborate.

“A small subset” is not the worldwide syndrome.

“Developed or deployed” does not distinguish a laboratory capability from field use.

The two agencies did not publicly identify an attacker.

Still, the shift mattered because it showed that new reporting had changed at least some analysts’ view of foreign technical capability. The January 2025 assessment said exactly that: new information led the dissenting components to revise their judgments about whether a foreign actor possessed a capability that could cause biological effects consistent with some reported symptoms.

This was the first major public crack in the post-2023 consensus.

Then, in 2026, the story destabilized again.

Congressional committees began referring publicly to reports that the U.S. government had acquired a foreign-made device producing pulsed radio-frequency energy and was testing it. A January 2026 House Homeland Security Committee letter sought details about an alleged Homeland Security Investigations purchase costing more than ten million dollars. The committee’s public language was careful enough to matter: the reports concerned a device allegedly linked to anomalous health incidents, not a device proven to have caused them.

In February, *The Washington Post* reported an even stranger episode. According to four people familiar with the matter, a Norwegian government scientist who had been skeptical that pulsed-energy systems could produce serious neurological effects built a device based on classified information and tested it on himself in 2024. He reportedly developed neurological symptoms. U.S. officials later traveled to Norway to examine the result. The Post also reported that the separately acquired foreign-made device contained some Russian-origin components, while emphasizing that the U.S. government had not established who built it or that it caused any AHI.

This reporting belongs in evidence Class 2, not Class 1.

It is serious investigative journalism sourced to people familiar with classified events.

It is not a declassified technical report.

No public waveform, dosimetry, device architecture, exposure geometry, clinical record, or independent replication accompanies the story. The Norwegian researcher has not been publicly identified. The device reportedly tested in Norway was not the same device the United States purchased. The effects were reportedly similar to, but not identical with, classic AHI presentations.

The reporting is important because it potentially explains why two intelligence components shifted in 2025.

It does not close the causal chain.

Then politics entered the chain directly.

In June 2026, House Intelligence Committee Chairman Rick Crawford announced that Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard had recalled the 2023 and 2025 intelligence community assessments on anomalous health incidents. Crawford characterized those assessments in extremely strong terms, calling them flawed and manufactured and alleging failures of analytic integrity.

That statement is an official congressional statement about an executive-branch action.

Its allegations about why the assessments were flawed are political and investigative claims, not themselves proof that the original analysis was deliberately falsified.

As of this writing, a full declassified replacement assessment has not appeared in the public record available for this book.

That leaves the public evidence in an unusual state.

The 2023 and 2025 assessments still exist as historical documents showing what the intelligence community judged at those times.

The 2026 recall means they no longer function as uncontested current analytic products.

The recall does not automatically make their factual observations false.

Nor does it validate the directed-energy attribution hypothesis.

A recalled assessment can fail because sources were weighted badly, dissent was mishandled, tradecraft standards were violated, new evidence emerged, political leadership changed, or some combination of those things. Determining which requires the underlying analytic record.

This is where intelligence epistemology stops being an abstract chapter and becomes the subject itself.

An intelligence assessment is not raw evidence.

It is a structured judgment built from sources the public often cannot inspect.

The public therefore receives two products at once: a conclusion and an institutional promise that the analytic process behind it was sound.

When the process is later challenged, the conclusion loses authority faster than the underlying facts can be reassessed.

That creates an evidentiary vacuum.

Into the vacuum rush testimony, leaked fragments, political accusations, investigative reporting, classified-device stories, patient accounts, and old Soviet research. Some of those may be true. Some may be incomplete. Some may be irrelevant. The disappearance of trust does not sort them.

The 2026 moment is therefore more dangerous epistemically than either the 2023 dismissal or the 2022 plausibility finding.

A simple institutional anchor has been removed.

The right response is not to reverse the sign on every prior judgment.

It is to go back down the chain.

What is the current public evidence that a foreign state has built a relevant device?

What is the current public evidence that such a device can produce the acute core characteristics under realistic exposure conditions?

What is the current public evidence connecting a specific device, operator, or state to a specific AHI event?

What evidence was available to the dissenting intelligence components in 2025?

What, specifically, did the 2026 review find wrong in the earlier analytic process?

Until those questions are answered with inspectable material, certainty remains expensive.

The 2025 split did not prove an attack.

The 2026 recall did not prove a cover-up.

They did something almost as important.

They destroyed the idea that the official story had reached a stable endpoint.
