# What the Effect Actually Proves

Scientific facts are often weaker than the stories built from them.

That is not a defect in science. It is the cost of keeping the verbs honest.

The microwave auditory effect proves that electromagnetic energy, delivered in the right pulse structure, can produce an auditory sensation in a human being without an ordinary airborne sound entering the ear. It proves that the body can convert a radio-frequency pulse into a mechanical pressure transient. It proves that an experience can feel acoustic while the external stimulus is not conventional sound.

That is already remarkable.

It does not prove that a person reporting a strange noise was exposed to microwaves.

It does not prove that a person reporting dizziness after a noise was injured by microwaves.

It does not prove that a transmitter can deliver the necessary field through any wall from any distance.

It does not prove that a covert adversary did so.

These distinctions feel pedantic only if the desired conclusion has already been chosen.

A useful way to think about the subject is as a chain with independent failure points.

There is a source. There is propagation. There is coupling into the body. There is an immediate biological or sensory effect. There may be tissue injury. There may be persistent symptoms. There is a person’s memory and report of the event. There is an investigation. There is attribution.

Evidence can be strong at one link and weak at the next.

This seems obvious in ordinary engineering. A tire can fail catastrophically; that does not prove sabotage. A chemical can cause a particular poisoning syndrome; that does not prove it was administered. A radar can detect a target; that does not prove the target is hostile. Yet in controversial biomedical intelligence problems, mechanism and attribution are repeatedly fused because each makes the other narratively satisfying.

The fusion runs in both directions.

People convinced that no adversary campaign occurred sometimes write as though that settles the physics. It does not. An intelligence service can conclude that Russia probably did not conduct a global attack campaign while microwave hearing remains a textbook bioeffect. Likewise, a panel can conclude that pulsed RF is physically plausible as a causal mechanism for some reported symptoms without possessing evidence that Russia used it.

The 2022 Intelligence Community Experts Panel was unusually explicit about this. It was tasked to study causal mechanisms, not attribution. Its declassified summary said a subset of anomalous health incidents could not easily be explained by known environmental or medical conditions, and that pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly radio-frequency energy, plausibly explained the core characteristics. The same public release noted that a separate intelligence effort was investigating responsibility.

The division was methodologically correct.

Public discussion promptly recombined it.

The 2023 Intelligence Community Assessment then moved on the attribution question. Most agencies judged it very unlikely that a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported incidents. That assessment drew on intelligence collection, lack of a coherent adversary pattern, technical considerations, and medical evidence. It did not erase the expert panel’s narrower statement that certain energy mechanisms were plausible.

By January 2025, the public assessment remained mostly negative on foreign responsibility, but the consensus had developed a crack. Five intelligence components continued to judge foreign responsibility very unlikely. Two shifted to a low-confidence judgment that there was roughly an even chance a foreign actor had developed or deployed a novel weapon or prototype device in a small subset of cases. The language matters: low confidence; small subset; development or deployment; not a finding that the broad syndrome was an attack campaign.

A public debate trained on binaries had trouble with that sentence.

The problem is partly linguistic. “Havana syndrome” suggests a single disease. “Anomalous health incidents” suggests a single incident category. But the population of reports may contain multiple causes. Some may be ordinary medical events. Some may be environmental exposures. Some may be functional or stress-related phenomena. Some may be unknown. Even if an external device caused several priority cases, it would not follow that the same mechanism explains every later report gathered under the same administrative label.

Aggregation can manufacture a false contradiction.

Suppose ten cases share a mechanism and five hundred do not. A study of all 510 may find no common biomarker. An intelligence review may find no global attack campaign. Both results can be true while the ten remain unresolved. Conversely, if investigators isolate ten cases because they look most like a hypothesized mechanism, selection can inflate apparent coherence. The act of creating a “core” subset is scientifically useful and statistically dangerous.

That is why case definition becomes part of causality.

The 2022 expert panel used four core characteristics: acute onset of sound or pressure, often lateralized; near-simultaneous symptoms such as vertigo, imbalance, or ear pain; a strong sense of locality or directionality; and no known environmental or medical explanation. Those features are not a diagnosis. They are a filter designed to separate a more coherent phenomenology from the larger administrative pool.

A filter can improve signal.

It can also encode the hypothesis into the sample.

If directionality is considered a core feature because a directed source is suspected, then the selected cases will naturally look more compatible with a directed source. The right test is not whether the filtered cases resemble the filter. It is whether independent measurements—RF recordings, dosimetry, biomarkers, spatial gradients, physical damage, device recovery, adversary communications—converge on the same mechanism.

That convergence has not been publicly demonstrated.

This is the uncomfortable center of the book.

There is enough established physics to make categorical dismissal lazy.

There is not enough public event-specific evidence to make categorical attribution responsible.

The intellectually easy positions live at the edges. One says the government has already admitted energy weapons are real and therefore the attacks happened. The other says the intelligence community found no adversary campaign and therefore the victims were victims only of stress or suggestion.

Both are substitutions.

A person can have disabling symptoms without a visible MRI lesion.

A person can misattribute the cause of genuine symptoms.

A government can fail to detect an adversary operation.

A government can also spend years chasing a mechanism that never caused the events.

A scientific panel can identify physical plausibility and still be wrong about the field case.

An intelligence service can identify absence of adversary evidence and still miss a small compartmented program.

Those statements are not evasions. They are the actual shape of uncertainty.

The first responsibility of an anomalous-phenomena book is not to make the mystery bigger.

It is to keep each piece the size it really is.
