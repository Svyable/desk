# The Head as Transducer

The phrase “microwave hearing” gives the radio too much credit.

The head does most of the interesting work.

Imagine a pulse of radio-frequency energy short enough that it deposits energy faster than the tissue can thermally relax. The amount of heating can be tiny. The important fact is not that the head becomes hot. It is that a rapid, spatially uneven temperature change causes an equally rapid expansion. Expansion produces stress. Stress produces a pressure wave. The wave travels through tissue and bone. The cochlea responds.

The radio did not become sound in the air.

The body made the sound.

This mechanism, now usually described in terms of thermoelastic expansion, is satisfying because it replaces a seductive mystery with a chain of ordinary physical events. Each link can be measured. Absorption depends on frequency and tissue properties. Temperature change depends on deposited energy. Pressure amplitude depends on expansion coefficients, geometry, pulse duration, and acoustic propagation. Perception depends on whether the mechanical transient reaching the cochlea exceeds an auditory threshold.

By the mid-1970s, this account was no longer merely a story told after the fact. Arthur Guy, Chung-Kwang Chou, James Lin, Daniel Christensen and colleagues compared microwave-induced acoustic effects in animals and physical materials. In 1976 Lin explicitly compared possible transduction mechanisms, including radiation pressure, electrostriction-like forces, and thermal expansion. His calculation suggested that thermal expansion could dominate the alternatives. In 1977 he used thermoelastic models to calculate frequencies and thresholds. Chou and Guy’s 1979 guinea-pig work varied pulse duration and found threshold behavior in strong agreement with the thermal-expansion theory.

That matters because mechanism constrains fantasy.

If the head is a thermoacoustic transducer, then the effect has a bandwidth, a threshold, and a dose geometry. A transmitter does not get to choose arbitrary percepts simply because the signal is electromagnetic. The cochlea is still involved. The dimensions of the head matter. Pulse energy matters. Repetition rate matters. The beam has to put energy in the relevant tissue. The sensation is generated through a pressure transient, not by some unspecified electromagnetic command delivered directly to consciousness.

This does not make the phenomenon trivial.

It makes it engineering.

Engineering questions can be uncomfortable because they often produce answers between yes and no. A system can be physically possible and operationally awkward. It can work across a laboratory distance and fail as a covert field device. It can create perception at one exposure level and require unacceptable heating or conspicuous hardware to create injury. It can be directional in a technical sense without being surgically precise through real buildings. It can be measurable without being useful.

The public debate around anomalous health incidents repeatedly skips this middle.

One camp hears “microwave auditory effect” and concludes that a covert RF weapon is therefore straightforward. Another hears that high peak power may be required and concludes that the entire mechanism is impossible. Both positions save themselves from doing the system calculation.

The system calculation is the book.

Start with auditory threshold. Reviews of microwave hearing emphasize that perception depends strongly on energy in an individual pulse. Average power can remain modest when the duty cycle is low even though instantaneous peak power is high. That is why a low average number can mislead. But the reverse mistake is equally common: quoting peak power as though it were continuously deposited exaggerates heating and electrical demand. Pulse width and repetition rate tie the two together.

Then consider distance. Electromagnetic fields spread unless an antenna focuses them. A narrow beam requires an aperture sized for the wavelength and desired angular resolution. At microwave frequencies, a device can be directional, but “directional” is not the same as a pencil through any wall at any distance. Antenna gain, sidelobes, near-field behavior, reflections, polarization, obstructions, and building materials matter. A covert system would have to solve those problems without announcing itself through visible hardware, radio-frequency leakage, power supply, thermal management, or interference.

Then consider tissue absorption. The body is not transparent at all radio frequencies. Different frequencies penetrate differently. Energy deposition is spatially structured. A pulse that produces a useful thermoelastic wave must be absorbed somewhere. If the proposed target is the head, the beam geometry has to put sufficient energy into the head under whatever orientation and shielding the real encounter provides.

Then consider the desired effect.

A click is easy to define. Incapacitation is not.

If the target effect is an auditory sensation, we have human threshold data and a plausible mechanism. If the target effect is pain, disequilibrium, vestibular disruption, nausea, cognitive impairment, or long-lasting neurological symptoms, the evidentiary base changes. One cannot simply turn a knob labeled intensity and assume the same mechanism scales smoothly from click to injury.

At sufficiently high exposures, heating becomes an obvious hazard. But bulk thermal injury would have its own signatures. An operational concept that depends on avoiding burns or measurable warming while causing selective neurological effects needs evidence for those selective effects. This is where the debate over anomalous health incidents becomes genuinely difficult.

The 2021 paper by Kenneth Foster, David Garrett, and Marvin Ziskin asked directly whether the microwave auditory effect could be weaponized. Their answer was skeptical of the leap from perception to injurious weapon. They analyzed the pressure generated by thermoelastic expansion and argued that exposures required to produce damaging acoustic pressures would raise other problems. Their paper did not deny the auditory effect. It denied that the existence of the auditory effect by itself solves the weapon problem.

That is exactly the distinction a serious account needs.

The 2020 National Academies committee and the 2022 Intelligence Community Experts Panel looked at a broader problem. They were not restricted to the classic auditory mechanism at threshold. The 2022 panel concluded that pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly in the radio-frequency range, could plausibly explain the core characteristics of a subset of anomalous health incidents, while emphasizing information gaps. The panel reported that sources capable of generating the required stimulus could in principle be concealable and have moderate power requirements. Much of its technical discussion remained classified or redacted.

Those claims cannot simply be merged with Foster’s analysis because they may not be talking about the same pulse regime, endpoint, or mechanism.

This is the first place secrecy starts doing epistemic damage.

When a public skeptic calculates one specific mechanism and says the weapon extrapolation looks implausible, and a classified expert panel says multiple pulsed-electromagnetic pathways remain plausible, the public cannot inspect whether they actually disagree. They may be using different assumptions. They may be modeling different devices. They may be considering data unavailable to one another. Or one may simply be wrong.

Classification removes the shared worksheet.

The result is argument by institutional reputation.

People who trust the intelligence panel cite its access to classified reporting. People who distrust it cite the absence of public engineering. People who trust independent academics cite equations. People who distrust them say the equations ignore classified capabilities. Neither side can force the other through the missing calculation.

That does not make all positions equal.

A classified assertion is not evidence available for independent scientific reproduction. A public calculation may also fail because its model excludes the relevant mechanism. The correct response is to label the evidentiary states rather than pretending the disagreement has been resolved.

Verified: pulsed RF can produce auditory perception through a thermoelastic mechanism under studied conditions.

Credible but incomplete: other pulsed electromagnetic exposure regimes may create acute sensory or neurological effects beyond ordinary microwave hearing; expert panels have judged this plausible, but public mechanistic data are limited.

Disputed: a practical covert device can cause the full pattern of reported anomalous health incidents at operational distances while remaining undetected.

Speculative: any particular reported incident was caused by such a device.

Those are not four degrees of belief in the same sentence. They are four different claims.

The head as transducer gives us a rare luxury in a controversial subject: something solid enough to build from.

The mistake would be using the foundation as proof that the entire building already exists.
