# How to Test an Invisible Weapon

A good experiment does not ask whether Havana syndrome is real.

It asks for a result that one mechanism predicts and another does not.

The phrase “invisible weapon” creates a bad instinct. Because the suspected stimulus cannot be seen, investigators behave as though direct measurement is impossible. Radio-frequency energy is invisible in the same way Wi-Fi is invisible. Instruments see it perfectly well if they are looking in the right band with the right time resolution and dynamic range.

The first requirement is therefore not another retrospective interview.

It is instrumentation.

A serious prospective AHI protocol would treat diplomatic residences, embassies, and other high-risk sites as environmental laboratories without turning them into unlivable sensor farms. The system would need wideband RF monitoring capable of capturing short pulses rather than only average field strength. It would need synchronized acoustic and ultrasonic recording. It would need power-line monitoring, environmental sensors, and precise timekeeping. It would need enough spatially separated detectors to estimate direction and field gradients.

Most importantly, the raw data would need a preservation chain.

A report made at 2:17 a.m. should be linkable to the sensor record at 2:17 a.m. without a technician later deciding which segment looks interesting.

That sounds straightforward.

It is not.

Wideband monitoring inside a secure facility can capture sensitive emissions. Sensor systems themselves can become surveillance vulnerabilities. Data retention creates intelligence risks. False alarms can overwhelm analysts. A detector optimized for one hypothesized waveform can miss another. A foreign service that knows the detector design can adapt.

Those are engineering problems, not excuses.

The second requirement is a preregistered case definition.

The government’s 2022 expert panel used core characteristics including abrupt onset, lateralized sound or pressure, simultaneous vestibular or ear symptoms, locality or directionality, and lack of a known alternative explanation. A new protocol could refine those criteria prospectively.

The point is not to exclude people from care.

The point is to separate surveillance sensitivity from research specificity.

Every reported event can receive medical attention. Only events meeting prespecified criteria enter the primary mechanism analysis. That prevents the case definition from drifting after researchers see the data.

The third requirement is immediate biology.

If investigators believe a brief pulsed-energy exposure can cause acute neurological effects, waiting weeks for blood and imaging is a design failure. Samples should be collected as soon as safely possible and then on a defined schedule: hours, one day, several days, weeks, months.

The panel should decide in advance what it expects to change.

Neurofilament light?

GFAP?

Inflammatory markers?

Vestibular-evoked potentials?

Auditory brainstem responses?

Oculomotor measures?

Autonomic markers?

Metabolomics?

None of those is automatically specific to RF exposure. That is why controlled exposure research matters.

The fourth requirement is a lawful exposure platform.

This is where the reported 2026 device developments could become scientifically decisive. If the United States possesses hardware or waveforms considered relevant, independent cleared laboratories should characterize them. Human experimentation must remain within ethical safety bounds. Animal models, tissue phantoms, organoids, ex vivo preparations, and noninjurious human threshold work can still map a great deal of the mechanism.

The protocol should not begin by trying to recreate injury.

It should begin by establishing the exposure physics.

Can the waveform produce the reported auditory or pressure sensation?

At what field strength?

What internal pressure transient results?

Does orientation matter?

Does common building material attenuate or reshape it?

Does the waveform produce measurable vestibular responses below thermal safety limits?

Do repeated exposures change the response?

What instruments detect it reliably?

A field detector can only be designed once the target signal is known.

The fifth requirement is blinding.

This subject desperately needs experiments in which neither participant nor assessor knows whether an exposure occurred during a given trial. Expectation is not an insult. It is a variable. If a subject can reliably identify active versus sham RF exposure under blinded conditions, that is powerful. If symptoms occur equally during sham, that is powerful too.

The sixth requirement is independent replication.

No single classified laboratory should own the effect.

At least two or three teams should reproduce key results using separately calibrated equipment. If classification prevents publication of exact waveforms, the teams can still preregister outcome measures and publish blinded biological data. An independent statistical center can analyze coded results.

The seventh requirement is adversarial design.

The best skeptics should help design the experiment.

So should the best proponents.

Foster’s engineering objections should be translated into predicted limits. Experts who believe nontraditional pulse regimes can produce stronger effects should specify those regimes and the expected endpoints. The experiment should be built where those predictions diverge.

The eighth requirement is an explicit stopping rule.

A hypothesis that cannot lose is not a research program.

Suppose three independent laboratories characterize every device or waveform considered relevant, find no vestibular or neurological effect at safe nonthermal exposures beyond the known auditory phenomenon, and determine that injurious effects require conspicuous thermal or acoustic-equivalent doses incompatible with reported events. Belief in a covert nonthermal RF injury mechanism should fall substantially.

Suppose instead a blinded experiment finds reproducible acute vestibular disruption and a transient biomarker signature at exposures that can be delivered through ordinary building materials by compact hardware. Belief should rise substantially.

Then the field investigation becomes easier because the laboratory result predicts what sensors and biomarkers should find during a real event.

The final requirement is to keep attribution out of the laboratory.

A waveform does not have a nationality.

Even if a foreign-made device produces a relevant biological effect, identifying who used it in a particular case remains a separate intelligence task. Engineers should not be asked to infer an operator from a pulse. Intelligence analysts should not be allowed to turn technical plausibility into attribution.

The point of the experiment is smaller.

Can an invisible stimulus leave a visible causal chain?

If the answer is yes, the mystery starts to become ordinary.

That should be the goal.
