# Front Matter

A sound can exist without entering through the ear.

That sentence sounds mystical until it is made mechanical.

In 1962, Allan H. Frey published a short paper in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* describing human perception of pulsed radio-frequency energy as clicks, buzzes, or chirps. The power involved was low in average terms. The effect was directional. The sensation seemed to arise in or near the head. Later experiments, animal work, modeling, and physical measurements converged on a prosaic mechanism: a brief microwave pulse deposits a tiny amount of heat; tissue expands thermoelastically; the expansion launches an acoustic pressure wave; the cochlea receives it through bone conduction. The skull is not a radio receiver in the science-fiction sense. It is a transducer.

That part is real.

Then comes the trouble.

Beginning in Havana in late 2016, American diplomats, intelligence officers, and family members reported abrupt episodes involving sound or pressure, ear pain, vertigo, disequilibrium, headache, cognitive complaints, and other symptoms. The incidents multiplied across countries. The name “Havana syndrome” stuck even as the government shifted to the colder term “anomalous health incidents.” The causal debate became a thicket of medicine, acoustics, radiofrequency physics, counterintelligence, classified collection, institutional failure, political pressure, and injured people who had good reason to resent being turned into abstractions.

The existence of microwave hearing soon became evidence for a microwave weapon. The plausibility of a microwave weapon became evidence for Russian responsibility. The absence of intelligence linking Russia to most incidents became evidence that radio-frequency injury was impossible. A normal MRI became evidence that nothing happened. An unusual clinical presentation became evidence that something exotic must have happened. One question kept being used to answer another.

This book refuses that trade.

It separates three propositions that have to be tested independently.

First: can pulsed radio-frequency energy create an auditory sensation in humans? Yes. That is one of the better-established nonthermal or microthermal bioeffects in the old microwave literature, with a mechanism that is not especially mysterious.

Second: can some pulsed electromagnetic exposure produce the acute and persistent constellation reported in at least a subset of anomalous health incidents? The public evidence is incomplete. Scientific panels have judged some external-energy mechanisms plausible; other investigators have emphasized dose, engineering, and pathology problems. Plausibility is not demonstration.

Third: did a foreign actor deploy such a device against U.S. personnel? That is an intelligence attribution question, not a bioeffects question. As of the public January 2025 intelligence assessment, most U.S. intelligence agencies still judged foreign responsibility for the reported incidents very unlikely, while two components shifted to a low-confidence judgment that there was roughly an even chance a foreign actor had developed or deployed a novel capability in a small subset of cases. That split matters. It is not a verdict in either direction.

The categories in the research notes are intentionally blunt. Verified or declassified facts are one class. Credible but incomplete reporting or testimony is another. Disputed allegations are a third. Speculative synthesis is a fourth. The manuscript does not graduate a claim merely because it has been repeated for years.

The central mystery here is not “what really happened in Havana?” That question is too large for the public record and too easy to answer by temperament.

The more useful mystery is how a real physical effect becomes an evidentiary shortcut.

A strange sound inside the head is not a weapon.

A weapon is not an attack.

An attack is not attribution.

Once those sentences are kept apart, the case becomes harder, and much more interesting.
