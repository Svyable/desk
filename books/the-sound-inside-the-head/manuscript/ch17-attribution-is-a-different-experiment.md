# Attribution Is a Different Experiment

Mechanism asks what can happen.

Attribution asks who did what, where, when, and why.

Those questions are often confused because a mechanism can make an accusation feel technically respectable. Once a device is imaginable, people begin assigning it to the adversary most associated with that technology. Russia has occupied that role throughout the Havana debate because of Soviet-era bioelectromagnetics research, modern Russian security operations, reports of Russian intelligence presence near some affected personnel, and investigative journalism alleging links between GRU personnel and particular events.

Those are leads.

Attribution requires more.

The public allegation sharpened in 2024 when a joint investigation by *The Insider*, *60 Minutes*, and *Der Spiegel* argued that members of a Russian military intelligence unit had temporal or geographic connections to some reported AHI cases and that Russian programs had explored acoustic or directed-energy technologies. Members of Congress cited the reporting in demanding renewed investigation.

The reporting should be taken seriously.

It should also be kept in its evidence class.

Journalists can uncover facts intelligence agencies miss or decline to publish. Location records, travel documents, phone metadata, leaked personnel files, awards, procurement records, and source testimony can create a strong circumstantial case. But proximity is not deployment. A Russian officer being in the same city as a U.S. official does not identify a device. A unit’s interest in a technology does not prove it used that technology. A classified program name does not establish performance.

The strongest attribution would join independent lanes.

Technical lane: a measured exposure or recovered device.

Operational lane: an identified unit, operator, tasking chain, or surveillance pattern.

Biological lane: effects matching the device under controlled exposure.

Intelligence lane: communications, documents, human sources, procurement, or doctrine connecting the capability to the operation.

Temporal lane: presence and activity at the time and place of the event.

Motive lane: a reason to target the person, if motive is necessary to the hypothesis.

No single lane should be asked to impersonate the others.

This is where some discussions of Unit 29155 become too convenient. The unit has been associated publicly with sabotage, assassination plots, and clandestine operations in Europe. That history makes it a plausible investigative target. It also creates confirmation risk. Once a unit has a reputation for covert violence, unexplained harmful events near its members become easier to attribute to it.

Reputation should raise attention.

It should not lower proof.

The 2023 U.S. intelligence assessment said collection did not support a foreign campaign. The 2025 update preserved that majority judgment while two agencies shifted toward greater concern about foreign technical capability in a small subset. The 2026 political and analytic reassessment has now undermined confidence in treating those products as the final official word.

None of those developments supplies public event-level attribution.

The distinction between capability and use is especially important in intelligence history.

States develop weapons they never deploy.

They deploy surveillance systems that have side effects.

They imitate adversary systems for testing.

They circulate technical concepts that fail.

They exaggerate their capabilities for deterrence.

They hide successful capabilities.

They also plant false stories about capabilities they do not possess.

A technically plausible effect therefore increases the number of intelligence hypotheses rather than reducing them.

Consider four possibilities.

One: a foreign service deliberately used a pulsed-energy system to injure U.S. personnel.

Two: a foreign service used an electromagnetic surveillance or collection system whose emissions caused unintended effects.

Three: a foreign service possessed relevant technology but had nothing to do with the reported incidents.

Four: no relevant foreign system caused the incidents, and the apparent technical connections are retrospective pattern matching.

All four are compatible with the existence of microwave hearing.

Only the first two explain events through external RF exposure.

The second possibility deserves more attention because intent shapes expected evidence. A weapon program should have doctrine around target effects. A surveillance system might be optimized for electronics or sensing and only incidentally expose people. Repeated symptoms near sensitive facilities could arise from a collection architecture rather than an assassination architecture.

This is speculative synthesis, not an assertion about Havana.

Its value is methodological: it prevents investigators from assuming the purpose of a source from the harm attributed to it.

Attribution also has a political clock.

Governments cannot wait indefinitely to decide how to protect personnel. If analysts assess a plausible threat, defensive measures may be warranted before proof. Embassy shielding, detectors, travel guidance, medical protocols, and counterintelligence collection can all be justified under uncertainty.

But protective action creates its own feedback.

Training employees to recognize AHI symptoms increases reporting.

Deploying detectors can create false positives if thresholds are poorly chosen.

Naming a suspected adversary can bias future investigations.

Compartmenting threat intelligence can prevent physicians from knowing what to test for.

The response changes the data-generating environment.

That is why attribution should be treated as a separate experiment with explicit hypotheses.

What observation is predicted if Russia is responsible?

What observation is predicted if a different state is responsible?

What observation is predicted if no foreign actor is involved?

Where do those predictions differ?

If all hypotheses predict “some cases remain unexplained,” unexplained cases cannot discriminate among them.

The greatest danger in an ambiguous national-security problem is letting mystery itself become evidence for the most dramatic actor.

Unknown is not a country.
