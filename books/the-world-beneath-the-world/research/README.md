# Research Brief — The World Beneath the World

## Purpose

This book explains ordinary sensations by tracing them into the hidden systems that produce them. The goal is not to make every everyday object mysterious. It is to show how much coordination, physics, biology, standardization, maintenance, and accumulated engineering can be compressed into an experience that feels simple.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. Original scientific papers and systematic reviews.
2. Government agencies and national laboratories.
3. Standards bodies and metrology institutes.
4. Utility, manufacturer, engineering, or operator documentation for system-specific claims.
5. High-quality secondary reporting for context, never as the sole support for a technical claim when the original is available.

For historical claims, seek contemporaneous records, archival material, patent documents, standards, company reports, or reputable scholarship that cites them.

## Claim rules

- Record units and definitions exactly.
- Separate a standard value from a typical real-world value.
- Avoid turning a U.S.-specific standard into a universal rule.
- Distinguish correlation from mechanism.
- Do not infer health effects beyond what authoritative evidence supports.
- Avoid implying that a detectable smell means danger; sensory thresholds and toxicological thresholds can differ dramatically.
- Distinguish a system’s technical function from the social or economic institutions that govern it.
- When numbers vary substantially by geography, climate, building type, technology, or time, show the range or name the boundary conditions.

## Recurring sensory questions

For each chapter, ask:

- What does the reader already see, hear, feel, taste, or smell without knowing what it signifies?
- What invisible variable is changing underneath that sensation?
- What instrument would measure it better than a human body?
- What infrastructure controls or responds to it?
- Who maintains the system?
- Which standard or convention makes different components compatible?
- What failure makes the hidden system suddenly obvious?
- What part of the simple story is misleading or incomplete?

## Chapter 1 source trail — Air Is Not Empty

Primary and high-authority sources used in the first draft:

- NOAA JetStream, “Air Pressure.” Standard sea-level pressure is 1013.25 hPa; pressure changes with air density and weather systems. https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/air-pressure
- NOAA National Data Buoy Center, “What is air pressure?” Describes the atmosphere as a weighted column of gases; identifies nitrogen and oxygen as the dominant constituents and gives typical sea-level pressure. https://www.ndbc.noaa.gov/education/pressure.shtml
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “Particulate Matter (PM) Basics.” Defines PM10 and PM2.5, describes particle sources and atmospheric formation, and notes that fine particles are much smaller than a human hair. https://www.epa.gov/pm-pollution/particulate-matter-pm-basics
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “Sources of Indoor Particulate Matter (PM).” Notes that indoor air contains outdoor particles plus particles generated by cooking, combustion, dust, biological material, consumer activities, and indoor chemistry. https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/sources-indoor-particulate-matter-pm
- NIST, “Gravimetric Hygrometer.” Explains humidity in terms of water mole fraction and the dependence of derived quantities such as relative humidity and dew point on pressure and temperature. https://www.nist.gov/laboratories/tools-instruments/gravimetric-hygrometer
- Jiang et al., “Biosynthesis of the earthy odorant geosmin by a bifunctional Streptomyces coelicolor enzyme.” Establishes geosmin production by Streptomyces and notes exceptionally low human detection thresholds. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3013058/
- Becher et al., Nature Microbiology (2020), “Developmentally regulated volatiles geosmin and 2-methylisoborneol attract a soil arthropod to Streptomyces bacteria promoting spore dispersal.” Demonstrates an ecological signaling role for the compounds that humans recognize as earthy odors. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41564-020-0697-x

## Falsification note for Chapter 1

The chapter’s useful claim is not that the unaided human senses provide a reliable air-quality monitor. They do not. Odorless hazards exist, harmless compounds can be perceptible at very low concentrations, humidity perception is confounded by temperature and airflow, and particle concentrations require instruments for meaningful quantification. The claim is narrower: ordinary sensations can be clues that direct attention toward real atmospheric variables and processes, provided those clues are checked against measurement rather than treated as measurement themselves.

- **v0.1:** The book will distinguish *sensation as clue* from *sensation as measurement* throughout. This distinction is foundational and should not be weakened later.
