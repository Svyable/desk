# Back Matter

## A Note on Evidence

This book begins with perception but does not treat perception as proof.

The detailed source trail for technical claims is maintained with the working manuscript in `research/README.md`. The hierarchy favors original scientific papers, government agencies and national laboratories, standards bodies, and first-party technical references. Sources are kept outside the narrative so the chapters can remain readable while the claims remain auditable.

Several principles guided the research.

A standard value is not automatically a typical real-world value. A U.S. regulation is not a universal rule. A smell threshold is not a toxicological threshold. A detectable change is not necessarily a dangerous one. A household example should not be silently scaled into a global claim. A clean-looking system can contain invisible risk, and an unpleasant-looking or -smelling system can be operating within its intended range.

Where facts vary by climate, building type, geography, technology, operating condition, or time, the manuscript tries to name the boundary rather than hide it.

## A Small Glossary of Hidden Systems

**Activated sludge** — A biological wastewater-treatment process in which microorganisms suspended in aerated wastewater consume biodegradable material and are later separated from treated water, with some biomass commonly returned to the process.

**Air pressure** — Force per unit area exerted by the atmosphere. Standard sea-level pressure is 1013.25 hPa, but real pressure varies with altitude and weather.

**Alternating current (AC)** — Electrical current whose direction changes periodically. Much of the North American grid operates at a nominal 60 Hz.

**Barcode** — A machine-readable symbol encoding identifiers or other structured data according to defined standards. In ordinary retail use, the code typically identifies the item; the price is looked up elsewhere.

**Biofilm** — A community of microorganisms attached to a surface and embedded in material they produce. Biofilms can be beneficial, neutral, or problematic depending on context.

**Cold chain** — A sequence of temperature-controlled storage and transportation conditions intended to keep products within required temperature ranges from production through use.

**Corrosion control** — Water-chemistry and treatment practices used to reduce the tendency of drinking water to release metals from plumbing and distribution materials.

**Dew point** — The temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure and water-vapor content, for saturation and condensation to begin under the relevant conditions.

**Frequency** — Number of repeating cycles per unit time, measured in hertz (cycles per second). Frequency can describe sound, electrical waveforms, mechanical vibration, and other periodic phenomena.

**Gradient** — A change in a quantity across space. Differences in pressure, temperature, concentration, electric potential, or elevation can drive flows of matter or energy.

**Hardness** — A water-quality property associated mainly with dissolved calcium and magnesium. Hardness affects scaling and soap behavior but is not by itself a health hazard.

**Harmonic** — A frequency component at an integer multiple of a fundamental frequency. In a 60 Hz electrical system, the third harmonic is 180 Hz.

**Heat pump** — A device that uses work to move thermal energy from a cooler region to a warmer one. Refrigerators and air conditioners are heat-pump systems arranged for different purposes.

**Intermodal container** — A standardized freight container designed for transfer among transport modes such as ship, rail, and truck without unloading the cargo at every transfer.

**Leachate** — Liquid that has moved through waste and picked up dissolved or suspended material, requiring collection and management in engineered landfill systems.

**Particulate matter (PM)** — Solid particles and liquid droplets suspended in air. Regulatory and monitoring frameworks commonly distinguish size categories such as PM10 and PM2.5.

**Relative humidity** — The amount of water vapor in air relative to the saturation condition at the same temperature and pressure, usually expressed as a percentage. It changes when temperature changes even if actual water-vapor content does not.

**Rolling resistance** — Energy dissipated as a tire or other rolling body deforms and recovers while moving. In tires, it depends on design, load, inflation, speed, temperature, and surface conditions.

**Standard** — An agreed specification, method, terminology, interface, dimension, or other rule intended to make measurement, safety, quality, or interoperability more consistent.

**Storm sewer** — A drainage system for stormwater. In many separated systems, stormwater is distinct from sanitary sewage and may be discharged to receiving waters without passing through a conventional wastewater-treatment plant.

**Tribology** — The science and engineering of friction, wear, lubrication, and interacting surfaces in relative motion.

**UTC (Coordinated Universal Time)** — The international reference time scale used as the basis for civil timekeeping around the world, realized through comparisons of precision clocks and international coordination.

## Twelve Field Exercises

These exercises are designed for ordinary public or household environments. Do not open energized equipment, enter restricted areas, taste non-food substances, approach hazardous machinery, or use sensory impressions to make health or safety judgments that require instruments or professional testing.

1. **Find the air.** Sit in a familiar room for one minute with your eyes closed. Identify airflow, background hums, odors, temperature differences, and pressure-related movement at doors. Then turn off one safe appliance and notice what disappears.

2. **Map a thermal border.** Walk slowly from sun to shade, indoors to outdoors, or carpet to tile. Separate what changes instantly from what changes slowly. Ask whether you are sensing air temperature, radiant heat, conduction, or moving air.

3. **Borrow the sky’s clock.** Compare the time on several devices. Put one offline for a while, then reconnect it and watch whether it corrects. Notice how little effort you normally spend setting clocks.

4. **Listen to electricity without touching it.** From safe public distance, identify transformer hum, motor whine, fan noise, chargers, elevators, or HVAC equipment. Treat every sound as a clue, not a diagnosis.

5. **Read wear.** Find a polished stair tread, worn handle, tire track, scuffed threshold, or grooved floor. Ask what repeated movement created the pattern and where friction is being encouraged or reduced.

6. **Read a glass of water.** Compare tap water cold and at room temperature. Look for kettle scale or faucet deposits. Check your utility’s published water source and water-quality information rather than trying to infer safety by taste.

7. **Find microbial control.** In a kitchen, notice refrigeration, drying, salt, acidity, sealed packaging, cleaning, and heat as environmental controls. Observe fermentation only in foods intended for it.

8. **Hunt standards.** Find five markings on ordinary objects: electrical ratings, tire information, a barcode, a certification mark, a container code, a pipe label, a screw designation, or a paper size. Ask which interactions depend on each agreement.

9. **Look for the night shift.** Identify evidence of work performed before you arrived: inspection tags, full shelves, clean floors, fresh trash liners, maintenance stickers, repaired surfaces, stocked supplies, or delivery staging.

10. **Follow rain.** During safe weather, watch where water flows from a roof or street. Find a curb inlet, bioswale, ditch, pond, or other drainage feature. Determine whether your local system separates stormwater from sanitary sewage.

11. **Follow heat outward.** Find the warm side of a refrigerator, air-conditioning condenser, dryer exhaust, computer, or charger casing. Ask where heat is being rejected and what supplied the energy that moved it.

12. **Read one room as a system.** Return to where you began. Identify at least one flow of air, water, heat, electricity, information, labor, material, time, microbes, and waste. Do not force an answer. The missing category is often the most interesting one to investigate.

## Closing Note

The world beneath the world is not underground.

It is the set of relationships that ordinary interfaces make easy to forget.

Once seen, they do not need to remain constantly in attention. That would defeat the point of good infrastructure. The useful change is knowing that the simple surface is an achievement rather than an explanation.

A switch is not electricity.

A thermostat is not temperature.

A clock is not time.

A drain is not disappearance.

A barcode is not a product.

A clean floor is not self-cleaning.

A quiet bearing is not frictionless.

A clear glass is not chemically empty.

A room is not empty air.

The interfaces are small because the systems behind them are large enough to make them small.
