# Chapter 11 — Where Everything Goes

Throw something away and pay attention to the grammar.

*A way.*

The phrase contains a direction while refusing to name the destination.

The wrapper leaves your hand. The toilet flushes. The sink drains. Rain disappears into a curb inlet. Warm air leaves a condenser. Dryer exhaust passes through a wall. A restaurant dumpster is emptied before breakfast. The disappearance is so complete at the point of use that it becomes easy to imagine removal as annihilation.

Nothing goes away.

It goes somewhere else, becomes something else, or is dispersed enough that you stop perceiving it.

A storm drain is one of the most misleading holes in a city because it looks like a cousin of every other drain.

In many communities with separate sewer systems, the difference is fundamental. Sanitary sewers carry wastewater from homes and businesses toward treatment plants. Municipal separate storm sewer systems collect runoff from streets, parking lots, and other surfaces and often discharge it to local water bodies without passing through a conventional wastewater treatment plant. EPA emphasizes this distinction because pollutants washed into storm drains can travel directly into streams, rivers, lakes, or coastal waters.

The curb inlet is therefore not a trash portal.

It is part of the watershed.

Rain makes the connection visible. Water hits roofs, leaves, cars, pavement, soil, construction sites, and parking lots. It picks up sediment, oil residues, tire and brake particles, litter, nutrients, metals, organic material, and whatever else happens to be on the surface. Some water infiltrates into soil. Some evaporates. Some is captured or stored. Some enters storm drains and is conveyed toward an outfall.

A dry street and a wet river can be the same system at different times.

Combined sewer systems complicate the picture further. In some older cities, stormwater and sanitary wastewater share pipes. In dry weather the flow goes to treatment. During sufficiently heavy rain, the combined volume can exceed the capacity of parts of the system, creating combined sewer overflows that discharge mixed stormwater and sewage to receiving waters under conditions governed by permits and control programs.

A rainstorm can therefore become a wastewater event because urban surfaces, pipe history, and treatment capacity meet at the same hour.

Drainage is geography accelerated by infrastructure.

The water always had somewhere to go. Cities decide how quickly, through which path, with how much storage and treatment, and with what consequences downstream.

Now follow the other drain.

Flush a toilet and the bowl refills clean enough that the event feels finished. In a sewered city, the wastewater joins flows from showers, sinks, dishwashers, washing machines, businesses, and sometimes industries. Gravity carries much of it downhill. Pump stations lift it where topography refuses to cooperate. Screens remove large debris. Grit systems remove dense inorganic material such as sand. Settling removes some suspended solids.

Then biology takes over much of the work.

In an activated-sludge plant, the microbial city from Chapter 8 is deliberately supplied with wastewater and oxygen. Organisms consume biodegradable material. Air blowers may be among the largest and loudest pieces of equipment on the site because supplying oxygen to a large biological process requires energy. Clarifiers separate biological solids from treated water. Some solids are returned to maintain the process. Excess solids move into their own treatment and disposal or beneficial-use pathways.

The toilet was connected to an ecosystem operated by electricians and mechanics.

That sentence is strange only because plumbing hides distance.

Wastewater treatment does not turn everything into nothing. It separates, transforms, concentrates, disinfects where required, and transfers material among water, gases, biomass, solids, and receiving environments.

Treatment is organized transformation.

The same is true of a landfill.

A garbage truck compacts waste because volume is expensive. At a transfer station, material may be consolidated into larger vehicles. At a landfill, waste is placed in an engineered site with systems intended to manage water, gas, cover, stability, and environmental impacts. Modern regulated landfills are not simply holes where trash is dumped, though design and performance vary across place and time.

Buried organic waste continues changing.

In oxygen-poor conditions, microorganisms break down degradable material and generate landfill gas containing methane and carbon dioxide among other compounds. Landfills may collect gas for flaring or energy use. Water moving through waste can create leachate that must be collected and managed in lined facilities.

The bag you tied shut in the kitchen remains part of a chemical system after the truck leaves.

Disposal changes who has to think about it.

Recycling changes the path, not the need for a path.

A bottle placed in a recycling bin still requires collection, sorting, contamination control, processing, transport, markets, and a manufacturer willing and technically able to use the recovered material. Different materials behave differently. Metals can have strong recycling pathways because material value and reprocessing technologies align. Paper fibers degrade with repeated use. Plastics span many polymer types, additives, colors, shapes, and markets that complicate recovery. Glass is heavy and local economics matter.

The recycling symbol is not a teleportation device.

It is an invitation into another supply chain.

This is why a material can be technically recyclable and still not be accepted by a local program, or accepted and still fail to become a new product if contamination, sorting, economics, or market conditions intervene. EPA’s national waste statistics themselves are limited to particular categories and reporting years. “How much is recycled?” sounds like a simple question until definitions, geography, material type, collection method, and destination are specified.

Waste statistics are standards problems wearing environmental clothing.

Food scraps reveal another route.

Put them in a landfill and they become part of an anaerobic waste mass. Put them in a well-managed composting system and aerobic microorganisms transform them into a stabilized soil amendment under controlled conditions of moisture, oxygen, temperature, and feedstock balance. Send food waste through a wastewater grinder and it increases the organic load moving toward sewers and treatment. Feed suitable scraps to animals where regulations and practices allow and the material enters a biological system by another route.

The same banana peel has several possible futures.

“Away” is a policy choice.

Electronic waste makes the point sharper because an object that looks small can contain materials from a global supply chain. A discarded phone includes metals, glass, polymers, a battery, circuit boards, and components produced through mining, refining, chemical processing, semiconductor manufacturing, assembly, shipping, and energy use. Throwing the device in a drawer hides the waste at home. Exporting it can move environmental and labor burdens to another region. Specialized recycling can recover valuable materials but requires disassembly, separation, processing, and safe handling of hazardous components.

Disposal has geography and power.

Communities hosting landfills, incinerators, transfer stations, wastewater plants, recycling facilities, ports, and industrial waste sites experience the systems that other people experience mainly as removal. The convenience of disappearance is unevenly distributed.

This is not unique to material waste.

Heat has a disposal system too.

The refrigerator from Chapter 3 rejects heat into the kitchen. An air conditioner rejects indoor heat outdoors. A power plant rejects waste heat through cooling systems. A data center carries heat away from processors. A car radiator moves engine heat into passing air. Industrial furnaces exhaust hot gases. Electrical resistance turns some energy into heat almost everywhere current flows.

We rarely call this waste because heat is invisible.

But every energy conversion has consequences somewhere.

A city on a hot night is partly a landscape of rejected heat: buildings leaking thermal energy, air conditioners warming outdoor air, vehicles cooling engines, pavement releasing stored solar energy, industrial processes exhausting heat, people metabolizing food. The atmosphere becomes the receiving environment for many separate systems.

Waste heat is “away” in another form.

So is sound.

An HVAC fan moves air for one person and creates acoustic pollution for another. A highway transports freight and broadcasts tire noise into adjacent neighborhoods. A backup generator protects a hospital while making a loud mechanical environment. An airport connects cities while distributing noise around flight paths.

Systems export side effects across boundaries.

The boundary is often the whole trick.

A factory gate defines which costs appear inside an operating budget. A property line defines responsibility for a pipe. A national border can separate consumption from manufacturing emissions. A sewer bill separates household behavior from treatment operations. A garbage fee compresses hauling, landfill space, labor, equipment, regulation, and long-term management into a monthly line item.

The world beneath the world is full of accounting borders that do not stop matter or energy.

Water crosses them. Air crosses them. noise crosses them. pollutants cross them. heat crosses them. microbes cross them.

This is why good infrastructure planning repeatedly runs into the same question: where does the consequence go?

A green stormwater system tries to keep more rain near where it falls through infiltration, vegetation, storage, or reuse. A heat-recovery system captures energy that would otherwise be exhausted. Industrial symbiosis tries to turn one process’s by-product into another process’s input. Wastewater reuse treats water for another use instead of discharging it after one pass. Anaerobic digestion can recover energy from organic material.

These systems do not abolish waste.

They redraw the path.

Sometimes the redraw creates a new problem. Concentrating a contaminant creates a residual that still needs management. Recycling consumes energy and can generate its own emissions. Reusing water can require additional treatment and distribution. Capturing landfill gas never recovers every molecule. Composting can produce odors or leachate if poorly managed.

There is no circular economy in which physics agrees to stop entropy.

There are loops that can reduce extraction, disposal, or energy use compared with alternatives under particular conditions.

The honest comparison is between systems, not between a real system and an imaginary one with no losses.

You can learn a surprising amount about a place by following its leftovers.

Walk downhill during rain. Find where gutter flow enters a drain. Look for an outfall at a river or creek if public access allows. Notice whether storm drains carry “drains to river” markings. Watch what accumulates at curb inlets: leaves, cigarette butts, sediment, plastic fragments. Find grease traps behind restaurants, cardboard compactors behind stores, used cooking-oil tanks, recycling cages, loading docks, stormwater ponds, bioswales, sewer manholes, cooling towers, exhaust stacks, and utility vents.

These are the exit wounds of convenience.

Listen too.

Waste systems are noisy because removal requires movement. garbage trucks lift and compact. blowers aerate wastewater. pumps move sewage. fans exhaust heat. sorting equipment shakes, screens, separates, and conveys. compactors crush cardboard. street sweepers brush and vacuum.

“Gone” has a soundtrack.

Smell can be more direct, though just as ambiguous as elsewhere in this book. Wastewater plants, compost sites, landfills, sewers, food waste, and stagnant stormwater can produce recognizable odors, but smell does not map cleanly to health risk or treatment performance. Some hazardous gases have strong odors at low concentrations; others offer poor warning. Operators use instruments for the same reason air-quality professionals do.

The nose says material has traveled.

The instrument says what and how much.

The final hidden system is not waste itself.

It is the social decision to put consequences out of sight.

Buried pipes move sewage under streets. collection schedules remove garbage before daylight. industrial exhaust rises above rooflines. storm drains carry runoff below grade. server fans push heat behind walls. recycling bins replace the complex destination with a color-coded lid.

These systems are valuable precisely because we do not want waste accumulating where we live.

The danger is forgetting that removal is not erasure.

Flush the toilet. The water leaves your house, not the planet.

Throw away the package. It leaves your kitchen, not matter.

Turn on the air conditioner. Heat leaves the room, not existence.

A functioning civilization is partly a choreography of transfers.

The moral vocabulary comes later.

First, follow the flow.
