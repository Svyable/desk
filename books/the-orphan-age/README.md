# The Orphan Age

*What Happens When Creation Gets Cheaper Than Care*

We are entering an age in which making a thing can take minutes and caring for it can take decades.

That inversion changes the economics of civilization.

Software can be generated faster than anyone can maintain it. A company can launch before it has a succession plan. A satellite can reach orbit in less time than its debris may remain there. A well can stop producing while its steel and cement keep interacting with groundwater and atmosphere. A dataset can survive the team that understood its columns. A device can become unrepairable while every material inside it still exists. An automated decision can continue affecting people after the assumptions that produced it have gone stale.

The central argument of *The Orphan Age* is that abundance creates a new class of externality: **orphan load**. Every durable creation opens a future claim on attention, money, authority, documentation, repair, monitoring, succession, retirement, or disposal. When that claim loses a responsible steward, the creation becomes an orphan system.

This is not an argument against making things. It is an argument that creation is only the first half of ownership.

The book follows orphan systems through code, infrastructure, records, institutions, energy, space, AI, consumer products, hazardous waste, and the ordinary objects of daily life. It asks a question that product culture usually postpones: *Who owns the ending?*

The final chapters turn from diagnosis to design. They look at decommissioning funds, closure plans, repairability, inventories, reversibility, handoff documents, sunset clauses, succession, and graceful decay—not as bureaucratic afterthoughts, but as technologies of freedom. A world that can end things well can create more boldly because it does not force every new idea onto the shoulders of the future.
