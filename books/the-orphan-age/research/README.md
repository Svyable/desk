# Research Trail — The Orphan Age

This directory records the factual spine behind *The Orphan Age* while keeping the book’s central interpretive concept—**orphan load**—clearly distinguished from sourced facts.

The research method is simple:

1. Prefer primary, regulatory, standards, archival, scientific, or otherwise authoritative sources.
2. Map major factual claims to sources.
3. Record what would materially weaken or reverse the chapter’s argument.
4. Treat metaphors, proposed terminology, and synthesis as the author’s analysis rather than established external fact.
5. Avoid implying that evidence from one domain automatically proves the thesis in another.

## Chapter map

### Chapter 1 — The Button That Never Says Finished

**Evidence role:** establishes the general lifecycle problem: systems persist after creation and continue creating maintenance, custody, security, and retirement obligations.

**Primary support:** NIST software supply-chain guidance; NARA digital preservation guidance; GAO legacy IT work.

**What would make this false?** If durable systems routinely ceased imposing meaningful costs or obligations once initial creation ended, the chapter’s core framing would be overstated.

### Chapter 2 — Every New Thing Makes a Future Job

**Evidence role:** supports the claim that creation opens future maintenance and lifecycle work.

**Primary support:** GAO legacy IT; NIST supply-chain guidance; EPA/NRC closure and decommissioning regimes.

**What would make this false?** If most long-lived assets required negligible future maintenance, migration, compliance, security, or disposal work, the proposed “future job” framing would be weak.

### Chapter 3 — Orphan Code

**Evidence role:** documents maintainer burden, dependency concentration, and software supply-chain risk.

**Primary support:** GitHub open-source maintainer and supply-chain publications; NIST software supply-chain guidance.

**What would make this false?** If critical software dependencies were broadly maintained by well-resourced, redundant teams with low succession risk, the orphan-code argument would need substantial revision.

### Chapter 4 — Dead Accounts Still Own Things

**Evidence role:** supports persistence of identifiers, registrations, credentials, and ownership states after active stewardship lapses.

**Primary support:** ICANN expired-registration and recovery policy; broader identity/credential lifecycle principles in security practice.

**What would make this false?** If expired or abandoned digital identities automatically and safely relinquished all consequential ownership and access, the chapter’s risk framing would be overstated.

### Chapter 5 — The Forgotten Machine

**Evidence role:** supports maintenance, mechanical integrity, repair, and lifecycle obligations for physical equipment.

**Primary support:** OSHA mechanical-integrity guidance; FTC right-to-repair report.

**What would make this false?** If aging machines rarely required specialized maintenance, parts, diagnostics, or repair access, the chapter’s continuity argument would weaken.

### Chapter 6 — Wells Nobody Wants

**Evidence role:** provides a strong physical example of assets whose productive life can end before environmental and financial obligations do.

**Primary support:** U.S. Department of the Interior orphaned-well program materials.

**What would make this false?** If orphaned wells posed negligible environmental/safety risk or if responsible operators reliably funded closure in nearly all cases, this chapter would need to be reframed as an edge case.

### Chapter 7 — Bridges Outlive Budgets

**Evidence role:** demonstrates long-lived infrastructure condition and recurring inspection/maintenance burden.

**Primary support:** Federal Highway Administration National Bridge Inventory condition data.

**What would make this false?** If bridges generally remained safe and serviceable without recurring inspection, maintenance, rehabilitation, or replacement funding, the chapter’s budget-lifecycle mismatch would be overstated.

### Chapter 8 — Space Is Filling with Ghosts

**Evidence role:** documents persistent orbital objects and debris risk after mission usefulness ends.

**Primary support:** ESA Space Environment Report; NASA Orbital Debris Program.

**What would make this false?** If inactive space objects rapidly and harmlessly left operational orbits without intervention, the chapter’s orphan analogy would be substantially weaker.

### Chapter 9 — The Ship at Anchor

**Evidence role:** documents abandoned and derelict vessels as navigational, environmental, and removal burdens.

**Primary support:** NOAA Marine Debris Program materials on abandoned and derelict vessels.

**What would make this false?** If derelict vessels rarely created hazards or costly removal obligations, the chapter’s negative-value ownership example would weaken.

### Chapter 10 — Waste with a Half-Life

**Evidence role:** supports the proposition that some technological outputs create obligations long after productive use ends.

**Primary support:** U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission spent-fuel storage and decommissioning materials; EPA hazardous-waste closure and post-closure requirements.

**What would make this false?** If hazardous residuals reliably became benign on timescales shorter than the institutions managing them, the chapter’s central intergenerational argument would weaken.

### Chapter 11 — The Law After Its Author

**Evidence role:** this chapter is primarily institutional analysis rather than a claim about one legal regime. It uses lifecycle logic: rules, obligations, and records can remain operative after their original authors and contexts disappear.

**Primary support:** archival and records-management practice; public-law continuity as a general institutional reality.

**What would make this false?** If rules and institutional decisions routinely expired when their original authors left, the chapter’s analogy would fail.

### Chapter 12 — The Founder Is a Temporary Condition

**Evidence role:** supports succession as a continuity problem rather than a personality problem.

**Primary support:** cross-domain governance principles; chapter argument is mainly analytical.

**What would make this false?** If organizations could reliably retain operational continuity, authority, relationships, and tacit knowledge without succession planning, founder dependency would be less consequential than argued.

### Chapter 13 — Data Without a Steward

**Evidence role:** documents preservation risk, format obsolescence, and the need for active digital stewardship.

**Primary support:** NARA digital-preservation risk guidance; Library of Congress sustainability-of-digital-formats resources.

**What would make this false?** If digital data reliably remained interpretable and accessible without format management, metadata, migration, or custodial attention, this chapter would be overstated.

### Chapter 14 — Decisions Without an Owner

**Evidence role:** documents governance and decommissioning expectations for AI systems.

**Primary support:** NIST AI Risk Management Framework; GAO AI Accountability Framework.

**What would make this false?** If deployed automated decision systems did not require ongoing governance, monitoring, accountability, or retirement processes, the chapter’s ownerless-decision argument would weaken.

### Chapter 15 — The Dependency Chain

**Evidence role:** supports the idea that system continuity depends on often-hidden upstream components and maintainers.

**Primary support:** GitHub supply-chain publications; NIST SBOM and software supply-chain guidance.

**What would make this false?** If modern software workloads generally had few dependencies and those dependencies were transparent, substitutable, and well-resourced, the dependency-chain risk would be overstated.

### Chapter 16 — Retirement Is a Feature

**Evidence role:** supports retirement/decommissioning as an explicit lifecycle activity rather than an emergency afterthought.

**Primary support:** NIST AI RMF decommissioning guidance; DOE deactivation/decommissioning; NRC and EPA closure regimes.

**What would make this false?** If safe retirement required no planning, authority, funding, or technical work, treating retirement as a designed feature would add little value.

### Chapter 17 — Reversible by Default

**Evidence role:** supports repairability, portability, and reduced lock-in as ways to preserve future choice.

**Primary support:** FTC right-to-repair report; general lifecycle and interoperability principles.

**What would make this false?** If repair restrictions, closed interfaces, and non-portable data had negligible effects on user exit and product lifetime, the chapter’s reversibility case would weaken.

### Chapter 18 — Handoff Is Architecture

**Evidence role:** supports documentation, provenance, records, and transferability as continuity infrastructure.

**Primary support:** NIST SBOM/supply-chain guidance; NARA records and digital-preservation practice.

**What would make this false?** If systems transferred smoothly between stewards without documentation, provenance, credentials, or institutional memory, the chapter’s architectural framing would be overstated.

### Chapter 19 — The Right to Decay

**Evidence role:** combines repair, preservation, and retirement evidence to argue that responsible stewardship includes planned reduction and ending.

**Primary support:** FTC repair work; NARA/Library of Congress preservation practice; DOE/NRC/EPA decommissioning regimes.

**What would make this false?** If indefinite continuation of old systems generally imposed negligible cost, risk, opportunity cost, or compatibility burden, the case for planned decay would weaken.

### Chapter 20 — The Stewardship Economy

**Evidence role:** synthesizes observed maintenance, decommissioning, preservation, and lifecycle markets into a forward-looking economic thesis.

**Primary support:** GAO maintenance spending; EPA/NRC financial-assurance regimes; GitHub maintainer funding initiatives; the cross-domain evidence in preceding chapters.

**What would make this false?** If lower creation costs did not increase installed-base complexity or future maintenance/retirement demand, or if those needs could not support meaningful markets and institutions, the stewardship-economy thesis would be overstated.

## Interpretive boundary

The following are proposed concepts or synthesis used by the author, not terms claimed to have established external definitions:

- **orphan load**
- **maintenance surface**
- **stewardship density**
- **creation clock / care clock**
- **negative-value ownership** as used across the book’s examples
- **disappearance test**
- **ending capacity**
- **continuity without captivity**

Their value should be judged by whether they help explain and predict real lifecycle failures across domains—not by treating the terminology itself as evidence.
