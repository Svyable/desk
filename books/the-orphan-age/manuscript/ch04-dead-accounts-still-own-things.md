# Chapter 4 — Dead Accounts Still Own Things

The email address works until it does not.

For years it may sit at the center of a person’s digital life without attracting much attention. It receives receipts, password resets, tax documents, calendar invitations, security alerts, account confirmations, domain-renewal notices, bank messages, cloud invoices, and the occasional email from someone who has not updated an address book since college.

Then the person leaves a job.

Or a company closes.

Or a family stops paying for a domain.

Or an administrator deletes the mailbox because the employee has been gone for six months and storage is not free.

Nothing dramatic appears to happen.

The address goes quiet.

The things that still trust it do not.

This is one of the purest forms of orphanhood in the digital world. Authority disappears faster than dependency.

A recovery email is not merely an address. It is a key disguised as contact information. A domain is not merely a name. It can be the root from which email, certificates, login systems, websites, APIs, and institutional identity grow. An account is not merely a profile. It may own documents, repositories, advertising campaigns, encryption keys, payment relationships, app-store listings, phone numbers, or the only copy of a configuration nobody remembers creating.

Digital ownership has a peculiar habit of attaching itself to people because people are convenient identifiers.

A startup founder registers the company domain from a personal account before the company has an IT department. A researcher creates a cloud project using a university email address. A volunteer opens a social-media account for a local organization. An engineer creates an application-signing key. A consultant configures analytics. A treasurer opens a payment account. A marketing employee buys a domain for a campaign.

At the time, each action is rational.

The organization needs the capability now.

The orphan load arrives later, when the distinction between the person and the institution becomes important.

The problem is not that the account is inactive.

Inactive things can be perfectly healthy.

The problem is that the route from current responsibility to future authority has broken.

Who can renew the domain?

Who receives the recovery code?

Who can prove the organization owns the account?

Who can transfer the files?

Who can rotate the credential?

Who can cancel the subscription?

Who can respond if the service provider detects suspicious activity?

Who can delete the data when retention is no longer justified?

The account may have an owner in a database and no owner in reality.

That difference is where trouble accumulates.

Domain names make the structure unusually visible because expiration is explicit. ICANN’s policies require registrars to provide renewal and expiration notices and establish procedures around expired registrations. Registrars may offer grace periods and recovery mechanisms, but a domain that is not renewed can eventually leave the registrant’s control.

The name does not remember who used to own it.

The surrounding world might.

Old business cards continue to exist. Links remain in documents. People keep addresses in contact lists. Password-reset systems may still send mail to the old domain. Software configurations may contain URLs written years earlier. Customers may have bookmarked a portal. A certificate-transparency log may show the domain’s former technical history. Search results, archived pages, package metadata, and public records can preserve associations after legal control changes.

The result is a strange mismatch between memory and authority.

Humans and systems remember continuity.

The registry records a transfer.

This can create security risk, but the larger lesson is about endings. An organization that stops using a domain has not necessarily finished owning the consequences of that domain’s previous use. The same is true of phone numbers and email addresses. Identifiers are reusable in technical systems and sticky in social systems.

We think of deletion as an ending.

Often it is only the end of our access.

The external references remain.

A dead account can therefore continue to own things in three different senses.

First, it can remain the formal owner. The service still lists the departed employee as administrator. Nobody else has equivalent rights.

Second, it can remain the recovery authority. Other accounts treat it as the place to prove identity.

Third, it can remain the remembered owner. People and systems continue routing trust toward it even after formal control has changed.

Each failure requires a different remedy.

Formal ownership needs transfer.

Recovery authority needs redundancy and succession.

Remembered ownership needs deliberate retirement: redirects, changed contact information, revoked credentials, updated documentation, and enough time for the old identity to stop carrying trust.

This is why offboarding is not the administrative inverse of onboarding.

Onboarding adds a person to an institution that already exists. Offboarding has to remove a person without removing the institution’s ability to act.

That is harder.

The clean version is familiar. An employee leaves. Their manager receives the files. Shared credentials are rotated. Access is revoked. Email is forwarded for an appropriate period. Code ownership is reassigned. Devices are returned. Vendor accounts are transferred. The identity system disables the user. A documented process knows which systems to touch.

The real version is messier because the official identity system rarely contains every relationship the person created.

There is the cloud account opened during an experiment.

The mailing list created for a conference.

The repository under a personal namespace.

The certificate requested manually.

The vendor portal that allowed only one primary administrator.

The dashboard linked to a spreadsheet.

The automation connected through an individual token.

The account that nobody knew existed because it never caused a problem.

Offboarding is therefore an inventory test.

The departure reveals how much of the organization was legible before the departure.

The same thing happens after death, where the institutional problem becomes personal.

A modern estate may contain bank accounts, investment accounts, subscriptions, cloud storage, photographs, social identities, domain names, cryptocurrency keys, business records, loyalty balances, digital purchases, devices protected by passcodes, and accounts whose existence is known only through an email inbox the family cannot access.

Law can transfer property.

It cannot transfer knowledge that was never recorded.

A will can name an executor. It cannot tell the executor that the family photographs are in an encrypted archive whose password lives only in the deceased person’s memory.

The future job exists without a map.

This is an important distinction because digital-estate advice often collapses into a password list. Passwords matter, but a list of secrets is not a stewardship system. Secrets change. Multi-factor authentication depends on devices. Recovery channels expire. Services redesign interfaces. Some credentials should never be written casually. Some accounts permit designated legacy contacts; others rely on legal processes. Some assets should transfer; others should be deleted.

The durable object is not the password.

It is the succession path.

A succession path answers how a legitimate next steward can obtain authority without weakening security for the current steward.

That tension is real.

Systems designed for strong personal security often resist transfer. That is usually good. A bank should not make it easy for someone claiming to be a relative to take over an account. An encrypted service should not keep a secret back door merely to make inheritance convenient. Strong authentication is meant to block unauthorized succession.

But a system that cannot distinguish unauthorized takeover from legitimate succession has not eliminated the problem.

It has pushed it to courts, customer support, legal documents, recovery procedures, and sometimes permanent loss.

Good stewardship does not mean every account must be inheritable.

It means the ending is intentional.

Some accounts should die with the person. Some data should be deleted. Some identities should become memorials. Some business assets must transfer. Some records must remain available for tax, legal, scientific, or historical reasons. Some credentials should be revoked immediately because their continued existence creates risk.

The design question is not preservation at all costs.

It is controlled disposition.

Organizations face the same question at dissolution.

A company can stop operating while its accounts continue billing, renewing, authenticating, storing, or exposing data. The legal entity may be wound down more cleanly than its technical identity. Software subscriptions keep charging a card. Cloud storage keeps retaining customer information. Domains remain registered. Certificates remain valid. API tokens remain active. Old apps remain downloadable. DNS records still point somewhere.

The company is dead.

Its technical shadow is alive.

This shadow is one reason mergers and acquisitions contain so much hidden work. Buying a company means buying not only contracts, employees, intellectual property, and customers but a landscape of identities whose provenance may be incomplete. The acquiring organization has to discover what exists, which accounts matter, what can be consolidated, which licenses transfer, and which personal ownership shortcuts need to be turned into institutional ownership.

Acquisition is stewardship archaeology.

The same archaeology happens in less dramatic settings every day.

A school district replaces a superintendent and discovers that a service is tied to a former administrator.

A nonprofit changes volunteers and learns that the donation platform belongs to somebody’s personal email.

A family changes internet providers and discovers that an address used for twenty years was never truly portable.

A small business closes a location and realizes the phone number still receives authentication codes.

A software team migrates an application and finds that the production certificate is renewed from a machine nobody included in the architecture diagram.

None of these failures are advanced technical mysteries.

They are failures to separate identity from stewardship.

The separation matters because people are temporary conditions inside durable systems.

That sounds cold until we recognize that institutions were invented partly to solve this exact problem. A corporation can own property beyond the life of a founder. A university can hold a library through generations of faculty. A trust can preserve assets across time. A public office belongs to a role rather than the temporary officeholder.

Institutional identity is a technology for surviving human turnover.

Digital systems often regress from that achievement by making the easiest unit of ownership an individual account.

The user interface encourages it.

“Sign in with Google.”

“Continue with Apple.”

“Create workspace.”

“Start free.”

The product needs a user. The organization needs continuity.

Those are not the same requirement.

Enterprise identity systems try to close the gap through single sign-on, role-based access, centralized lifecycle management, group ownership, shared resources, audit logs, and administrative control. These mechanisms are sometimes treated as corporate bureaucracy layered on top of elegant consumer products.

They are actually anti-orphan infrastructure.

A resource owned by a group can survive a person.

A role can have a successor.

A centrally managed identity can be disabled without losing every asset it touched.

An audit log can help reconstruct what authority existed.

A recovery process can route through the institution rather than an employee’s private phone.

The principle extends beyond enterprise software.

A healthy community project should not depend on one account that only one person can recover.

A family archive should not depend on one device whose passcode nobody else can obtain.

A public service should not depend on a contractor’s personal credentials.

A company domain should not depend on a founder remembering to renew it from an old inbox.

The purpose is not to eliminate individuals from ownership.

It is to make succession visible where succession matters.

There is a subtle privacy risk here.

Systems built for succession can become systems built for surveillance if institutional access is too broad. A company should be able to preserve business records without casually exposing private employee communications. A family should be able to settle an estate without inheriting every intimate conversation. A government should preserve official records without treating all personal data as permanently collectible.

Stewardship has boundaries.

Knowing that something exists does not automatically grant a right to inspect it.

This is why good ending design includes both transfer and deletion.

The right successor gets what must continue.

What should not continue is deliberately destroyed.

That distinction becomes more important as storage gets cheaper. Cheap storage creates the illusion that keeping everything is safer than deciding. Organizations accumulate old accounts and old data because deletion feels irreversible while retention feels neutral.

Retention is not neutral.

Old accounts can be attacked. Old data can be breached. Old permissions can be abused. Old identifiers can be recycled. Old records can create legal obligations. Old integrations can remain trusted after their purpose disappears.

Indefinite retention is another form of orphan load.

The future job is not only to keep things.

It is to keep deciding what deserves to remain.

This brings us to a useful test for digital ownership.

Imagine the person who created an account becomes permanently unreachable tomorrow.

Does the organization know the account exists?

Can a legitimate successor obtain control?

Can the organization revoke the old authority?

Can it identify what depends on the account?

Can it export what must survive?

Can it delete what should not?

Can it prove, months later, why those actions were legitimate?

If the answer is no, the account is not fully owned.

It is merely occupied.

Occupancy can last a long time.

A founder can renew a domain personally for ten years. An employee can remain the sole administrator of a service through multiple promotions. A volunteer can keep a community account alive with extraordinary care.

The system works.

Then the human changes.

The Orphan Age is not created by unreliable people. It is created by systems that require reliable people to remain unchanged.

People are supposed to change.

They leave jobs. They retire. They become ill. They lose phones. They move. They change names. They forget. They die. Organizations merge. Teams dissolve. Vendors close. Roles disappear.

A durable system accepts this as a design input.

The goal is not immortality of the account.

The goal is continuity of legitimate authority.

That is why dead accounts still own things.

We built the digital world around identities that are easy to create and hard to inherit.

The next generation of stewardship systems will have to treat succession the way security engineering learned to treat authentication: not as an exceptional event, but as part of the lifecycle.

The most important recovery question is not “What is the password?”

It is “Who becomes responsible next?”
