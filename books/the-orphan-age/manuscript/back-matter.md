# Back Matter — The Orphan Test

The Orphan Test is a practical way to ask whether something you are creating, operating, funding, regulating, or depending on is becoming a future burden without a future steward.

It is not a scorecard for virtue. It is a way to surface hidden obligations before they become somebody else’s emergency.

Ask the questions while agency still exists.

## 1. Who owns the ending?

Name the person, team, institution, contract, or legal entity responsible for ending the system responsibly.

If the answer is “the company,” ask which role.

If the answer is “operations,” ask which budget.

If the answer is “the vendor,” ask what happens if the vendor disappears.

If nobody can be named, the ending is already orphaned in principle.

## 2. Can the owner still act?

Ownership without authority is ceremonial.

Can the named steward actually shut the system down, transfer it, export its data, spend money on repair, dispose of physical assets, revoke credentials, retire a model, or preserve required records?

If not, authority and responsibility have separated.

That is a common precursor to orphan load.

## 3. Is there money for the ending?

Many systems become orphaned not because nobody understands the work, but because the revenue ended before the obligation did.

Estimate the cost of retirement, migration, remediation, decommissioning, archival preservation, material disposal, or customer transition.

Then ask where that money will come from when the system no longer generates income.

An unfunded ending is an ending assigned to luck.

## 4. What happens if the creator never returns?

Run the disappearance test.

Assume the founder leaves.

Assume the maintainer stops responding.

Assume the vendor closes.

Assume the employee who knows the password is gone.

Assume the original research team cannot be contacted.

Assume the agency reorganizes.

Assume the contractor loses the account.

What still works?

What stops?

What becomes dangerous?

What becomes impossible to interpret?

A system that only survives when one particular person comes back is not resilient. It is waiting.

## 5. Can ownership transfer?

A durable system should not require permanent loyalty to its first steward.

Can another maintainer receive the documentation, credentials, keys, source, parts information, data definitions, contracts, operating history, and decision rights needed to continue?

If transfer is legally allowed but practically impossible, the system is still captive.

Handoff is architecture.

## 6. Can the system be reduced before it is abandoned?

Full operation and total shutdown are not the only states.

Can the system become read-only?

Can a connected product retain local function?

Can a public program stop new enrollment while honoring existing commitments?

Can an interactive archive become static?

Can an old service keep export available after creation features stop?

Graceful degradation often prevents abrupt orphaning.

## 7. Can users leave with what is theirs?

Portability is one of the cleanest tests of lifecycle quality.

Can users export meaningful data in a usable format?

Can customers migrate configurations?

Can an organization extract records without reverse-engineering the system?

Can an owner replace a service provider without recreating years of history?

A dependency becomes captivity when exit destroys accumulated value.

## 8. Are the dependencies visible?

Most orphan systems are not alone.

They depend on libraries, accounts, vendors, certificates, formats, models, APIs, physical parts, skilled labor, licenses, utilities, regulatory permissions, or other institutions.

List the dependencies.

Then ask which of them can disappear first.

The dependency chain is usually where the ending begins.

## 9. What records must outlive operation?

Retirement does not always mean deletion.

Some records may need to survive for legal, scientific, historical, contractual, safety, audit, or human reasons.

Separate operational continuity from memory.

Decide what must remain interpretable after the system stops running.

Preservation is not the same as keeping everything alive.

## 10. What should be deleted?

Keeping data also creates obligations.

Identify information whose continued existence no longer serves a legitimate purpose.

Ask whether it can be deleted completely enough to reduce risk, cost, and ambiguity.

A stewardship plan that only preserves and never disposes eventually becomes an accumulation plan.

## 11. Can the thing be repaired?

For physical systems, inspect whether maintenance is economically and technically possible.

Are parts available?

Are diagnostics accessible?

Can qualified people service it?

For digital systems, ask the equivalent questions.

Can the code be changed?

Can dependencies be upgraded?

Can security failures be patched?

Can another maintainer understand the architecture?

Repairability preserves the option to continue.

## 12. Can the thing be retired?

Repair is only half the lifecycle.

Can the system be shut down without causing uncontrolled failure elsewhere?

Can credentials be revoked?

Can integrations be removed?

Can customers be notified?

Can stored materials be disposed of safely?

Can a model be removed from a decision path?

Can an organization dissolve without losing necessary records?

Retirement is a feature.

## 13. Is the support horizon explicit?

Temporary systems become dangerous when everyone assumes somebody else intended permanence.

State the expected support horizon.

For an experiment, it may be weeks.

For a device, years.

For infrastructure, decades.

The exact number matters less than making continuation visible as a decision.

A review date can be as valuable as an expiry date.

## 14. What would make continued operation unjustified?

Define the stopping conditions before incentives harden around continuation.

Security risk.

Maintenance cost.

Loss of qualified operators.

Obsolete assumptions.

Environmental burden.

Replacement availability.

Low usage.

Regulatory change.

Better alternatives.

A system with no stopping condition will tend to survive by inertia.

## 15. Who is harmed by the ending?

Do not confuse low average usage with low consequence.

Identify last users, dependent communities, vulnerable users, safety obligations, public records, and downstream systems.

An ending can be justified and still require transition work.

The stronger the dependency you encouraged, the stronger the transition duty may be.

## 16. Who is harmed by continuation?

The inverse question matters too.

What opportunity, money, land, attention, energy, security capacity, institutional bandwidth, or environmental capacity does continued operation consume?

Keeping something alive is not neutral.

Future people pay an attention tax on the unresolved past.

## 17. Is there a smaller state worth preserving?

When full continuity is unjustified, ask what minimum state still carries the important value.

A static copy.

A final export.

A museum object.

A source archive.

A read-only account.

A reduced service.

A reusable component.

A documented API specification.

A decision log.

A legal record.

The best ending often preserves less than the original system and more than nothing.

## 18. Is the ending rehearsed?

Backups that are never restored are beliefs.

Exit plans that are never tested are beliefs too.

Try the transfer.

Try the export.

Try the restore.

Try revoking the account.

Try operating without the founder.

Try rebuilding from the documentation.

Try retiring a noncritical instance.

The rehearsal reveals where stewardship exists only on paper.

## 19. Is the obligation recorded where successors will find it?

A plan hidden in one person’s memory is not institutional memory.

Record the owner, support horizon, dependencies, funding assumptions, archive requirements, transition steps, and retirement path where future maintainers will actually look.

The repository should win the argument.

The asset register should win the argument.

The contract should win the argument.

The maintenance record should win the argument.

Memory is too fragile for durable obligations.

## 20. Does the system know how to finish?

This is the final test.

Imagine that the system has completed its useful work.

No scandal.

No emergency.

No bankruptcy.

No heroic rescue.

Just completion.

What happens next?

If the answer is clear, financed, transferable, documented, and executable, the system has an ending.

If the answer is silence, the future has been assigned a job without being asked.

That is orphan load.

The purpose of the test is not to stop creation.

It is to make creation more trustworthy.

A system that can be repaired can be depended on more safely.

A system that can be transferred can survive its creator.

A system that can be archived can stop operating without losing memory.

A system that can be deleted can stop imposing unnecessary custody.

A system that can be decommissioned can finish without becoming somebody else’s emergency.

And a system whose ending has an owner gives future people something more valuable than permanence.

It gives them choice.
