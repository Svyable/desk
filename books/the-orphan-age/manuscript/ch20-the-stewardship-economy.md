# Chapter 20 — The Stewardship Economy

The largest new industry of an age of creation may be taking care of what the age creates.

That sounds less exciting than artificial intelligence, robotics, biotechnology, cheap energy, reusable rockets, synthetic media, autonomous systems, and software generated on demand.

It should not.

Every one of those technologies expands the surface of things that can persist beyond the attention that made them.

More software means more migration, security, compatibility, retirement, and preservation.

More robots means more inspection, repair, parts, certification, decommissioning, and material recovery.

More satellites mean more traffic coordination, servicing, disposal, tracking, and debris mitigation.

More models mean more evaluation, provenance, monitoring, replacement, audit, and retirement.

More data means more classification, custody, deletion, migration, and interpretation.

More companies mean more succession, dissolution, records, contracts, and abandoned accounts.

More infrastructure means more maintenance.

Abundance does not eliminate work.

It changes where valuable work lives.

The twentieth century taught rich economies to become astonishingly good at producing standardized objects at scale. Manufacturing, logistics, finance, advertising, retail, software, and global supply chains made creation and distribution cheaper.

The twenty-first century is extending that curve into information and intelligence.

The cost of making a competent first version of many digital things is collapsing.

A website that once required a team can be generated from a conversation.

A report that took a week can appear in minutes.

A software prototype can emerge in an afternoon.

Images, translations, analyses, simulations, and interfaces can be produced at scales that would have been economically absurd not long ago.

This is good news.

It is also why stewardship becomes valuable.

Scarcity used to filter creation.

If building a system required a million dollars and two years, the difficulty itself forced some planning. Capital committees, technical teams, procurement, and organizational commitment stood between the idea and the artifact.

Cheap creation removes that filter.

Good.

Many worthwhile things were never created because the gate was too expensive.

But when the gate disappears, another capability has to grow downstream.

Selection.

Which creations should become durable?

Which should remain experiments?

Which deserve maintenance?

Which should merge with something else?

Which should be archived?

Which should disappear?

The economy of abundance needs institutions that can answer those questions without rebuilding scarcity at the entrance.

That is the stewardship economy.

It is not one industry.

It is a layer across industries.

Maintenance is part of it.

Repair is part of it.

Cybersecurity is part of it.

Records management is part of it.

Archives are part of it.

Insurance is part of it.

Asset management is part of it.

Facilities management is part of it.

Decommissioning is part of it.

Waste management is part of it.

Open-source maintenance is part of it.

Model governance is part of it.

Data stewardship is part of it.

Succession planning is part of it.

Standards bodies are part of it.

Restoration ecology is part of it.

The jobs already exist.

What changes is their strategic status.

Today many of these functions are treated as overhead: necessary costs around the real work of building, selling, and expanding.

In the Orphan Age, the ability to steward becomes a competitive advantage because customers will increasingly encounter products whose creators can disappear faster than the products themselves.

Trust moves toward the provider who can answer the future tense.

Will this still work?

Can I repair it?

Can I leave?

Can another operator take over?

Can I export my history?

Will you delete my data?

What happens if your company closes?

Who can fix this after the original team is gone?

How does this end?

These questions sound conservative until one realizes what they enable.

A customer who trusts the exit can adopt faster.

An investor who trusts succession can fund a company less dependent on one person.

A city that trusts decommissioning can experiment with temporary infrastructure.

A company that trusts portability can adopt a vendor without treating the decision as irreversible.

A research institution that trusts preservation can retire old operational systems.

A society that trusts waste management can use powerful technologies without pretending residual obligations do not exist.

Stewardship increases risk capacity.

This may be its most important economic effect.

We often try to encourage innovation by subsidizing creation.

Fund research.

Reduce startup costs.

Improve access to capital.

Provide tools.

Train builders.

Remove barriers.

All useful.

Another way to increase innovation is to reduce the cost of failure, transfer, and closure.

Make companies easier to wind down responsibly.

Make data easier to move.

Make equipment easier to repair and resell.

Make experiments easier to retire.

Make software dependencies easier to replace.

Make ownership easier to hand off.

Make environmental obligations financially legible before insolvency.

Make records easier to preserve after systems end.

An economy with good endings can tolerate more beginnings.

That is not merely a slogan.

It follows from option value.

If a decision can be reversed or closed at reasonable cost, the downside of trying it is smaller. Lower downside allows more experimentation at the same risk tolerance.

Stewardship is therefore infrastructure for entrepreneurship.

This changes what a startup opportunity can look like.

The glamorous startup builds the new machine.

The stewardship startup keeps a million machines useful after their manufacturers move on.

The glamorous AI company builds the model.

The stewardship company inventories deployed models, tracks lineage, evaluates drift, preserves decision evidence, and orchestrates retirement.

The glamorous cloud company hosts new applications.

The stewardship company discovers abandoned resources, finds owners, exports data, closes accounts, and proves deletion.

The glamorous space company launches spacecraft.

The stewardship company services, relocates, coordinates, or removes them.

The glamorous robotics company deploys fleets.

The stewardship company maintains mixed fleets across vendors after the original hardware generations age.

These are not second-class businesses.

They become more valuable as the installed base grows.

The installed base is the future market.

This is already how many mature industries work. New-car sales attract attention, but repair, parts, insurance, used vehicles, inspection, financing, fleet management, roadside service, recycling, and infrastructure form enormous surrounding economies.

Buildings support property management, trades, renovation, inspection, insurance, cleaning, security, energy services, and demolition.

Aircraft support maintenance, repair, overhaul, training, parts, certification, leasing, and records ecosystems.

Creation creates stewardship markets.

Digital technology is simply reaching the age where its installed base is large enough for the same pattern to become obvious.

The difference is that digital objects can multiply faster than physical service industries can.

That is why stewardship itself will need automation.

Humans cannot manually inventory every generated application, model, agent, dataset, cloud resource, credential, and integration.

The stewardship economy will use machines to maintain machine abundance.

AI can inspect repositories for abandoned dependencies.

Agents can identify accounts whose owners left the company.

Systems can detect data stores with no active consumers and trigger appraisal.

Models can summarize maintenance histories.

Robots can inspect infrastructure.

Sensors can predict failure.

Software can continuously map dependencies.

Automated migration tools can translate formats and APIs.

Digital twins can preserve knowledge about physical assets.

Autonomous spacecraft may service other spacecraft.

The same abundance engine that creates orphan load can help manage it.

This is the optimistic turn.

The Orphan Age is not a warning that technology creates too much.

It is an invitation to build the second half of the technological system.

Creation technology answers: how do we make this possible?

Stewardship technology answers: how do we keep possibility from becoming burden?

The second half has been underbuilt because it often arrives later.

A new industry first rewards capability.

Maintenance becomes important after the installed base ages.

Security becomes important after systems become valuable targets.

Interoperability becomes important after multiple vendors exist.

Migration becomes important after customers have history.

Decommissioning becomes important after the first generation reaches end of life.

Succession becomes important after founders become institutions.

The stewardship market is delayed demand.

That delay makes it easy to underestimate during periods of rapid growth.

Growth can hide maintenance debt because revenue and attention arrive faster than obligations mature.

Then the installed base ages together.

This is where waves of orphan load can appear.

A generation of infrastructure reaches replacement age.

A generation of software loses support.

A generation of founders retires.

A generation of renewable-energy equipment reaches end of life.

A generation of satellites needs disposal.

A generation of AI systems becomes legacy automation.

The future will contain synchronized endings because technologies are adopted in waves.

That creates business opportunities for anyone who can see the wave early.

The stewardship entrepreneur asks a different question from the frontier entrepreneur.

Not “what can now be created?”

“What has already been created at scale that will soon need a better ending?”

That question can reveal markets years before they look fashionable.

Solar-panel recycling.

Battery diagnostics and second-life systems.

Wind-turbine blade reuse and disposal.

Cloud exit and application retirement.

AI model lifecycle tooling.

Digital estate administration.

Orbital servicing.

Industrial robot refurbishment.

Legacy data interpretation.

Infrastructure inspection.

Open-source maintenance funding.

Device longevity and secure post-vendor operation.

Some of these markets already exist. Some are emerging. Some will take forms we cannot predict.

The common economics are clearer than the specific winners.

Value accumulates where unresolved obligations accumulate.

The stewardship economy also changes finance.

Traditional finance is good at funding assets that generate cash flows.

Ending obligations often generate negative cash flows after revenue stops.

That timing mismatch invites new products and stronger use of old ones: reserves, bonds, insurance, escrow, warranties, pooled funds, lifecycle fees, performance guarantees, and financial assurance.

The goal is to attach money to the obligation while someone still has money.

This is one of the bluntest lessons of abandoned wells and decommissioning.

Do not wait until the owner is insolvent to discover that closure costs money.

Digital systems need lighter versions of the same logic.

A hosted service could maintain a closure reserve proportional to customer migration burden.

A marketplace could require sellers of long-lived connected devices to provide lifecycle commitments or transferable support arrangements.

A foundation could hold critical open-source assets when maintainers leave.

An escrow service could preserve source, keys, or documentation under carefully defined release conditions.

An insurer could price weak lifecycle controls as operational risk.

Not every idea will be sensible or necessary. Some would impose costs greater than the risks they address.

The point is that finance can move responsibility across time.

That is exactly what stewardship needs.

There will also be new professions.

We have job titles for making things.

Product manager.

Software engineer.

Architect.

Founder.

Designer.

Data scientist.

Model engineer.

We will need more prestige and specialization around ending things.

Decommissioning engineer.

Digital archivist.

Lifecycle architect.

Model retirement lead.

Dependency steward.

Data disposition specialist.

Robotic fleet maintainer.

Succession operator.

Closure designer.

Some titles will sound ridiculous until the underlying burden becomes expensive enough.

Then they will sound obvious.

Cybersecurity once looked like a specialized concern around computer rooms. Today it is a board issue because digital dependency expanded. Privacy engineering grew as data dependency expanded. Cloud financial operations grew as cloud spending expanded.

Stewardship roles will rise for the same reason.

The installed base creates the profession.

Education will have to change too.

Engineering education celebrates design problems. Students build bridges, circuits, programs, robots, experiments, and models.

What if every capstone also asked for an ending?

How is the thing inspected?

How is it repaired?

What records must survive?

How does ownership transfer?

What is the failure mode after the original team disappears?

How is it decommissioned?

What materials or data remain?

What should happen if the project becomes unexpectedly successful?

The exercise would teach students that lifecycle is part of architecture rather than an operations concern for someone later.

Business education could do the same.

Every venture plan could include a closure path.

Not because founders should expect failure.

Because customers, employees, data, contracts, and assets deserve an answer if failure occurs.

A company that can close responsibly is easier to trust while it is alive.

Government procurement can reward stewardship too.

Public institutions buy systems expected to survive vendor turnover. They can ask for open formats, export, maintenance access, documentation, transition support, end-of-life plans, and ownership clarity. The exact requirements should match the risk; procurement can easily become overburdened with checklists.

But public buyers have unusual leverage because they purchase on long horizons.

They can make lifecycle quality economically visible.

Consumers can do the same if markets expose the information.

Imagine two devices with similar price and function.

One has a sealed battery, cloud-only controls, no published support horizon, and no data export.

The other has a replaceable battery, useful local function, a five-year security commitment, and a documented data path.

The second device has future value that ordinary price comparison does not show.

Lifecycle labels, warranties, reputation systems, regulation, and review culture can make that value legible.

Markets respond to what buyers can see.

The stewardship economy therefore needs measurement, but measurement should remain humble.

There will be pressure to create a universal stewardship score.

Resist the temptation to make one number carry every context.

A satellite, dataset, bridge, AI model, and coffee machine have different obligations. A long support horizon is good for one product and unnecessary for a temporary tool. Repairability matters differently for a toy and a medical implant.

The useful metrics are domain-specific.

Mean time to repair.

Portability completeness.

Owner coverage.

Dependency concentration.

Decommission funding.

Archive recoverability.

Model review status.

Parts availability.

Restore success.

Closure time.

The score is less important than whether the metric changes a real stewardship decision.

This is the final warning against turning the book into a compliance industry.

The stewardship economy should remove burden, not merely document burden.

A dashboard showing five thousand ownerless resources is useful only if it helps someone close, transfer, or justify them.

A model inventory is useful only if retirement can follow.

A maintenance system is useful only if work occurs.

A lifecycle plan is useful only if money, authority, and capability exist to execute it.

Evidence should lead to action.

Otherwise stewardship itself becomes orphan load.

The best stewardship products will therefore make the responsible action easier than neglect.

One click to transfer ownership.

One workflow to export and close.

One standard interface to replace a component.

One automatic record when maintenance occurs.

One clear successor state when a service retires.

One funded path from operation to decommissioning.

Good systems align convenience with responsibility.

This is a design challenge worthy of the same talent we currently devote to growth.

Growth design asks how to reduce friction into a product.

Stewardship design asks how to reduce friction through the whole relationship, including out of it.

The best companies of the next era may compete on both.

Easy to begin.

Safe to depend on.

Possible to leave.

That is a stronger promise than lock-in.

It is also a more ambitious one.

We can now return to the opening question.

What happens when creating becomes cheaper than caring?

At first, orphan load rises.

We produce more systems than our old institutions know how to maintain. Dependencies multiply. Accounts linger. Models drift. Infrastructure ages. Founders become bottlenecks. Data outlives meaning. Debris keeps moving. Negative-value assets wait for somebody else to pay.

Then, if we are competent, care itself becomes a field of invention.

We build better inventories.

Better repair systems.

Better archives.

Better handoffs.

Better financial assurance.

Better decommissioning technology.

Better lifecycle standards.

Better automation for maintenance.

Better ways to transfer authority.

Better ways to let temporary things remain temporary.

Better ways to preserve what matters without keeping everything alive.

The Orphan Age is not a permanent diagnosis.

It is a transition between two kinds of abundance.

First comes abundance of creation.

Then must come abundance of stewardship.

That second abundance may be less visible because its greatest achievements are absences.

The bridge did not fail.

The account did not become a breach.

The well did not leak.

The satellite did not become a collision.

The customer did not lose their records.

The founder left and the company continued.

The model retired before its assumptions became dangerous.

The building came down without leaving contamination.

The old system disappeared and nobody had to become a hero.

Maintenance rarely gets a launch video.

A clean ending rarely rings a bell on an exchange.

But civilization is built as much from prevented failure as visible invention.

The steward’s product is a future that remains available.

That is what all these chapters have been circling.

Stewardship is not reverence for old things.

It is respect for future choice.

Maintain what deserves continuation.

Transfer what another steward can carry better.

Repair what still has useful life.

Archive what deserves memory.

Delete what no longer deserves custody.

Decommission what has finished its work.

Fund the obligations before the revenue disappears.

Design the handoff before the person leaves.

Preserve the exit before the dependency becomes captivity.

And when you create something new, ask one additional question before calling it done.

Who owns the ending?

If the answer is clear, creation becomes safer.

If the answer can change hands, creation becomes more durable.

If the ending can be financed, executed, and understood, creation becomes more trustworthy.

And if the thing can eventually disappear without throwing its unfinished work onto strangers, then we have learned something deeper than how to build.

We have learned how to finish.

A world that learns how to end things well earns the freedom to begin more of them.
