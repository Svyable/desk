# Chapter 11 — The Law After Its Author

A rule can outlive the argument that produced it.

The people who negotiated the language leave office. The industry changes. The technology changes. The agency reorganizes. A court interprets a phrase nobody expected to matter. A temporary exception becomes familiar. A reporting requirement survives because every system downstream has learned to expect the report.

The law remains on the page.

That permanence is one of law’s strengths.

A society could not function if obligations evaporated whenever the people who wrote them retired. Contracts, property rights, criminal rules, safety standards, corporate forms, tax systems, environmental requirements, building codes, and administrative procedures all depend on continuity beyond individual memory.

Law is a technology for making decisions durable.

Durability creates its own maintenance surface.

A rule does not rust, but the world around it changes.

Words acquire new contexts. New technologies fit old categories awkwardly. Agencies learn where enforcement is difficult. Regulated parties adapt. Courts create precedent. Other laws overlap. Data systems encode the requirement. Forms grow around it. Professional routines assume it. Entire industries can organize around interpretations that did not exist when the original text was enacted.

The result is institutional sediment.

That is not automatically bad.

Sediment can be the accumulated experience that makes a legal system predictable. Precedent lets people plan. Stable standards reduce coordination costs. Repeated interpretations clarify ambiguity. A mature regulatory regime can embody lessons learned through accidents, litigation, engineering, and enforcement.

The danger appears when durability loses review.

A rule can remain formally owned by an institution while practically orphaned from its purpose.

People keep complying because the rule exists.

Administrators keep administering because the program exists.

Software keeps collecting the field because the form asks for it.

Auditors keep checking the report because the procedure includes it.

Nobody can easily explain what current harm the requirement prevents or what evidence would justify changing it.

The rule has become self-sustaining.

This is the institutional cousin of the forgotten machine.

A machine can keep running after nobody remembers why a strange sequence matters.

A rule can keep operating after nobody remembers why a strange clause was written.

In both cases, ignorance does not prove uselessness.

The forgotten detail may encode an important lesson.

Removing it casually can recreate an old failure.

This is why legal maintenance is harder than deregulation slogans or regulatory accumulation statistics make it sound.

Counting rules is not enough.

One long rule can be less burdensome than ten short ones. A complex safety standard can simplify life by establishing common expectations. A reporting requirement can generate data necessary for oversight. A permit can coordinate uses that would otherwise conflict. A redundant provision can sometimes protect against a failure elsewhere.

The question is not whether rules are old.

It is whether institutions retain the capacity to evaluate them.

That capacity includes memory of purpose, evidence about effects, authority to amend, and mechanisms for review.

The same four ingredients appear throughout the Orphan Age.

What is this for?

What is it doing now?

Who can change it?

When must somebody look again?

Rules become orphans when those questions stop having practical answers.

Sunset clauses are one response.

A sunset provision causes a rule, program, authorization, or other legal arrangement to expire unless it is renewed. The attraction is obvious: permanence has to be earned again. The future decision is put on the calendar.

But sunsets are not magic.

A poorly timed sunset can create uncertainty around a program that requires long-term planning. Legislatures and agencies can renew provisions mechanically without meaningful review. Political congestion can allow useful authorities to lapse for reasons unrelated to performance. Some fundamental rules are valuable precisely because people should not have to reopen them constantly.

A calendar cannot substitute for judgment.

Still, the sunset idea contains a powerful stewardship principle: *default states shape institutional memory*.

If the default is continuation, forgetting preserves the system.

If the default is expiration, forgetting ends it.

Neither default is universally safe.

The important design choice is deciding which failure mode is less dangerous for the domain.

Safety rules may deserve durable defaults with periodic review rather than automatic expiration.

Emergency powers may deserve stronger sunset mechanisms because temporary authority can become normalized.

Pilot programs may deserve explicit decision points before scaling.

Data-retention mandates may need review as technology changes the cost and risk of storage.

Technical standards may require versioning rather than legal disappearance.

Different systems need different endings.

That sentence should now feel familiar.

The Orphan Age is not searching for one universal lifecycle template.

It is asking every durable system to possess a lifecycle at all.

Legal systems have another useful technology for this: codification.

Laws accumulate through separate acts, amendments, repeals, cross-references, court decisions, and administrative rules. Codification and revision processes attempt to make the current body of law legible. Without consolidation, users would have to reconstruct today’s rule from a historical pile of changes.

Software engineers solve the same problem with current source code and version history.

The current code should tell you what the system does now.

The history should tell you how it got there.

Confusing the two creates trouble.

A legal system that preserves only current text loses context.

A legal system that preserves only historical enactments becomes unusable.

Stewardship needs state and history.

This is exactly what digital systems need when automated decisions enter regulated life. If a model changes over time, an institution may need to know both which model is current and which version made a past decision. If a data schema changes, current applications need the current schema while audits may require the historical interpretation.

Versioning is an ending technology because it lets old states stop governing the present without disappearing from the record.

We often treat replacement as destruction.

Versioning shows another possibility.

The old rule can cease to be operative while remaining inspectable.

The old software can cease to receive traffic while remaining reproducible for audit.

The old model can stop making decisions while its provenance remains available.

The old policy can be superseded while the reasons for its creation remain in archives.

This is graceful institutional decay.

The distinction between *operative* and *preserved* is one of the most useful tools for reducing orphan load.

Organizations often keep systems running because they confuse availability with preservation.

A database must remain queryable, so the application stays alive.

A report might be needed, so the entire generation pipeline remains operational.

A legal interpretation may matter, so an obsolete process stays in place.

If the required historical state can be preserved in a more passive form, active maintenance can end.

Archives let us retire machinery.

This sounds obvious to archivists.

Product culture is still learning it.

Law also teaches us that ownership can be distributed through institutions rather than assigned to one person.

A statute does not have a product manager in the ordinary sense. Responsibility is spread across legislators, agencies, courts, regulated parties, counsel, auditors, and the public. That distribution is messy, but it creates resilience. No single author’s departure makes the law inaccessible.

Distributed responsibility can also produce diffusion of responsibility.

Everyone is involved, so nobody owns the ending.

An agency may administer a rule it cannot repeal. A legislature may have authority to change it but little operational knowledge. Courts interpret cases but do not redesign the whole system. Regulated parties know where burdens fall but have interests that do not perfectly match the public purpose.

The result can be a system with many stewards and no lifecycle steward.

This is common in complex organizations.

Security owns risk.

Legal owns compliance.

Engineering owns the application.

Finance owns the vendor contract.

Operations owns availability.

Data governance owns retention.

No one owns retirement.

The system survives because every department has a reason not to be the one that turns it off.

The same structure can preserve obsolete rules.

This suggests a role that institutions rarely name explicitly: the steward of continued justification.

Someone or some process should be responsible not only for asking whether a system functions, but whether its continued existence still earns the obligations it creates.

For law, this can occur through legislative oversight, regulatory review, program evaluation, audits, commissions, judicial development, public consultation, or formal retrospective review.

For companies, it can occur through portfolio review.

For software, architecture governance.

For models, lifecycle review.

For infrastructure, asset management.

The mechanism changes.

The question does not.

Does this thing still deserve to be alive in its current form?

That question is politically dangerous because it can be weaponized.

A review process can be used to weaken protections rather than improve them. A requirement to repeatedly justify a rule can advantage well-funded interests with the resources to contest it. A sunset can create bargaining leverage unrelated to policy quality. An efficiency review can undervalue benefits that are difficult to measure, especially prevention, equity, dignity, or ecological protection.

Stewardship is not neutral simply because it uses the language of maintenance.

Someone chooses what counts as value.

This is why the book’s argument has to remain modest.

It cannot tell a society which laws should exist.

It can insist that durable rules deserve durable mechanisms for memory, evidence, review, amendment, and where appropriate retirement.

The goal is not perpetual churn.

The goal is conscious continuity.

A rule that survives repeated informed review is less orphaned than one that survives because no institution can reconstruct the consequences of changing it.

The same is true of old technology.

Keep it if it still serves.

But know why.

Standards offer another example because they sit between law, engineering, and coordination.

A standard may define dimensions, protocols, testing methods, electrical characteristics, safety requirements, terminology, or data formats. Once widely adopted, infrastructure grows around it. Compatibility becomes a network effect.

The standard becomes hard to change precisely because it succeeded.

This is not orphanhood.

It is lock-in with stewardship implications.

A mature standards organization manages revisions carefully, preserves backward compatibility where valuable, publishes editions, invites technical expertise, and creates transition periods. The old version does not disappear the instant a new version exists.

There is a migration path.

This is law’s version of a rolling upgrade.

The comparison is imperfect but useful.

Changes to durable rules need transition architecture.

Removing a requirement can create as much orphan load as adding one if institutions built around it are not given a path to adapt.

A data-reporting rule ends; what happens to historical series?

A licensing regime changes; what happens to existing licensees?

A technical mandate updates; what happens to installed equipment?

A subsidy ends; what happens to contracts based on it?

A jurisdiction adopts a new building code; what happens to old buildings?

Endings create successors.

That is the chapter’s deepest point.

We tend to think of repeal as subtraction.

In a complex system, repeal is another form of change that creates future work.

Some records must remain accessible.

Some rights may vest.

Some contracts must transition.

Some enforcement matters continue.

Some old and new standards coexist.

Some people rely on the prior arrangement.

The end has a maintenance surface too.

This is why good institutional design rarely produces the clean erasure that political rhetoric promises.

The world has memory.

Responsible change accounts for it.

The law after its author is therefore not a story about rules refusing to die.

It is a story about how human societies make obligations survive people, and how that success creates a second obligation: keep the durable system connected to living judgment.

A law that changes every week cannot coordinate behavior.

A law that cannot change at all cannot learn.

Between those failures lies stewardship.

Remember why the rule exists.

Observe what it does now.

Preserve enough history to understand consequences.

Give legitimate institutions authority to revise it.

Create occasions when continuation becomes a conscious choice.

Then let old versions stop governing without pretending they never existed.

The author is temporary.

The institution is the handoff.
