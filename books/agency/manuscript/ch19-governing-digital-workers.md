# Governing Digital Workers

The phrase “digital worker” is useful and misleading in equal measure.

Useful because an agent can now occupy part of a role rather than merely answer a question.

Misleading because the analogy to employment can smuggle in assumptions that do not fit software.

A digital worker does not need encouragement after a difficult week. It does not become loyal because the company treated it fairly. It does not deserve a career path. It does not have a family, dignity, labor rights, or a claim on the institution’s moral concern in the way a human employee does.

Yet once an agent can hold context, use tools, persist across tasks, maintain state, coordinate with other systems, and exercise delegated authority, the organization needs more than software procurement.

It needs governance.

The governance begins with role definition.

What is this agent for?

This sounds trivial until a general-purpose assistant becomes connected to everything.

The same system can research, code, write, schedule, purchase, monitor, and communicate. Because capability is broad, organizations may be tempted to grant authority broadly. The agent becomes a universal employee with whatever permissions the user happens to possess.

Human organizations rarely work that way.

They define roles because authority, context, and accountability need boundaries.

An accounts-payable specialist sees different information and has different powers from a recruiter. A developer can change code but should not automatically edit payroll. A salesperson can talk to customers but cannot usually bind the company to any contract she chooses. A chief executive can authorize many things and still operates under law, board authority, fiduciary duties, and institutional process.

Role is a governance primitive.

Agents need roles too.

Not because the model needs an identity story.

Because the principal needs a legible bundle of purpose, context, permissions, verification, and escalation.

A good agent role can often be described in one paragraph.

Maintain routine customer account records from verified requests; never alter billing terms, legal names, or cancellation status without a policy-backed source; surface conflicts between systems; log every write; escalate when identity cannot be established.

That role is more useful than “customer operations agent.”

It says what the system is allowed to make true.

The second governance layer is appointment.

Which model, toolset, and orchestration should occupy the role?

Organizations often choose models as if they were software brands. A benchmark leads. A vendor is trusted. Procurement signs a contract. The model becomes default.

Agentic roles deserve more local evaluation because the consequences depend on the work.

The best general model may not be the best role occupant.

A cheaper model may be good enough for structured, reversible work.

A specialized model may outperform a frontier generalist on a narrow domain.

A slower model may be worth using where reasoning quality dominates latency.

A local model may be preferable where data control matters more than raw capability.

A deterministic system may outperform every model where the role is actually rules disguised as intelligence.

Appointment should follow evidence about the role.

This is analogous to hiring, but with an important difference: swapping the worker can be much cheaper.

That means model selection should be less sacred.

If context, permissions, state, and evaluation are designed independently enough, the organization can treat models as replaceable components inside a durable role.

This is a form of institutional sovereignty.

The role survives the worker.

Human organizations aspire to the same property. A company that collapses when one employee leaves has a key-person problem. An agentic organization should not create a key-model problem unnecessarily.

The third governance layer is probation.

New authority should be earned locally.

A model has a public reputation. It may have benchmark scores. It may perform impressively in demonstrations. None of that is the same as evidence inside the organization’s own workflow.

Probation can begin in shadow mode.

The agent observes and recommends without acting.

Its decisions are compared with actual outcomes.

Then reversible actions are enabled.

Then routine classes can become autonomous.

Higher-consequence boundaries expand only where the evidence justifies expansion.

This is slower than granting full authority on day one.

It is often faster than recovering from an authority mistake nobody understood.

The fourth layer is supervision.

Human management traditionally combines several functions: setting direction, allocating resources, monitoring work, coaching, resolving conflict, evaluating performance, and carrying accountability upward.

Agents need some of these and not others.

They need direction.

They need resource limits.

They need monitoring.

They need evaluation.

They may need conflict resolution when multiple agents pursue incompatible local objectives.

They do not need emotional coaching, although the humans around them certainly might.

The useful supervisory question is not, “What did the agent do all day?”

It is, “What evidence tells us this role is creating the intended outcome inside its authority?”

This changes performance management.

A human employee can be evaluated partly through qualitative observation because the manager sees behavior over time.

An agent may execute millions of actions the manager never sees.

The evaluation system has to move toward distributions.

Success rate.

Exception rate.

False escalation rate.

Policy violations.

Cost per governed outcome.

Time to recovery.

Frequency of human override.

Quality under unusual cases.

Drift after model or context changes.

These measures are not universal. Each role needs a scorecard matched to consequence.

The dangerous metric is automation rate.

“How much work did the agent do without a human?”

This can be useful as an efficiency indicator after safety and quality are established. As a primary objective, it rewards removal of oversight regardless of whether oversight was valuable.

The purpose of digital labor is not to maximize the amount of labor done digitally.

It is to improve the institution’s capacity.

The fifth layer is incident management.

Agents will fail.

Some failures will be obvious: a tool call errors, a transaction is rejected, a test fails.

Others will be semantic. The system does exactly what it was told and violates what the principal meant.

Still others will be systemic. One piece of context changes and many agents begin behaving differently at once.

This is why agent incidents need investigation beyond “the model hallucinated.”

Which layer failed?

Intent?

Context?

Permissions?

Model capability?

Tool reliability?

Verification?

Orchestration?

Escalation?

Human review?

The model may be the visible actor while the root cause sits in the institution around it.

A useful incident review should preserve this distinction because otherwise organizations will respond badly.

If the problem was overly broad permission, better prompting will not fix it.

If the problem was stale context, switching models may not fix it.

If the problem was an inadequate test, adding human approval may create ritual without assurance.

If the problem was ambiguous intent, the same mistake may reappear across vendors.

Agent incidents are organizational incidents.

The sixth layer is change management.

A digital worker can change overnight.

The underlying model updates.

A retrieval system changes.

A new tool becomes available.

A policy is edited.

A prompt is revised.

A memory is added.

A vendor silently improves or deprecates behavior.

The role can remain nominally the same while operational behavior shifts.

This is unlike a human employee, whose capabilities and habits usually change more gradually and visibly.

Agentic organizations need version awareness.

What changed?

When?

Which evaluations were rerun?

Which workflows are sensitive to the change?

Can behavior be rolled back?

Do high-consequence roles require a staged upgrade rather than immediate adoption?

Frontier progress creates pressure to update because better models can unlock real value. Governance that freezes models for stability can make the organization brittle to progress.

The better design is controlled upgradeability.

Move fast where evidence is cheap.

Stage changes where failure is expensive.

Keep the role’s tests and context separate enough that a new model can be evaluated against the institution rather than the institution being redesigned around every model.

The seventh layer is separation of duties.

One agent should not automatically become requester, approver, executor, verifier, and auditor for the same consequential action.

Again, the issue is not machine corruption in the human sense.

It is correlated error and loss of independent checks.

An agent that writes code can run tests. That is useful. The tests should not merely be tests the agent invented to validate its own interpretation if the behavior is important. A financial agent can prepare a transaction. A separate policy and approval path may release it. A research agent can synthesize claims. Independent evidence should support material claims.

Digital labor becomes more trustworthy when roles can disagree.

This creates a new kind of organizational chart.

Not boxes of people.

Boxes of authority.

Some boxes may contain people. Some models. Some deterministic software. Some vendors. Some committees. Some markets.

The chart tells us not who sits where but which mechanism can cause which consequence.

That may become a more accurate representation of a modern firm than headcount alone.

The eighth governance layer is termination.

Can the agent be removed cleanly?

This is where many supposedly flexible systems reveal lock-in.

The agent owns memory that cannot be exported.

Its prompts encode undocumented business rules.

Its tool integrations use provider-specific abstractions.

Its actions created state that no other system can interpret easily.

Its logs are inaccessible or too voluminous to reconstruct.

The role cannot be terminated without losing operational continuity.

That is a governance failure even if the agent performed well.

A mature role has an offboarding path.

Export durable context.

Revoke credentials.

Identify unfinished work.

Transfer ownership of active commitments.

Preserve required records.

Confirm that automated triggers no longer invoke the agent.

Reassign the role.

This sounds exactly like employee offboarding because the institutional problem is similar: authority and memory need to survive the departure of the actor.

The ninth layer is ownership.

Every agent role should have a human or governing body that owns the consequences of the role.

Not every action.

The role.

This owner decides what success means, approves authority boundaries, accepts residual risk, and is responsible for redesign when evidence says the system is failing.

Without role ownership, agents become orphan processes.

They keep running because nobody wants to disable something useful. People assume somebody else understands the setup. The workflow survives reorganizations. Permissions persist. A model update changes behavior. An incident finally reveals that the organization has been receiving machine labor from a worker nobody manages.

Human institutions accumulate orphan processes already.

Agents can create them faster.

The tenth layer is human impact.

Governing digital workers cannot be separated from governing what happens to human workers around them.

An agent may increase one employee’s leverage and erase another employee’s task. It may make a job more interesting by removing clerical work or more exhausting by increasing the amount of consequential review one person must perform. It may flatten hierarchy or increase surveillance. It may make an expert’s judgment more valuable while reducing entry-level opportunities.

These are not side effects of a technical deployment.

They are changes to the institution’s distribution of agency.

Who gets more discretion?

Who becomes a reviewer of machine work?

Who loses contact with customers?

Who can override the agent?

Who is blamed when the system fails?

Who benefits from the productivity gain?

Who loses bargaining power because part of the role became software?

A company can implement agents responsibly at the technical layer and irresponsibly at the organizational layer.

Good governance has to see both.

This is one reason employee involvement matters in workflow redesign.

The people closest to the work often know which exceptions matter, where policy and reality diverge, and which “inefficiencies” are actually protective friction. They also know which tasks are dead weight and should disappear immediately.

Treating employees only as users to be trained after the agent is built discards local institutional intelligence.

The strongest deployments will recruit that intelligence into the design.

This is not a promise that every current job remains.

It is an argument that people affected by a change often understand the production system better than the system diagram does.

Digital labor deserves governance because it changes the locus of action.

The models do not become citizens of the company.

The company becomes more software-defined in how it exercises authority.

That is a profound transformation.

Organizations have spent centuries developing norms around human delegation: managers, fiduciary duties, professional standards, audits, internal controls, appeals, signatures, chains of command, and institutional memory.

Agents do not erase the need for those ideas.

They expose which ideas were truly about humans and which were about delegated power.

The latter survive.

Authority needs a role.

A role needs an owner.

An owner needs evidence.

Evidence needs independence.

And every system powerful enough to act on behalf of an institution needs a way for the institution to say: stop.
