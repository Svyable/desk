# Intelligence Is Cheap; Agency Is Scarce

For most of human history, intelligence was expensive in a very literal way.

If you needed a competent legal opinion, a financial model, a translation, a software program, a market analysis, a lesson plan, a diagnosis, a design, a research synthesis, or a piece of persuasive writing, you needed time from somebody who had learned how to produce it. The output might fit on one page. The capability behind the page could have taken twenty years to build.

That relationship made cognitive production scarce.

Scarcity shaped institutions around it.

Experts controlled queues. Firms accumulated specialists. Professional credentials reduced the cost of deciding whom to trust. Managers rationed analytical effort because every additional analysis consumed labor. A junior employee might spend a day preparing material so that a senior employee could spend twenty minutes exercising judgment. The physical artifact was often cheap. The intelligence required to create it was not.

Artificial intelligence changes the price of the artifact first.

A draft that once required an hour can appear in seconds. A hundred variations cost little more than ten. A competent explanation can be generated on demand. Code can be proposed, tested, revised, and proposed again at a speed that makes the old unit of keystrokes almost irrelevant. A model can read more background material than a person would rationally pay another person to read for a minor decision.

This can feel like the arrival of cheap intelligence.

In an important sense, it is.

But “intelligence” is a dangerous word because it compresses too many economically different activities into one noun.

Generating a plausible answer is not the same thing as knowing which question deserves an answer.

Producing options is not choosing among them.

Explaining a tradeoff is not accepting the tradeoff.

Recommending an action is not having authority to take it.

Executing an instruction is not owning the consequence.

When one scarce activity becomes abundant, value tends to migrate toward whatever remains difficult.

Photography offers a simple example. When film and development were expensive, people rationed photographs. A family might return from a vacation with two dozen images because every exposure cost money and the result would not be visible until later. Digital cameras collapsed the marginal cost of taking another picture. The immediate effect was obvious: people took more pictures.

The second-order effect was different.

Taking the photograph stopped being the main bottleneck. Finding the photograph became harder. Choosing the photograph became harder. Storing, sorting, sharing, and deciding what was worth preserving became part of the problem. The value migrated from exposure toward selection and organization.

Generative AI does this to cognitive output.

When a competent draft is expensive, producing the draft is work.

When a competent draft is cheap, deciding whether the draft is true, useful, necessary, appropriate, timely, original, or aligned with the actual objective becomes more of the work.

This is not a philosophical point.

It changes where organizations should spend attention.

Suppose a strategy team used to produce three market-entry scenarios because producing each one required research, modeling, and writing. The constraint encouraged compression. The team had to decide which possibilities deserved analysis before doing the analysis.

Now an agent can produce thirty scenarios.

The team has not escaped scarcity.

It has moved scarcity downstream.

Which scenarios are distinct rather than cosmetic variations? Which assumptions matter? Which data is stale? Which downside cases deserve deeper work? Which recommendation would still hold under reasonable uncertainty? Which scenario is being included because the model can imagine it rather than because the business should care?

Thirty scenarios can create less clarity than three.

The problem is familiar. Herbert Simon argued in 1971 that an abundance of information creates a scarcity of attention. Information consumes the attention of its recipients, so producing more information without improving filtering can make the system worse rather than better.

Agentic systems extend the insight.

Actions consume governance.

A wealth of possible action creates a poverty of supervision unless the system becomes better at deciding what deserves human attention.

This is why agency, not intelligence, becomes the scarcer economic resource.

Agency is the capacity to form an intention, commit to it, and carry it into the world while remaining able to revise course as reality pushes back.

A person with agency does not merely know things.

She can decide what to do about them.

A company with agency does not merely possess data and employees.

It can coordinate action without losing itself in coordination.

A state with agency does not merely publish policy.

It can implement policy, observe effects, correct mistakes, and maintain legitimacy while doing so.

An AI agent is interesting because it can supply part of this chain. It can carry intention farther into action.

But the agent does not make the principal’s agency irrelevant.

It raises the return on it.

This is the part of the AI economy that productivity statistics struggle to capture.

Productivity usually asks how much output is produced for a given amount of input. That is essential. If an assistant helps a support worker resolve more customer issues per hour, the gain is real. A large field study of customer-support agents found a meaningful average productivity increase from generative AI assistance, with especially large gains among less experienced workers. An experiment on professional writing found workers completing certain writing tasks faster and with higher-quality results when they had access to ChatGPT. Research with management consultants found strong gains on tasks inside the model’s capability frontier and worse performance on a task outside it.

These findings matter because they show that artificial intelligence can change the production function of existing work.

Agentic leverage asks a different question.

What happens when the person can do work that would not have been attempted at all?

A solo founder who once had enough time to investigate three customer segments can now run structured research across thirty, build prototypes for five, and keep a live record of objections without hiring a temporary research team.

A small nonprofit can examine a thousand pages of public records before deciding which five pages deserve a lawyer’s attention.

An engineer can ask an agent to test a migration approach that would previously have been rejected because the experiment itself cost too much time.

A manager can maintain a decision log because the agent can extract candidate decisions from meetings and ask for confirmation, turning an administrative practice that once died from neglect into something close to ambient infrastructure.

In each case, the old task may not get merely faster.

The feasible set expands.

Economists care about this distinction because technological change often creates its largest effects through new tasks and new products rather than through perfect substitution for old ones. Cheap electricity did not only reduce the cost of existing mechanical power. It changed factory design. Cheap computing did not merely automate clerical arithmetic. It created search engines, digital markets, software businesses, real-time logistics, and industries whose basic products were impossible under the old cost structure.

Cheap cognition will create work that was never worth staffing.

Cheap agency will create institutions that were never worth coordinating.

The second claim is more radical.

A one-person business can already look larger than one person from the outside. Contractors, platforms, cloud software, APIs, global marketplaces, and on-demand logistics have been shrinking the minimum efficient size of many operations for years. Agents add a new layer because the owner can delegate not only standardized services but portions of ambiguous cognitive workflow.

This does not make the owner omnipotent.

It makes the owner a more highly leveraged principal.

That distinction matters because the quality of the principal becomes more important as the cost of the agent falls.

When intelligence is expensive, organizations spend effort deciding where to deploy experts.

When intelligence is cheap, organizations must spend effort deciding where *not* to deploy it.

The default can otherwise become production for production’s sake.

More reports because reports are cheap.

More experiments because experiments are cheap.

More features because features are cheap.

More outreach because personalization is cheap.

More monitoring because analysis is cheap.

More meetings summarized, more competitors tracked, more ideas scored, more dashboards generated, more documents revised, more scenarios modeled.

The result can be a company that has solved the cost of thinking and created an epidemic of things to think about.

This is not hypothetical. Organizations already suffer from metric proliferation, dashboard overload, meeting accumulation, and communication volume. AI can relieve those burdens by filtering and summarizing. It can also make the upstream production of information so effortless that the total burden grows.

A manager who once received ten weekly reports may receive one synthesized report from an agent. Good.

If the organization then creates a hundred new automated analyses because “the agent can handle it,” the manager may eventually receive a synthesized report about a synthetic bureaucracy.

The technology did not remove the organizational problem.

It hid it one level down.

Agency requires subtraction.

This is one reason people with strong taste, judgment, and priorities may capture disproportionate value from capable systems. They know what not to ask for.

Taste is sometimes treated as a decorative quality, relevant to design, writing, or culture. In an abundant-output economy, taste becomes operational. It is a compression function. It reduces a large possibility space to a smaller set worth pursuing.

Judgment performs a similar role under uncertainty. It converts analysis into commitment.

Priority converts an endless queue of potentially useful work into a sequence.

Authority converts recommendation into action.

Accountability converts action into ownership of consequence.

These are all forms of agency.

None becomes cheap merely because a model becomes more articulate.

Some may become more valuable precisely because the model becomes more articulate.

Consider expertise.

One early story about generative AI was that it would flatten expertise because novices could receive expert-like output. Some evidence supports part of that story. Lower-performing workers often receive larger gains from AI assistance because the system can transmit patterns that experienced workers already possess.

But agentic work produces another possibility.

Expertise can become more leveraged rather than less valuable.

Anthropic’s 2026 analysis of agentic coding sessions found that people with more domain expertise tended to get more work from the system per instruction and had somewhat higher success rates. This should not be surprising. A person who understands the domain can delegate at a higher level because the person knows which parts are safe to leave implicit, which constraints deserve explicit statement, and which outputs require inspection.

A novice often compensates for lack of domain knowledge with more interaction.

An expert can compress.

That is what good management has always looked like.

A senior surgeon can give an operating-room instruction that carries enormous context because the team shares training, vocabulary, protocols, and expectations. A construction supervisor can notice from one sentence that a subcontractor has misunderstood the sequence. A great editor can mark three paragraphs and change the trajectory of a book. Expertise allows sparse signals to govern complex action.

Agents turn that compression into a new kind of leverage.

The person who knows what matters can spend fewer tokens describing the obvious and more attention on the exception.

This suggests that the economic divide of the agentic era may not be simply between people who use AI and people who do not.

It may be between people who can convert capability into controlled consequence and people who can only convert it into output.

Output is easy to display.

Consequence is harder to measure.

A person can demonstrate that an agent wrote fifty pages. It is less obvious whether the fifty pages improved a decision. A development team can show that coding activity doubled. It is harder to know whether releases accelerated after review, testing, security, integration, and deployment bottlenecks are included. A sales team can show that personalized outreach volume rose. It is harder to know whether trust survived the increase.

The distinction becomes crucial as organizations try to calculate return on AI investment.

The shallow calculation asks how many labor hours the tool appears to save.

The deeper calculation asks what new decisions, experiments, products, relationships, and operating capacity the saved labor made possible.

Then it asks what new verification and coordination costs appeared downstream.

This is why the language of “ten times more productive” is often less informative than it sounds. Ten times more of what?

Ten times more code can be useful if code was the binding constraint.

If review is the binding constraint, ten times more code can be a denial-of-service attack on the reviewers.

Ten times more leads can be valuable if sales capacity was limited by lead generation.

If customer trust and sales follow-up are limited, the additional leads may be noise.

Ten times more research can be valuable if the missing insight was buried in a large evidence base.

If the decision depends on one unresolved factual uncertainty, more prose is not more leverage.

The bottleneck defines the value of abundance.

Agentic leverage therefore begins with locating scarcity correctly.

Where does the system still have to wait for a human?

Where does the human still have to wait for the system?

Which step is expensive because it requires judgment rather than production?

Which step is expensive only because nobody has automated the clerical part?

What increases consequence if it becomes faster?

What merely increases volume?

Where does one more possible action improve the choice?

Where does it make the choice harder?

These questions are less glamorous than demonstrations of autonomous agents. They are also where economic value lives.

The principal mistake is to assume that the cheapest new resource should be maximized.

When storage became cheap, good systems did not respond by storing every possible representation of everything forever without design. When bandwidth became cheap, good organizations did not conclude that every employee should be copied on every message. When compute became cheap, good software did not abandon efficiency entirely.

Abundance removes one reason for discipline.

It does not remove the need for discipline.

The agentic economy will create the same temptation. If analysis is almost free, analyze everything. If action is almost free, automate everything. If agents can operate in parallel, launch a thousand of them.

The mature response will be different.

Use intelligence where intelligence expands agency.

Use agents where delegation expands consequence without destroying control.

Do not confuse the number of machine actions with the amount of human leverage.

A person surrounded by agents can still be powerless if every system is working on objectives chosen by somebody else.

A company can automate every internal process and still have no strategic agency if it cannot change direction without breaking those processes.

A society can deploy intelligent systems everywhere and still reduce individual agency if the systems become opaque gates between people and essential institutions.

Agency is not a synonym for activity.

It is the capacity to choose and carry choices into the world.

The economic promise of artificial intelligence is not that machines will become the only agents that matter.

It is that human intention can travel farther.

That promise becomes real only when intention remains attached to the consequence.
