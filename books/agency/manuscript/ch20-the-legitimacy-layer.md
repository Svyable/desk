# The Legitimacy Layer

A system can be correct and still lack the right to decide.

This distinction is easy to ignore in technology because engineering culture often treats better decisions as the obvious goal. If a model predicts more accurately, routes more efficiently, or allocates resources better, why preserve a slower human process?

Sometimes there is no good reason.

Sometimes the process exists because decisions are not merely optimization problems.

They are exercises of authority.

Authority needs legitimacy.

Legitimacy is the recognized right to make a consequential choice and to be held responsible for it.

The word usually appears in politics, where the distinction is obvious. A policy can be economically efficient and politically illegitimate if imposed by an actor without lawful authority. A court decision is not legitimate merely because a smart person agrees with it. Procedure, jurisdiction, appeal, transparency, and institutional role matter because society cares not only about the answer but about who had the right to produce the answer.

Agentic systems bring this issue into ordinary economic life.

Who has the right to deny the claim?

Who has the right to terminate the account?

Who has the right to rank the employee?

Who has the right to refuse the loan?

Who has the right to publish the accusation?

Who has the right to negotiate on behalf of the company?

Who has the right to decide that a student cheated, a driver is unsafe, a customer is fraudulent, or a citizen is ineligible?

A machine may assist every one of these decisions.

The fact that it can make the decision reliably does not settle whether it should carry the authority.

This is the legitimacy layer.

The legitimacy layer sits above capability and verification.

Capability asks whether the system can do the work.

Verification asks whether we have sufficient evidence that it did the work well.

Legitimacy asks whether the institution has the right to let this system’s output become consequence in this way.

The three often align.

A fraud agent blocks an obviously stolen card transaction. The action is within policy, highly reversible, observable, and socially accepted. The legitimacy problem is small.

Other cases are different.

A company uses an opaque agent to score employees for layoffs. Even if the system predicts future performance better than managers, employees may reasonably demand to know which criteria govern a decision that changes their livelihood, whether prohibited or inappropriate factors influenced it, and who can hear an appeal.

The quality of the prediction does not erase the institutional obligation.

Legitimacy often requires procedure.

Procedure can look inefficient because its value appears only when people disagree with the outcome.

Appeal rights.

Notice.

A reason for the decision.

A chance to correct the record.

Separation between investigator and adjudicator.

A human official willing to sign.

Records showing what rule was applied.

These mechanisms create friction.

They also turn raw power into accountable authority.

Agentic leverage creates pressure to remove them because the machine can act faster than the institution can process dissent.

That pressure should be resisted selectively, not reflexively.

Some procedures are obsolete artifacts of manual systems. A form may exist only because three databases historically could not talk to one another. A weekly committee may exist only because information took a week to assemble. A signature may be ceremonial when a stronger digital control is available.

Other procedures encode values.

They create time for objection.

They separate roles.

They make reasons visible.

They ensure that a person can confront the evidence used against them.

An agentic redesign should know which kind of friction it is deleting.

This is a recurring problem in government because administration sits at the intersection of scale and rights.

Public institutions process enormous numbers of cases: taxes, permits, benefits, licenses, inspections, immigration decisions, procurement, enforcement, and public records. Agents can reduce administrative burden dramatically. They can help citizens understand requirements, assemble evidence, track deadlines, identify missing documents, and route cases.

The same systems can also make government action more difficult to contest if the decision path becomes opaque.

A citizen should not have to reverse-engineer a model to understand why a benefit was denied.

Legitimacy requires a reason at the level the citizen can challenge.

That does not mean exposing every internal model weight or chain of reasoning. Explanation must be meaningful, not theatrical.

Which rule applied?

Which fact triggered it?

Which source supplied the fact?

How can the fact be corrected?

Who can reverse the decision?

These questions can often be answered by system design without pretending that the model itself has a human-like explanation of its cognition.

The legitimacy layer therefore favors structured decision records.

The agent may use sophisticated reasoning internally. The institution should preserve the externally relevant basis of the action.

This matters in private institutions too.

Platforms already exercise quasi-governmental authority over participants. They suspend accounts, rank visibility, decide refunds, adjudicate disputes, and enforce rules. Agents can scale these functions further.

A merchant whose livelihood depends on a marketplace does not experience account suspension as a casual software feature. It is an exercise of private power.

If the suspension is automated, the marketplace still owns the legitimacy problem.

Is the rule clear?

Is the evidence accurate?

Can the merchant appeal?

Can a person correct an obvious machine error?

Does the system distinguish suspicious behavior from behavior merely outside the dominant pattern?

The agent may reduce the cost of enforcement.

That can tempt the institution to enforce more.

Cheap action expands the feasible surface of control.

This is another reason agency must be examined distributively.

The principal’s leverage may be someone else’s loss of agency.

A landlord’s agent can optimize tenant screening.

A tenant may experience the system as an opaque gate.

An employer’s agent can monitor productivity continuously.

An employee may experience the system as permanent surveillance.

A lender’s agent can adjust pricing and limits dynamically.

A borrower may lose the ability to understand which behavior changes the terms.

A government’s agent can detect noncompliance at scale.

Citizens may face more enforcement without a corresponding increase in access to advice, correction, and appeal.

The same technology can strengthen principal agency and weaken subject agency.

Legitimacy is where the two claims meet.

A powerful institution needs constraints not because constraint is intrinsically virtuous but because the people affected by its decisions have agency too.

This becomes especially important as agents become persuasive.

An agent can do more than decide. It can explain, negotiate, nudge, personalize, and adapt communication based on what it knows about the recipient.

That capability can improve service.

A benefits agent can explain a confusing process in the citizen’s language. A health system can tailor reminders to the patient’s needs. A financial service can help a user understand a tradeoff.

The same capability can manipulate.

A debt collector can discover which phrasing makes a vulnerable person most likely to pay immediately.

A platform can personalize retention messages around the user’s emotional weak points.

A political actor can adapt persuasion to individuals at scale.

A sales agent can identify which objection is easiest to bypass rather than which product is actually suitable.

The legitimacy layer therefore includes method, not only outcome.

Some ways of obtaining consent undermine the meaning of consent.

A person agreeing after transparent explanation is different from a person behaviorally optimized into agreement by a system that knows far more about the person than the person knows about the system.

This is not a new ethical problem. Advertising, sales, politics, and interface design have always influenced behavior.

Agents make influence adaptive and cheap.

The institution has to decide which powers it refuses to exercise even when the metric improves.

That is constitutional restraint.

Companies rarely use that phrase because it sounds grand. The underlying act is ordinary.

We could do this.

We choose not to.

We could use this customer data to optimize conversion.

We do not believe that use is consistent with the relationship.

We could remove human appeal because error is rare.

We keep appeal because the cost of an uncorrectable error falls on the person, not the average.

We could let the agent negotiate this clause automatically.

We require human authorization because the clause allocates legal risk the company intends someone to own consciously.

We could monitor every employee action.

We choose a less intrusive measure because dignity is part of the employment relationship.

Agency is not demonstrated by using every available power.

Sometimes it is demonstrated by refusing power.

The legitimacy layer also matters inside organizations because managers will increasingly receive agentic recommendations about people.

Who should be promoted?

Who is overloaded?

Who is underperforming?

Which team is likely to miss a target?

Which employee might leave?

These systems can be helpful. They can notice patterns managers miss and reduce arbitrary human judgment.

They can also create a new kind of false objectivity.

A score looks neutral because it came from data.

The data reflects choices about what was measured, what was visible, and what counted as performance. People who perform relational, preventative, or invisible work may appear weaker because the system sees outputs more easily than contributions.

The model can improve the prediction and still inherit the ontology.

Legitimacy requires contestability.

The person affected should sometimes be able to say: the system is measuring the wrong thing.

That is different from saying the prediction is statistically inaccurate.

A manager needs the authority to recognize that the metric does not fully define the job.

This is another place where human judgment remains valuable not because humans are always less biased, but because institutions need a location where the definition of success can be challenged.

Agents operate inside objective functions.

Legitimacy includes the right to dispute the objective function.

The layer becomes more complicated when agents act on behalf of multiple principals.

A workplace assistant serves the employee and the employer.

A healthcare agent may serve the patient, clinician, insurer, and health system under different obligations.

A marketplace agent may serve buyers while the platform earns money from sellers.

A financial agent may advise the customer while operating inside a company with products to sell.

Conflicts of interest have always existed. Agentic interfaces can make them less visible because the user experiences one apparently unified helper.

Who is the agent actually agent for?

The question should become a standard part of product design.

Whose objective is primary?

Which conflicts must be disclosed?

Which actions require explicit consent because another principal benefits?

Can the user inspect whether recommendations are sponsored or constrained?

Can the agent refuse an employer instruction that would violate a duty to the user?

These are not merely trust-and-safety questions.

They define the principal relationship.

A system that appears to be your agent while secretly optimizing another party’s objective is not simply a bad assistant.

It is a corrupted agency relationship.

The legitimacy layer therefore sets a boundary around the entire book.

Maximizing agentic leverage cannot mean maximizing one actor’s capacity to cause outcomes regardless of who bears them.

That would be power, not agency in a society of other agents.

Useful agency has to coexist.

A company should be able to act efficiently while customers retain meaningful recourse.

A manager should gain leverage while employees retain dignity and contestability.

A government should reduce administrative friction while citizens retain rights.

A person should gain negotiating power without turning every human interaction into automated strategic manipulation.

The difficult work of the new economy will be building systems that scale consequence without erasing the conditions under which consequence is accepted as legitimate.

This is not a technical finish line.

Legitimacy is maintained socially over time.

Institutions earn it through consistency, restraint, explanation, correction, and responsibility.

Agents can help perform those functions.

They cannot absolve the institution that deploys them.

The machine can make the decision.

The principal still has to deserve the right to let it count.
