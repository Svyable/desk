# Work Learned to Act

The easiest way to miss a technological change is to describe it with the vocabulary of the thing it is replacing.

Early automobiles were horseless carriages. Radio was wireless telegraphy. The web was an information superhighway, a phrase that made the new medium sound like a faster road for documents because documents were what people already knew how to imagine moving. Smartphones were phones long after calling became one of the least interesting things they did.

Artificial intelligence entered ordinary work as an answer machine.

Ask a question. Receive text.

The interface encouraged the analogy. A blank box waited for language. The user typed. The system replied. Even when the answer was astonishing, the basic interaction resembled search, messaging, or a very fast conversation with a colleague who happened to know a strange amount about everything.

Then the answer acquired tools.

A coding model could inspect a repository instead of waiting for code to be pasted into the chat. A research system could search. A workplace assistant could read the files a user had permission to read. A model could call software, run commands, manipulate a browser, update records, and continue after the first response. The interaction changed from question and answer to objective and attempt.

That change is larger than it first appears.

A question has no consequence until somebody acts on the answer.

An agent can cross that gap.

The history of work is full of technologies that crossed other gaps. Steam crossed the gap between muscle and mechanical force. Telegraphy crossed the gap between distance and communication. Mass production crossed the gap between craft and repeatability. Computing crossed the gap between rules and speed. Networks crossed the gap between isolated computers and coordinated systems. Software-as-a-service crossed the gap between owning a program and accessing a continuously updated capability.

Agentic software crosses the gap between instruction and execution.

That is why the useful comparison is not with a better search engine.

It is with delegation.

Delegation is one of civilization’s oldest technologies. A ruler cannot collect every tax. A merchant cannot personally captain every ship. A factory owner cannot stand beside every machine. A chief executive cannot negotiate every contract or hire every employee. A family cannot grow all its food, repair every appliance, teach every subject, and perform every medical procedure. Human beings expand their reach by asking other human beings and institutions to act on their behalf.

The difficulty is that delegation has always been expensive.

It takes time to find the right person. The person needs context. The job may require training, salary, supervision, incentives, trust, contracts, coordination, and some way to know whether the work was done properly. The delegate has a schedule, a personality, a career, competing demands, private information, and interests that may not perfectly match the principal’s.

Organizations exist partly to solve this problem repeatedly.

They are machines for durable delegation.

A company assembles people, processes, capital, information, and authority so that one objective can be pursued through many actions without renegotiating every transaction from scratch. It is imperfect. The org chart is full of lag. A request crosses time zones. A meeting exists because an email was insufficient. A manager waits for an analyst who waits for data from another team. Somebody has the password. Somebody else is on vacation. The right person is not in the room. The person in the room does not have authority. The person with authority has not read the document.

We tolerate this friction because organized human capability is enormously valuable.

Now some portion of that capability can be invoked as software.

The economic importance is not that the software has no cost. It has compute costs, licensing costs, integration costs, security costs, review costs, and failure costs. Nor is the importance that the software never sleeps. Machines have been running around the clock for a long time.

The unusual property is that the system can accept a goal stated at a relatively high level and produce part of the path itself.

A spreadsheet does what its formulas say. A traditional workflow follows branches somebody specified in advance. An agent can encounter a situation that was not exhaustively enumerated and decide what intermediate step should happen next.

That sentence contains both the opportunity and the danger.

If every intermediate step had to be known in advance, there would be no need for agency. We would simply automate the procedure.

Agency becomes useful where the path depends on context.

Investigate why refunds rose last week.

Do not merely run one query. Decide which data matters. Compare time periods. Inspect categories. Notice that one product is different. Look at support tickets. Test whether the spike is geographic. Check whether a recent release aligns with the timing. Prepare an explanation. Flag what is known and what is merely plausible.

Fix the failing test suite.

Do not merely change one line. Read the errors. Find the relevant code. Infer intent from neighboring tests. Make a change. Run the suite. Notice a new failure. Revise. Stop if the fix requires a breaking public interface.

Find three viable suppliers.

Do not merely search a directory. Read the specification. Interpret which requirements are hard constraints. Collect evidence. Exclude the supplier whose certification is expired. Compare lead times. Explain the uncertainty. Draft the outreach, but wait for approval before sending.

These tasks were already delegable to humans because humans can navigate the spaces between explicit instructions.

That space is where agents are entering.

By 2026, the change was visible even though broad enterprise deployment remained early. Stanford’s AI Index reported that AI use of some kind had become common across surveyed organizations, while scaled use of AI agents was still in the single digits in nearly every business function. This is an important combination. The base technology is no longer exotic. The operating model for delegated action is not yet settled.

That is exactly when economic doctrine matters.

Once a technology is mature, its conventions become invisible. Nobody needs a philosophy of whether the accounting system should add numbers correctly. The organization has standards, audits, permissions, vendors, procedures, and professional expectations built around the assumption that accounting software exists.

Agentic systems are not there yet.

We are still deciding what counts as a job for an agent.

We are still deciding whether a model should ask permission before sending a message, changing a record, committing code, making a purchase, moving money, or publishing an artifact. We are still learning which logs are useful and which merely create the feeling of traceability. We are still discovering where human approval improves safety and where it becomes a rubber stamp. We are still confusing impressive benchmark performance with dependable work in messy environments. We are still learning how much context to provide without turning every task into a data-governance problem.

And the systems are improving while those conventions are being invented.

METR’s work on task-completion time horizons offers one way to see the technical movement. Rather than asking whether a model can solve a particular benchmark question, the researchers estimate how long a comparable task would take a skilled human and then measure the duration at which an AI agent has a given chance of success. The exact numbers move as models and evaluations change. The durable observation is that the frontier is being expressed in time: systems are becoming able to sustain successful action across longer sequences of work.

Time matters because work is made of dependency.

A one-minute task rarely requires much recovery. A two-hour task does. The longer the task, the more chances there are for stale information, ambiguous instructions, tool errors, unexpected states, hidden assumptions, permission failures, and local choices whose consequences appear later. The ability to remain useful over time is not simply more intelligence. It is more agency.

This is why coding became such an early proving ground.

Code has unusual properties. The environment is structured. Actions leave artifacts. Tests can provide feedback. Repositories preserve history. Failure is often recoverable. A model can inspect, change, execute, and iterate inside a relatively legible world. The task has genuine complexity without requiring the system to manipulate physical objects or understand every nuance of human relationships.

By mid-2026, Anthropic reported on hundreds of thousands of Claude Code sessions and found a pattern that deserves attention far beyond programming: people tended to make more of the planning decisions, while the system made more of the execution decisions. Expertise did not disappear. More expert users were often able to extract more work per instruction.

That is an early picture of the agentic economy.

Human intention moves upward.

Machine execution expands downward.

The boundary between them becomes one of the most valuable design decisions in the workflow.

This does not mean planning is permanently human and execution permanently machine. Agents can plan. Humans will still execute. The useful point is that a new division of cognitive labor is possible. The person no longer has to specify every keystroke to preserve ownership of the work.

That distinction will be difficult for institutions built around visible effort.

For a long time, many professional jobs used production as a proxy for judgment. The analyst who built the model was assumed to understand the model. The lawyer who drafted the contract was expected to notice the clause. The programmer who wrote the code had at least encountered the decisions embedded in it. The manager who prepared the plan had wrestled with the tradeoffs.

This relationship was never perfect.

People copy formulas. Lawyers use templates. Programmers inherit code. Managers delegate decks. But the cost of producing an artifact created incidental exposure to the thinking inside it.

Agents weaken that link.

A person can receive a finished artifact without having traveled through the intermediate decisions that produced it.

This is not necessarily bad.

Nobody insists that an architect quarry the stone in order to understand a building. Specialization is useful precisely because understanding can be layered. The problem is knowing which layer the person must still understand in order to be responsible.

The agentic economy will force that question into every profession.

If a financial analyst does not write the spreadsheet formula, what must she know to sign the recommendation?

If a programmer does not type the implementation, what must he know to approve the pull request?

If a doctor uses an agent to prepare a differential diagnosis, what must remain personally examined before treatment?

If a teacher uses an agent to generate feedback for every student, what must the teacher still see in order to know the class?

If a founder uses agents to conduct research, draft outreach, build prototypes, analyze users, and prepare investor updates, what work remains irreducibly the founder’s?

The answer will not be “everything.”

It cannot be.

The point of delegation is to stop personally performing everything.

The answer will also not be “nothing.”

A principal who retains no meaningful knowledge of the objective, constraints, evidence, or consequence is not using leverage. The principal has disappeared.

This gives us the first important difference between automation and agency.

Automation removes a task from the human’s active workflow by encoding how it should happen.

Agency removes part of the path while leaving the objective live.

A payroll calculation is automation. “Resolve the discrepancies in this month’s payroll and bring me the cases where policy is unclear” is agency.

A mail merge is automation. “Identify the customers most likely to benefit from this change, prepare individualized explanations, and do not contact anyone whose account has an unresolved complaint” is agency.

A nightly backup is automation. “Investigate why last night’s backup failed, restore redundancy if the standard path is safe, and escalate before deleting or overwriting anything” is agency.

Once work is framed this way, the bottleneck stops being the number of steps a person can personally perform.

The bottleneck becomes the number of objectives a person can meaningfully govern.

That is a very different constraint.

It is tempting to hear this and imagine a future manager staring at a wall of hundreds of digital workers. That image is probably too literal. Good interfaces compress complexity. A chief executive does not hold a daily one-on-one with every employee in a ten-thousand-person company. Hierarchies, teams, metrics, procedures, and delegated authority exist because direct supervision does not scale.

Agentic systems will develop equivalent layers.

One agent will call another. A system will route tasks to specialized models. A controller will watch budgets. A verifier will test outputs. A human will see exceptions rather than steps. Much of the orchestration will be software talking to software.

The organizational question becomes where human attention enters.

This is more important than whether humans are “in the loop,” a phrase so broad that it can describe everything from active collaboration to ceremonial approval.

A human can be in the loop and have no agency at all.

Consider the employee who is shown a hundred machine decisions an hour and must click approve unless something looks obviously strange. The system technically contains human review. Economically, the human is serving as a liability sponge. There is too much volume to inspect meaningfully, too little context to reconstruct the decision, and too much pressure to keep the queue moving.

The interface has preserved a human hand while removing human control.

The opposite can also happen. A system may execute thousands of low-level actions without asking permission, while a person retains real control because the objective is clear, the permissions are bounded, the consequences are reversible, the work is observable, and exceptions are surfaced intelligently.

Agency is not measured by the number of clicks reserved for a person.

It is measured by the person’s ability to determine and redirect consequential action.

This is why the new economy will reward people who learn to design delegation rather than merely consume answers.

An answer is finished when it is read.

Delegation creates an ongoing relationship between intention and execution.

The better the systems become, the more economically important this relationship becomes. When the agent is mediocre, the user naturally checks everything. When the agent is excellent, trust rises. More work moves onto the system. The cost of an unnoticed exception rises with the depth of dependency.

Competence therefore does not remove governance.

Competence makes governance scalable enough to matter.

This is an old organizational lesson. A company does not create internal controls because every employee is incompetent. It creates them because competent people exercising authority at scale can create consequences that no single person can casually reconstruct. The more the organization can do, the more it needs ways to understand what it has done.

Agents bring that logic into software.

The most consequential change is not that software has become conversational.

It is not even that software has become intelligent, although that is the technical precondition.

The change is that work is becoming addressable at the level of intention.

Tell the system what should be true.

Let it attempt the middle.

That is an extraordinary form of leverage.

It is also a transfer of authority.

The rest of this book is about learning to tell the difference.
