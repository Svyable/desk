# Judgment Becomes Production

A strange thing happens when the cost of making falls.

Choosing starts to feel like making.

For most of modern professional life, judgment sat downstream of production. Someone gathered the facts, built the spreadsheet, drafted the document, wrote the code, prepared the options, or performed the analysis. Then a more senior person reviewed the work and made a decision.

The sequence reflected economics.

Production consumed many hours. Judgment consumed fewer.

The organization therefore treated judgment as the scarce final step and production as the visible bulk of the work.

Artificial intelligence changes the ratio.

When an agent can generate ten reasonable options in the time it once took a person to prepare two, the production stage shrinks. The choosing stage does not automatically shrink with it.

Sometimes it grows.

More possibilities create more comparison. More analysis creates more claims that might need verification. More code creates more review. More experiments create more interpretation. More drafts create more editorial responsibility.

The center of gravity moves.

Judgment becomes production.

That sentence is easy to misunderstand.

It does not mean everybody becomes a philosopher staring thoughtfully at machine output. Judgment is work only when it changes what happens.

A person exercises judgment by defining what counts as good, noticing what is wrong, ranking alternatives, identifying the missing variable, accepting a tradeoff, deciding that enough evidence exists, stopping the iteration, and taking responsibility for the choice.

These actions produce the final shape of the work.

They are not decorative review.

The distinction is clearest in creative fields.

A photographer can take thousands of images on a digital camera. The craft does not disappear because exposure is cheap. Part of the craft moves into selection. Which frame carries the story? Which image is technically perfect and emotionally dead? Which near-duplicate has the gesture that matters? What should be excluded so the sequence has rhythm?

The photographer who cannot choose is not more productive because the camera captured more files.

The same will become true across knowledge work.

A writer with an agent can generate pages at a rate that would once have looked supernatural.

The bottleneck becomes whether the writer can hear what is false, generic, over-polished, repetitive, unearned, or simply beside the point.

A designer can produce variations without end.

The bottleneck becomes taste and understanding of the user.

A strategist can model scenarios until the decision disappears under possibility.

The bottleneck becomes knowing which uncertainty actually matters.

An engineer can ask agents to propose architectures and implement prototypes in parallel.

The bottleneck becomes system judgment: maintainability, failure modes, interfaces, security, hidden coupling, and whether the product should carry the complexity at all.

As output becomes abundant, judgment stops being a final approval function.

It becomes the production function that makes abundance usable.

This is why expert performance may become more valuable in some domains even as entry-level production becomes easier.

Expertise contains compressed standards.

An experienced professional has seen enough cases to know what usually matters and, more importantly, what looks ordinary until it suddenly does not.

This is not mystical intuition.

It is accumulated discrimination.

A radiologist notices a pattern because thousands of images have trained attention. A lawyer recognizes that a clause that sounds standard changes liability in the context of the rest of the agreement. An engineer sees that a “simple” shortcut creates a future migration problem. An editor feels that the argument has reached its conclusion three pages before the author does.

These judgments can often be explained after the fact.

They are difficult to generate from first principles every time.

Agentic systems can capture some of this expertise. That is one of their economic strengths. A model trained or conditioned on high-quality examples can help less experienced workers perform closer to established patterns. Research on customer-support assistants has shown particularly large gains among less experienced workers, suggesting that AI can transmit behaviors that previously diffused through tenure and coaching.

But the same evidence contains a warning for how organizations redesign work.

If junior people stop performing the tasks through which experts were formed, where does future judgment come from?

This is not an argument for preserving obsolete busywork.

It is a question of production.

Many professions create experts through repeated exposure to intermediate decisions. The junior lawyer reads the document because someone has to read it. The junior analyst builds the model because someone has to build it. The junior programmer debugs because the bug exists. The resident examines the patient because medicine has to be practiced under supervision.

The output of the work is only one product.

The worker is another.

Automation can remove the task and accidentally remove the training loop.

This is the apprenticeship problem, which will return later in the book. For now, the important point is that judgment has a cost structure.

It has to be built.

The cheapness of machine production does not make human standards free.

In fact, machine abundance can increase the number of standards a person needs.

Before generative AI, a manager reviewing a ten-page report knew that another person had at least passed through the material. The report could still contain errors, but production imposed some friction. With agents, the report may contain ten times more synthesized information from ten times more sources without any individual human having inspected the full evidence base.

The manager needs stronger questions.

Where did this claim come from?

What evidence would change the conclusion?

Which source is doing the most work?

What uncertainty was compressed into the summary?

Where did the agent disagree with itself or with another source?

What part of the analysis cannot be independently verified cheaply?

These are not “AI questions.”

They are judgment questions whose importance rises because production expanded.

The same applies to code.

A pull request written by an agent can pass tests and still be wrong in ways the tests do not encode. In 2026, METR reported that roughly half of test-passing pull requests produced by recent agents on SWE-bench Verified tasks would not have been merged by repository maintainers in their evaluation. The lesson is not that tests are useless. The lesson is that passing the test is not identical to satisfying the maintainers’ definition of acceptable work.

The gap between formal success and human acceptance is judgment.

Every mature profession contains such gaps.

A contract can be legally valid and commercially foolish.

A marketing claim can be technically supportable and reputationally reckless.

A financial model can be mathematically correct and economically naive.

A hiring process can be procedurally consistent and still select for the wrong thing.

A medical recommendation can follow a guideline while missing the patient’s actual circumstance.

Judgment lives where the specification ends.

Agents make this boundary more visible because they are good at satisfying specifications.

The better they become, the more organizations will discover that many of their quality standards were never actually specified.

That discovery can be uncomfortable.

A manager says, “This isn’t quite right.”

The agent asks, effectively, “What does right mean?”

Humans have always asked that question too, but often less explicitly. They watched examples, absorbed culture, copied senior work, and learned through correction.

Agentic systems force some of the tacit standard into artifacts.

Examples become more valuable.

Rubrics become more valuable.

Tests become more valuable.

Decision records become more valuable.

Counterexamples become more valuable.

A collection of rejected work can become as useful as a collection of accepted work because it teaches the boundary of taste.

This does not mean every organization should turn its judgment into thousands of rules.

Over-specification can destroy the very discretion it is trying to preserve.

The goal is to encode what is stable and keep humans close to what is contextual.

That split changes as agents improve.

A standard that once required a human may become testable. A judgment that once depended on tacit experience may become partly representable in examples. A human review step that once examined every case may become an escalation point for unusual cases.

The important thing is to know why the human remains.

Too many workflows keep a person in the loop for ceremonial reasons.

The person is told to “review” the output without being given the time, evidence, authority, or expertise to challenge it. The organization gains the appearance of control and the cost of human labor without the substance of judgment.

Real review has a purpose.

The reviewer is looking for something the upstream system does not reliably catch.

The reviewer can stop the process.

The reviewer has enough context to recognize failure.

The reviewer sees evidence at a resolution appropriate to the consequence.

If none of those conditions is true, the review step is likely a ritual.

Ritual review is dangerous because it diffuses accountability. The machine produced the work. The human clicked approve. Everybody can later point to the other side.

Judgment requires ownership.

Ownership does not mean the reviewer personally generated the work.

It means the reviewer knows what standard he is applying and accepts responsibility for applying it.

This creates a new premium on what might be called *decision literacy*.

Decision literacy is different from information literacy.

Information literacy asks whether a source is credible, a claim is supported, a chart is misleading, or a statistic is appropriate.

Decision literacy asks what consequence follows if the information is accepted.

Which uncertainty is tolerable?

Which error is reversible?

What would be costly to learn only after acting?

What can be tested cheaply first?

What does the decision commit us to?

What future option disappears when we choose this path?

Agents can assist with these questions too.

They can generate premortems, search for counterevidence, model scenarios, and identify assumptions. The point is not to preserve a sacred human monopoly over judgment.

The point is to preserve a location where judgment is actually exercised.

A principal can use one agent to challenge another. A company can build automated evaluation systems. A model can score outputs against policy. A verifier can compare claims with sources. These mechanisms are forms of machine judgment.

They do not remove the principal’s problem.

They raise it one level.

Who chose the evaluator?

What does the test reward?

What kinds of failure does the policy recognize?

What happens when the generator and verifier share the same blind spot?

Which disagreements reach a person?

How much false reassurance is created by an automated green check?

At some level, an institution still chooses what evidence is enough.

That choice is governance.

This is where the economics becomes interesting.

If production costs fall faster than verification costs, the relative price of judgment rises.

A company may spend less on first drafts and more on evaluation.

A software team may need fewer hours of implementation and more investment in tests, observability, and architecture.

A research organization may spend less on initial synthesis and more on source integrity and experimental design.

A media company may produce more material but need stronger editorial systems to avoid becoming a fluent misinformation engine.

An individual may spend less time creating options and more time deciding what deserves commitment.

This is not a loss of productivity.

It is the new location of productivity.

The mistake would be to treat judgment as overhead because it does not create visible volume.

In an abundant-output economy, judgment is the scarce step that turns volume into value.

There is a financial analogy.

Capital allocators do not create every business they fund. Their work is selection under uncertainty. A bad allocator can deploy enormous resources into poor opportunities. A great allocator creates value through what receives capital, how much, under what conditions, and when the position changes.

Agentic principals become allocators of intelligence and execution.

They decide which objectives receive machine effort.

They decide how much autonomy the system receives.

They decide what evidence must come back.

They decide which results become commitments.

This makes judgment a form of capital allocation for cognitive work.

The more capable the agents, the more expensive bad allocation becomes.

A weak agent wastes a little time.

A strong agent can efficiently pursue a bad objective across a large surface area.

Leverage raises the cost of poor judgment.

It also raises the return to good judgment.

The principal who can identify a valuable problem, define it cleanly, set useful constraints, and recognize a good answer can command far more execution than before.

This is one reason the agentic economy may reward people who are unusually good at saying no.

No, this output is not evidence.

No, the metric does not capture the objective.

No, the extra feature does not justify the complexity.

No, this apparent consensus comes from the same source repeated five ways.

No, we do not need another scenario.

No, the agent should not have permission to send this yet.

No, the problem is not ready to be delegated because we have not decided what success means.

Refusal is not anti-technology.

It is one of the productive acts that keeps leverage attached to intent.

The opposite is also true.

Good judgment knows when to stop reviewing.

Perfection can become another form of lost agency. If every low-consequence action requires exhaustive human validation, the principal remains trapped in execution. The system has capability but no leverage.

Judgment therefore includes proportionality.

A reversible draft deserves less scrutiny than a published accusation.

A sandboxed code experiment deserves less scrutiny than a database migration.

A ten-dollar purchase deserves less scrutiny than a ten-million-dollar transfer.

A brainstorming claim deserves less sourcing than a claim placed in a legal filing.

The standard follows the consequence.

This is how judgment scales.

It does not inspect everything equally.

It decides where attention belongs.

Herbert Simon’s scarcity of attention becomes even more acute in the agentic economy because machines can create more candidate objects for attention than any person can consume.

The solution is not to become faster at looking.

The solution is to become better at deciding what must be looked at.

That is judgment as production.

The machine can make more.

The principal has to make it matter.
