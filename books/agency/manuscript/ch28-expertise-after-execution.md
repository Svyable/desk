# Expertise After Execution

The most interesting early evidence about agentic work may be a result that sounds contradictory.

Coding agents can make formal coding background less necessary for successful programming.

And domain expertise can still make people better at using coding agents.

Anthropic’s June 2026 analysis of roughly 400,000 Claude Code sessions found both patterns at once. People from many occupations could successfully use the coding agent. In typical sessions, humans made more of the planning decisions while Claude made more of the execution decisions. People with more domain expertise tended to get more work from the agent per instruction, and expert-rated sessions were more likely to reach verified success than novice-rated sessions.

This is a picture of expertise after execution becomes cheap.

The old story of expertise was partly a story of production.

The expert could do what the novice could not.

Write the code.

Build the model.

Draft the agreement.

Interpret the evidence.

Diagnose the system.

Produce the artifact.

Agents weaken that monopoly.

A novice can increasingly receive competent production without possessing all the skill historically required to create it manually.

The immediate conclusion is that expertise becomes less valuable.

The field evidence suggests a more interesting transition.

Expertise moves into steering.

The expert knows what to ask the system to preserve.

The expert recognizes when the task is unusual.

The expert can give a sparse instruction because the expert understands which details are safely implied and which cannot be left to inference.

The expert can inspect a strange result without being seduced by surface polish.

The expert knows when an apparent failure is harmless and when an apparently successful output violates the deeper standard.

The machine performs more execution.

The expert’s mental model governs a larger surface.

This is the agency premium in practice.

Consider a scientist using a coding agent.

The scientist may not have formal software-engineering training. The agent can write analysis code, manipulate data, generate visualizations, and build small tools. The scientist’s domain expertise lets her define the scientific question, understand the measurement, recognize impossible outputs, and know which statistical choice changes the interpretation.

A programmer without the science may write better code manually and produce worse science.

The agent lowers the cost of crossing the technical boundary.

Domain knowledge becomes the fulcrum.

The same pattern can appear in finance.

An experienced investor may use an agent to construct screens, process filings, write data pipelines, and simulate scenarios without being the strongest programmer. The agent expands technical reach. The investor’s advantage remains knowing which assumptions are economically meaningful, which reported number is misleading, and what evidence should change conviction.

In law, a domain expert may use agents to compare documents, trace authority, and produce structured drafts at a scale previously requiring junior teams. The lawyer’s value moves toward issue selection, interpretation, negotiation, professional responsibility, and the client-specific consequences that cannot be reduced to clause production.

In medicine, agents can help synthesize evidence and documentation. The clinician’s expertise remains attached to the patient, the uncertain case, the competing risks, and the obligation to act responsibly rather than merely generate a plausible recommendation.

The pattern is not universal.

Some expert production will become commodity work.

A person whose value came primarily from executing a standardized cognitive procedure may face real substitution. Expertise is not protected by the title “expert.” It is protected only to the extent that the expertise governs a scarce part of the workflow.

Agents reveal which layer was scarce.

This can be painful because professions often build identity around artifacts.

The architect draws.

The programmer codes.

The writer writes.

The analyst models.

The lawyer drafts.

The designer designs.

When the machine performs more of the visible craft, people can experience the change as a threat to authorship.

The response is not to pretend the craft never mattered.

The craft created judgment.

It may still be practiced for learning, pleasure, signaling, or high-consequence cases.

But economic authorship can move upward.

The writer becomes responsible for what deserves to be said, not merely for typing every sentence.

The programmer becomes responsible for system behavior, not merely for entering every line.

The designer becomes responsible for the user’s experience and the product’s taste, not merely for drawing every variation.

The analyst becomes responsible for the decision and the integrity of evidence, not merely for assembling every table.

This transition creates a new kind of expert error.

The expert may overdelegate because domain confidence is high.

A senior person knows the field and assumes that this makes machine output easy to supervise. But the agent may introduce technical, procedural, or cross-domain risks outside the expert’s competence.

The scientist understands the science and misses a software-security problem.

The lawyer understands the law and misses that the agent exposed confidential data through a tool integration.

The investor understands the economics and misses a data-engineering flaw that biased the result.

The doctor understands the medicine and misses that the model’s source retrieval was stale.

Agentic expertise is therefore composite.

The principal needs enough knowledge of the domain to own the objective and enough understanding of the agentic system to know where domain expertise stops protecting the workflow.

This does not mean every professional becomes an AI engineer.

It means the professional needs control literacy.

What context is the agent using?

What can it do?

How is the result verified?

Which failure modes are not visible to me from the domain side?

When should another specialist enter?

The strongest principals will know when to orchestrate expertise rather than impersonate it.

This is where agents can improve multidisciplinary work.

Historically, crossing domains has high coordination cost. A scientist needs a programmer. A lawyer needs a data specialist. A founder needs design, research, finance, and operations. Small needs do not always justify formal collaboration, so people either accept lower quality or avoid the task.

Agents provide a bridge.

They let one expert reach farther into adjacent domains for exploratory and bounded work.

When the consequence rises, real specialists can be brought in with better-prepared questions and artifacts.

The system can therefore reduce the coordination cost of knowing when you need another person.

This is a subtle but important form of leverage.

The principal does not replace expertise.

The principal consumes expertise more intelligently.

Anthropic’s finding that typical users made more planning decisions while the agent made more execution decisions gives this division a concrete early shape.

Planning is not a permanent human monopoly. Agents can plan, and future systems will do more of it.

The useful distinction is between local planning and objective ownership.

An agent can decide which file to inspect next, which function to modify, which source to query, or which subtask to delegate. These are execution-level plans.

The principal can remain responsible for the larger constraint: why this work exists, what should not change, which risk is tolerable, and what counts as finished.

Expertise lets the principal operate at that level without becoming vague.

A bad high-level instruction is merely under-specified.

A good high-level instruction is compressed knowledge.

“Make the code faster” is vague.

“Reduce the p95 latency of this endpoint without changing the public response contract or increasing database load under the current traffic profile” contains expertise.

“Improve the contract” is vague.

“Reduce ambiguity in the termination provisions without changing the approved liability allocation or the customer’s existing renewal economics” contains expertise.

“Analyze churn” is vague.

“Identify which recent churn is plausibly addressable by product behavior rather than pricing, seasonality, or intentionally unprofitable customers” contains expertise.

The expert does not specify every step.

The expert preserves the structure of the problem.

That is why sparse instructions can be powerful.

They are not sparse because the principal knows less.

They are sparse because the principal knows which few facts govern many downstream choices.

This is compression through understanding.

The novice can imitate the words and miss the model underneath them.

The future of expertise may therefore look less like possession of secret information and more like possession of high-quality abstractions.

Models make information cheap.

Search makes facts retrievable.

Agents make execution callable.

The expert’s advantage moves toward knowing which abstraction survives contact with reality.

This is hard to benchmark because it often appears as choosing what not to do.

The expert avoids the attractive dead end.

The expert knows which metric is misleading.

The expert recognizes that the customer’s request is a symptom.

The expert refuses the technically elegant solution that creates organizational complexity.

The expert stops the agent after two iterations because the missing information cannot be generated from the available context.

These decisions produce no flashy artifact.

They create leverage by preventing wasted consequence.

This is why the agentic economy may make judgment more visible even as production becomes less visible.

A senior professional’s output may increasingly be a set of decisions, constraints, approvals, exceptions, and redesigns rather than a pile of personally authored artifacts.

Organizations will need better ways to evaluate that work.

Lines written will mean less.

Documents produced will mean less.

Hours spent will mean less.

The person who caused a good system to produce reliable outcomes may appear less busy than the person constantly rescuing a poorly designed workflow.

This is an old management problem with new machinery.

The best operating leader often looks quiet because the institution works.

Agentic leverage can make that distinction sharper.

The field test also points toward education.

If non-programmers can successfully direct coding agents, should everyone learn to code?

The answer depends on what “learn to code” means.

Fewer people may need to memorize syntax or become competent at routine implementation.

More people may benefit from computational thinking, data structures, system models, debugging logic, and enough code literacy to inspect what an agent is doing.

The valuable layer moves upward without disappearing.

The same principle applies in every field affected by agents.

Teach the mechanics that create the mental model.

Automate the mechanics whose only purpose was production.

Preserve enough direct practice that judgment remains calibrated.

Then let the expert use the machine to expand territory.

The final lesson from the Claude Code sessions is not that everyone becomes a programmer.

It is that programming begins to behave more like a callable capability inside other professions.

That is what agents do to expertise.

They turn some expert execution into infrastructure.

The people who capture the most value are not necessarily the ones who know how to perform every infrastructural step manually.

They are the ones who know enough about the domain to command the infrastructure without becoming its passenger.

Execution becomes cheaper.

Expertise moves closer to authorship.

That is not the end of expertise.

It is the reason expertise can suddenly reach farther.
