# The Agency Dividend

Every general-purpose technology creates a surplus before society decides who receives it.

A machine makes one worker more productive.

A process gets cheaper.

A product gets better.

A new capability becomes possible.

Then bargaining begins.

Customers may receive lower prices.

Workers may receive higher wages or fewer jobs.

Owners may receive higher margins.

New competitors may enter and erase the margin.

Platforms may collect rent because they control distribution.

Governments may tax part of the gain.

The surplus can diffuse widely or concentrate around the scarce assets that remain.

Agentic leverage creates the same question at unusual scale.

If one person can command more intelligence and execution than before, who gets the benefit?

That is the agency dividend.

The simplest version is time.

An agent completes work that consumed two hours.

Two hours become available.

What happens to them?

The employee may receive less work.

The employee may receive more work.

The company may increase output expectations until the two hours disappear into a larger queue.

The person may use the time for higher-value judgment, learning, relationships, or rest.

The organization may cut the role.

All of these outcomes are compatible with the same productivity gain.

Technology creates capacity.

Institutions decide what capacity becomes.

This is why the future of work cannot be inferred from model benchmarks alone.

A capability result tells us something about what machines can do.

It does not tell us who owns the saved hour.

The agency dividend depends on property, bargaining power, market structure, law, culture, and the design of the workflow.

Consider an employee whose agent increases output by forty percent.

If the employee is paid hourly and the employer captures the entire efficiency gain, personal agency may not rise much. The worker simply produces more under the same authority.

If the worker is salaried and the organization redesigns the role upward, the person may gain discretion and responsibility.

If the worker owns the business, the gain may become margin, free time, faster experimentation, or lower prices.

If the gain is easy for every competitor to obtain, competition may pass much of it to customers.

The same technology can feel liberating, threatening, or invisible depending on the institutional position of the user.

The agency dividend therefore begins with ownership of the objective.

This is why principals can capture unusually large returns from agents.

The owner of a small business does not need permission to convert saved effort into a new product, a shorter workweek, more customers, lower prices, or a larger margin. The person occupies the point where intelligence meets decision rights.

Employees can gain similar leverage when organizations distribute decision rights rather than only tools.

Giving every employee an agent while keeping every meaningful choice centralized creates production leverage without much human agency.

Giving teams clear authority over bounded outcomes lets the same technology expand the territory they can own.

This is an organizational choice.

The agency dividend can therefore be distributed through autonomy.

A company does not have to share every productivity gain as money in order to share it meaningfully.

It can share scope.

You no longer spend half the week assembling the report. You own the decision process the report supports.

You no longer manually triage every case. You own the system that defines the policy and resolves the exceptions.

You no longer write every line. You own a larger component, architecture, or customer outcome.

You no longer schedule and summarize the project. You own the tradeoffs that determine whether the project should continue.

This is the best version of augmentation because automation removes subordination to the task and returns authorship over a larger unit of work.

It will not happen automatically.

Organizations can use agents to do the opposite.

Break work into narrower measured units.

Increase monitoring.

Set higher throughput targets.

Reduce discretion because the system now supplies recommendations.

Turn managers into exception processors.

Turn professionals into approvers of machine work.

The worker becomes more productive and less agentic.

This is the central irony of the new economy.

Technology built around agents can reduce human agency if institutions use it primarily to centralize control.

The dividend can also be distributed through entrepreneurship.

Lower coordination and production costs reduce the threshold for trying something.

A person can launch a service without first hiring a team.

A specialist can productize expertise.

A local business can maintain capabilities that once required corporate scale.

A creator can operate a publication with institutional consistency.

A community organization can analyze, coordinate, and communicate at a level previously reserved for larger institutions.

The economy can gain more experiments.

Most will fail.

That is part of the dividend.

Cheap experiments allow more people to discover which ideas deserve capital and which do not before the mistake becomes expensive.

The agency dividend can therefore appear as option creation rather than immediate income.

More people can afford to ask, “What if I tried?”

This may matter greatly for people whose main constraint was not lack of ideas but lack of organizational support.

A skilled employee with a family may not be able to quit a job and hire three people to test a business idea. An agentic stack can reduce the initial fixed cost enough that the experiment fits nights, weekends, or a short runway.

A scientist may explore an adjacent question without assembling a full collaboration first.

A neighborhood group may examine public data without fundraising for a consultant.

An individual fighting a bureaucratic error may maintain records and persistence that once required professional help.

Agency can diffuse because institutional capability becomes more divisible.

The dividend can also appear as dignity.

This is less measurable and no less real.

People spend enormous portions of life on coordination friction that exists because institutions cannot economically provide individualized attention.

Wait on hold.

Repeat the story to another representative.

Fill the same information into another form.

Translate a requirement written for professionals.

Track the deadline the institution will not remind you about.

Search a hundred pages for the clause that determines whether you qualify.

A good personal agent can absorb some of this friction.

The person arrives at the institution better prepared and less dependent on institutional patience.

This is a redistribution of agency even if GDP barely notices the saved frustration.

The dividend can appear as accessibility.

Language barriers fall.

Technical interfaces become conversational.

A person who cannot write code can still build a small tool.

A person who struggles with bureaucratic prose can receive an explanation at the right level.

A small organization can access analytical methods once confined to specialists.

A novice can receive tutoring on demand.

These gains are not equal and they are not free of risk. Bad advice can also scale. Unequal access can create new gaps. But the baseline capability available to an individual can rise significantly.

This is why the agency dividend should not be reduced to labor displacement.

Jobs matter enormously because income, identity, training, social structure, and bargaining power are organized around them.

The technology also changes what people can do outside a job.

That may become one of its most important effects.

The distribution question extends to companies.

Which firms capture the gains?

If frontier intelligence is expensive and concentrated, model providers capture a large share.

If capability commoditizes, complementary assets become more important.

Context.

Distribution.

Customer trust.

Verification infrastructure.

Capital.

Brands.

Rights to data.

Physical assets.

Regulatory permission.

The agency premium described earlier becomes the mechanism by which some firms retain the dividend while others pass it to customers or suppliers.

Competition matters.

A world with many substitutable intelligence providers creates different bargaining power from a world with a few deeply integrated platforms controlling models, memory, tools, and distribution.

Portability matters because it keeps the dividend contestable.

If a company can switch intelligence suppliers, productivity gains are more likely to flow through competition.

If customers are captive to one agent ecosystem, the ecosystem can capture more of the surplus.

This is why sovereignty is not only defensive strategy.

It shapes distribution.

The same is true for workers.

A worker whose agentic skills and context are portable across employers has more bargaining power than a worker whose productivity depends entirely on one company’s proprietary system.

This suggests a new category of career capital.

Not prompt tricks.

Portable principal skills.

Problem framing.

Judgment.

Delegation design.

Verification.

Domain expertise.

Orchestration.

The ability to build and govern agentic systems across tools.

These capabilities travel better than familiarity with one assistant interface.

Workers who accumulate them can capture more of the dividend because they bring leverage with them.

Professional institutions may need to help make that portability legible. Credentials historically signal knowledge and skill. The agentic economy may value evidence that a person can govern machine work in a domain, not merely perform the old production manually.

What does a senior agentic accountant know?

Not simply how to make the model draft a reconciliation.

How to define the control, recognize exceptions, preserve auditability, evaluate the system, and own the financial consequence.

What does a senior agentic engineer know?

Not simply how to generate code quickly.

How to shape systems, specify invariants, design tests, bound machine authority, review high-consequence changes, and maintain architecture across abundant implementation.

What does a senior agentic lawyer know?

Not simply how to draft faster.

How to identify the issue the client actually has, verify authority, distinguish legal uncertainty from model uncertainty, preserve confidentiality, and accept professional responsibility for advice.

This is the human side of the dividend.

Expertise moves upward from production into governance without becoming abstract management.

The best professionals remain close enough to the work to know what matters and far enough above the mechanics to command larger systems.

Society also has a claim on the dividend.

If agents increase productive capacity substantially, public institutions will confront familiar questions in new forms.

How should productivity gains affect working time?

How should displaced workers be supported?

How should education change when entry-level tasks disappear?

How should competition policy treat agent platforms whose memory and distribution create lock-in?

How should liability be assigned when machine action crosses organizational boundaries?

How should privacy rules apply when agents infer far more context than conventional applications needed?

How should public services use agents without making citizens subject to opaque authority?

There will be no single “AI policy” that settles these questions.

They belong to labor law, competition, education, consumer protection, administrative law, privacy, professional regulation, taxation, and social policy because agents are becoming part of ordinary economic infrastructure.

The agency dividend will be political because its distribution will be political.

That does not mean the individual principal has to wait for policy.

The book began with a practical opportunity.

Intelligence is acquiring hands.

You can use that now.

The final discipline is to decide what kind of dividend you want from the leverage.

More output?

More income?

A smaller team?

A larger mission?

More experiments?

More time?

More independence?

Better service?

More ability to say no?

The answer matters because agents will optimize the work you give them before they can answer what the work was for.

A person can build an extraordinary machine for moving faster and discover that the machine has made speed the objective.

A company can automate everything that employees dislike and use the saved capacity to create more things employees dislike.

A society can make cognitive production abundant and fill the abundance with noise.

The dividend does not arrive merely because efficiency improves.

It arrives when somebody chooses what the efficiency is supposed to buy.

That choice is agency.

The deepest promise of the agentic economy is not that the machines become autonomous.

It is that human beings can become less trapped by the mechanics between intention and consequence.

The parent can spend less time navigating paperwork and more time caring.

The founder can spend less time moving tasks and more time deciding what company deserves to exist.

The expert can spend less time reproducing standard output and more time on the case that breaks the standard.

The employee can own a larger outcome instead of a narrower procedure.

The citizen can arrive at an institution with memory, evidence, and persistence on her side.

The small organization can attempt work once reserved for larger ones.

The large institution can become more responsive without becoming more chaotic.

None of this is guaranteed.

Leverage does not choose its beneficiary.

Principals do. Markets do. Managers do. Laws do. Cultures do.

The machines will become better at acting.

The remaining question is whether we become better at authoring what their action is for.

That is where the dividend lives.

Not in the number of agents.

Not in the number of tokens.

Not in the volume of work produced while nobody was watching.

In the distance between an intention and a consequence that still feels like yours.

The new economy will make that distance enormous.

Keep your hand on the fulcrum.
