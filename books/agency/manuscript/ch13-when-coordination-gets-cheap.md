# When Coordination Gets Cheap

Ronald Coase asked a deceptively simple question in 1937.

If markets are so effective at coordinating economic activity, why do firms exist?

Why does an entrepreneur hire employees, create departments, issue instructions, and organize work inside a company rather than purchase every needed action through the market?

His answer began with transaction costs.

Markets are not free to use. Somebody has to discover prices, find counterparties, negotiate, write contracts, monitor performance, and adapt when circumstances change. Sometimes it is cheaper to organize a set of transactions inside a firm than to keep purchasing them one by one.

The firm exists partly because coordination is expensive.

Agentic systems reduce a new class of coordination costs.

Not all of them.

Enough to change the shape of the firm.

Consider the small business owner who needs five different forms of expertise in one week.

Analyze recent customer churn.

Rewrite the onboarding emails.

Compare three payroll providers.

Fix a bug in the website.

Prepare a cash-flow scenario for a bank meeting.

Before agents, the owner could do the work personally, hire employees, use freelancers, buy specialized software, or leave some of the work undone. Each option had coordination cost. Briefing a freelancer can take longer than a small task is worth. Hiring a full-time employee makes sense only when enough related work can be bundled into a role. Specialized software works when the task can be standardized. Personal effort becomes the default because the owner already has the context.

Agents change the minimum economic size of delegation.

A task can be too small to justify a contractor and large enough to justify an agent.

A role can be too variable to automate with traditional software and structured enough for a model with tools.

A need can be too intermittent to justify employment and frequent enough to benefit from durable context.

This creates a new middle layer between software and labor.

The economics of firms will move around that layer.

One immediate effect is unbundling.

Jobs are bundles of tasks partly because human labor arrives bundled. A company does not hire 7 percent of an analyst, 12 percent of a project manager, 4 percent of a researcher, and 2 percent of an editor for a Tuesday afternoon. It hires people whose jobs contain a portfolio of activities large enough to justify a durable relationship.

Agents can absorb fragments.

The fragments do not have to form a coherent human career.

This may allow organizations to redesign roles around the work that benefits from human judgment, relationships, accountability, physical presence, or deep local expertise while delegating intermittent cognitive production around those cores.

That sounds like ordinary automation until one notices that the fragments can be ambiguous.

Traditional software is easiest to justify when a process repeats in a stable form.

Agents make less standardized fragments economically addressable.

Investigate this unusual variance.

Prepare for this one-off negotiation.

Compare the implications of these three proposals.

Trace why these records disagree.

Adapt this policy for a new jurisdiction and surface what legal counsel must decide.

These tasks were historically difficult to productize because their instructions change with context.

Agents can absorb some of the variation.

That lowers the coordination cost of specialization.

A company can call a capability when needed instead of building a full role around it.

This will not necessarily shrink firms.

Coase’s logic cuts both ways.

If internal coordination becomes cheaper, firms can become larger because management can organize more activity before internal costs overwhelm the benefit.

If market coordination becomes cheaper, firms can become smaller because they can obtain more capability externally without hiring.

Agents reduce both kinds of cost.

An internal agent can help a large company navigate its own complexity.

An external agent marketplace can help a small company buy specialized outcomes.

The final structure depends on which side improves faster.

This is why claims that AI will simply produce “companies of one person” or “companies with no employees” are too easy.

Some organizations will become much smaller.

Others may become much larger in scope because a relatively small management layer can coordinate more work.

The important variable is managerial span.

How much activity can one principal govern before attention, judgment, verification, and accountability become the bottleneck?

Human organizations limit span partly through hierarchy.

A manager can directly supervise only so many people well. Managers therefore manage managers. Information is summarized as it moves upward. Objectives are decomposed as they move downward. The hierarchy reduces direct coordination at the cost of translation, delay, politics, and loss of local detail.

Agents can alter this tradeoff.

A manager can receive synthesized state across far more work.

Routine questions can be answered by shared context.

Exceptions can be routed automatically.

Status can be collected without meetings.

Decisions can be recorded and propagated without relying on memory.

The manager’s span can increase.

But span does not become infinite.

The more activity under one principal, the greater the number of tradeoffs that can collide.

One agent can optimize customer retention while another optimizes cost. One can accelerate release while another enforces risk. One can reduce inventory while another protects service levels. The principal eventually confronts conflicts that cannot be resolved by local optimization because they express strategy.

Coordination gets cheaper until judgment becomes the price.

This is the first limit.

The second is legitimacy.

People do not obey organizations only because instructions are efficient. They participate because authority is recognized through contracts, norms, professional identity, law, trust, and mutual obligation. A manager cannot replace every social function of management with a routing algorithm and assume the firm remains intact.

Employees need to know who is responsible.

Customers care who stands behind a promise.

Partners need relationships when contracts are incomplete.

Communities judge companies as actors, not merely as systems of optimized transactions.

A firm is not only a coordination mechanism.

It is a bearer of responsibility.

Agents can cheapen the first function without replacing the second.

The third limit is tacit coordination.

A good team develops rhythms that are not fully represented in documents. People notice hesitation. They learn how a colleague thinks. They understand when an unusual request signals a deeper problem. They negotiate tradeoffs through conversation that would be cumbersome to encode formally.

Agents can participate in these systems. They can preserve context, summarize, suggest, and sometimes mediate. The assumption that all coordination is information transfer understates the social technology of a team.

Some coordination creates commitment by occurring between people.

The weekly meeting can be useless status theater.

It can also be where a group realizes that nobody believes the plan.

Reducing coordination cost should not mean deleting every interaction whose information can be summarized.

It should mean becoming more intentional about which interactions are actually doing social work.

This may improve organizations if agents absorb administrative coordination and leave more room for human coordination that requires trust, conflict, mentoring, creativity, or collective judgment.

Or organizations may take the savings entirely as fewer people and less contact.

Technology does not choose the institution.

Managers do.

A fourth limit is external dependency.

A company can replace internal coordination with agentic services and become operationally smaller while becoming strategically dependent on external infrastructure.

The org chart shrinks.

The dependency graph expands.

Model providers, cloud platforms, data vendors, identity systems, payment systems, tool integrations, and agent marketplaces may sit behind the apparent simplicity of the organization.

A one-person company can become a network of rented institutions.

This can be wonderful.

It can also mean that the company’s ability to operate is shaped by terms, prices, permissions, and outages outside its control.

The economics therefore depends on switching cost and sovereignty, not merely payroll.

A fifth limit is verification.

Cheap coordination can create more activity than the firm can assure.

Imagine a consulting company where every professional can dispatch research, analysis, modeling, presentation, and outreach agents in parallel. The productive frontier expands quickly.

If quality control remains designed for a world of human-paced output, review becomes the bottleneck.

The company can respond by increasing automated verification, changing the unit of human review, reducing the number of deliverables, or accepting lower confidence.

The correct choice depends on the work.

Coordination is cheap only if the cost of assurance is included.

This gives Coase’s question a new form.

Why does this action happen inside the firm, outside the firm, or inside an agent?

The answer will depend on the relative transaction costs of each arrangement.

A human employee may be best where tacit context, loyalty, relationship, ongoing accountability, and flexible judgment dominate.

An external specialist may be best where rare expertise matters and the task can be scoped.

Traditional software may be best where the process is stable and determinism is valuable.

An agent may be best where the task is variable, context can be supplied, verification is economical, and action can be bounded.

A market may be best where many providers can compete on a clearly specified outcome.

The firm becomes a portfolio of coordination mechanisms.

This has a profound implication for management.

Managers will spend less time deciding who should do a task and more time deciding what coordination mechanism should own the task.

Employee?

Agent?

Software?

Vendor?

Market?

Hybrid workflow?

The choice becomes architectural.

A manager who routes every new need to a person will underuse the technology.

A manager who routes every cognitive task to an agent will underuse the institution.

The valuable skill is matching work to the mechanism that carries its consequence best.

This changes budgeting too.

Today, labor budgets, software budgets, consulting budgets, and cloud budgets often live in different planning systems. Agentic work blurs them.

A team may substitute model usage for contractor hours.

It may spend more compute to reduce headcount, or increase headcount because each employee can now manage more revenue. It may replace a software license with an agent that operates across general tools. It may buy a specialized agent outcome rather than buy software or hire a specialist.

The accounting categories lag the production function.

Companies will eventually need to understand the cost per governed outcome rather than only cost per seat, token, or employee.

What did it cost to resolve the customer case with acceptable quality?

What did it cost to ship the feature including generation, tests, review, and incident risk?

What did it cost to produce a verified market recommendation?

What did it cost to maintain compliance across the workflow?

The cheapest execution is not necessarily the cheapest outcome.

This is where organizations may discover that some human labor was doing invisible coordination work that agents do not replace automatically.

The assistant did not merely schedule meetings. She knew which meeting should not happen.

The project manager did not merely update tickets. He recognized that two teams were quietly building incompatible assumptions.

The senior analyst did not merely check formulas. She knew which question the executive would misunderstand.

The operations manager did not merely approve exceptions. She carried memory across a system full of turnover.

If an organization automates the visible task and discards the invisible function, costs can reappear elsewhere.

This is why job decomposition must begin with purpose rather than activity.

What coordination problem was the person solving?

Which part can be made cheaper?

Which part was social insurance against ambiguity?

Which part was merely historical residue?

Agents make these questions financially urgent.

The companies that answer them well can redesign around cheaper coordination without hollowing out capability.

The companies that answer them badly may become efficient diagrams and fragile institutions.

There is a larger economic consequence.

When coordination costs fall, the minimum scale required to attempt ambitious work falls too.

A smaller organization can manage more functions.

A temporary organization can form around one objective.

A person can assemble capability without first assembling a payroll.

A community can coordinate information and action that once required a formal institution.

This is the democratic promise of cheap coordination.

It is also the competitive threat.

Large firms have historically enjoyed advantages from accumulated coordination capability. They can maintain legal teams, procurement teams, analytics teams, operations teams, finance teams, and technical specialists because their scale justifies the fixed cost.

Agents can make some of that institutional competence available on demand.

Small organizations gain pieces of large-organization capability without becoming large organizations.

Large organizations gain the ability to coordinate their scale with less friction.

Both sides become stronger.

The competitive outcome is not predetermined.

The scarce resource moves again.

If coordination is cheaper for everyone, advantage shifts toward better intent, proprietary context, trusted relationships, capital, distribution, data, legitimacy, and the ability to verify and commit.

The firm does not disappear when coordination becomes cheaper.

It reveals what the firm was for besides coordination.
