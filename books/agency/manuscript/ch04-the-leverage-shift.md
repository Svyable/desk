# The Leverage Shift

A lever is interesting because the person does not become stronger.

The person becomes consequential at a greater distance from his own muscle.

That distinction is worth preserving as we talk about artificial intelligence. Much of the language around AI focuses on personal improvement. Write faster. Analyze faster. Code faster. Learn faster. Answer faster.

All of that can be useful.

It is also the least surprising form of leverage.

A faster person is still one person moving through a queue.

Agentic leverage changes the queue.

To see why, it helps to look at the older forms.

Labor leverage comes from other people.

One person can do one person’s work. Ten coordinated people can attempt something that one person cannot. The constraint is not simply wages. It is coordination. Every additional person creates interfaces: instructions, handoffs, meetings, incentives, misunderstandings, schedules, trust, specialization, management, and the possibility that the whole becomes slower than the parts.

Capital leverage comes from resources controlled beyond the owner’s immediate labor.

A person can invest in machines, inventory, land, infrastructure, or financial assets and receive returns from productive capacity he did not personally operate. Capital separates output from hours worked more dramatically than labor leverage does, but it introduces risk, financing costs, and the need to decide where capital should be allocated.

Code leverage comes from repeatability.

A useful program can run one time or a billion times. The second execution does not require a second programmer to type the code again. Software therefore created businesses that could serve enormous numbers of customers without growing labor proportionally.

Media leverage comes from distribution.

A teacher can stand in one room and teach thirty people, or record a lesson once and reach a million. A writer can spend a year on a book that is read for decades. The creator pays the cost once and distribution carries the work farther.

Networks add another form.

The value of the system grows because participants contribute to one another. Marketplaces, social platforms, payment systems, standards, and open-source ecosystems can compound usefulness through participation.

Agentic leverage borrows something from all of these and adds a property of its own.

It can interpret.

A recorded lecture does not notice that one student misunderstood the second idea and choose a different explanation. Code can branch according to conditions, but the branches were historically specified by developers. A human employee can interpret, but cannot be duplicated instantly. Capital can finance action but does not decide the intermediate path. A network can coordinate enormous activity but usually requires participants to supply the local judgment.

An agent can accept an objective, inspect the state, choose a next step, use a tool, observe the result, and choose again.

That makes it a kind of reusable labor without requiring the work to be reduced fully to procedure in advance.

The qualification matters.

The agent is not a person. It does not bring the full social, embodied, ethical, legal, and institutional capacities of a human employee. It does not automatically understand what a company values or what a client will forgive. It can behave strangely outside the distribution of its experience. It can fail with confidence. It may need permissions that create unacceptable risk. The cost of verification may erase the apparent savings.

But where the environment is legible enough, the objective is governable enough, and the consequence is bounded enough, the economics are different.

The unit of delegated work can shrink.

A company does not hire a new employee to spend seven minutes cleaning one dataset, twenty minutes checking a supplier’s website, twelve minutes comparing contract clauses, and eight minutes tracing a failed test. Human labor is bundled because people need continuity, compensation, management, and a coherent job.

Agents can be invoked in fragments.

That makes previously uneconomic delegation possible.

It also means work can be parallelized at a scale that would be absurd with human coordination.

A strategist can ask ten agents to examine ten independent evidence lanes, then ask another to find contradictions across them. A programmer can dispatch separate investigations into unrelated failures. A founder can test multiple names, product flows, positioning hypotheses, and customer segments at once. A scientist can ask for independent literature maps before deciding where a human expert should spend time.

Parallelism changes the meaning of speed.

Suppose a task normally takes a person ten hours.

The obvious AI improvement is to complete the task in five.

Agentic leverage asks whether five versions of the task can be pursued at the same time, whether parts can be decomposed safely, whether one system can continue while the human sleeps, and whether the human can spend the saved time on the decision rather than the production.

The gain can therefore come from compression, concurrency, persistence, or expansion of scope.

These are different economic mechanisms.

Compression means the same work takes less time.

Concurrency means more independent work happens at once.

Persistence means work continues across time without constant human presence.

Scope expansion means the principal attempts work that would previously have been omitted.

The last one is the easiest to underestimate because it does not appear as hours saved.

If a small business never performed systematic win-loss analysis because hiring an analyst was unjustified, an agent-assisted version does not “save” the business any analyst hours. There were no analyst hours to save.

The technology creates a capability that was economically absent.

This is one reason measurements of AI impact can look small at the aggregate level while individual users feel a dramatic change. Productivity statistics are designed to observe output in recognized categories. New capabilities often begin as changes in quality, variety, experimentation, or consumer surplus before they become visible in conventional accounting.

There is another property that makes agentic leverage unusual: composability.

The output of one agent can become the context, specification, test, or input for another.

This creates a chain in which machine work can organize machine work.

The research agent produces a source set.

The analysis agent extracts competing explanations.

The skeptic agent looks for disconfirming evidence.

The writer produces a draft.

The verifier checks factual claims against the source set.

The principal reviews the disputed points and the recommendation.

Nothing about this chain requires pretending that each agent is an independent personality. They can be calls to the same underlying model with different contexts and permissions. The economic fact is that the workflow can allocate cognitive roles in software.

That is new enough to deserve its own category of leverage.

Traditional software pipelines already compose functions. Data moves from one service to another. Tests run after builds. Alerts trigger responses. Business rules route transactions. What changes with agents is the flexibility of the interface between stages.

The research stage does not have to return a rigidly predetermined schema if the next stage can interpret ordinary language. The verifier can inspect an unexpected output rather than failing because one field is missing. The orchestrator can adapt the path when a source is unavailable or a test fails.

Flexibility lowers the cost of constructing workflows around work that was previously too variable to automate.

It also lowers the cost of constructing bad workflows.

This is where leverage becomes dangerous.

Every additional agent can create apparent competence while adding another layer the principal does not directly inspect. A multi-agent system can produce the aesthetic of organizational rigor: researcher, critic, planner, reviewer, auditor. If every role shares the same blind spot, the choreography adds confidence without independence.

A committee of identical errors is still one error.

The principal has to ask what diversity actually exists.

Different sources?

Different tools?

Different models?

Different objective functions?

Independent tests?

A human with local knowledge?

Or merely the same system wearing different name tags?

This is why agent count is a poor measure of agentic leverage.

The point is not how many digital workers are running.

The point is how much additional useful consequence the principal can govern.

A single well-designed agent with clear permissions and a strong verification loop may provide more leverage than a swarm of loosely supervised systems.

There is a second reason to resist counting agents.

Leverage should reduce friction at the bottleneck.

If the bottleneck is human review, adding more generating agents can make the bottleneck worse.

This is visible in software development. Coding tools can increase the volume of produced code dramatically. But production is not the entire delivery system. Code still has to be reviewed, tested, integrated, secured, deployed, observed, and maintained. Recent research has begun separating changes in coding activity from changes in final shipped output precisely because faster generation can move the constraint downstream.

The same principle applies everywhere.

A legal department can generate contracts faster than counterparties can negotiate them.

A marketing team can produce campaigns faster than the brand can learn from customers.

A research organization can generate hypotheses faster than experiments can test them.

A venture firm can screen startups faster than partners can form conviction.

A government can draft policy options faster than democratic institutions can legitimate them.

A person can generate life plans faster than life can be lived.

Leverage is only leverage if it increases controlled throughput at the system level.

Otherwise it is pressure.

This gives us a practical equation, though not a numerical one:

Your agentic leverage is bounded by your slowest consequential control.

If an agent can create a thousand actions but you can only meaningfully verify ten, the useful leverage is not a thousand.

If the remaining 990 actions are low consequence, reversible, and well constrained, perhaps they do not need direct verification. Excellent. The system has reduced the control cost.

If the 990 actions can create material consequences and simply pass without inspection, the system has not solved the bottleneck.

It has bypassed it.

The distinction between reducing and bypassing a control is one of the most important in the agentic economy.

A better test suite can reduce the need for manual code review on certain classes of change.

A higher approval threshold can reduce the number of transactions a manager must see by allowing safe low-value transactions to flow automatically.

A policy engine can reduce review by encoding clear rules.

A verifier can reduce review by surfacing only disputed claims.

These mechanisms remove human attention by creating substitute assurance.

Bypassing simply removes the human attention.

The dashboard no longer shows the warning because there are too many warnings.

The agent sends the email because approval was inconvenient.

The generated code merges automatically because review became a queue.

The research summary drops citations because checking citations slowed the workflow.

Speed rises.

Control silently falls.

This is why the most valuable agentic systems will not be those that minimize human involvement in the abstract.

They will minimize the cost of meaningful human involvement.

That often means changing what the human sees.

Do not show every action. Show the exception.

Do not ask for approval on every low-value purchase. Ask when the pattern changes.

Do not make the lawyer reread unchanged boilerplate. Highlight the clauses whose risk allocation moved.

Do not make the manager inspect every reconciled record. Surface the records that fail the policy and the reasons they fail.

Do not make the developer read every generated line if tests, types, static analysis, sandboxing, and diff boundaries can establish most of the mechanical assurance. Preserve attention for architecture, security, unusual changes, and things the tests do not know how to notice.

The best leverage compresses supervision as well as execution.

That is the difference between an agentic workflow and a machine that merely works faster.

There is another implication.

The returns to process design increase.

In a human organization, a mediocre process can sometimes be rescued by good people. Somebody notices that the form is wrong. Somebody calls the customer. Somebody knows the unofficial exception. Somebody stays late. Somebody remembers that the database was migrated in 2019 and the old field means something strange.

Agents can sometimes make those recoveries too.

But once a workflow scales, every hidden assumption becomes leverage in both directions. A good default repeats cheaply. A bad default repeats cheaply. A missing stop condition disappears across thousands of actions. A stale piece of context becomes organizational doctrine because every agent reads the same file.

The process becomes executable policy.

This is why agentic leverage will push companies to confront knowledge they have historically left informal.

What does “good customer” mean here?

When can a refund be issued without escalation?

Which source wins when two internal documents conflict?

What is the acceptable downside of this experiment?

Which data may leave this jurisdiction?

Who can approve a breaking change?

What counts as sufficient evidence for this claim?

Humans often navigate these questions socially.

Agents force some of them into artifacts.

That can be healthy. It can also create false precision. Not every judgment should become a rule simply because software wants a rule.

Mature leverage preserves ambiguity where ambiguity reflects reality and creates escalation where judgment is genuinely required.

The goal is not to turn the organization into one enormous prompt.

The goal is to make more of its capability callable without making more of its values accidental.

This is where the leverage shift lands for the individual.

A person’s economic power will depend less on how much production she can personally complete and more on how effectively she can assemble, direct, and verify systems that complete production on her behalf.

This does not remove craft.

It changes the place where craft compounds.

A mediocre writer with agents can produce more pages.

A great editor with agents can explore more possibilities and still recognize the one that deserves to exist.

A mediocre programmer with agents can generate more code.

A great engineer can shape larger systems, test more alternatives, and preserve invariants across more machine-written implementation.

A mediocre manager with agents can issue more instructions.

A great manager can create clearer objectives, safer authority, stronger feedback, and more room for capable execution.

Leverage magnifies what sits at the fulcrum.

If the fulcrum is judgment, judgment gets stronger.

If the fulcrum is confusion, confusion gets stronger too.

This is why the agentic economy will not simply make everyone equivalent by giving everyone the same intelligence.

The intelligence may become widely available.

The leverage will still depend on what the principal knows how to do with it.
