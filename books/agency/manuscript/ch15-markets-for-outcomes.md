# Markets for Outcomes

Most markets sell inputs.

Hours.

Seats.

Licenses.

Tokens.

Storage.

Compute.

Consulting days.

Advertising impressions.

Access to a person, a machine, a platform, or a piece of capacity.

The buyer assembles the input into an outcome.

Agents make another unit economically possible.

The outcome itself.

Not every outcome. Not magically. Not without specification, evidence, and disputes about what counts as success.

But a surprising amount of economic activity may move in that direction because agentic systems can absorb more of the path between purchase and result.

Consider software.

A company can buy a developer’s time.

It can buy a coding tool seat.

It can buy model tokens.

Or it can increasingly imagine buying something closer to: fix this class of bug, migrate this service, upgrade these dependencies, reduce this test suite’s runtime, implement this feature against these acceptance criteria.

The seller decides how much machine work, human work, compute, tooling, and internal verification to use.

The buyer cares about the accepted change.

This is an outcome market.

The concept is not new. Construction contracts, logistics, outsourced business processes, insurance, advertising, legal contingency fees, and performance contracts already price various forms of result rather than raw labor. What changes with agents is the number of cognitive tasks for which the provider can bear the coordination cost of producing and verifying the result.

Cheap internal intelligence makes outcome pricing easier.

A provider can decompose the task, dispatch agents, use specialists only where needed, verify mechanically where possible, and absorb variation across cases. What looks like one outcome to the buyer can be produced by a flexible internal system.

This has major consequences for software economics.

Per-seat pricing assumes value scales roughly with the number of humans using the tool.

Agentic systems weaken that assumption.

One employee may run ten times as much work through a system. An agent may use a service without a human seat at all. A workflow may call a capability only when a task appears. The relationship between headcount and value becomes unstable.

Usage pricing moves closer to the work, but tokens and tool calls remain inputs.

Customers do not ultimately want tokens.

They want resolved cases, shipped code, qualified leads, reconciled transactions, approved claims, completed research, scheduled appointments, recovered revenue, or lower risk.

As agentic systems become more reliable, vendors will face pressure to price nearer to those outcomes.

This is attractive to buyers because it shifts execution risk.

If a vendor charges for the outcome, the vendor has an incentive to become efficient internally. Better models, cheaper compute, improved orchestration, and stronger verification increase margin instead of merely increasing usage billed to the customer.

The buyer does not need to manage every technological improvement.

This is exactly why many businesses prefer a shipping price to paying separately for driver hours, truck depreciation, route-planning software, fuel hedges, warehouse labor, and dispatch calls.

They buy delivery.

Agentic vendors can package cognitive delivery.

But outcome markets create a familiar problem:

Define the outcome.

“Resolve the support ticket” can mean close the ticket or solve the customer’s problem.

“Generate a qualified lead” can mean someone who filled out a form or someone with a painful need and buying authority.

“Fix the bug” can mean make the test pass or restore the intended behavior without creating another defect.

“Recover revenue” can mean collect money or preserve the customer relationship while collecting what is actually owed.

The easier an outcome is to measure, the more tempting it becomes to optimize the measurement instead of the value.

Outcome markets therefore intensify the principal-agent problem.

A vendor paid per closed case will find efficient ways to close cases.

A vendor paid per booked meeting will find efficient ways to book meetings.

A vendor paid per accepted pull request will optimize for acceptance criteria.

The contract has to represent enough of the principal’s real objective that success remains worth buying.

This is not an AI-specific challenge. It is the old problem of incentives with machine-speed execution behind it.

The strongest outcome markets will emerge where verification is cheap and definitions are crisp.

Delivery arrived or it did not.

The payment reconciled or it did not.

The code passes a sufficiently rich acceptance suite or it does not.

A requested dataset was produced with specified coverage and provenance or it was not.

A booking exists under explicit constraints or it does not.

These markets can settle automatically because the evidence of performance is relatively clear.

Other outcomes are harder.

“Improve strategy.”

“Write a great book.”

“Hire the right person.”

“Make the brand stronger.”

“Give good legal advice.”

“Educate the student.”

These outcomes unfold over time, depend on many causes, contain disputed standards, or require judgment that cannot be reduced cheaply to a contract.

Outcome pricing will not swallow the entire economy.

It will advance along the frontier of verifiability.

This is one reason verification technology may create markets that intelligence alone cannot create.

If a task can be performed by an agent but not cheaply verified, providers will hesitate to guarantee the result. Buyers will hesitate to pay as if the result were guaranteed. The market remains tied to inputs: consulting hours, access, usage.

Once verification becomes reliable, the transaction can move toward performance.

The economic value of verification is therefore larger than quality control.

It can make new contracts possible.

This may create specialized assurance providers.

A coding service produces the patch.

An independent evaluation service verifies tests, security properties, scope, and maintainability signals.

A research service produces a report.

An evidence service validates material claims against source standards.

A marketing agent generates and runs campaigns.

A policy layer checks prohibited claims, target restrictions, and spending rules.

The verification service becomes the trusted third party that allows buyer and seller to transact on outcomes without sharing the same internal system.

Markets have always created such roles.

Inspectors.

Auditors.

Rating agencies.

Testing laboratories.

Escrow services.

Clearinghouses.

Certification bodies.

Agentic markets will develop equivalents because one party’s AI saying “I succeeded” is not enough to settle a valuable contract.

This creates a second novelty: machine-readable contracts may become more operational.

Not “smart contracts” in the narrow blockchain sense.

Contracts whose relevant obligations, thresholds, and evidence can be interpreted directly by workflow software.

A service agreement might specify that routine cases are resolved automatically, cases involving a protected class of data require a human, refunds above a threshold need approval, response quality is sampled against a rubric, and unresolved disputes return to the buyer.

Today these terms may live in prose and be enforced through management.

Tomorrow more of the contract may become executable policy.

This can reduce transaction cost.

It can also create brittle literalism if people confuse what can be encoded with what the relationship actually requires.

The strongest contracts will preserve a path for ambiguity.

Markets need judges because contracts are incomplete.

Agentic markets will too.

There is another possible outcome market: personal procurement of agency.

Instead of buying software category by category, a person may tell an agentic broker what outcome is needed and let the broker assemble the services.

Move me to a new apartment by October under this budget and these constraints.

Set up the business in this jurisdiction, but escalate every legal choice that creates long-term liability.

Plan and book the trip while keeping cancellation flexibility above this threshold.

Reduce my recurring household costs without changing these services or creating annual commitments.

The broker searches providers, compares terms, executes reversible parts, requests approval at commitment points, and maintains the state of the project.

The user buys orchestration.

This could weaken brand power in some markets.

Brands partly reduce search cost. Consumers choose a familiar airline, insurer, hotel, retailer, software vendor, or professional because evaluating every alternative is expensive.

An agent can evaluate alternatives continuously.

If switching cost is low and the agent can represent the user’s preferences faithfully, loyalty may move from provider to principal.

The agent knows what the user values.

The market becomes more contestable.

This is the optimistic version.

The opposite is possible too.

The agentic broker itself becomes the new gatekeeper.

If one platform controls discovery, memory, payment, reputation, and execution, sellers compete for access to the agent rather than access to the consumer. The consumer gains convenience and loses visibility into how the market was filtered.

Search engines and app stores already demonstrate this dynamic.

Agents could intensify it because the interface may remove the list of options entirely.

The system simply acts.

A recommendation market becomes an execution market.

That makes agent neutrality economically important.

Whose interests does the agent serve?

Does the user’s principal relationship survive commercial incentives from suppliers?

Are sponsored choices visible?

Can the user inspect alternatives?

Can another agent compete for the same context?

Can the user change the objective without losing the history?

Agency depends on whether the broker remains an agent of the principal or becomes an agent of the marketplace.

The old principal-agent problem returns again.

Outcome markets also change labor markets.

A worker today sells time, expertise, and responsibility to an employer. A freelancer often sells a scoped project. Agents allow skilled individuals to sell more outcomes because the individual can build an internal production system behind the contract.

A security expert can sell verified assessments while agents perform evidence collection and routine checks.

A lawyer can package a narrower class of transaction with agent-assisted preparation while preserving human advice at commitment points.

An analyst can sell recurring decision intelligence rather than hours spent building reports.

A creator can sell a publication cadence supported by agentic production while keeping editorial judgment human.

The individual becomes a small outcome firm.

This can increase earnings for people with strong judgment and distribution because leverage sits behind the personal brand.

It can also increase competition by making it easier for more providers to enter.

When production cost falls, prices may fall too.

Leverage does not guarantee that the producer captures the surplus.

Customers, platforms, model providers, and new competitors may capture it instead.

This is why the agentic economy needs a theory of bargaining power, not merely productivity.

Who owns the customer relationship?

Who owns the context?

Who can switch models?

Who bears failure risk?

Who defines the acceptance test?

Who controls distribution?

Who can prove performance?

The answers determine who captures the outcome premium.

Consider two service providers using equally capable agents.

Provider A bills hours and treats AI as internal efficiency. The work takes half as long, so revenue falls unless the provider raises rates, does more work, or keeps billing conventions unchanged.

Provider B charges for a verified outcome. The cost of production falls while the price remains tied to customer value. Provider B captures more of the efficiency gain, at least until competition lowers prices.

This creates a strategic incentive to move pricing away from inputs when agents improve productivity.

But the provider can only do that credibly if the outcome is legible.

Again, verification becomes market infrastructure.

There is a darker form of outcome market too.

If systems can optimize outcomes aggressively, markets may reward methods people would reject if the methods were visible.

A lead-generation service may use manipulative outreach.

A debt-recovery system may pressure vulnerable people.

A growth service may exploit addictive patterns.

A reputation service may flood public channels with synthetic persuasion.

The buyer purchases the metric and becomes intentionally ignorant of the method.

This is moral outsourcing.

Agents can make it easier because the internal path becomes technically complex and commercially abstracted.

“I bought the outcome” does not erase responsibility for how the outcome was produced.

Markets need constraints on method as well as definitions of success.

This is familiar in physical supply chains. A company cannot treat low price as the only relevant outcome if the supplier achieves it through illegal labor or environmental harm. Procurement systems increasingly include provenance and compliance because society cares how the result was made.

Agentic outcome markets will face the same demand.

Did the research violate privacy?

Did the sales agent deceive?

Did the content system plagiarize?

Did the hiring service use prohibited attributes?

Did the coding workflow introduce insecure dependencies?

Did the negotiation agent make commitments outside authority?

The path remains part of the product when the path carries values.

Outcome markets therefore do not eliminate governance.

They relocate it into the contract, the verification system, and the market’s rules.

This is the broader pattern of the agentic economy.

Every time execution gets cheaper, something upstream or downstream becomes more important.

Cheap output raises the value of judgment.

Cheap action raises the value of permission.

Cheap delegation raises the value of intent.

Cheap coordination raises the value of institutional design.

And markets for outcomes raise the value of proving what actually happened.

The economic unit is moving closer to consequence.

So must accountability.
