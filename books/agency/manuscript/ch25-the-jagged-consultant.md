# The Jagged Consultant

The first useful field test for agentic leverage begins before agents became the dominant vocabulary.

In 2023, researchers working with Boston Consulting Group ran an experiment with 758 consultants. The technology was GPT-4. The work resembled ordinary consulting tasks: creative product development, analysis, writing, persuasion, and a second class of work deliberately designed so that the model’s apparent competence could lead people astray.

The result became famous for one phrase: the jagged technological frontier.

Inside the frontier, AI-assisted consultants completed more tasks, moved faster, and produced work rated higher in quality. Outside the frontier, the technology could damage performance when people relied on it in a task whose structure exceeded what the model could handle reliably.

The popular lesson was that AI is uneven.

That is correct.

The more interesting lesson for agents is that leverage has a boundary the user may not see.

A traditional tool usually advertises its boundary through function. A spreadsheet will not suddenly produce a legal opinion because the user asked nicely. A calculator does arithmetic. A database returns data according to the system built around it. The capability may be broad, but the interface still creates a rough expectation about what kind of work belongs there.

A general model is different.

It can answer on both sides of its competence boundary in the same fluent voice.

The consultant does not see a cliff.

The consultant sees another text box.

That is the jagged frontier as an agency problem.

A principal delegates through an interface that does not automatically reveal where delegation stops being rational.

The risk grows when the model can act.

If a weak answer remains a draft, the cost may be a bad paragraph. If the same weakness sits inside an agent that can choose data, contact a customer, modify code, or move money, the jagged frontier becomes a jagged authority frontier.

The agent can be competent at ninety-nine adjacent actions and unreliable at the one action that creates the consequence.

This is why the BCG experiment matters years later even as models become much better.

The frontier moves.

Its shape remains.

Stanford’s 2026 AI Index described exactly this broader pattern. Models can perform at extraordinary levels on some professional and academic tasks while remaining unreliable on others that appear surprisingly simple to humans. The point is not that current systems are weak. The point is that aggregate intelligence does not create uniform competence.

Agentic leverage therefore requires local maps.

What kinds of work are inside the reliable frontier for this workflow?

Which are near the edge?

Which should remain recommendation-only?

Which can be delegated but require independent verification?

Which should stop when ambiguity appears?

These maps cannot come entirely from public benchmarks.

The BCG study itself demonstrated why. The same model produced large positive effects on one class of consulting task and worse outcomes on another. A single number for model quality would have concealed the practical distinction.

The principal needs task-level evidence.

This is why progressive autonomy is stronger than ideological autonomy.

Let the system earn scope where the workflow demonstrates reliability.

Do not ask whether the model is “good enough for consulting.” Consulting is not one task. Neither is law, software engineering, medicine, finance, recruiting, management, research, or customer service.

Every profession contains a collection of frontiers.

A system can draft a contract competently and fail to identify the unusual commercial fact that makes one clause dangerous.

It can explain a codebase and still choose an implementation that violates an unwritten architectural constraint.

It can summarize medical evidence and still fail to recognize that the patient in front of the clinician does not match the population behind the evidence.

It can prepare a market analysis and still miss that one number is structurally incomparable across countries.

The principal’s skill is partly knowing where the frontier is likely to be jagged.

Experts possess an advantage here because expertise contains a map of difficulty that outsiders often lack.

A novice sees two tasks that look similar.

An expert knows that one depends on a hidden exception.

This is one reason AI can make expertise more leveraged rather than simply obsolete. The expert can delegate the broad interior of the frontier and spend attention on the edge.

The edge is where the work becomes interesting.

The BCG experiment also identified two broad collaboration patterns that later became useful metaphors. Some workers behaved like “centaurs,” dividing work between human and AI. Others behaved like “cyborgs,” integrating the model more continuously throughout the workflow.

Agentic systems complicate that distinction because the machine can now perform longer sequences. The human may no longer alternate step by step. Still, the underlying design question survives.

Where is the boundary between human and machine cognition placed?

A centaur-like workflow might let an agent perform research and drafting while reserving decision framing and final judgment for the person.

A cyborg-like workflow might involve continuous back-and-forth, with the person and agent shaping the problem together.

A highly agentic workflow might let the system carry an entire reversible sequence and surface only the exception.

None is universally superior.

The right configuration depends on where verification is cheap and where the frontier is uncertain.

This is why “human in the loop” is too crude.

The loop should move.

When evidence shows that a routine class is safely inside the frontier, the human can move upward.

When a new class of task appears, the human may move back into the work.

When the model changes, the organization may temporarily narrow autonomy until local evaluation catches up.

The boundary becomes adaptive.

The deeper lesson from the jagged frontier is humility about similarity.

Humans use analogy constantly. This task looks like the last task. This customer looks like that customer. This bug looks familiar. This legal clause resembles the standard one.

Models do the same kind of pattern matching through different machinery.

The dangerous case is when surface similarity hides a different causal structure.

A model that learned the common pattern may confidently apply it to the exception.

A human expert can do the same.

The difference is throughput.

An agent can repeat the mistaken analogy across thousands of cases before the principal sees the pattern.

That makes exception detection economically important.

Not every case deserves review.

Cases whose relevant features place them near a known frontier deserve different treatment.

This requires the organization to know what makes a case unusual.

That knowledge often exists tacitly among experienced people.

“This looks normal unless the customer is in Germany.”

“This migration is easy unless the account predates 2019.”

“This clause is standard unless the counterparty is also a data processor.”

“This support refund is routine unless the subscription was purchased through a reseller.”

“This metric is comparable unless the business changed its revenue recognition.”

Agentic deployment has a habit of turning these sentences into infrastructure.

That is valuable.

It is also dangerous if the sentence becomes a permanent rule when the frontier moves.

The field test therefore produces three operating lessons.

First, evaluate workflows, not models in the abstract.

A model can be frontier-class and locally wrong for the one task that matters.

Second, preserve the ability to detect and escalate cases that differ from the common distribution.

The exception is often where agency belongs.

Third, revisit the boundary as capability improves.

A workflow designed around the weaknesses of GPT-4 in 2023 should not become doctrine in 2026. Better systems deserve more authority where evidence supports it. Old human checkpoints can become obsolete. New forms of machine failure can appear as the system gains tools and longer horizons.

The frontier is jagged and moving.

The principal needs both facts.

There is an organizational temptation to avoid this complexity by choosing one of two simple stories.

Story one: AI is unreliable, so humans must remain responsible for everything important.

Story two: AI is improving so quickly that designing around current limitations is pointless; automate aggressively and let capability catch up.

Both avoid the work of governance.

The first freezes the human-machine boundary.

The second abandons it.

The agentic approach treats the boundary as an empirical object.

Measure it.

Move it.

Keep the consequences proportional to what you know.

This is what a pilot does when conditions change. This is what a trader does when a model leaves its historical regime. This is what a good manager does when a subordinate demonstrates new capability. Authority expands through evidence.

The BCG consultants were not studying agent governance when the experiment began.

They were studying whether generative AI improved professional performance.

The field test nevertheless gave the agentic economy one of its earliest durable warnings.

The intelligence does not become uniformly safe because the average gets better.

Leverage has geography.

Know where you are standing before you push.
