# The Agency Premium

When a technology becomes common, advantage rarely comes from mere access for long.

The first spreadsheet users had an advantage because spreadsheets were unusual.

Then spreadsheets became ordinary and the advantage moved toward what people could model with them.

The first companies on the web gained from being present.

Then presence became table stakes and advantage moved toward distribution, product, data, logistics, brand, and execution.

The same transition is already visible with artificial intelligence.

Access spreads.

Capability improves.

Interfaces simplify.

The difference between having the model and not having the model narrows.

What remains unequal is the ability to turn the model into agency.

That difference is the agency premium.

The agency premium is the return earned by people and institutions that can convert abundant intelligence into controlled consequence better than their peers.

It is not one skill.

It is an economic effect produced by several scarce capabilities sitting around increasingly common models.

Problem selection.

Intent.

Judgment.

Domain knowledge.

Context.

Permission design.

Verification.

Orchestration.

Distribution.

Trust.

Capital.

The right to decide.

These resources determine whether a capable agent produces an interesting output or changes an outcome that matters.

This is why “AI literacy” will eventually sound too small.

Literacy means being able to use the medium.

Agency means being able to govern what the medium can do on your behalf.

A person can be highly fluent with AI and still possess little agency.

An employee may generate analyses, drafts, code, and plans at great speed while lacking authority to choose priorities, access the necessary data, change the workflow, or act on the result.

The productivity gain is real.

The agency gain accrues partly to the employer.

A founder using the same tools may capture more of the benefit because the founder owns the objective and can immediately convert insight into action.

This is one reason the economic returns to agents will depend heavily on institutional position.

Capability does not distribute authority.

A junior employee can have access to the same frontier model as the chief executive and still be unable to commit the organization to anything consequential.

A small business owner may have weaker technical resources than a large company and still move faster because the owner can decide and act without a chain of approvals.

Agentic leverage multiplies the authority already present in the role.

This can flatten some hierarchies and strengthen others.

It flattens when agents give individuals access to expertise, production, and coordination that previously required organizational status.

It strengthens when decision rights remain concentrated and the people at the top use agents to command a much wider span of execution.

Both can happen simultaneously.

This is why the agentic economy cannot be described only as democratization.

It is a redistribution of leverage whose final shape depends on who owns the bottlenecks.

One bottleneck is domain expertise.

Early evidence from agentic coding suggests that expertise does not simply become irrelevant when agents become more capable. Anthropic’s analysis of hundreds of thousands of coding sessions found that more expert users tended to get more work from the system per instruction and somewhat better outcomes. The result is intuitive.

Experts know where to spend words.

They know what can be left implicit.

They know when an output that looks strange is actually clever and when an output that looks polished is dangerous.

They can specify at the level of invariant rather than keystroke.

This creates compression.

A novice often needs to supervise the path because the novice does not know which deviations matter.

An expert can delegate more because the expert possesses a richer model of acceptable consequence.

Expertise therefore becomes leverageable capital.

This does not mean every expert benefits equally.

Some expertise is tied closely to production that agents can absorb. A professional whose market value came largely from speed at a standard task may face pressure even if the person remains capable.

Other expertise sits in problem framing, rare exceptions, client trust, integration, and judgment. Agents can make those forms more productive.

The market will separate them more sharply.

The agency premium may therefore reward *depth plus delegation* rather than depth alone.

The specialist who insists on personally producing every artifact may underuse the new tools.

The generalist who delegates without enough understanding may create fragile work.

The valuable combination is enough domain depth to govern and enough systems skill to expand beyond personal throughput.

A second bottleneck is taste.

When generation becomes cheap, the market floods with adequacy.

Adequate writing.

Adequate design.

Adequate software.

Adequate analysis.

Adequate outreach.

The average quality floor rises while differentiation becomes harder.

Taste becomes the ability to recognize what deserves to survive abundance.

This can sound elitist because taste is often discussed as mysterious refinement.

Operational taste is more practical.

It is a stable preference for certain tradeoffs under a particular objective.

This interface is simpler than the extra feature is valuable.

This argument needs one more source and one fewer paragraph.

This code is technically elegant and organizationally too expensive to maintain.

This opportunity is exciting and not ours.

This candidate is polished and does not think at the level the role requires.

This campaign will probably convert and make us a company we do not want to become.

Taste is the ability to spend the power to say no.

Agents increase the return on that power by producing more things worth rejecting.

A third bottleneck is distribution.

A person may be able to create a product, publication, course, application, research service, or consulting offer with far less production cost than before.

So can everyone else.

When supply expands, reaching the right people becomes more valuable.

An audience, customer relationship, reputation, channel, trusted community, or distinctive point of view can capture more of the value than the production machinery itself.

This is not new. Cheap publishing already moved scarcity toward attention. Agents accelerate the movement because they lower the cost of creating credible output.

The agency premium therefore belongs partly to whoever can turn output into a relationship.

A fourth bottleneck is proprietary context.

If models become interchangeable at the frontier, the context around them differentiates performance.

A logistics company knows its lanes, exceptions, carrier histories, customer promises, and seasonal failures.

A software company knows its architecture, user behavior, incident history, and unwritten product constraints.

A skilled professional knows the client, the case, the domain, and the standards that matter.

This local knowledge can make the same general intelligence much more useful.

Organizations that own high-quality context and can make it safely available to agents gain leverage that cannot be purchased instantly from a model provider.

A fifth bottleneck is verification infrastructure.

Two companies may generate equally good first-pass work.

One can verify it cheaply.

The other requires expensive manual review.

The first company can delegate more deeply and capture a larger margin.

Verification becomes a productivity asset.

This is why mature agent adoption may widen gaps between firms even when model access equalizes. The frontier model is a shared input. The surrounding operating system is not.

A sixth bottleneck is decision rights.

This may be the most underrated.

A person who can identify the correct action but cannot authorize it has produced potential agency, not realized agency.

Organizations often lose enormous value in the gap between knowing and deciding.

The analysis is complete.

The meeting is scheduled.

The recommendation is circulated.

Three approvals remain.

Agents can make the information phase dramatically faster and thereby make institutional delay more visible.

The competitive advantage may shift toward organizations with clear, bounded decision rights.

If a team knows exactly what it can decide without escalation, agents can turn intelligence into action quickly.

If authority is vague, faster analysis simply reaches the same political bottleneck sooner.

This suggests that organizational design becomes part of AI strategy.

Who is allowed to decide what?

At which risk level?

Using which evidence?

What can be reversed locally?

Which exceptions require central authority?

These questions determine the agency premium more than the number of model licenses purchased.

There is a seventh bottleneck: trust.

Trust is slow to build and fast to spend.

Agents can produce communication at enormous scale. They can personalize, follow up, answer instantly, and maintain relationships across time. The temptation is to use the efficiency fully.

The market may punish exactly that.

When everyone can generate polished outreach, the recipient becomes more suspicious of polish.

When every company can respond instantly, responsiveness stops differentiating.

When every message can be personalized, personalization loses some signaling value.

Human attention given deliberately can become more valuable because it becomes more expensive relative to generated attention.

The agency premium therefore does not mean replacing every human touch.

It means spending human presence where presence carries information the agent cannot cheaply reproduce: commitment, accountability, care, taste, courage, relationship, and the willingness to stand behind a decision.

This creates an interesting labor-market possibility.

Some work will become more machine-mediated and more human-signaling at the same time.

The routine legal draft becomes automated while the client pays more attention to whether the lawyer personally endorses the advice.

The routine analysis becomes automated while the executive values the analyst’s willingness to say the machine-supported consensus is wrong.

The routine lesson becomes personalized by software while the student values a teacher who notices something the system did not know to ask.

The routine customer interaction becomes agentic while the escalation to a person signals that the company recognizes consequence.

Human effort becomes more valuable when intentionally scarce.

This is not romanticism.

It is signaling economics.

When an action is cheap, the fact that someone paid the higher cost can itself communicate importance.

A handwritten note means something partly because it was not the cheapest way to send text.

A senior executive joining the call means something partly because the executive’s time is scarce.

An expert personally reviewing the case signals that the case crossed a threshold.

Agents make such allocation more precise.

This is how human attention can become a premium layer rather than an accidental bottleneck.

The agency premium will not accrue only to individuals.

Companies can earn it.

So can cities, schools, governments, nonprofits, and families.

Any institution that can turn intelligence into reliable action better than peers gains capacity.

A small municipality with clean records, clear decision rights, and well-designed agentic workflows may provide better service than a larger one with fragmented systems and procedural ambiguity.

A school that uses agents to reduce administrative burden while protecting teacher judgment may increase educational agency more than a school that deploys more AI products without changing the underlying work.

A family with an agentic system for managing care, benefits, paperwork, and finances may withstand institutional friction better than one with access to the same models but no organized context.

The premium belongs to the system, not the slogan.

There is a final complication.

Premiums attract imitation.

If a particular orchestration, workflow, or prompt creates unusual advantage, competitors copy it. Vendors productize it. The model learns the pattern. The premium moves again.

Durable agentic advantage will therefore come from resources that compound faster than they commoditize.

Trusted relationships.

Proprietary learning loops.

Accumulated context.

Institutional judgment.

Distribution.

Brand.

Data created through real activity.

A culture that can change its process without losing accountability.

These assets get better through use.

They are difficult to download in one afternoon.

The agency premium is not a permanent tax paid to early adopters.

It is a moving return to whichever scarce capability sits next to abundant intelligence.

That is the strategic discipline of the new economy.

Do not ask only what AI makes cheap.

Ask what becomes expensive because AI made something else cheap.

Then own that.
