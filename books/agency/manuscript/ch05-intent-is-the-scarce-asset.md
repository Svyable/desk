# Intent Is the Scarce Asset

A person can be busy for years without being able to answer a simple question:

What are you trying to make true?

The question sounds embarrassingly basic. Most work arrives already labeled. Reply to this email. Prepare the forecast. Fix the bug. Hire the role. Build the feature. Review the contract. Increase conversion. Reduce costs. Improve retention. Launch by Friday.

These instructions are useful because they point toward action.

They are also dangerous because action can hide the absence of intent.

A task is not an intention.

A task is one proposed method for serving an intention.

The distinction was always important. Agentic systems make it economically unavoidable.

When the cost of execution is high, the person performing the work often has time to rediscover the purpose while doing it. The analyst building a model notices that the metric does not match the decision. The writer preparing the memo realizes the audience cares about a different question. The engineer implementing the feature discovers that the customer’s real problem is elsewhere. Slow production is inefficient, but it creates friction in which hidden assumptions can become visible.

When execution becomes cheap, a bad instruction can travel much farther before anybody notices.

The agent can produce the forecast, fix the bug, draft the contract, launch the campaign, summarize the meetings, generate the code, and populate the dashboard with admirable speed.

The wrong objective becomes operational.

This is why intent is one of the scarce assets of the agentic economy.

Intent is not a sentence that begins with “I want.”

It is a hierarchy.

At the top sits the state of the world you care about.

Below it sit constraints: what you are unwilling to trade away to get there.

Below those sit strategies: the approaches that seem likely to work.

Below those sit tasks.

Most organizations communicate from the bottom up because tasks are easy to assign and easy to measure.

Agentic leverage works better from the top down.

Consider a company trying to reduce customer churn.

The task might be: email every customer whose usage fell this month.

The strategy might be: intervene early when disengagement appears.

The constraint might be: do not damage trust with manipulative or excessive outreach.

The intention might be: help customers continue receiving enough value from the product that staying is rational.

These statements are not interchangeable.

An agent given only the task can optimize the email.

An agent given the strategy can explore several forms of intervention.

An agent given the constraint can avoid a class of harmful success.

An agent given the intention can notice that some customers should probably leave because the product no longer serves them.

That last conclusion may reduce the short-term churn metric and improve the long-term business.

Or the opposite.

The point is not that the agent will magically infer the wise answer once the mission is written poetically. The point is that the quality of delegated action depends on which layer of the objective survives transmission.

This is familiar to anyone who has managed people well.

A weak manager delegates tasks and then complains when the subordinate does exactly what was asked.

A stronger manager explains the purpose, the constraints, and the decision rights, then leaves room for the person closest to the work to adapt.

The problem is that good management is expensive.

It takes attention to articulate intent. Many leaders avoid it by staying close enough to the work that they can correct direction through constant interaction.

Agentic systems expose the cost of that habit.

If every digital worker needs the principal to answer hundreds of micro-questions, the principal has not gained much leverage.

The machine is fast.

The organization is still waiting for the same person.

To scale agency, intent has to become more portable.

Portable intent is not a giant instruction manual.

It is the minimum context required for local decisions to preserve what matters.

This is difficult because human beings often do not know their own intent until a choice forces it into view.

A founder says, “We need to grow.”

Does growth mean revenue, users, cash flow, market share, valuation, geographic reach, organizational capability, or survival long enough to discover the business?

A manager says, “Make the team more efficient.”

Does efficiency mean lower cost, shorter cycle time, fewer errors, lower burnout, less management attention, or more capacity for unplanned work?

A person says, “Help me be more productive.”

Does productivity mean more completed tasks, more money, more creative output, fewer obligations, more time with family, or the ability to stop carrying work mentally after dinner?

Agents will happily optimize vague nouns.

That is precisely the problem.

Fluency can make ambiguity look solved.

The system produces a plan. The plan contains milestones. The milestones contain metrics. The metrics create motion. Nobody notices that the original objective never became specific enough to choose among tradeoffs.

In the old economy, unclear intent was often absorbed by human social systems. Employees asked colleagues. Managers interpreted the boss. People learned what “quality” meant by watching what got praised and what got rejected. A company’s real strategy lived partly in hallway conversations and remembered decisions.

In the agentic economy, unspoken intent becomes a tax.

Either the agent guesses, the principal keeps re-entering, or the organization externalizes enough of its judgment that the work can proceed.

This is one reason decision logs will become more important than meeting notes.

Meeting notes record what was discussed.

Decision logs record what became true because of the discussion.

What was decided?

Why?

Which assumption mattered?

What would cause the decision to be revisited?

What constraint was intentionally preserved?

This information is more valuable to an agent than a transcript of twelve people circling the question for forty minutes.

The transcript is information.

The decision log is intent made portable.

The same applies at an individual level.

Imagine an agent that helps manage a person’s professional commitments. The system can read email, calendar, documents, and messages. It sees invitations, requests, deadlines, opportunities, and obligations.

Without intent, the agent becomes a more efficient servant of incoming demand.

It keeps the calendar full.

It answers faster.

It reminds more reliably.

It makes the person excellent at living other people’s priorities.

A good personal agent needs negative intent as much as positive intent.

What do you not want your life to become?

Which commitments are you protecting against?

Which projects are allowed to expand?

Which are deliberately bounded?

What kinds of opportunity are attractive but strategically irrelevant?

What promises should never be made on your behalf without explicit approval?

These are not preferences in the superficial sense.

They are the shape of agency.

A person who has not articulated them may discover that automation increases responsiveness while decreasing authorship of life.

The same is true for companies.

Agentic leverage does not begin with the list of tasks suitable for automation.

It begins with the list of outcomes the organization is actually willing to own.

This becomes especially important because capable agents can make weak strategies look productive.

A company can automate outbound sales around a bad positioning decision.

It can produce thousands of personalized messages that faithfully scale a misunderstanding of the customer.

It can generate content around a brand proposition nobody believes.

It can optimize advertising toward a market the product should not serve.

Traditional inefficiency sometimes limits the damage of a weak strategy.

Agentic leverage removes that mercy.

This is why speed can increase the value of reflection.

The old choice was often between thinking and doing because both consumed the same people’s hours.

When agents absorb more execution, the principal can spend more time clarifying intent.

That sounds like a luxury until one sees the economics.

If a ten-minute instruction triggers ten hours of machine work, improving the instruction is no longer ten minutes of overhead.

It is capital allocation.

The principal should therefore become better at distinguishing goals from proxies.

A proxy is a measurable signal correlated with the thing you care about.

Organizations need proxies because many important outcomes are slow, ambiguous, or difficult to observe. Revenue is a proxy for value creation. Test coverage is a proxy for software confidence. Customer satisfaction is a proxy for relationship quality. Response time is a proxy for service. Employee retention is a proxy for organizational health.

The danger appears when the agent can optimize the proxy more directly than the world can deliver the underlying value.

If the system is asked to maximize support resolution rate, it may discover behaviors that increase the number without improving support.

If it is asked to maximize engagement, it may increase attention without increasing wellbeing.

If it is asked to reduce hiring time, it may narrow evaluation in ways that make the organization faster and worse.

If it is asked to maximize the number of experiments, it can create an experiment factory that consumes more attention than it creates insight.

Humans game metrics too.

Agents simply change the speed and search space of the game.

One discipline helps: pair every important metric with the reason the metric exists.

Not because the agent needs a motivational speech.

Because the reason defines what should count as suspicious success.

“Reduce average support response time so customers get useful help before frustration compounds.”

Now a response that is fast but useless is visibly outside the intent.

“Increase qualified sales conversations so we can learn which customers have a painful problem we can solve profitably.”

Now empty calendar volume is not success.

“Reduce review time on low-risk code changes so senior engineers can spend attention on architecture, security, and unusual behavior.”

Now eliminating review everywhere would violate the purpose of the initiative.

Portable intent creates local stop conditions.

It lets the system recognize when apparent progress is actually tradeoff destruction.

The principal also needs to distinguish preferences from principles.

Preferences are often cheap to violate.

Use this airline if the price difference is small.

Write in this format if convenient.

Schedule mornings when possible.

Principles define identity or unacceptable consequence.

Do not send confidential data outside approved systems.

Do not claim evidence we do not have.

Do not commit money above this threshold without approval.

Do not misrepresent uncertainty to close the sale.

Do not change production data while investigating a bug.

A mature agentic system should not treat these categories as equally negotiable.

Humans infer the difference partly through culture.

Agents need it represented in permissions, policies, examples, tests, and escalation logic.

This is where intent meets infrastructure.

The principal cannot rely on prose alone to carry everything.

The system needs hard boundaries where the cost of misinterpretation is high.

That is not a contradiction.

Human organizations do the same thing.

A company can say, “We value responsible spending,” and still configure an approval threshold. The value is soft. The authority boundary is hard.

Agentic leverage works when the soft and hard layers reinforce one another.

The soft layer gives the agent enough purpose to handle ordinary ambiguity.

The hard layer protects the principal when interpretation fails.

This also explains why the best agentic users may spend less time prompting than beginners.

Beginners often write long instructions because every task begins from zero. The prompt carries context, preferences, examples, constraints, and formatting requirements repeatedly.

Mature systems externalize durable intent.

The project has a brief.

The repository has conventions.

The company has policies.

The customer record has history.

The team has a decision log.

The agent has access only to the tools appropriate for the role.

The prompt can become shorter because the environment remembers what the organization already decided.

This is not merely convenience.

It is compounding agency.

Every clarified decision makes the next delegation cheaper.

Every reusable standard reduces future explanation.

Every preserved rationale gives the system better context for an exception.

Every explicit boundary reduces the number of moments that require live supervision.

Intent becomes infrastructure.

This creates a new kind of managerial asset.

A company with clear intent can adopt better agents faster because its knowledge is already shaped for delegation.

A company whose real strategy exists only in the founder’s head can buy the same models and receive less leverage. Every workflow keeps returning to the founder for interpretation.

The founder becomes the context API.

That works until it does not.

The strange possibility is that agentic systems may force some organizations to become more articulate about themselves.

Not more verbose.

More explicit about what they will and will not trade.

That could improve human work as much as machine work.

Employees benefit from knowing which objectives are real, which metrics are proxies, which decisions are reversible, and which constraints are sacred. Much organizational frustration comes from being told to optimize one thing and punished for ignoring another thing that was never stated.

A good delegation surface makes hidden tradeoffs visible.

The machine is not the only beneficiary.

There is a warning here.

Once intent becomes an artifact, people may confuse the artifact with the intent.

A strategy document can become stale. A policy can preserve yesterday’s fear. A preference can outlive the person who expressed it. An agent can follow a beautifully written mission while the market has changed underneath it.

Portable intent must therefore remain revisable.

The principal’s most important power is not to specify the objective once.

It is to change the objective when reality teaches something new.

This is why agency is not merely optimization.

Optimization assumes the target is given.

Agency includes choosing the target.

The distinction is small in language and enormous in life.

A system can become extraordinarily good at getting you where you said you wanted to go.

You still need the ability to decide that the destination was wrong.
