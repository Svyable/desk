## Cross-chapter claim guardrails

### Agent adoption

Use S01 only for a dated 2025/2026 adoption picture. Do not turn “single-digit deployment across nearly all business functions” into a permanent structural claim.

### Time horizons

Use S03 with METR’s own caveats: the tasks are primarily software engineering, ML, and cybersecurity; they are cleaner than ordinary jobs; a human-equivalent time horizon is not elapsed autonomous runtime or a percent of jobs automated.

### Expertise

Use S04 as observational product-usage evidence. Do not infer that formal software training is unnecessary for every coding setting or that expertise causally produces all of the success difference.

### Productivity

Use S05 and S06 within their studied task environments. Do not generalize 14%, 25%, 40%, or other study-specific effect sizes to “knowledge work” as a whole.

### Software output bottlenecks

Use S08 as a recent institutional summary of research. The book’s durable claim is bottleneck migration, not any one percentage increase in coding activity.

### Labor-market effects

Use S01 cautiously. The AI Index reports patterns, including weaker outcomes for some younger workers in exposed occupations. The manuscript does not claim AI is the sole causal mechanism.

### Principal-agent theory

S10 supports the historical vocabulary of agency costs. Machine agents do not have human incentives, private consumption, or career motives. The book’s machine-agent extension concerns misalignment between instruction, context, proxy, authority, and principal intention.

### Human factors

S12 supports the historical pattern in which automation can leave humans responsible for exceptional conditions while reducing normal direct involvement. Modern language-model agents are not industrial control systems; the manuscript treats this as a recurring design problem rather than identity of mechanisms.
