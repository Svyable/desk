# Agency — research brief

This folder is the research trail for *Agency: Maximizing Your Agentic Leverage in the New Economy* by Sven Hardy Benson.

Research freeze for this Desk edition: **2026-09-04**.

## Research question

The manuscript asks a narrower question than “How capable will AI become?”

It asks what becomes economically scarce when increasingly capable models can plan and act through tools, and what people and institutions need to preserve in order to turn that capability into useful leverage without surrendering principal control.

The resulting thesis is that the economic advantage of agents is not merely faster cognitive production. Agents lower the transaction cost of delegation by handling more of the path between intention and execution. As intelligence and execution become cheaper, scarcity migrates toward intent, judgment, verification, permission, trustworthy context, decision rights, legitimacy, and the ability to exit a dependency.

The book calls the useful conversion of intention into delegated consequence **agentic leverage**.

## Evidence strategy

The manuscript deliberately combines three classes of evidence.

### 1. Current empirical AI evidence

The 2026 Stanford AI Index, METR time-horizon work, Anthropic’s large-scale Claude Code usage analysis, METR’s maintainer review study, the BCG/HBS jagged-frontier experiment, the NBER/QJE customer-support field study, and recent software-productivity research summarized by MIT Sloan provide evidence for the moving technical and organizational frontier.

These sources are used for claims such as:

- organizational AI use is widespread while scaled agent deployment remains early;
- frontier agents can complete increasingly long well-specified technical tasks, but METR explicitly warns against reading this as automation of ordinary jobs;
- domain expertise can remain valuable in agentic coding even as coding execution becomes more accessible;
- test-passing agent code can fail richer maintainer standards;
- AI productivity gains can be large in specific workflows and heterogeneous across workers and tasks;
- faster upstream production can expose downstream human bottlenecks.

These claims are deliberately scoped to the studies that support them.

### 2. Older economics and organization theory

Coase supplies the transaction-cost lens for the boundary of the firm. Jensen and Meckling supply the agency-cost tradition. Herbert Simon supplies the scarcity-of-attention argument used as an analogy for abundance shifting scarcity into a complementary resource.

The manuscript extends these ideas; it does not pretend the original authors wrote about modern AI agents. Phrases such as “actions consume governance,” “agency debt,” “agentic leverage,” “the sovereignty premium,” and “the agency dividend” are original conceptual framing in this manuscript.

### 3. Human factors and governance

Bainbridge’s *Ironies of Automation* supports the longstanding observation that highly automated systems can leave humans responsible for abnormal situations while reducing the direct practice needed to handle them. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework / Generative AI Profile supports a lifecycle and context-sensitive risk-management approach.

The manuscript uses these sources to resist two symmetrical mistakes: assuming that a human click creates meaningful control, and assuming that capable automation removes the need for accountable institutional design.

## What the evidence does not prove

The manuscript is a trade-nonfiction argument, not a claim that a single causal model of the agentic economy has already been empirically established.

Current evidence does **not** prove:

- that agents will automate a fixed percentage of jobs by a particular date;
- that time-horizon trends will extrapolate indefinitely;
- that every firm will become smaller;
- that “one-person companies” will dominate the economy;
- that experts will always benefit more than novices;
- that productivity gains observed in customer support, consulting, or software generalize mechanically to other work;
- that open model portability or multi-vendor architecture is always worth its cost;
- that human review is inherently safer than automated verification;
- that the concepts introduced in the manuscript are directly measurable as single numerical variables.

Where the book moves beyond empirical evidence, it marks the move as analysis, mechanism, or scenario rather than historical fact.

## Falsification and revision notes

The thesis should be revised materially if several of the following become true.

### F1 — Verification cost collapses as fast as generation cost

A central claim is that verification becomes a binding complement when generation and action become cheap. If agents become able to produce strongly trustworthy, independently checkable evidence for most consequential knowledge work at negligible cost, the verification bottleneck would weaken. The book’s emphasis would move toward intention, legitimacy, and physical constraints.

### F2 — Model capability becomes so uniform that task-level jaggedness stops mattering economically

The manuscript assumes that capability remains uneven enough that workflow-specific evidence matters. If future systems become uniformly reliable across ordinary cognitive work, the “jagged authority frontier” becomes less important, though permission and legitimacy remain.

### F3 — Agent deployment remains a niche interface pattern

The argument assumes that systems able to pursue goals through tools become a meaningful production layer. If organizations overwhelmingly reject agentic execution and keep AI in recommendation-only roles, the transaction-cost and firm-boundary claims would need to be narrowed substantially.

### F4 — Deep model/platform lock-in proves economically trivial

The sovereignty-premium argument assumes that memory, context, permissions, and orchestration can create material switching costs. If durable portability becomes effortless and standardized across providers, the premium shrinks and competition among intelligence suppliers strengthens.

### F5 — Apprenticeship reorganizes without a training gap

The apprenticeship-reversal chapter predicts a risk rather than an inevitability. If firms, schools, professional associations, and agent products rapidly create effective deliberate-practice pathways that maintain expert pipelines while entry-level production falls, the chapter should become a story of successful institutional adaptation rather than warning.

### F6 — Agentic productivity does not survive system-level accounting

If repeated high-quality studies find that agentic tools raise local activity while having negligible or negative effects on accepted, deployed, customer-valued, or economy-level output after all downstream costs are included, the book should reduce the scope of its leverage claims.

## Volatile claims requiring refresh at future release

Before a later Shelf edition, refresh at minimum:

1. Stanford AI Index organizational agent adoption and labor-market figures.
2. METR task-completion time-horizon measurements and methodology notes.
3. Anthropic or comparable cross-provider evidence about real-world agentic work and expertise.
4. Agent coding verification / maintainer acceptance evidence.
5. Software development research separating coding activity from accepted or launched output.
6. Any legal or regulatory language added in later revisions; this first edition stays mostly at the governance-mechanism level to avoid dating the book to one jurisdictional moment.

## Editorial sourcing rules

- Concrete empirical claims map to sources in `chapter-source-map.md`.
- The fuller bibliographic and caveat record is in `source-registry.md`.
- No fabricated quotation, statistic, case, or attribution is permitted.
- A provider-authored study is labeled as such in the research record and not treated as neutral causal proof.
- A benchmark is not described as a job-automation percentage unless the source itself supports that interpretation; METR explicitly warns against it.
- Current labor-market observations are not presented as clean causal effects of AI where the cited source does not establish causality.
- Historical theory is used as theory. The manuscript’s AI extensions remain the author’s analysis.

## Research status

The Desk draft has a source trail adequate for its current concrete claims and four field-test chapters that make the empirical boundary visible. A future scholarly or heavily footnoted edition could add a larger labor-economics literature, organizational-design case studies, law/regulation by jurisdiction, and more primary company evidence without changing the central thesis.
