# The Belt We Made

## Starfish Prime and the Day Humanity Changed Near-Earth Space

**Sven Hardy Benson**

### Thesis

On July 9, 1962, the United States detonated a 1.4-megaton nuclear device roughly 400 kilometers above the Pacific. The blast did more than make an aurora and produce an electromagnetic pulse. It injected energetic electrons into Earth's magnetic field strongly enough to create an artificial radiation belt that persisted far longer than many planners expected and contributed to the degradation or loss of satellites already in orbit. Soviet high-altitude nuclear tests later that year produced additional artificial belts. For a brief Cold War interval, states were not merely placing machines in near-Earth space. They were changing the environment those machines had to survive.

The book's central claim is deliberately narrower than its title sounds. Starfish Prime was not proof that humans can casually engineer a planet. It was proof that a technological system can cross a threshold where its side effects become geophysical. The experiment collided with a space age still discovering the Van Allen belts, with electronics that had not been designed for an anthropogenic radiation environment, with military interest in EMP and communications effects, and with a treaty regime that shut down further atmospheric and outer-space nuclear testing the following year. The result is a rare historical control specimen for a question that now matters again: what does it mean when the operating environment itself becomes something humans can modify?

### Evidence classes

1. **Verified / declassified / documented** — Operation Fishbowl; Starfish Prime's date, altitude and announced yield; measured artificial trapped-electron populations; satellite degradation and failures linked by contemporary investigators to enhanced radiation; U.S. and Soviet high-altitude nuclear testing; the 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty.
2. **Credible but incomplete** — exact attribution of every satellite anomaly to Starfish rather than pre-existing vulnerability or other space-weather effects; the precise contribution of EMP to every reported Hawaiian electrical failure; retrospective counts of satellites "lost" because different sources use different causal thresholds.
3. **Disputed / unconfirmed** — maximalist claims that Starfish nearly collapsed an entire electrical grid, that every satellite failure in the period was caused by the test, or that high-altitude tests demonstrated a practical way to permanently deny orbital regions.
4. **Speculative synthesis / open conjecture** — that Starfish should be treated as an early accidental planetary-engineering experiment; that modern orbital civilization has already entered an era in which anthropogenic environmental modification can matter as much as hardware design; that the most useful lesson is not about nuclear EMP but about crossing from device engineering into environment engineering.

### Reader promise

This is not an EMP survival manual and not a nuclear-weapons book in disguise. It is a book about the moment the sky stopped being scenery. It follows one strange experiment outward: from Johnston Island to Hawaii, from beta-decay electrons to Telstar's wounded transistors, from Explorer XV to Soviet tests, from a burst lasting fractions of a second to particles that stayed for years. The odd fact earns the space.
