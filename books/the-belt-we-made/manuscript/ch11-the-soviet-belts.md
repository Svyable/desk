# 11. The Soviet Belts

Starfish is often told as an American singularity. It was not.

The Soviet Union conducted high-altitude nuclear tests in late 1962 that also injected energetic electrons into the magnetosphere and produced artificial radiation belts. Later NASA reviews compared the locations and decay behavior of U.S. and Soviet-created belts, including Soviet tests on October 22, October 28, and November 1.

This matters for two reasons.

First, it prevents a moral simplification in which one country's reckless experiment is treated as the entire phenomenon. The superpowers were probing the same physical environment under the same strategic pressure.

Second, it creates comparative physics.

Different burst locations and yields produced different trapped populations. Some Soviet-created belts decayed over weeks rather than the multiyear tail associated with Starfish's inner-zone injection. The magnetosphere responded differently because the geometry was different.

That comparative evidence is one reason we know Starfish's persistence was not simply "what any nuclear explosion in space does." Location in magnetic coordinates mattered.

The Soviet tests also complicate satellite attribution. A spacecraft operating through the second half of 1962 could encounter a radiation environment shaped by natural belts, Starfish electrons, solar activity, and later Soviet injections. A tidy one-event causal story is not always possible.

The temptation in Cold War history is to make secrecy the explanation for everything we cannot see. Here secrecy is real, but physics provides a better organizing principle.

Two adversarial states altered the same planetary system. The magnetic field did not care which flag launched the warhead.
