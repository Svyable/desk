# 16. Space Weather With a Human Signature

NASA now uses the phrase space weather for the changing conditions produced by solar activity and their effects on Earth's near-space environment and technology.

Starfish creates a grammatical problem.

The event looked like space weather in its consequences—radiation, aurora, geomagnetic disturbance, communication effects—but its source was human.

NASA has explicitly discussed Cold War high-altitude nuclear tests as human-linked space-weather events. That framing is more than a public-outreach curiosity. It places Starfish inside a larger category: anthropogenic forcing of geophysical systems.

The category is increasingly useful.

Orbital debris is an anthropogenic environment. Radio-frequency interference is an anthropogenic environment. Light pollution changes the astronomical environment. Greenhouse gases change the climate system. Large constellations change sky brightness and collision statistics. None is equivalent to an artificial radiation belt, but all share the property that infrastructure modifies the environment that later infrastructure must inhabit.

This is where the phrase planetary engineering becomes tempting and dangerous.

Starfish was not a controlled attempt to optimize Earth's magnetosphere. Calling it geoengineering can imply more mastery than existed. The event is better understood as accidental planetary engineering: a technological action whose side effects became geophysical in scale.

The distinction matters because the next era of planetary engineering may also arrive by accident.

We may notice only after the environment has become part of the machine.
