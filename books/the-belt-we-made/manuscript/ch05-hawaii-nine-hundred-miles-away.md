# 5. Hawaii, Nine Hundred Miles Away

The most famous terrestrial story of Starfish is about streetlights.

Honolulu was far from the detonation. Yet strings of streetlights failed around the time of the burst. Other electrical disturbances were reported. The event became a canonical historical example in discussions of high-altitude electromagnetic pulse.

Then the story inflated.

In some retellings, "streetlights failed" became "the Hawaiian grid went down." In others, the event is used as a direct analogue for what a modern high-altitude burst would do to a vastly more electronic society. The first is an exaggeration. The second can be a legitimate starting point only if the enormous differences in infrastructure, geometry, device characteristics, grid topology, hardening, and system dependence are made explicit.

Historical evidence should not be forced to carry more than it can.

A later Oak Ridge analysis revisited the Hawaiian failures and supported EMP as the likely cause of particular streetlight-circuit damage. That is strong evidence for an effect. It is not evidence for every apocalyptic scenario built from the effect.

This distinction is not pedantic. It is the difference between engineering and mythology.

Starfish is useful because there was an actual event. We can ask what failed, what did not, over what distances, with what circuit geometries, under what infrastructure conditions. We can compare contemporary records to later models. We can identify where evidence is direct and where a modern vulnerability claim is extrapolation.

The discipline of scale is especially important because EMP occupies a cultural niche where technical vocabulary and catastrophe imagination overlap. Terms such as E1, E2, and E3 entered public discourse long after 1962. They describe different temporal components and coupling mechanisms. A single historical anecdote about Honolulu cannot validate every claim made under the umbrella word EMP.

The event still matters.

An explosion hundreds of kilometers above Earth produced electrical effects on infrastructure more than a thousand kilometers away. That is already a violation of ordinary intuition. The important thing is not to make it bigger. The important thing is to understand exactly how strange the verified version is.
