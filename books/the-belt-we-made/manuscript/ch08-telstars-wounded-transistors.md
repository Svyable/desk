# 8. Telstar's Wounded Transistors

Telstar 1 is the case that turns a large geopolitical event into something small enough to hold between two fingers: a damaged transistor.

Launched on July 10, 1962, just after Starfish Prime, Telstar became one of the icons of the communications-satellite age. It relayed television, telephone, and data across the Atlantic. It also flew into a radiation environment that had been radically altered the day before.

In November, Telstar suffered loss of command function. Bell Laboratories engineers investigated. Their 1963 analysis concluded that the command-system malfunction was caused by surface damage to transistors in the redundant command decoders from the enhanced radiation of the inner Van Allen belt.

That document matters because it is not folklore written decades later. It is an engineering failure analysis written by people trying to recover a spacecraft.

The system behaved strangely before it died cleanly. Engineers found ways to work around parts of the failure. They changed command procedures. They exploited differences in ground-station geometry. Some decoder function recovered, then problems returned. Radiation damage was not a cinematic instant in which the satellite went dark. It was degradation, diagnosis, adaptation, partial recovery, and renewed failure.

This is what environmental damage often looks like in real systems.

The transistor is also a useful antidote to the phrase "space was irradiated." That phrase is too large to be explanatory. The actual chain ran from a nuclear detonation to an artificial trapped-electron population to accumulated radiation exposure to semiconductor surface damage to a vulnerable command circuit to operational workarounds and eventual loss.

Every link can be investigated.

That is why Telstar is stronger evidence than a dramatic anecdote about satellites disappearing. The smaller story is more powerful because it closes.
