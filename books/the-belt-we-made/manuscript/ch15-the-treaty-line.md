# Chapter 15 — The Treaty Line

On August 5, 1963, the United States, Soviet Union and United Kingdom signed the Treaty Banning Nuclear Weapon Tests in the Atmosphere, in Outer Space and Under Water. It entered into force on October 10.

The treaty did not mention Argus, Starfish, Telstar or artificial radiation belts by name. It did not need to. By prohibiting nuclear explosions in outer space, it closed the experimental route that had created them.

The Limited Test Ban Treaty is usually remembered through fallout and atmospheric testing. That is correct and incomplete. It is also one of the earliest durable legal boundaries placed around human modification of the space environment by weapons testing.

The verification logic was important. Underground tests remained difficult because distinguishing and inspecting seismic events created political disputes. Atmospheric, underwater and outer-space explosions were more observable at distance. The State Department’s treaty history notes that by 1963 both sides could accept a ban in environments where national technical means could police compliance more readily.

This is an inversion of the usual secrecy story. The global nature of the effect helped make prohibition verifiable.

A high-altitude nuclear explosion is difficult to keep private from a mature monitoring system. Optical signatures, electromagnetic disturbances, radiation injection and orbital consequences create evidence across domains. The very property that made Argus attractive—the use of Earth’s field as a global medium—also made repeated testing hard to hide indefinitely.

The treaty did not make outer space demilitarized. It did not prohibit all military satellites. It did not eliminate nuclear weapons. Four years later the Outer Space Treaty would prohibit placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit or stationing them in outer space, while leaving many military activities untouched.

Still, the line matters.

Argus belongs to a short historical interval when nuclear powers could treat near-Earth space as both laboratory and proving ground. The Limited Test Ban Treaty ended that interval for its parties.

There is a useful governance lesson here. Regulation sometimes follows catastrophe. Sometimes it follows demonstration. The artificial belts had shown enough.
