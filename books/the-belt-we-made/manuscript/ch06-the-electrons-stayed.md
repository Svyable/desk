# 6. The Electrons Stayed

The explosion was brief. The electrons were not.

This is where Starfish stops being mainly a story about nuclear testing and becomes a story about a modified planetary environment.

The artificial belt consisted largely of energetic electrons associated with the radioactive decay of fission products and their subsequent trapping in Earth's magnetic field. The particles did not simply radiate outward in a spherical shell until they became irrelevant. Magnetic trapping turned a transient source into a persistent population.

NASA's Wilmot Hess published early analyses of the artificial belt. Later measurements followed its structure and decay. Work by D. S. Beall, C. O. Bostrom, and D. J. Williams tracked the Starfish belt from 1963 into 1965; other NASA reporting documented artificial electrons into 1966 and later historical reviews described residual populations lasting still longer in parts of the inner zone.

The exact phrase "lasted for years" is therefore safer than a theatrical claim that the belt "stayed for a decade." Detectability depends on energy, location, instrument sensitivity, and what one means by the belt. The point does not need inflation.

Years is enough.

A system planner thinking in milliseconds had changed a system whose response had a multiyear tail.

That temporal mismatch is one of the most important facts in the book. Technology often creates externalities because the timescale of decision and the timescale of consequence are different. A chemical discharge takes minutes and contaminates sediment for decades. A carbon emission takes seconds and perturbs climate statistics for far longer. A software decision can create a standard that ossifies for years.

Starfish belongs to the same family of mismatch, but in a place where human beings had barely begun to operate.

The test did not merely cause an event in space. It changed the boundary conditions for everything else already there.
