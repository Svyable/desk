# Chapter 7 — Explorer IV Finds the Evidence

Explorer IV was already in orbit when Argus began. It had launched on July 26, 1958, carrying radiation detectors designed by Van Allen’s group. The timing turned the satellite into something more than a survey instrument. It became a witness to a deliberate planetary perturbation.

The 1959 paper by Van Allen, McIlwain and Ludwig is wonderfully unromantic about this. The detectors “easily and promptly observed” the trapped electrons from all three Argus detonations. More than 160 passes through the artificial shells supplied profiles of intensity and evolution.

There is no need to inflate the result. The sentence is remarkable enough.

A nuclear test was conducted from a ship in the South Atlantic. A small satellite repeatedly flew through the radiation structure created by the explosions. The detector output traced human causation in near-Earth space.

The experiment therefore had something most environmental interventions lack: a before, an injection time, a known source and repeated measurements afterward. Natural magnetospheric variability complicated the picture, but source attribution was unusually strong. Scientists did not have to infer whether a particular shell had been produced by solar activity or by an unknown event. They knew when the bomb went off.

This made Argus a primitive tracer experiment at planetary scale.

The artificial electrons also helped researchers study particle motion. Their energy spectrum and temporal evolution could be compared with theoretical expectations. Changes in intensity helped constrain loss processes. Spatial observations connected local measurements to magnetic coordinates.

It would be too neat to say weapons research accidentally created magnetospheric physics. Van Allen’s work was already underway, and natural radiation belts had been discovered before the test. But Argus supplied a perturbation experiment almost immediately after the field’s birth.

The relationship between classified operation and open science was uneasy. Details of the tests remained secret initially, while scientific observations circulated through conferences and papers in partial form. In 1959 the operation became public. Van Allen’s papers could then discuss the artificial injections directly.

The result complicates simple stories about secrecy strangling science. Secrecy did restrict information and distort timing. Yet the program also funded measurements and created data that entered open geophysics. The same institutions could withhold and produce knowledge at once.

This is a recurring feature of military science. The public record after declassification often looks oddly discontinuous because parts of the evidence were produced under different disclosure rules. A technical paper may describe an effect without explaining the strategic question that motivated the experiment. An official history may describe the strategic question decades later without reproducing the raw scientific data. Reconstructing the whole event requires joining records that were never designed to sit together.

Explorer IV is the cleanest bridge.

Its detectors did not care why the electrons were there. They counted particles.
