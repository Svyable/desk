# Chapter 13 — The Soviet Belts

The United States did not own the idea of changing near-Earth radiation with nuclear explosions.

In 1962 the Soviet Union conducted high-altitude nuclear tests that also injected energetic electrons into the magnetosphere. NASA’s later review of artificial radiation belts treats the Soviet events and American tests in one physical taxonomy: low-yield injections forming relatively narrow shells, high-yield events distributing electrons across broader regions.

This matters because it turns artificial radiation belts from an American curiosity into a Cold War environmental phenomenon.

The Soviet tests are less familiar in American popular history partly because the documentary record is different. American technical literature could measure their consequences without possessing complete operational details. Once again the magnetosphere acted as shared evidence. A state could conceal the device design or precise objective, but not every consequence of putting energetic charged particles into a planetary magnetic field.

That asymmetry became relevant to arms control. High-altitude tests had detection signatures. Satellites, radio systems and geomagnetic instruments could observe effects. Verification did not require inspectors standing beside the launch vehicle.

The 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty emerged from a much larger negotiation over nuclear testing, fallout and verification. It prohibited nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, underwater and in outer space. The treaty did not require negotiators to solve every technical ambiguity of high-altitude effects. It simply closed the environment to that class of test for parties.

The Soviet belts also complicate any story that treats Starfish as a singular warning followed immediately by enlightenment. Both superpowers were conducting large atmospheric and high-altitude test programs in 1962. Knowledge of risk and willingness to test coexisted.

This coexistence is common in technological history. Evidence does not automatically produce restraint. It changes the argument over restraint.

By late 1962, there was no longer serious room to claim that high-altitude nuclear explosions were purely local events. Artificial trapped radiation had been measured repeatedly. Spacecraft damage had occurred. The physics was increasingly public.

The question was becoming political: whether near-Earth space would remain a nuclear test range.
