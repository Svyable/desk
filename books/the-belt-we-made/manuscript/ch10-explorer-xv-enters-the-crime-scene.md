# 10. Explorer XV Enters the Crime Scene

NASA's answer to the artificial belt was beautifully literal: launch something into it.

Explorer XV went up on October 27, 1962, with experiments designed to measure the new trapped-radiation environment. The spacecraft's orbit was chosen to cross the relevant regions, and the instruments were intended to characterize particle distributions, injection, trapping, and loss.

The speed of the response is one of the least appreciated parts of the story.

A nuclear test had changed the environment. Existing satellites were degrading. Scientists needed measurements faster than the normal cadence of a space program liked to provide them. Explorer XV became a crash investigation conducted in orbit.

The metaphor should not be stretched too far. The artificial belt was not a static wreckage field. It was evolving. Every delay changed the thing being measured.

That makes the mission epistemically interesting. Explorer XV was trying to reconstruct a transient planetary perturbation while still inside it.

This is a recurring problem in anomaly science. By the time an unexpected event is recognized as important, the event has already changed. Investigators arrive late. Instruments were not optimized for the question. Baselines are imperfect. Causal variables are entangled.

The best response is often not a grand theory but a new sensor.

Explorer XV embodied that instinct.

The mission also caught something history had not scheduled neatly: Soviet high-altitude tests in October and November 1962 produced additional artificial radiation populations. The United States had launched a spacecraft to study the residue of its own experiment and found itself measuring a planet being perturbed by both superpowers.

That is not a metaphor. It is the geometry of the data.
