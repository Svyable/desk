# 9. Ariel, TRAAC, Transit

Telstar is famous because communications satellites are easy to romanticize. The less famous casualties are more useful for understanding the breadth of the environmental problem.

Ariel I, the first British satellite, was already in orbit when Starfish occurred. Transit IVB and TRAAC were U.S. navigation and research spacecraft. Contemporary NASA reporting said all three were seriously affected within a month by the enhanced radiation environment.

The effects were not identical.

Solar-cell degradation mattered. Electronic vulnerability mattered. Orbit mattered. The phrase "within a month" is itself important because it shows the mechanism was not simply prompt electromagnetic pulse striking every spacecraft at detonation. A persistent radiation environment kept administering dose after the flash was gone.

This separates two stories that are routinely collapsed.

Starfish produced EMP effects on Earth and radiation-belt effects in space. Both came from the same detonation. They had different coupling mechanisms and different timescales. Confusing them makes historical analysis easier and engineering analysis worse.

Ariel, TRAAC, and Transit force the distinction because their degradation played out over time.

There is another uncomfortable feature. These spacecraft were not participants in the weapon test. They were third-party infrastructure occupying the same planetary environment.

That makes Starfish one of the earliest demonstrations of an orbital commons problem. An action by one state altered conditions encountered by other systems. The space age was only a few years old and it had already discovered externalities.
