# Chapter 11 — Starfish

On July 9, 1962, the United States detonated Starfish Prime roughly 400 kilometers above the Pacific near Johnston Island. The yield was about 1.4 megatons. Compared with Argus, this was not a larger version of the same experiment so much as a change of regime.

The blast produced spectacular optical effects across the Pacific. Electromagnetic pulse caused failures in Hawaii. For this book, the quieter consequence matters most: the explosion injected energetic electrons that became trapped in Earth’s magnetic field and formed an intense artificial radiation belt.

NASA’s later technical histories emphasize that the magnitude was not expected. One 1965 NASA account states that predictions anticipated a temporary increase in the natural belts but not radiation as intense as Starfish produced. Within a month, Ariel I, Transit IVB and TRAAC were seriously affected.

This is where the thought experiment became infrastructure damage.

Argus had shown that artificial belts could be formed. Starfish showed that an artificial belt could outlive the test event long enough to interact destructively with satellites that had nothing to do with the detonation.

The satellite population was tiny by modern standards. That fact makes the number of affected spacecraft more striking, not less. A handful of early machines happened to occupy the altered environment and became accidental dosimeters.

Starfish also corrected an assumption implicit in some Argus conclusions. The failure of low-yield artificial shells to persist as a useful military shield did not mean the magnetosphere could not retain dangerous bomb-produced electrons for consequential periods. Scale and injection geometry mattered.

The event therefore moved the problem from speculative strategy to spacecraft engineering. Radiation hardness could no longer be treated only as preparation for natural belts. Human beings had become a source term.

This distinction survives in modern space-weather modeling. Natural energetic particles originate in solar and magnetospheric processes. Anthropogenic populations can come from high-altitude nuclear explosions. The equations governing particle motion do not care about intent, but risk assessment does.

Starfish is also a warning against the phrase “contained test.” The fireball was remote. Fallout in the conventional sense was not the primary orbital hazard. The test altered a field-connected environment through which other systems later traveled.

Containment is not only geography. It is mechanism.
