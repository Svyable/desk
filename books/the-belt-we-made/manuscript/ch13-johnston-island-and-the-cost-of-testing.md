# 13. Johnston Island and the Cost of Testing

A clean diagram of Starfish begins with a launch arrow and ends with a burst.

The actual test program included rockets that failed.

Operation Fishbowl suffered unsuccessful launch attempts before and after Starfish. In at least one case, a Thor missile carrying a nuclear test device had to be destroyed after loss of trajectory confidence. Another launch malfunction ended with destruction of the missile and warhead. Later accidents contaminated parts of Johnston Island with plutonium-bearing debris and required cleanup.

The exact accident sequence deserves page-indexed primary reconstruction before publication, which is why this draft keeps the operational detail bounded. The point is not the spectacle of failure. The point is that testing a hazardous system produces risk before the intended experiment begins.

That is easy to forget when history remembers only the shot that worked.

Project Pluto had test stands. Chemical weapons programs had proving grounds. Biological programs had containment failures and exposure controversies. High-altitude nuclear testing had launch vehicles, warheads, range-safety decisions, weather constraints, tracking systems, ships, aircraft, and island infrastructure.

The environment-changing effect in space therefore rested on a very terrestrial industrial system.

This is useful when evaluating claims about hidden advanced programs. Exotic effects do not abolish logistics. They create more logistics.

Starfish looked impossible in the sky and embarrassingly ordinary on the ground: launch hardware, failures, cleanup, reports, committees, contractors, damaged equipment, and people whose job was to make the strange thing happen on schedule.
