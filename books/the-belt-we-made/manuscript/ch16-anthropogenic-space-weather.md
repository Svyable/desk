# Chapter 16 — Anthropogenic Space Weather

“Space weather” sounds natural by definition. Solar flares, coronal mass ejections and geomagnetic storms arrive from outside human industry. We forecast them the way we forecast hurricanes.

Argus and Starfish complicate the category.

NASA now uses the phrase “space weather events linked to human activity” when discussing high-altitude nuclear tests. The wording is useful because it avoids pretending that every anthropogenic effect looks exactly like a solar storm. Nuclear tests produced trapped radiation, geomagnetic disturbances, ionospheric changes and radio effects through a specific mechanism. They altered the same coupled environment in which natural space weather occurs.

The distinction between source and response matters. A geomagnetic disturbance does not become unreal because humans triggered it. Nor does a human source make every downstream effect controllable.

This is the core of anthropogenic environmental physics. We alter boundary conditions and source terms; the system evolves.

The magnetosphere is especially instructive because it is visibly coupled. Energetic particles, plasma waves, ionosphere and atmosphere exchange energy across scales. Injection at one location can create consequences far away along magnetic field lines or after drift around Earth.

The Argus observations from geographically distant stations made this tangible. NASA’s historical synthesis describes disturbances observed from Sweden to Arizona and researchers using arrival times to infer propagation speeds.

The phrase “planetary experiment” can sound inflated until one remembers that instruments across the planet were part of the experiment because the field itself connected them.

Modern space-weather science has become much more sophisticated. Satellites monitor particle populations. Models forecast radiation conditions. Operators understand that solar events can degrade navigation, communications and spacecraft electronics. Yet anthropogenic sources remain in the hazard literature because the old tests proved a second route to dangerous particle environments.

That route has not been exercised since the treaty era. This is fortunate. It also means contemporary models of nuclear-created belts still lean heavily on historical data, simulation and the physics inferred from a limited number of extreme events.

The archive is finite because restraint succeeded.

There is something worth noticing in that. Some environmental datasets should remain small.
