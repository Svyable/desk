# 4. A Bomb at Four Hundred Kilometers

Starfish Prime detonated at roughly 400 kilometers altitude with an announced yield of about 1.4 megatons.

Those numbers are repeated so often they become decorative. They should be treated as geometry.

Four hundred kilometers is not "deep space." It is low Earth orbit. It is above nearly all of the atmosphere by mass, yet inside Earth's magnetic environment. A burst there couples to systems in ways that do not resemble a surface explosion.

There is still electromagnetic radiation. There are prompt particles. There is ionization. There is an expanding plasma. There are geomagnetic disturbances. There are beta-decay electrons from radioactive debris. But the familiar mechanisms of blast damage on the ground are no longer the main story at continental distances.

The key point for this book is not how to design such an effect. It is why altitude changes what the word effect means.

At Starfish altitude, the explosion became an input into Earth's electromagnetic system. Contemporary Fishbowl planning explicitly sought data on electromagnetic pulse, auroral behavior, and radio-communication effects. The test series was not blind to the possibility that the upper atmosphere and magnetosphere would participate.

And yet participation is not prediction.

The early post-test literature contains a revealing tone: scientists knew enough to name the mechanisms but not enough to bound the consequences confidently. The artificial belt was measured quickly, then measured again, then followed because it refused to vanish on the expected schedule.

One of the most important historical documents is therefore not a dramatic photograph but a bureaucratic status report. It notes that the pre-test scientific review anticipated trapped electrons and judged that no hazardous effects to humans or prolonged interference with scientific observations would result. It then reports a reality intense enough to accelerate deterioration of satellite solar cells.

The gap is the book.

Risk assessment is often caricatured as a battle between experts and surprise. Real risk assessment is harder. Experts may correctly identify a mechanism, correctly recognize a hazard class, and still miss the tail of the outcome distribution because the system is coupled in ways measurements have not yet constrained.

The Starfish planners were not ignorant of trapped radiation. They were operating at the edge of empirical knowledge about how much, where, and for how long.

That distinction should haunt every modern proposal to alter a complex natural system deliberately. Geoengineering debates often ask whether a mechanism can work. Starfish suggests a more uncomfortable question: what happens when the mechanism works better, longer, or elsewhere than your model expects?
