# Chapter 8 — How to Build a Radiation Belt

A nuclear explosion does not manufacture a belt the way a factory makes a cable. It creates particles. The magnetosphere decides which of them remain.

The charged-particle motion can be described as three nested motions. An energetic electron spirals around a magnetic field line. As the field strengthens toward Earth, conservation of its magnetic moment can cause the electron to mirror and travel back toward the opposite hemisphere. At the same time, gradients and curvature in the field make its guiding center drift around the planet.

A trapped particle can therefore occupy an enormous path without ever being stationary.

This geometry explains why altitude alone is a poor language for radiation belts. Space physicists often use L-shell coordinates, which roughly label magnetic field lines by where they cross the magnetic equator in Earth radii. A particle injected at one magnetic shell experiences a different environment from a particle at another even if their instantaneous geographic altitudes overlap.

Argus took advantage of that geometry. High-altitude detonations supplied energetic electrons at locations where some would be captured into long-lived trajectories. The beta-decay spectrum of fission products gave the artificial population an identifiable energy character.

But trapping is not permanent. Particles can scatter into the atmospheric loss cone. Waves can change pitch angle. Geomagnetic storms distort fields and alter populations. Collisions remove particles at low altitude. Natural variability can shorten or reshape an artificial belt.

This is where the original military proposal ran into the planet’s refusal to behave like a passive component.

DTRA’s historical summary says Argus demonstrated that Earth’s magnetic field could not maintain the kind of long-lasting radiation shield envisioned. That conclusion should not be generalized too far: later high-yield explosions, especially Starfish Prime, produced artificial radiation that persisted far longer and caused real spacecraft damage. The correct lesson is not that artificial belts cannot last. It is that lifetime depends strongly on injection conditions and environment.

The physics also makes “radiation belt weapon” a broad phrase hiding several possible effects. A belt might damage solar cells through cumulative dose. It might degrade semiconductor electronics. It might raise background counts in detectors. It might endanger humans. Its effect on radio propagation is different from its direct ionizing-radiation effect on hardware.

These pathways have different thresholds and timescales.

Separating them matters because military planning tends to compress mechanism into outcome. If the requirement is “disable satellites,” then any mechanism that produces satellite failure can appear equivalent. Engineering is less forgiving. Solar-cell degradation, single-event effects, total ionizing dose and electromagnetic pulse demand different models and mitigations.

Argus was early enough that many of these distinctions were still becoming operationally concrete.

The artificial belts therefore belong to the history of reliability engineering as much as to nuclear strategy. They forced satellite builders to think of orbit as a material environment rather than empty vacuum.
