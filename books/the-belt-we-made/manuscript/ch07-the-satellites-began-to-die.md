# 7. The Satellites Began to Die

Causality is easy when the machine explodes next to the bomb.

Starfish damaged spacecraft by changing an environment, which makes the forensic problem harder.

Within months of the test, satellite operators and researchers were reporting degradation and failures associated with the enhanced trapped-radiation environment. NASA historical material names Ariel I, Transit IVB, and TRAAC among spacecraft seriously affected. Other later summaries use larger counts, sometimes saying ten satellites were damaged or lost because of radiation effects associated with the artificial belts.

The number should not be treated like a scoreboard.

Different spacecraft failed differently. Some lost solar-cell performance. Some suffered electronic degradation. Some had command-system anomalies. Some were already vulnerable to radiation. Some flew through regions where Starfish electrons were an important addition to the natural environment. Later Soviet tests added their own artificial populations.

The scientifically interesting question is not "How many did Starfish kill?" but "What causal chain can be demonstrated for each spacecraft?"

That is a more difficult book to write, and a more useful one.

A satellite is a bundle of components with different sensitivities. Solar cells accumulate damage. Semiconductor devices can suffer ionizing effects. Detectors can saturate or degrade. A command system may fail intermittently before failing permanently. Orbit determines dose history. Shielding and component selection determine vulnerability.

The artificial belt therefore functioned like an environmental stressor that exposed design assumptions.

Early satellites were not foolishly built. They were built for the best available model of space. Then the model changed because the environment changed.

This is the part of Starfish that feels most modern.

We are again building systems whose operating environment can be altered by other actors: congested orbits filled with debris, radio-frequency environments filled with interference, cyber systems filled with adversarial traffic, even machine-learning systems deployed into social environments that adapt to them.

The lesson is not that every environment will become hostile.

The lesson is that a specification can become obsolete after launch.
