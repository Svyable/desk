# 15. The Treaty Closes the Window

In August 1963, barely thirteen months after Starfish Prime, the United States, Soviet Union, and United Kingdom signed the Limited Test Ban Treaty.

The treaty prohibited nuclear weapon tests and other nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, underwater, and in outer space. It entered into force on October 10, 1963.

The treaty was not written solely because of artificial radiation belts. Atmospheric fallout, arms-control politics, verification, the Cuban Missile Crisis, domestic and international pressure, and strategic calculation all mattered. Any book that turns Starfish into the single cause of the treaty is laundering chronology into causation.

But the sequence still matters.

The outer-space nuclear-testing regime that produced Argus, Starfish, and Soviet artificial belts ended almost immediately after science had discovered how consequential those experiments could be.

This created an unusual epistemic closure.

The largest experiments were followed by prohibition rather than refinement. Scientists had to learn from the belts already made. Engineers had to reconstruct damage from spacecraft already exposed. The natural experiment would not be repeated openly at comparable scale.

That is one reason 1962 remains so central to modern HEMP and artificial-belt discussions. The data are old because the experiment became politically unacceptable.

The treaty therefore did more than constrain weapons testing. It froze a class of geophysical experimentation into history.

There are cases where ignorance persists because nobody can build the instrument.

Here ignorance persists partly because the instrument was a nuclear explosion and the world decided not to use it again.
