# Chapter 12 — Satellites as Casualties

The cleanest evidence that an environmental weapon can hurt unintended systems is not a strategic memo. It is a maintenance record.

Telstar 1 launched on July 10, 1962, one day after Starfish Prime. The satellite became an icon of transatlantic communications. It also flew through an orbital environment that had just been altered by a nuclear explosion.

By November its command system had malfunctioned. A NASA technical report on the failure records the conclusion that enhanced inner-belt radiation damaged surfaces in vulnerable transistors in the redundant command decoders. Engineers used laboratory work and unconventional command procedures to restore function temporarily. The malfunction returned in February 1963 and tracked changes in radiation intensity.

This is wonderfully concrete. An artificial belt is no longer a contour on a plot. It is damage in a transistor.

Ariel 1 tells a parallel story. The satellite had launched in April 1962 as the first spacecraft in the U.S.-U.K. Ariel program. On July 12, three days after Starfish, it began malfunctioning. A later NASA materials-history report describes reduced solar-cell output consistent with radiation damage from trapped Starfish electrons. The document predicts roughly 20 percent degradation and notes reasonable agreement with the observed power behavior.

Not every Ariel failure can be blamed on Starfish. The same report is careful about the tape recorder, which had performed badly in ground testing and might have failed for ordinary engineering reasons. That caution is worth preserving. A changed environment can become an attractive explanation for every subsequent anomaly. Good engineering resists monoculture.

This is why component-level histories matter. They discriminate effects.

Solar cells respond to accumulated particle damage. Semiconductor command circuits can suffer ionizing-radiation effects. Mechanical recorders have belts, shorts and manufacturing defects that do not require a nuclear explanation. The spacecraft is a bundle of failure modes crossing one another.

The Starfish casualties also expose an asymmetry between weapons experimentation and infrastructure growth. In 1962 there were few satellites. Today there are thousands of active spacecraft, including navigation, weather, communications, Earth observation and military systems. A comparable environmental perturbation would encounter a much larger and more heterogeneous population.

That does not mean a historical high-altitude nuclear test can simply be scaled into a modern catastrophe estimate. Satellite designs, orbits, shielding and architectures differ. It means the dependency surface is larger.

Argus asked whether Earth’s magnetic field could hold bomb-produced electrons. Telstar and Ariel answered a different question: what happens when useful machines have to live inside the answer?
