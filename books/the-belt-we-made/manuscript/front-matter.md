# The Belt We Made

## Project Argus, Artificial Radiation, and the First Human Edit to Near-Earth Space

Sven Hardy Benson

---

There is a peculiar photograph from the opening months of the Space Age: James Van Allen bent over a satellite small enough to hold in your arms, inspecting the instrument that had just revealed an invisible structure around Earth. The discovery was not a continent or a moon. It was a region of charged particles trapped by the planet’s magnetic field. Space, which public imagination still treated mostly as distance, turned out to have weather, geography and hazards.

Then the United States put a nuclear bomb in it.

Not metaphorically. In August and September 1958, a secret naval task force in the South Atlantic launched three small nuclear devices hundreds of kilometers above Earth under Operation Argus. The purpose was to test a proposal by the physicist Nicholas Christofilos: energetic electrons from a high-altitude nuclear detonation might become trapped by Earth’s magnetic field and form an artificial radiation belt. The military possibilities were obvious enough to receive urgent attention. Perhaps such a belt could disrupt radio or radar. Perhaps it could interfere with missiles. Perhaps it could become a kind of electromagnetic defensive screen.

The experiment worked in the narrow physical sense. Explorer IV detected the injected electrons. James Van Allen, Carl McIlwain and George Ludwig later published measurements from more than 160 satellite passes through the artificial shells. The military dream was less cooperative. The shells did not persist in the way a practical planetary shield would have required.

That should have been the end of a strange footnote. It was not.

Four years later, the much larger Starfish Prime explosion created an artificial radiation environment intense enough to damage satellites. The British-American Ariel 1 began malfunctioning three days after the test; NASA technical histories later described solar-cell degradation consistent with trapped fission electrons. Telstar’s command system failed months later, and Bell Telephone Laboratories traced the malfunction to transistor surface damage from the enhanced inner-belt radiation. Soviet high-altitude tests injected their own populations. By the time the Limited Test Ban Treaty entered into force on October 10, 1963, humans had already demonstrated something that still feels ahead of its date: the near-Earth environment could be deliberately modified, measured from orbit, weaponized in concept, damaged in practice and constrained by international agreement.

This book is about that short interval.

It is tempting to turn Argus into a parable. Resist the temptation. The story becomes stranger when its original dimensions remain separate. There is the weapons program, trying to find strategic use in a young and poorly understood physical environment. There is the scientific program, using detectors aboard primitive satellites to discover both natural and artificial radiation. There is a secrecy system that concealed the operation while depending on civilian and academic expertise. There is a treaty system that soon prohibited the class of experiment. And there is a modern space economy that has made the old radiation-belt problem newly concrete, because thousands of satellites now occupy an environment that 1958 engineers had barely begun to map.

The evidence is unusually good for a subject this esoteric. The Defense Threat Reduction Agency’s historical report reconstructs the planning and execution of Argus and is candid about gaps in the surviving record. NASA retains technical reports on artificial radiation belts, Starfish and spacecraft damage. Van Allen’s 1959 paper describes the electrons observed by Explorer IV. The State Department preserves the treaty text that closed outer space to nuclear testing. Modern Los Alamos work on radiation-belt remediation begins from the same physical problem in a different key: if energetic electrons can be injected and trapped, can they also be deliberately removed?

That last question is one reason this is not merely Cold War history. We have become better at creating dependencies in space than at governing the environment those dependencies inhabit. Space weather forecasts now sit beside launch schedules. Satellite operators design for radiation. Military planners worry about orbital resilience. Researchers study ways to precipitate dangerous electrons out of the belts. The magnetosphere is no longer an abstraction above politics. It is infrastructure with physics.

The story begins before anybody knew that clearly.
