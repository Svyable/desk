# Chapter 15 — Mars Below Mars

Mars is easiest to declare dead when we look at the part we can see.

The surface is cold, dry, oxidizing and irradiated. Liquid water is unstable across much of it today. Ultraviolet radiation is harsh. Organic molecules are exposed to destructive chemistry. None of that is good news for an exposed biosphere.

The deep-Earth lesson is that surface hostility does not settle subsurface habitability.

In 2021 Jesse Tarnas and colleagues asked a deliberately narrower question: could radiolysis in present-day martian crust generate enough redox energy to support Earth-like sulfate-reducing microorganisms if groundwater is present? Using compositions from martian meteorites and models of radiolytic production, they concluded that some crustal regions could in principle support cell densities comparable to low-biomass terrestrial deep-subsurface environments.

This is a habitability calculation. It is not evidence that such cells exist.

The distinction deserves repetition because Mars has suffered from a century of interpretive inflation. Canals became civilizations. Seasonal color became vegetation. Methane has repeatedly threatened to become biology before its sources and even its measurement consistency were settled. A radiolysis model should not be asked to carry that historical burden.

What it legitimately changes is mission logic.

If radionuclide-bearing rock plus water can create hydrogen and oxidized sulfur compounds, then a modern martian biosphere need not depend on organic carbon raining down from an ancient surface. It could, in principle, be maintained locally in wet fracture systems. The most interesting sites become places where ancient crust, radiogenic elements, sulfide minerals, fractures and groundwater overlap.

That pushes exploration downward.

Unfortunately, downward is expensive. Drilling meters on Mars is difficult. Drilling hundreds of meters or kilometers is a different category of mission. We therefore need proxies: exposed deep rocks uplifted or excavated by impact, recurring slope or fracture features where subsurface material reaches the surface, mineralogical maps of likely energy-rich terrains, and eventually dedicated drilling technology.

The contamination problem also becomes vicious. A deep martian sample is precisely where terrestrial microbes might survive best if accidentally delivered. Planetary protection and life detection merge.

The deep-Earth comparison gives us a test hierarchy. First show water or credible past/present hydration. Then constrain temperature. Measure radionuclide inventory. Identify sulfide and sulfate minerals. Model hydrogen and oxidant production. Search for isotopic and molecular signatures that cannot be explained by abiotic chemistry alone. Use multiple independent biosignatures before invoking organisms.

The most important Mars may be the Mars no camera can photograph.

But until we drill it, that sentence belongs to Class 4.
