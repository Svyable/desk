# Chapter 2 — What Radioactivity Does to Water

The phrase “nuclear-powered life” is almost designed to create the wrong picture.

There is no reactor vessel in the fracture. No microbe is absorbing gamma rays the way a leaf absorbs visible light. The useful chemistry is indirect. Radioactive decay deposits energy in water and rock. Water molecules are ionized or excited. Reactive products appear. Among the products, hydrogen can accumulate. That hydrogen becomes an electron donor available to organisms capable of coupling its oxidation to an electron acceptor such as sulfate.

The biology begins after the physics has already done something useful.

This matters because radiation occupies a peculiar place in public intuition. We think of it as an anti-life force. At sufficiently high dose, that is sensible. Ionizing radiation breaks bonds, damages DNA and proteins, generates reactive oxygen species, and kills cells. But the same energy deposition can also manufacture chemical disequilibrium. A geologic radiation field is therefore not biologically summarized by the word “dangerous.” The right question is what molecules are produced, at what rates, in what volumes, under what water-rock conditions, and whether biology can harvest the resulting free energy faster than the environment destroys the machinery doing the harvesting.

Li-Hung Lin and colleagues put numbers behind this in their 2005 analysis of deep South African fracture waters. They measured hydrogen concentrations in groundwater from deep siliciclastic and volcanic rock units and compared candidate production mechanisms. Their conclusion was unusually strong for a geochemical source argument: radiolytic dissociation of water, driven by decay of uranium, thorium and potassium in host rocks, was sufficient to explain the observed hydrogen, including extremely high concentrations in some samples. Other proposed mechanisms were not needed to close the budget under those conditions.

That is a much better fact than the simplified claim that “microbes eat radiation.”

The rock supplies radionuclides. Their decay supplies energetic particles and photons. The water absorbs part of that energy. Radiolysis generates reducing and oxidizing species. Hydrogen can persist. Microbes can consume the hydrogen. The chain has steps, and every step can fail in a different planetary environment.

The oxidizing side of radiolysis is just as important as the hydrogen side. If water splitting produced only a reductant, biology would still need an acceptor. In some deep environments sulfate is available. Later astrobiology work, especially models aimed at Mars, has emphasized that radiolysis can in principle create both reducing and oxidizing chemical products and can drive oxidation of sulfide minerals, generating sulfate locally. That possibility matters because it weakens one of the obvious objections to calling a deep ecosystem sunlight-independent: perhaps the electron acceptor need not be imported from an oxygenated surface at all.

“Can” is doing work there.

At any specific site, the actual sulfate budget has to be measured, not narrated. Some sulfate may be ancient. Some may derive from mineral oxidation. Some may have been transported. The relative importance of these sources depends on mineralogy, water residence time, fracture connectivity, radiation dose and reaction kinetics. A satisfying conceptual cycle is not the same thing as a demonstrated site-specific cycle.

The deep subsurface rewards this kind of discipline because the rates are so slow. A process that looks negligible on laboratory timescales can dominate over millions of years. Conversely, a concentration measured today may reflect accumulation over a long interval rather than a high current production rate. Concentration is inventory; metabolism depends on flux.

That distinction will recur. A fracture can contain abundant hydrogen because production has been steady and consumption sparse. A microorganism can survive on a power level that would look like starvation to a fast-growing laboratory culture. A groundwater sample can preserve a chemical memory far older than any biological experiment.

The seduction of radiolysis is that it seems to solve a cosmic problem elegantly. Radioactive elements are widespread in rocky bodies. Water or ice may be widespread. Therefore chemical energy should be available in darkness.

The sober version is still exciting. Wherever radionuclides, water and appropriate rock chemistry coexist, radiolysis is one candidate source of sustained redox disequilibrium. On Earth, there are sites where the measured chemistry and the biological community fit that story well. On Mars or an icy moon, the same physics can be calculated, but habitability remains conditional on liquid water, accessible nutrients, temperatures, porosity, timescale and biological machinery we have not observed.

Life does not eat radiation.

Life eats the chemistry that radiation leaves behind.
