# Chapter 17 — Radiation as Food and Poison

Radiation is not good for life.

That sentence needs to survive this book intact.

Ionizing radiation damages DNA, proteins and membranes. It creates reactive species and breaks chemical bonds. Organisms exposed to high enough doses die. The fact that radiolysis can generate useful hydrogen does not invert toxicology into nutrition.

The more precise statement is stranger: radiation can damage organisms while simultaneously creating chemical substrates that organisms exploit.

Those processes operate at different scales. A microbe does not need to absorb a radioactive particle as nourishment. It can live centimeters or meters away from the radionuclide-bearing grain and consume hydrogen produced in surrounding water. Geological material absorbs the dose; chemistry carries the usable energy into metabolism.

This distinction prevents a recurring category error in discussions of radiotrophic life. Melanized fungi observed in high-radiation environments, claims about radiation-enhanced growth, and radiolysis-powered lithotrophy are different phenomena. Evidence for one does not establish the others.

*Desulforudis* is compelling because the causal chain can be written without exotic radiation biology. Ordinary radiochemistry produces H2. Ordinary anaerobic respiration consumes it. The organism still has to withstand the ambient radiation environment, but tolerance is not the energy-capture mechanism.

The dual role of radiation matters beyond Earth. Europa’s surface radiation is lethal to many terrestrial organisms and chemically productive at the same time. Mars receives ionizing radiation that destroys exposed organics while also driving subsurface radiolysis. A habitat’s radiation field can therefore reduce preservation at one depth and create metabolic opportunity at another.

This is one reason “radiation level” is a poor single-number habitability metric. Dose rate, shielding, chemical products, water abundance, repair capability and transport all matter.

There is also a temporal asymmetry. A short radiation pulse can sterilize. A low chronic dose can steadily generate chemistry over millions of years. The same total deposited energy can have radically different biological consequences depending on rate and geometry.

The deep crust again gives us the less cinematic version. Natural radionuclides are dilute. Decay is continuous. Water sits against mineral surfaces for immense periods. The resulting chemical gradients can be small but durable.

Food and poison are not opposites when the energy first passes through geology.

They are two outputs of the same physics.
