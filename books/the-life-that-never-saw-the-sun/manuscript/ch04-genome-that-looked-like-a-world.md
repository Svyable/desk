# Chapter 4 — The Genome That Looked Like a World

A genome is not an ecosystem. The 2008 *Desulforudis* paper became famous because, for a moment, one genome looked close enough to both that the distinction seemed almost rude.

The sample came from fracture water at roughly 2.8 kilometers depth. Its microbial diversity was extraordinarily low. Researchers assembled a complete genome from the dominant organism and found a metabolic repertoire unusually well suited to a chemically austere, isolated habitat. The bacterium could reduce sulfate, fix carbon dioxide, fix nitrogen, use hydrogen, move with flagella and form spores.

Each capability answers a separate survival problem.

Carbon fixation matters because importing organic carbon from the surface would make the ecosystem less independent than it first appears. Nitrogen fixation matters because fixed nitrogen can be limiting in isolated environments. Sporulation matters because the deep crust is not steady simply because it is slow; fractures open and close, chemistry changes, energy flux can vary, temperature and pressure histories are long. Motility matters because a fracture is spatially heterogeneous at scales that matter to a cell.

The genome also contained signs of evolutionary borrowing. The original authors noted machinery shared with archaea, suggesting horizontal gene transfer may have contributed to this unusually self-sufficient package. That makes evolutionary sense. In an environment where partners are rare, acquiring pathways that reduce dependence on partners has obvious potential value.

But the phrase “single-species ecosystem” still overstates what a genome alone can certify.

First, genomic potential is not expression. A gene’s presence shows capability under some conditions, not continuous activity in the sampled fracture. Second, a species can be numerically dominant while depending on rare partners for metabolites, genetic exchange, viral dynamics or episodic services. Third, the surrounding rock may host attached communities poorly represented in water samples. Fourth, dead biomass and ancient organic matter can be chemically important even if contemporary living diversity is low.

The clean story is nevertheless valuable because it gives us a limiting case. Ecologists usually teach dependence through complexity: producers, consumers, decomposers, predators, symbionts. *Desulforudis* asks how much of that network can collapse into one lineage when environmental chemistry performs part of the ecosystem’s work.

The rock is doing things a plant would otherwise do.

Not photosynthesis, obviously. The analogy is functional. The geological environment maintains chemical disequilibria. Radiolysis can supply hydrogen. Mineral reactions supply substrates. Water transports molecules through fractures. Radioactive decay is not alive, but it can keep a metabolism supplied with reducing power. In that sense, the abiotic environment occupies more of the trophic architecture than it does in a forest.

This suggests a useful way to think about extraterrestrial life. We often ask whether a planet has the ingredients for organisms. A better question is how much ecological work the geology is already doing.

A world with abundant abiotic hydrogen, carbon dioxide, sulfate or other acceptors, liquid water and trace nutrients may permit small communities to be metabolically simple. A world where every useful molecule requires a biological partner may demand an evolutionary history and community complexity much harder to originate or sustain.

That does not mean single-cell simplicity is inevitable underground. The later discovery of nematodes in South African fracture waters made that clear. Nor does the 2024 Swedish work support the idea that *Desulforudis* is always alone. At 975 meters in Swedish bedrock, the lineage again dominated the prokaryotic community, but the broader community included other microbes and detectable eukaryotic diversity.

That later result is more interesting than a simple replication. It turns *Desulforudis* from a one-site curiosity into a recurring deep-subsurface player while weakening the romance of the universal one-species ecosystem.

Science improves when a memorable claim becomes less pure and more general.

The organism is not remarkable because it proves one cell can be a planet.

It is remarkable because it shows how close geology can come to being part of an organism’s metabolism.
