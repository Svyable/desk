# Chapter 12 — Metabolism at Geological Speed

Laboratory microbiology trains the eye to expect growth.

A flask becomes cloudy. Colonies appear. Cell counts rise. A metabolic rate can be measured over hours or days. Deep subsurface life often refuses this tempo.

When energy supply is tiny but persistent, selection can favor maintenance over expansion. Cells may spend most of their power repairing macromolecules, preserving ion gradients and replacing damaged components. Division can become an event rather than a rhythm.

This creates a conceptual problem. We tend to treat life as a process visible through growth. But an ecosystem can remain alive while producing biomass at rates too low for ordinary experiments to notice easily.

The deep biosphere therefore pushes biology toward an energy-accounting definition. How much power does a cell require simply to remain viable? How low can maintenance energy fall? How long can macromolecules persist? At what point does a cell stop being metabolically active and become merely preserved?

Those are not philosophical questions when the available power is supplied by radiolysis. Radioactive decay is slow and steady. If a fracture community consumes hydrogen faster than radiolysis and mineral reactions replenish it, the system runs down. If consumption is low enough, the same weak geologic flux can support life for extraordinary periods.

This is where concentration again misleads. A fracture can hold a substantial hydrogen inventory but have a low production flux. A dense microbial population could temporarily exploit the reservoir and collapse. A sparse population might match the replenishment rate indefinitely.

For extraterrestrial habitability, flux is therefore more useful than inventory.

A patch of Mars may contain sulfate and hydrogen today. The question is whether their ongoing production can support maintenance. A Europan ice layer may generate oxidants through radiation. The question is whether transport delivers them to zones with reductants and liquid water at a biologically meaningful rate.

Deep life changes our intuition about the minimum viable planet because time enters the equation twice. Geological processes can produce tiny amounts of energy for very long periods. Organisms can demand tiny amounts of energy if they are willing to live slowly enough.

The match between those two slownesses may be more important than abundance.

This also complicates biosignature searches. Rapidly growing cultures produce strong chemical signals. Maintenance-dominated ecosystems may sit near geochemical equilibrium and barely perturb bulk chemistry. Their signatures may be isotopic, microscopic or localized to mineral surfaces.

The most alien thing about deep terrestrial life may therefore not be its chemistry.

It may be its patience.
