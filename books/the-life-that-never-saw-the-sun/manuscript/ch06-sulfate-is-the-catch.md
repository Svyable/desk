# Chapter 6 — Sulfate Is the Catch

A hydrogen economy still needs someone to accept the electrons.

For *Desulforudis*, sulfate is central. The organism reduces sulfate to sulfide while oxidizing hydrogen, harvesting energy from the redox difference. This is where the clean phrase “life powered by radioactivity” becomes chemically conditional.

Radiolysis can explain the hydrogen. What explains the sulfate?

One answer is inheritance. Sulfate can enter crustal fluids from surface-derived waters or from minerals altered during periods when oxygenic photosynthesis had already changed Earth’s surface chemistry. Under that scenario, the local metabolism is independent of contemporary sunlight but not fully independent of a planet whose biosphere helped manufacture oxidants.

Another answer is local generation. Radiolysis creates not only reducing species but oxidants. In sulfide-bearing rock, those oxidants can convert reduced sulfur into sulfate. Modern Mars studies explicitly exploit this possibility: if radionuclides can generate both hydrogen and oxidized sulfur species within wet rock, then a sulfate reducer could in principle receive both halves of its redox couple without a surface oxygen cycle.

The difference between these mechanisms is not semantic. It determines whether the Earth system is a transferable analogue for a planet that may never have had oxygenic photosynthesis.

This is why element-by-element bookkeeping is more useful than calling an ecosystem “independent.” Hydrogen has one provenance. Sulfate another. Carbon another. Nitrogen another. Phosphorus another. Trace metals another. The water has its own age and transport history. Permeability determines whether any of them meet.

A metabolic pathway is a contract among all of these supplies.

The Opalinus Clay work illustrates the complication. Researchers reconstructed a hydrogen-driven microbial network in deep clay and emphasized chemolithoautotrophic sulfate reduction as a basis for primary production. Yet they also noted that sulfate itself can ultimately be linked to surface oxidation. A dark ecosystem can therefore be locally chemolithotrophic while still carrying a chemical debt to sunlight.

That is not a failure of the concept. It is a warning about levels of description.

For astrobiology, the strongest systems are those in which both donor and acceptor can be produced abiotically in situ. Tarnas and colleagues argued in 2021 that radiolysis in present-day martian crust could generate sufficient redox energy to support sulfate-reducing microbes, provided groundwater exists. Their model included radiolytically generated oxidants acting on sulfide minerals as a route to sulfate.

The result is a habitability calculation, not evidence of martian organisms. That distinction must remain hard.

Still, it is a meaningful shift. Mars does not need to reproduce Earth’s Great Oxidation Event in order to reproduce one particular sulfate-reducing energy scheme. The crust itself may be able to manufacture both reducing and oxidizing chemical partners.

The catch, then, becomes a research program. Measure sulfur speciation. Measure mineralogy. Measure radiogenic element abundance. Estimate radiolysis rates. Constrain fracture water age and transport. Test whether sulfate is being regenerated or merely depleted from an ancient inventory. Compare the metabolic demand to the chemical production flux.

Only after that do we get to use the word self-sustaining.
