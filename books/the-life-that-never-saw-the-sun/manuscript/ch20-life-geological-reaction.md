# Chapter 20 — Life Is a Geological Reaction

The usual picture of life begins with an organism.

Deep underground, it is often more accurate to begin with the rock.

A uranium atom decays. Energy enters water. Hydrogen is produced. Minerals offer sulfur, carbon or metals. A fracture keeps water and substrates in contact. A bacterium couples one reaction to another and keeps the free energy difference for itself.

Nothing in that chain requires sunlight at the point of use.

That is the profound result. It is also the result most likely to be ruined by exaggeration.

The deep biosphere does not prove that life is inevitable. It does not prove that Mars is inhabited. It does not prove Europa’s ocean contains cells. It does not prove that a radiolytic ecosystem can originate from nonlife. It does not even prove that every celebrated deep terrestrial community is fully independent of surface-derived chemistry.

What it proves is narrower and more disruptive: photosynthesis is not the only planetary process capable of maintaining biologically usable chemical disequilibrium over long periods.

Radioactive decay can participate in ecology.

Once that is admitted, several old boundaries soften. Biology and geology become less separable. A biosphere is not only organisms exchanging metabolites with one another; it can include mineral and radiochemical processes doing work that, at the surface, is supplied by sunlight and larger food webs. The question “what does this organism eat?” becomes “what planetary process keeps its redox pair apart?”

That is a better question on Earth and elsewhere.

The *Desulforudis* genome made one fracture look briefly like a world encoded in a cell. The later record makes the picture better. Hydrogen in deep South African waters can be radiolytic. Ancient Canadian fracture fluids show how long water-rock systems can persist. Nematodes show that some deep microbial food webs can support consumers. Global compilations show that the continental subsurface contains substantial microbial biomass. Swedish bedrock shows *Desulforudis* returning as a dominant primary producer without the purity of a one-species ecosystem.

The evidence converges not on a single secret biosphere but on a category of planetary metabolism.

There are still awkward gaps. We need direct flux measurements instead of inventories. We need better element budgets. We need to know how much deep biomass is supported by recent surface products versus truly local geochemistry. We need cultivation and single-cell work. We need better clocks for groundwater and population isolation. We need to understand whether these lineages are ancient underground residents or repeated colonists from shallower habitats.

And beyond Earth, we need samples.

Until then, the safest large claim is methodological. Search for life where planets are chemically out of balance, not merely where they resemble a pleasant afternoon on Earth.

The rock may be doing more biological work than the sky.

A world can be dark and still be fed.
