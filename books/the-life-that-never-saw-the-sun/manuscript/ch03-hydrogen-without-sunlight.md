# Chapter 3 — Hydrogen Without Sunlight

Hydrogen is the deep biosphere’s small coin.

It is chemically simple, energetically useful and producible by several abiotic processes. Serpentinization can make it when water reacts with reduced minerals. Mechanical fracturing can expose reactive mineral surfaces. Radiolysis can make it wherever ionizing radiation deposits enough energy into water. In the deep crust, that diversity of production routes matters because the ecosystem cannot afford a narrow supply chain.

The Witwatersrand work is unusually valuable precisely because Lin and colleagues did not stop at finding hydrogen. They asked which mechanism could plausibly account for the concentrations under the measured conditions. Their thermodynamic comparison argued that radiolysis alone was sufficient in the sampled system. That does not make radiolysis the universal source of deep hydrogen. It makes the South African fracture waters a strong case where the source can be constrained instead of merely imagined.

Hydrogen-consuming microbes then face the second half of the transaction. Oxidizing H2 releases useful energy only when coupled to an electron acceptor. Methanogens can reduce carbon dioxide to methane. Sulfate-reducing organisms can reduce sulfate to sulfide. Other deep organisms use nitrate, iron, carbon compounds or combinations that depend on local chemistry.

What is unusual about *Desulforudis audaxviator* is how much of this deep-life economy appears packed into one genome. The organism can use hydrogen, reduce sulfate and fix carbon through the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway. It can also fix nitrogen, reducing dependence on a ready-made supply of biologically available nitrogen. In the 2008 reconstruction, the genome looked like a biochemical declaration of autonomy.

But autonomy has a budget.

Carbon fixation answers where biomass carbon comes from. Nitrogen fixation answers one important nutrient constraint. Sulfate reduction explains an energy-yielding respiratory pathway. None of those alone answers where phosphorus comes from, how trace metals are supplied, how much maintenance energy is required, or whether all necessary compounds can be regenerated indefinitely inside the isolated fracture system.

A planetary habitat can therefore be energetically favorable and biologically impossible. This is one of astrobiology’s recurring traps. We calculate one favorable reaction and unconsciously promote it into an ecosystem.

The opposite trap is equally common: because a habitat is dark, cold, radioactive, salty or nutrient-poor, we promote difficulty into impossibility.

Hydrogen helps keep both errors in check. It is measurable. Its sources can be modeled. Its concentration can be compared with known metabolic thresholds. Isotopes can sometimes constrain origin. If microbes consume it, the resulting products can leave chemical signatures. It turns a philosophical question — can life exist without sunlight? — into a series of smaller geochemical questions.

That move is what makes the deep subsurface scientifically powerful. It is not that the environment is mysterious. It is that the mystery can be disassembled.

Consider two underground worlds. In the first, organic carbon from the surface percolates downward and feeds microbes. It is dark, but not truly independent of photosynthesis. In the second, water reacts with rock to make hydrogen, microbes fix inorganic carbon, and electron acceptors are generated locally. Both are subsurface ecosystems. Only the second begins to justify the stronger phrase “independent of surface photosynthesis.”

Now consider a third world. Hydrogen is radiolytically produced, but sulfate derives from ancient oxidized material formed when surface oxygen was abundant. The current organisms may have no direct dependence on contemporary sunlight while their electron acceptor inventory is a geological inheritance from a photosynthetic planet.

That third case is probably closer to the epistemic difficulty we actually face.

The phrase “life without sunlight” survives, but it becomes specific. The local energy flux may be independent of sunlight. The carbon economy may be autotrophic. The ecosystem’s full elemental history may not be independent at all.

That distinction is not pedantry. It decides whether an Earth analogue transfers cleanly to a planet that never had oxygenic photosynthesis.

If we want to use South African fracture waters as a model for Mars, we have to know which parts of the system Mars could reproduce geochemically on its own. Hydrogen is one of the strongest candidates. That is why it matters.
