# The Mathematics of Maybe

In 1973, two papers changed how finance talked about uncertainty. Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published a method for valuing certain options; Robert Merton published a related treatment that generalized the logic. The equations became famous because they turned a slippery question into something a market could price. But the most useful idea for our purposes is not the formula. It is the strange kind of asset the formula was trying to value.

An option is valuable because the future is unresolved.

That sentence runs against ordinary intuition. Uncertainty is usually filed under risk, and risk is usually treated as something to reduce. If tomorrow's price, demand, technology, or policy were known, planning would be easier. Yet an option can become more valuable when the range of possible outcomes widens. The holder is protected on one side by the right not to exercise and remains exposed to favorable movement on the other.

The asymmetry is the asset.

This does not mean volatility is good. If you own a house with a leaking roof, uncertain rainfall does not improve your life. If you owe a fixed debt and your income becomes more volatile, uncertainty can be brutal. The option holder occupies a special position because the contract separates participation from obligation. That separation is expensive precisely because somebody else must absorb the corresponding exposure.

The habit of thought worth stealing is to ask where similar asymmetries exist outside a derivatives exchange.

A company considering a new mine may own mineral rights without immediately building the mine. The rights preserve the ability to invest if commodity prices and geology justify it. A pharmaceutical company can fund an early clinical program without committing today to global manufacturing capacity. A software company can build an interface that permits several data providers rather than binding itself to one. A student can take a course before enrolling in a multiyear degree. A city can acquire a corridor before deciding the exact transit technology that might someday use it.

These are not financial options in the strict contractual sense. Calling everything an option can become as lazy as calling every network a platform. The analogy earns its keep only when three conditions are present: a future action remains available, the holder is not fully obligated to take it, and new information can change whether taking it is attractive.

Once those conditions are visible, several everyday recommendations become more precise.

"Stay flexible" is not enough. Flexible with respect to what event? For how long? At what cost? "Keep your options open" can be wise or cowardly depending on whether the option is actually learning anything while it remains open. A person who refuses every commitment because another possibility might appear is not necessarily practicing sophisticated uncertainty management. They may simply be renting hope at an increasing price.

The math of options disciplines the romance of optionality because it insists that time, volatility, price, and exercise conditions matter.

Consider time. An option that expires tomorrow differs from one that expires next year because more can happen before next year. That longer interval can create value, but only if the right itself remains enforceable. Outside markets, we often misjudge the expiration date. A job offer expires. Fertility changes. A regulatory window closes. A competitor acquires the supplier. A piece of land is rezoned. A friendship thins from neglect. A political coalition exhausts itself. The future may remain uncertain while the relevant option quietly disappears.

The phrase "I can always do that later" is therefore a factual claim, not a mood.

Sometimes it is true. Often it is not.

Now consider the exercise price. Financial options make it explicit: exercising costs a known amount. Real decisions hide the cost across many ledgers. Launching a product requires manufacturing, support, reputation, and management attention. Moving cities costs money but also social capital, routines, and proximity to people who may not move with you. Changing strategy means not only funding the new direction but also absorbing the internal disappointment of teams built around the old one. If the exercise cost is understated, the option appears more attractive than it is.

The reverse error is also common. Organizations sometimes overstate the cost of trying because they treat every experiment as if it were the final scale decision. A prototype becomes burdened with enterprise security, internationalization, a permanent brand identity, and a five-year revenue model before anybody has learned whether users want the thing. The experiment is forced to carry costs that belong to exercise rather than exploration.

Good experimentation separates the price of learning from the price of committing.

This is one reason small firms can sometimes move faster than large firms even when the large firms have more money. The small firm can make a rough prototype whose purpose is to answer one question. The large firm may have systems that convert every prototype into a miniature production program. Governance intended to control risk can accidentally destroy cheap learning.

There is another variable in option theory that deserves translation: the value of the underlying asset.

A call option on something worthless is not valuable merely because it is flexible. Optionality needs a worthwhile possibility beneath it. This seems obvious, but personal and corporate strategy often collect options because options sound prudent. A company forms partnerships it never uses. A professional accumulates credentials unrelated to any desired work. A team builds modularity around hypothetical future integrations that no plausible customer has requested. The architecture becomes expensive insurance against futures nobody wants.

Optionality can become a form of hoarding.

The financial analogy also clarifies why the cost of waiting rises when the underlying asset pays benefits to current owners. In a stock-option model, dividends can make earlier exercise more attractive because waiting means missing cash flows. In a business, the equivalent may be learning-by-doing, customer relationships, distribution, or network effects. A company that waits for certainty before entering a market may discover that the important information was being generated by the firms already operating there.

This is the strategic version of the experience entry fee from the previous chapter.

Suppose two firms face the same uncertain technology. One waits until technical performance is proven. The other enters early with a small but real product. The early entrant may lose money and still gain something the observer cannot buy later at the same price: tacit knowledge. It learns which tolerances matter, which customer complaints are noise, which suppliers answer the phone at midnight, and where a laboratory result breaks under field conditions. Waiting preserved capital but sacrificed a stream of private information.

Now the decision is recursive. Acting changes what you know, and what you know changes the value of acting again.

This is why real options are harder than financial ones. A traded option typically does not change the company merely because you own it. A corporate experiment changes employees, suppliers, expectations, and capabilities. A pilot plant can create a constituency for a full plant. A small military deployment can create security obligations. A trial program can attract beneficiaries who reasonably organize to preserve it. The option itself alters the exercise environment.

The holder is inside the system being valued.

Economists studying investment under uncertainty have long emphasized irreversibility. When capital can be sold or repurposed without much loss, a mistaken investment is less dangerous. When an investment is highly specific, waiting can be valuable because new information may prevent an expensive mistake. The important word is not large. It is irreversible.

A billion-dollar inventory of a liquid commodity may be easier to unwind than a much smaller factory built for one obsolete process. A software system written in a common language with clean interfaces may be more reversible than a cheaper proprietary system wrapped around one vendor. A political promise can be more binding than a budget appropriation because the cost of reversal includes credibility.

Irreversibility has multiple currencies.

There is physical irreversibility: concrete poured, forests cut, machinery installed. Contractual irreversibility: penalties, covenants, exclusivity. Organizational irreversibility: teams trained, reporting lines created, careers attached. Reputational irreversibility: public promises and brand identity. Cognitive irreversibility: categories and habits that make alternatives harder to imagine. Emotional irreversibility: commitments that change relationships even if the formal decision can technically be reversed.

A decision can be cheap in dollars and expensive in one of these other currencies.

This suggests a useful exercise when the room is arguing about whether to move fast. Instead of asking, "How important is this decision?" ask, "What becomes harder to change after we make it?"

The answer often reduces drama. Some apparently huge decisions are surprisingly reversible. A company can enter a new country through a distributor before building a subsidiary. A person can live in a city for three months before purchasing a home. A product team can expose a feature to five percent of users. The first move need not contain the full commitment.

Other decisions hide a ratchet. A database schema becomes embedded in applications. A compensation metric attracts people optimized for it. A zoning rule induces construction that will stand for decades. A debt covenant constrains later financing. A war mobilization creates fears in another state even if no attack follows. In these cases, the first move deserves more skepticism than its immediate price suggests.

The arithmetic of expected value can miss this.

Suppose an investment has a fifty percent chance of producing 200 and a fifty percent chance of producing zero, against a cost of 80. A simple expected-value calculation makes the project look attractive: expected gross payoff 100 against cost 80. But if waiting one period reveals whether the project is in the good or bad state, and waiting costs only 5, the choice changes dramatically. The firm can pay 5 to avoid spending 80 in the bad state. What appears to be a modest delay is actually an information purchase.

Real life is rarely so clean. Information arrives partially. Competitors respond. The probabilities are disputed. Management can influence outcomes. The simple example is useful because it isolates the logic: a project can be attractive and still be unattractive today.

This distinction is often missing from political arguments. People debate whether a technology, infrastructure project, or regulation is good in the abstract when the practical question is whether this version should be adopted now, at this scale, under these conditions, with these exit mechanisms. "Yes or no" compresses timing into ideology.

There are times when timing is the ideology.

The same confusion appears in personal life. You may genuinely want a future and still reasonably decline to force it into the present. Wanting children does not make every moment equally suitable for having one. Wanting to start a company does not make every product idea a vehicle for that desire. Wanting to leave a job does not make the first available alternative wise. The statement "I want this" and the statement "I should exercise this option now" answer different questions.

On the other hand, expected learning can be used to rationalize endless delay. More information is always available in principle. Every decision can be studied for another week. Every partner can be evaluated through another season. Every investment case can receive another sensitivity analysis. If waiting is justified merely because uncertainty remains, nothing important will ever begin.

A practical threshold is needed.

One way to think about it is the last responsible moment: the latest point at which a decision can be made without unnecessarily reducing the quality of the outcome. The phrase appears in engineering and product-development traditions because sequencing matters. Decide an interface too early and you freeze assumptions before learning. Decide too late and downstream work cannot proceed.

The last responsible moment is not the last possible moment.

That difference separates strategy from procrastination. The procrastinator delays until external pressure removes alternatives. The strategist delays while protecting alternatives and actively collecting information. From the outside both may appear to be waiting. Internally they are opposites.

The strategist can answer four questions. What uncertainty are we trying to reduce? What evidence are we likely to receive before the deadline? What option is being preserved by waiting? What option is being lost by waiting?

If those questions have no answers, delay is probably not buying much.

There is a final implication from option mathematics that finance itself sometimes obscures: an option is not a prediction that the favorable state will occur. It is a structure for remaining solvent, capable, or free if it does.

This matters because people often feel foolish preserving options that expire unused. The backup supplier never gets called. The emergency fund sits untouched. The reversible architecture is never reversed. The evacuation plan never becomes necessary. The put option expires out of the money. It is tempting to conclude that the precaution was wasted.

That conclusion confuses outcome with decision quality.

Insurance that was not used may have been correctly purchased. A bridge built with extra clearance may never face the unexpected load it was designed to tolerate. A company that kept debt low before a crisis may appear inefficient during the boom. The value of resilience is often invisible precisely when it works.

Optionality shares this visibility problem. The futures that remain possible because a commitment was delayed do not show up in financial statements. The catastrophe avoided because a pilot failed early does not become a famous product story. A candidate who declines a prestigious but narrowing role may never know which later opportunity depended on remaining available.

Counterfactuals do not send receipts.

This makes option-preserving behavior culturally vulnerable. Organizations reward visible outputs. Promotions are attached to projects launched, deals closed, facilities opened. The person who prevents a bad commitment can look like the person who did nothing. It takes institutional maturity to value information that leads to cancellation.

One sign of such maturity is whether a team can celebrate a clean no.

A clean no is the result of an experiment that answered its question and stopped a larger investment. It is not failure hidden under euphemism. It is the exercise of the option not to exercise.

The phrase sounds circular because the logic is circular. We create a small reversible action to learn whether to take a large less-reversible action. The small action changes our information. That information changes the decision. The decision changes which information will matter next.

The process resembles steering more than solving.

A ship crossing open water does not calculate one perfect heading from departure to arrival and then refuse to touch the wheel. It takes a bearing, moves, observes drift, and corrects. Yet it also cannot reverse every mile without cost. The art is not maximal flexibility. It is controlled commitment.

Markets have equations for a narrow slice of that art. Institutions, companies, and lives do not. They have habits, contracts, pilot programs, review dates, reserves, modular designs, and judgment.

Those are the mathematics of maybe after the symbols have been put away.
