# The Company That Couldn't Quit

Projects acquire biographies.

They have origin stories, champions, crises survived, enemies overcome, anniversaries, internal jokes, special vocabulary, and people whose careers became intertwined with them. By the time the spreadsheet says a project should stop, the spreadsheet may be the least important thing in the room.

This is the human machinery of sunk cost.

Economics offers a clean instruction: costs already incurred and unrecoverable should not determine whether the next unit of investment is worthwhile. The past cannot be changed. Compare the future benefits of continuing with the future costs.

People know this rule and violate it anyway.

The violation is often explained as irrationality. Sometimes it is. But organizations add layers that make the simple version too thin.

Past investment can create real capabilities. A half-built plant may be worth completing even if nobody would start the plant from scratch today. A research program can accumulate tacit knowledge not visible on the balance sheet. A struggling product can have customers whose switching costs and trust obligations matter. Employees may have been promised a path. Contracts can impose penalties. Reputation can make abandonment costly.

The challenge is separating sunk cost from assets and obligations created by sunk cost.

That separation is hard because project advocates have an incentive to blur it.

"We have already invested $50 million" is not a reason to invest another dollar by itself. "The $50 million created a validated process that reduces the remaining technical risk" may be. "Stopping now would strand customers under support agreements" may be. The past matters through the present state, not through the pain of having paid.

This distinction is the beginning of a clean review.

The emotional problem arrives immediately after.

A project that stops turns uncertainty into an outcome. While the project continues, original forecasts remain potentially vindicable. The delayed product may still become a hit. The acquisition may still generate synergies. The drug candidate may still clear the next trial. Continuation preserves hope.

Closure realizes an accounting loss and a narrative loss.

The sponsor must say: this will not become what we said it would.

Organizations dislike that sentence because forecasts were often attached to authority. A senior executive sold the board. A minister announced the program. A founder recruited friends. An engineer spent five years. Stopping can feel like a retroactive attack on the intelligence or character of everyone involved.

So the discussion shifts.

The project is not failing; it is early. The market is not absent; it is developing. The integration is not broken; it is taking longer. The cost overrun is not evidence against the plan; it is evidence that stopping would waste what has already been spent.

Language becomes a bridge over the exit ramp.

The Concorde supersonic transport program became so associated in economics and popular discussion with escalation of commitment that "Concorde fallacy" became another name for the sunk-cost fallacy. The historical project involved national prestige, industrial policy, treaty commitments, engineering achievement, and complicated economics. Reducing it to a psychology experiment would be unfair to the actual governments and engineers. Yet the reason the metaphor endured is recognizable: large public projects accumulate justifications as they accumulate cost.

Scale creates reasons to continue.

The same pattern appears in acquisitions. A company pays a premium for another company, announces strategic logic, and begins integration. If performance disappoints, management faces two painful actions. It can impair the asset financially, admitting the original price was too high, or invest more to create the promised synergies.

Sometimes more investment is exactly right. Integration may require systems, sales training, product changes, or restructuring that were always necessary. Sometimes the follow-on spending is rescue capital for a thesis that has already failed.

The difference is prospective.

If this acquired business appeared today at its current enterprise value and current operating state, what would we pay to own and improve it? What specific future cash flows or capabilities justify the next investment? Which original assumptions have been falsified? Which remain intact?

The review must allow the acquisition to become a new object rather than a referendum on the old CEO's judgment.

This is easier after leadership changes, which is both useful and dangerous.

New leaders can write down old projects because their identity is less attached. They can also overcorrect, killing valuable programs because the programs belong to predecessors. Sunk-cost immunity can become successor bias.

Institutions need a method stronger than personality.

One method is precommitment to kill criteria before investment. State in advance what evidence would cause the project to stop, shrink, or change direction. The purpose is not to bind future managers mechanically. Conditions change. The purpose is to preserve a memory of what uncertainty originally justified the bet.

Without a record, every disappointment can be explained after the fact.

A venture was supposed to reach customer retention by month twelve. At month twelve it has not. The team now argues retention was never the right metric because engagement is rising. Maybe they are correct. But changing the metric after seeing the result deserves more skepticism than changing it after new causal evidence.

A decision journal makes this visible.

Forecasts written before outcomes are less vulnerable to memory's editing. Prediction markets, premortems, red teams, and independent reviews can serve similar purposes. Their value is not that outsiders are always wiser. It is that they have different identity exposure.

The project sponsor is inside the story. The reviewer can be outside long enough to ask impolite questions.

However, review systems can fail if reviewers are rewarded only for stopping things. Bureaucracies can become risk-averse in the opposite direction, killing uncertain programs before learning arrives. Every failed project becomes evidence that experimentation is waste; every successful project is treated as obvious in retrospect.

An innovative institution needs a budget for honorable failure.

That phrase should not become a slogan for sloppy work. Honorable failure has evidence. The team identified a real uncertainty, ran a competent test, reported the result, and stopped or changed when the result warranted it. Dishonorable failure hides uncertainty, moves goalposts, and consumes additional resources because nobody wants to own the no.

The cultural distinction is crucial.

If every stopped project damages careers, rational employees will avoid projects that can visibly fail. They will choose work with ambiguous metrics, long horizons, or powerful sponsors. The portfolio becomes less innovative precisely because leadership says it wants accountability.

Accountability without safe closure rewards untestable work.

This is a recurring theme in research funding too. If only positive findings publish and failed replications disappear, the scientific record becomes biased. If only successful pilots are celebrated, policy organizations overestimate intervention quality. If salespeople are punished for disqualifying bad prospects, pipelines fill with fantasy.

A clean no needs status.

One practical technique is to separate project outcome from process quality in review. A well-run experiment can fail its technical goal and receive a strong process evaluation. A profitable gamble can succeed and still receive a poor decision-process evaluation if risks were ignored.

This is difficult because outcome is vivid.

But without the separation, organizations train superstition. Winners become geniuses. Losers become fools. Everyone learns to imitate surface features of lucky outcomes.

The same problem exists personally.

A person spends years in a profession and begins to dislike it. Friends say, "You have invested too much to walk away." The statement can mean several different things.

It can mean the years created valuable skills that make staying economically attractive. That is prospective. It can mean pension vesting is close. That is prospective. It can mean dependents rely on current income. Prospective. Or it can mean leaving would make the years feel wasted. That is sunk-cost psychology.

Lives are especially vulnerable because time cannot be refunded.

The thought "I gave ten years to this" can become an argument for giving eleven.

But the opposite cliché—"sunk costs don't matter, just leave"—can be equally shallow. A career is not a poker hand. Relationships, obligations, identity, and skill matter. The right question is not whether the past matters. It is how the past has changed the present choice.

Memory without obedience again.

The years can be honored without being allowed to dictate the next one.

This is psychologically difficult because humans seek narrative coherence. We want past suffering to mean something. Continuing can retroactively justify sacrifice if the future succeeds. Stopping threatens to reveal that some pain had no compensating payoff.

Meaning becomes another sunk cost.

Tragic biographies often contain this mechanism. Visionaries persist through ridicule, poverty, illness, and repeated failure. When they eventually succeed, perseverance becomes the moral. The biographies of people who persisted just as fiercely and failed are less frequently written.

Survivorship bias turns stubbornness into destiny.

This does not mean perseverance is foolish. Difficult work often requires years without external validation. The problem is epistemic: how can the person distinguish a valley on the way to success from a road that does not lead there?

There is no general answer. That is why character is not enough.

Evidence must enter.

What has improved because of persistence? Are failure modes being retired or merely renamed? Are users returning? Is the science more reproducible? Is the craft getting stronger? Are costs falling? Are independent people adopting the work without being paid to praise it? Has the probability distribution changed, or only the emotional investment?

Progress needs a changed state.

This is a useful definition because activity can continue forever without changing the state of knowledge. More meetings, more pages, more code, more money, more applications—none proves that the central uncertainty has narrowed.

A project worth continuing should be able to say what it knows now that it did not know before.

If it cannot, continuation is maintenance of hope.

The company that cannot quit often has another problem: no one owns the exit.

Projects have executive sponsors to begin but rarely executive undertakers. Starting is distributed through planning processes; stopping requires somebody to create a visible loss. The rational bureaucratic move is to let budgets shrink gradually until the project becomes harmless enough to forget.

This produces organizational sediment.

Old systems remain supported by one person. Products remain in catalogs with tiny sales. Committees continue meeting. Real estate stays leased. Legal entities survive. Reports are generated because someone once requested them.

Each item is small. Together they consume the capacity needed for new options.

Optionality has a carrying cost at institutional scale.

One of management's least celebrated jobs is deletion.

Delete a meeting. Retire a product. End a partnership. Shut a facility. Remove a metric. Archive a system. Sell an asset. Close a legal entity. Stop funding an experiment. These acts create no new object to admire, but they return attention to the organization.

Attention is capital that does not appear on the balance sheet.

A cluttered company is strategically poor even if cash is abundant because every old commitment competes for managerial cognition.

This is why portfolio review should include not only financial return but option crowding. Which existing commitments prevent us from pursuing something more valuable? Which teams are maintaining legacy promises that no longer fit strategy? Which contracts reduce bargaining power elsewhere?

The opportunity cost of continuing is not zero merely because continuation fits within budget.

Sometimes the most expensive project is the one that prevents a better project from receiving senior attention.

Stopping creates its own option value.

A company that exits a weak business frees cash, talent, and narrative bandwidth. A person who leaves an obsolete goal creates time. A government that retires a failed program creates administrative capacity. Closure is not merely subtraction. It can reopen branches that persistence had hidden.

The emotional sequence matters.

People often imagine they need to know the next thing before ending the current thing. Sometimes that is prudent. Sometimes the current commitment consumes the exact energy needed to discover the next one.

Exit can precede clarity.

That is frightening because it creates an interval with no story.

Organizations dislike blank space too. An empty budget line invites questions. An unused office looks wasteful. A leader without a flagship initiative appears inactive.

But a system with no uncommitted capacity cannot respond to surprise.

The company that can quit has room for the future.

This is not because it loves failure. It is because it refuses to make continued existence the default success metric of a project.

A project exists to change something outside itself.

When it stops doing that, survival is not perseverance.

It is appetite.
