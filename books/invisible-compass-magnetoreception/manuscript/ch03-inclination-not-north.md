# Inclination, Not North

A human compass is easiest to imagine as a needle. One end points approximately north because a magnet has polarity. Early explanations of animal magnetoreception naturally borrowed that image. But the European robin's compass broke the analogy.

The 1972 Science result described an inclination compass. The bird interprets the axis of the geomagnetic field and the angle that field makes with gravity. In the Northern Hemisphere, field lines generally angle downward toward magnetic north. The bird's directional solution can therefore be framed as poleward versus equatorward rather than positive pole versus negative pole.

This is one of those technical details that quietly reorganizes an entire research program. If the behavioral output is axial, then a receptor mechanism that is insensitive to field polarity is no longer defective. It may be exactly the right class of physics.

Radical-pair chemistry enters here because magnetic fields can alter spin-state evolution and therefore reaction yields or lifetimes in ways that depend on molecular orientation. The Earth field is extremely weak compared with the fields used in many laboratory magnetic experiments, which makes the proposed biological system demanding. Molecules must generate a directional signal large and stable enough to survive thermal noise, molecular motion, retinal processing, and ordinary biological variation.

An inclination compass also reveals why `north` is a misleading word. The animal is not necessarily reading a labeled cardinal direction. It is extracting a relation among field geometry, gravity, and an inherited or learned migratory program. When researchers invert the vertical component or otherwise create magnetic configurations that do not normally occur at the test site, the birds' responses become tests not only of sensitivity but of the coordinate system the nervous system expects.

This kind of experiment is unusually informative because it can reject whole mechanism families. A receptor that only reports polarity cannot by itself explain a purely inclination-based output unless additional neural processing transforms the information. Conversely, a radical-pair compass that naturally yields an axial pattern has to solve a different problem: how that pattern is represented, stabilized, and compared with gravitational or visual reference frames.

Mechanistic arguments in magnetoreception are strongest when they inherit constraints from behavior instead of importing attractive physics. The bird has already told us what kind of compass it is using. The molecule has to meet the bird there.
