# The Noise in Oldenburg

Between 2004 and 2006, European robins tested on the University of Oldenburg campus stopped cooperating with one of the cleanest stories in animal navigation. In wooden huts they failed to show the expected magnetic orientation. The obvious suspicion was experimental failure.

The eventual result was stranger. In work published in Nature in 2014, the birds oriented when the huts were electrically grounded and screened with aluminum in a way that attenuated ambient electromagnetic noise over a broad frequency range. Remove the grounding and the compass failed again. Introduce artificial broadband noise and it failed. Move far enough from the urban electromagnetic environment and the screening was unnecessary.

The importance of this experiment is easy to inflate and just as easy to miss. It did not show that consumer electronics are generally harming birds in the wild. The behavioral work was performed under specific exposure, frequency, and experimental conditions. Nor did it prove a radical-pair mechanism by itself.

What it supplied was an interference signature. Weak radiofrequency fields can disturb spin-chemical reactions under suitable conditions. A magnetic sense based purely on a large, mechanically torqued permanent magnet would be expected to have a different vulnerability. The Oldenburg result therefore became one more constraint in a converging case for a chemically mediated avian compass.

It also exposed the unpleasant fact that laboratories are habitats. Electromagnetic noise is normally treated as instrumentation trouble—something to shield so the detector behaves. Here the animal was the detector, and the ambient city may have been entering the sensory mechanism itself.

That creates replication obligations. A result this surprising has to survive blinded protocols, measured spectra, altered shielding states, deliberate noise injection, and independent laboratories. The 2014 paper was explicit about double-blind conditions because electromagnetic-health research had accumulated a reputation for effects that disappeared under better controls.

The chapter's larger lesson is not that invisible pollution explains migration. It is that a mechanism can sometimes be approached through the pattern of ways it breaks. We did not yet know the receptor. But we had begun to learn what frequencies could confuse the animal that carried it.
