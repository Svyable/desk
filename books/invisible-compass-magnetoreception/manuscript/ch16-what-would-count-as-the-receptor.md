# What Would Count as the Receptor

Magnetoreception has spent decades naming candidates. It needs a stricter verb: demonstrate.

A convincing receptor claim should join several kinds of evidence that are too often allowed to substitute for one another. The structure must exist in the right cells. It must interact physically with biologically relevant magnetic fields. It must connect to a signaling pathway. Perturbing it should change neural or behavioral magnetic responses without destroying unrelated functions. Restoring it should restore the response where restoration is technically possible.

For a radical-pair receptor, molecular magnetic sensitivity is necessary and insufficient. The protein must be present in the relevant retinal cells in the correct chemical state. Its orientation and reaction kinetics must preserve directional information. A downstream partner must convert the spin-chemical outcome into cellular signaling. Genetic, pharmacological, or optical manipulations should alter magnetic behavior in predicted ways.

For a magnetite receptor, finding iron is nowhere near enough. The material has to be identified mineralogically and structurally, located in a reproducible sensory architecture, mechanically or otherwise coupled to a transduction element, and innervated appropriately. The macrophage episode demonstrated what happens when elemental presence is mistaken for sensory identity.

The decisive experiment may not be one experiment. Sensory systems are chains. Vision was not established by showing that retinal binds photons. Hearing was not established by finding a mechanically sensitive hair bundle without a nerve. Magnetoreception will probably be solved when anatomy, biophysics, cell biology, neurophysiology, and behavior can be walked in both directions without a gap large enough to hide an alternative mechanism.

That standard also makes room for pluralism. If birds possess separate compass and map systems, there may be more than one receptor to demonstrate. If different taxa evolved different solutions, there may never be a universal magnetic organ.

The receptor question becomes cleaner when the field stops asking where “the” magnetic sense is and starts asking which physical variable, in which animal, for which task, reaches which neurons through which molecular machinery.
