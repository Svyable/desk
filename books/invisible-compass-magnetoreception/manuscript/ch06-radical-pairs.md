# Radical Pairs

The phrase *quantum compass* has done magnetoreception both a favor and a disservice. It attracted attention to a genuinely quantum-mechanical mechanism. It also made an already difficult piece of spin chemistry sound like a portal to whatever philosophical claim happened to be waiting nearby.

A radical pair consists, in simplified terms, of two molecules or molecular fragments carrying unpaired electrons whose spin states are correlated by how the pair was formed. Chemical outcomes can depend on whether the electronic spin configuration evolves between singlet-like and triplet-like states before reaction or separation. Magnetic interactions change that evolution.

In the proposed avian compass, light initiates electron transfer in a flavoprotein such as cryptochrome. The resulting radical pair experiences internal hyperfine interactions and the external geomagnetic field. Because those interactions depend on the orientation of the molecule relative to the field, reaction yields or lifetimes can become direction-dependent.

Nothing in that sentence gives the bird a compass yet. A useful receptor has to anchor or orient enough molecules so that directional anisotropy is not averaged away. The chemistry must occur on suitable timescales. The magnetic effect has to survive spin relaxation and competing reactions. A downstream signaling system has to convert a fractional molecular difference into neural activity. The brain then has to compare that signal with visual and gravitational coordinates.

The Earth field is roughly fifty microtesla in much of Europe. That weakness is not an embarrassment to be solved by invoking quantum magic. It is the engineering specification. Any proposed radical pair has to be modeled and measured at or near biologically relevant field strengths and under chemical conditions that could exist in the receptor.

This is why the cryptochrome work matters. The radical-pair mechanism existed as physical chemistry before the specific biological receptor was known. It made predictions about light dependence, axial sensitivity, and radiofrequency disruption that could be compared with behavior. Later molecular experiments could then ask whether an actual avian protein possessed the necessary magnetic response.

The mechanism is quantum in the ordinary, demanding sense that chemistry is quantum. The extraordinary part is not that a bird escapes classical reality. It is that evolution may have found a way to preserve and amplify spin-dependent reaction dynamics long enough to read a planetary field.
