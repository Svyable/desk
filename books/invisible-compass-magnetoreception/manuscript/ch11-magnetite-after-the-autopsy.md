# Magnetite After the Autopsy

The death of the pigeon-beak receptor story left magnetite in an awkward position. The mineral did not become less magnetic because macrophages had been mistaken for neurons. Nor did every behavioral result involving magnetic pulses vanish. What disappeared was a confidently localized organ.

Magnetite-based transduction remains physically plausible because a magnetic particle can experience forces or torques in a field or field gradient. Biology already manufactures magnetic minerals in some organisms. Magnetotactic bacteria are the obvious example: chains of magnetosomes align cells relative to Earth's field, though that system is not a nervous sense and should not be imported wholesale into vertebrates.

In birds and other vertebrates, magnetic-pulse experiments have been used to probe whether a remanently magnetized component contributes to orientation or map-like behavior. Strong pulses can alter the magnetic state of single-domain particles in ways that a radical-pair system would not be expected to remember after the pulse ends.

The interpretation is difficult. A pulse can produce indirect physiological effects. Different magnetic tasks may use different receptors. A disruption of homing or map behavior does not identify the anatomical site. And the absence of the famous beak receptor means that researchers cannot simply connect pulse sensitivity to a known magnetite-bearing nerve ending.

This leaves magnetite not disproved but homeless.

That is scientifically useful. A mechanism should be allowed to survive the failure of one anatomical implementation if independent evidence still supports the mechanism class. But its status has to change. “There is a magnetite receptor in the pigeon beak” was a location claim. “Some vertebrate magnetic responses may involve ferromagnetic material” is a broader, weaker hypothesis.

The distinction also protects cryptochrome from becoming a monopoly by default. Biology often uses multiple sensors for related quantities. A light-dependent axial compass and a polarity-sensitive map receptor would solve different problems. The cleanest future may turn out to be plural rather than victorious.
