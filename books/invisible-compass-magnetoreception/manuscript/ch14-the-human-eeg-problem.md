# The Human EEG Problem

Humans are where magnetoreception becomes epistemically dangerous.

Most people do not report a magnetic sense. Decades of behavioral claims have been inconsistent. This creates a vacuum into which both overconfident dismissal and overconfident speculation can flow.

In 2019, Connie Wang and colleagues reported a repeatable change in human EEG alpha-band activity after rotations of Earth-strength magnetic fields under controlled conditions. The response depended on field geometry. In their experiments, some rotations with a downward-directed vertical component produced alpha event-related desynchronization; corresponding configurations with an upward vertical component did not. The authors argued that the polarity dependence was inconsistent with a simple radical-pair compass and left a ferromagnetic transduction mechanism viable.

This is not evidence that humans consciously sense magnetic north. It is not a navigation result. EEG alpha suppression is a neural response, not a reportable percept. The sample sizes were modest, effects varied among participants, and the result needs the same kind of replication discipline demanded elsewhere in this field.

The study is nevertheless interesting because it asks a better human question. Instead of starting with “Can people point north?”, it asks whether the brain discriminates controlled geomagnetic changes at all. A sensory input can be processed without reaching awareness or producing an obvious behavior.

The paper also creates an instructive contrast with the avian compass. The human response was reported as polarity-sensitive, which the authors used to argue against the axial radical-pair mechanism tested in birds. If the result survives, the human case may not be a degraded robin compass. It may belong to another mechanism class.

That possibility should make us more cautious, not more excited. “Humans are magnetoreceptive” is too broad. A laboratory neural response to specific field rotations is a narrower claim with a measurable replication target.

The human problem is useful because it exposes the hierarchy of evidence. A brain response can exist without conscious experience. A conscious experience can be claimed without a reliable behavioral assay. A behavioral assay can be reliable without identifying the receptor. Keeping those layers separate is the only way to study a sense that culture is eager to mythologize.
