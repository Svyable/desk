# A Molecule That Notices Earth

In 2021, a team led by Jingjing Xu reported something magnetoreception researchers had been trying to obtain for years: direct magnetic sensitivity in purified cryptochrome 4 from a night-migratory European robin.

The experiment did not involve a behaving bird. That limitation is also its strength. Remove the animal and ask the protein a narrower question. Under illumination, does robin CRY4 undergo radical-pair photochemistry whose yields change with magnetic field conditions? The answer was yes.

The team compared CRY4 from the European robin with homologous proteins from chicken and pigeon, two non-migratory species in the comparison. The robin protein showed stronger magnetic sensitivity in vitro. Site-specific mutations along the flavin-to-tryptophan electron-transfer chain altered the chemistry in ways that helped identify which radical pairs contributed to the effect.

This is unusually valuable evidence because it crosses a scale boundary without pretending to close it. Behavioral work had said that the bird owns a light-dependent magnetic compass. Spin chemistry had supplied a mechanism class. Retinal biology had supplied a candidate protein. The 2021 experiment made the candidate molecule itself magnetically responsive.

It still did not prove that CRY4 is the primary in-vivo receptor. Purified proteins in controlled solutions are not retinas. Field magnitudes, illumination, protein conformation, binding partners, redox state, molecular orientation, and cellular signaling can all differ. The question of how the protein is anchored and how a tiny chemical anisotropy becomes a neural code remains open.

The strongest version of the result is therefore also the least sensational: one avian protein proposed on independent grounds to participate in magnetoreception possesses the required kind of magnetic sensitivity in vitro. That is a major narrowing of possibility space.

The popular phrase “birds use quantum mechanics to navigate” skips exactly what makes this beautiful. All molecules use quantum mechanics. What is unusual is evidence that a particular weak-field-sensitive spin reaction may have been retained, organized, and read by a sensory system operating at body temperature in a noisy retina.
