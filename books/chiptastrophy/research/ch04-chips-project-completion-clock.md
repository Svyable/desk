# Chapter 4 research note — CHIPS projects have clocks too

## Claim to sharpen

Chapter 4 argues that authorized money and announced capacity are not the same thing as working capability when a disruption arrives. The current manuscript uses GAO's August 2026 implementation review to make that point, but the report contains a more concrete clock than the prose currently exploits.

## Primary evidence

GAO's December 11, 2025 review of the first 40 CHIPS manufacturing projects reported project milestones running from November 2024 through October 2033. Commerce's anticipated completion schedule put only 20 percent of those projects complete by the end of 2026, 67.5 percent by the end of 2028, and the final project in 2033. GAO stressed that these were anticipated completion dates, not guarantees.

The August 6, 2026 follow-up shows real implementation progress rather than a simple delay story. Commerce had expanded the portfolio to 49 projects across 24 companies. As of April 2026, awardees had completed all required milestones by their due dates, although some milestones were behind anticipated schedules, and Commerce had disbursed $13.1 billion, about 42 percent of the direct funding covered by GAO's review.

The later portfolio also complicates a fab-only caricature. GAO says 33 of the 49 projects included chip manufacturing, 11 included materials production, and nine included assembly, testing, or packaging; categories can overlap. The seven projects awarded under the second funding opportunity were aimed at semiconductor-supply-chain materials.

Sources:
- U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Semiconductors: Information on Projects Funded to Strengthen U.S. Supply Chain*, GAO-26-107882, December 11, 2025: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107882
- U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Semiconductors: Commerce Needs Plan to Meet CHIPS for America R&D Requirements*, GAO-26-109121, August 6, 2026: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109121

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

A long completion schedule is not evidence that CHIPS incentives are failing. Semiconductor facilities are large, technically demanding projects, and GAO found required milestones were being met by their due dates as of April 2026. Nor is the program confined to glamorous leading-edge fabs: the portfolio includes materials and packaging projects that fit this book's broader ecosystem thesis.

The defensible argument is narrower. Industrial policy has a time-to-capability just as a disrupted supply chain has a time-to-reroute. A project expected to become useful in 2029 may be good industrial strategy and still be irrelevant to a shortage in 2027. The manuscript should distinguish authorization, award, construction, milestone completion, qualification, and production rather than treating any one of them as synonymous with resilience already in hand.

## Editorial use

For a later Chapter 4 prose pass, consider replacing the abstract line "Execution has clocks too" with one concrete contrast from GAO's completion schedule. The strongest version would not attack the program; it would show why a serious resilience policy needs two calendars at once: the disruption clock and the capability-building clock.
