# Chapter 3 research note — who pays for resilience

## Claim under review

Chapter 3 argues that a firm can rationally decline to pay for redundancy even when a disruption would impose much larger costs on customers, workers, critical infrastructure, or national defense. The useful claim is not that private firms are incapable of valuing resilience. It is that the private return to a backup path can be smaller than the social return when disruption costs propagate across organizational seams.

## Primary-source evidence

The White House's June 2021 *Building Resilient Supply Chains, Revitalizing American Manufacturing, and Fostering Broad-Based Growth* 100-day review is useful context because it treats semiconductor resilience as an ecosystem problem spanning fabrication, packaging, materials, equipment, workforce, and demand rather than as wafer capacity alone. It also recommends public mechanisms—including incentives, research support, workforce investment, and international coordination—alongside private-sector action. This supports the chapter's institutional premise that some resilience investments have benefits or coordination requirements wider than a single buyer-supplier contract.

Source: The White House, *Building Resilient Supply Chains, Revitalizing American Manufacturing, and Fostering Broad-Based Growth: 100-Day Reviews under Executive Order 14017* (June 2021), especially the semiconductor supply-chain review. https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/100-day-supply-chain-review-report.pdf

A more recent boundary case appears in the January 14, 2026 Section 232 proclamation on processed critical minerals. Summarizing Commerce's investigation, the proclamation says price volatility can hinder private investment, reduce willingness to maintain capacity, and contribute to facility closures. This is not semiconductor-specific evidence and should not be presented as proof of a universal market failure. It is, however, direct government evidence for the mechanism Chapter 3 describes: commercially unattractive capacity can disappear even when policymakers judge the capability important to downstream continuity.

Source: The White House, *Adjusting Imports of Processed Critical Minerals and Their Derivative Products into the United States* (January 14, 2026), paragraphs 5–12, especially paragraph 8. https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/01/adjusting-imports-of-processed-critical-minerals-and-their-derivative-products-into-the-united-states/

## Strongest counterargument

Private markets already buy substantial resilience when customers can capture the benefit: multisourcing, inventories, long-term contracts, geographic diversification, insurance, spare capacity, supplier audits, and business-continuity planning are ordinary commercial practices. Concentrated supply can also reflect efficient specialization rather than neglect. Public intervention can preserve obsolete capacity, subsidize politically connected suppliers, weaken price discipline, or pay for redundancy that customers themselves do not value because the disruption risk is genuinely small.

The manuscript should therefore avoid saying that markets generally "under-purchase resilience" as though the conclusion follows from concentration alone. The narrower test is whether a specific backup path produces material spillover benefits that the firm paying for it cannot capture, or requires coordination across firms that no participant has an incentive to organize.

## Editorial use

If this evidence is promoted into Chapter 3, keep the existing substrate example and sharpen the sentence after it rather than adding another policy detour. The useful move is to turn "society collects part of the payout" from an intuition into a bounded economic mechanism: the case for public involvement grows when disruption costs travel farther than the contract and the party buying redundancy cannot recover enough of the avoided loss to justify the premium.

That boundary also preserves the chapter's strongest countercase: many seams should remain commercially managed, and not every single source deserves a subsidy or a government-designated backup.
