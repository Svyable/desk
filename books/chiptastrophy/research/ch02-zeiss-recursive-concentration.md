# Chapter 2 research note — ZEISS and recursive concentration

## Claim to support

Chapter 2 uses ASML and Carl Zeiss SMT to make a recursive-dependency point: an obvious semiconductor chokepoint can itself contain another concentrated supplier layer. The strongest version of that claim is not simply that “the only supplier has an only supplier.” It is that some strategically concentrated capabilities are produced by tightly integrated, long-lived supplier relationships that are difficult to reproduce quickly even when the relationship itself is healthy.

## Primary-source check

ASML’s 2025 annual report says the number of lithography systems it can produce is limited by the production capacity of Carl Zeiss SMT, which ASML identifies as its sole supplier of lenses, mirrors, illuminators, collectors, and other critical optical components. ASML says a prolonged inability by Carl Zeiss SMT to produce optics could prevent ASML from conducting its business.

The same reporting also supplies important counterevidence to a simplistic “single supplier equals unmanaged fragility” reading. ASML holds a 24.9% equity interest in Carl Zeiss SMT Holding, and its reporting describes the relationship as a strategic alliance governed by long-standing framework agreements. ASML and ZEISS have co-developed lithography optics for decades. This is not an arm’s-length commodity vendor that procurement simply failed to diversify.

## Strongest counterargument

Concentration can be the product of accumulated co-specialization rather than managerial neglect. A deeply integrated supplier may improve innovation, quality, coordination, and speed precisely because the partners have invested in capabilities that would be uneconomic to duplicate casually. For EUV optics, demanding a nominal second supplier could consume enormous capital and scarce technical talent while producing a weaker imitation rather than meaningful resilience.

That counterargument strengthens the book’s thesis if the prose keeps the boundary clear. The policy question is not “why didn’t ASML dual-source optics?” It is “given that this concentration is technologically real and may be economically rational, what recovery paths exist if the concentrated capability is disrupted, and how long would alternatives take?” Resilience may therefore come from continuity planning, geographic and operational hardening, spare capacity, inventories of service-critical parts, workforce depth, and allied arrangements rather than forced duplication of the core optical capability.

## Editorial use

If Chapter 2 is revised later, keep the memorable recursive-concentration example but resist presenting the ZEISS relationship as evidence that ordinary procurement diversification would solve the problem. The more interesting case is that a supply chain can contain a rational, productive, carefully governed concentration whose failure would still have extraordinary consequences.

This is a useful bridge to the book’s larger distinction between productive specialization and unrecoverable concentration: the same relationship can be both economically valuable and strategically consequential.

## Sources checked September 5, 2026

- ASML, *2025 Annual Report*, strategic-risk discussion: Carl Zeiss SMT is the sole supplier of critical optics; ASML says prolonged loss of that supply could prevent it from conducting its business. Existing ledger source `ct-ch02-002`.
- ASML, *2025 Annual Report Based on IFRS*: ASML reports a 24.9% equity interest in Carl Zeiss SMT Holding, which owns Carl Zeiss SMT GmbH, its single supplier of optical columns.
- ASML, *2024 Annual Report Based on IFRS*: describes Carl Zeiss SMT as ASML’s single supplier of optical columns, ASML as ZEISS SMT’s single customer for those columns, and the relationship as a strategic alliance under the principle “two companies, one business,” with framework agreements dating to 1997.

No manuscript factual claim needs changing on the basis of this check. The improvement is evidentiary boundary-setting for a future prose pass.
