# A Fab Is Not a Supply Chain

A semiconductor fab has the architectural confidence of a cathedral.

It is large enough to alter a skyline, expensive enough to alter a state budget, and complicated enough that even people who work in technology can stand outside one without quite understanding what happens inside.

That scale encourages a category error.

We see the building and imagine the supply chain has arrived.

In reality, the building is where thousands of supply chains meet.

A leading-edge wafer fabrication plant requires reliable electrical power, extraordinary water treatment, clean gases, precise chemicals, silicon wafers, photomasks, deposition systems, etch systems, ion implantation, cleaning equipment, furnaces, metrology, inspection, lithography, vacuum, robotics, software, process controls, replacement components, maintenance knowledge, and a workforce trained to keep all of it operating inside microscopic tolerances.

Before any of those things matter, somebody has to design the chip.

After the wafer leaves the fab, somebody has to cut, package, connect, test, qualify, and integrate the resulting dies into a system. A high-performance processor may need advanced packaging and high-bandwidth memory so tightly coupled that drawing a clean line around "the chip" becomes misleading. A mature microcontroller may need a different package, a different qualification regime, and a long-lived supply commitment because the product using it will remain in service for years.

The fab is central.

It is not complete.

This should sound obvious.

Yet much of the public semiconductor debate still uses "manufacturing" as a synonym for wafer fabrication and wafer fabrication as a synonym for security.

The distinction matters now because the United States is successfully adding fabs.

TSMC's Arizona expansion makes the point unusually well.

The company's first Arizona fab entered high-volume production on N4 technology in the fourth quarter of 2024. Construction of the second fab structure was completed in 2025, with N3 volume production targeted for the second half of 2027. A third fab began construction in 2025 and is planned for N2 and A16-class technologies. In early 2026, TSMC said initial construction had begun for a fourth fab and the first advanced-packaging facility in Arizona.

This is not symbolic capacity.

It is real industrial relocation.

It also demonstrates why the unit of analysis has to expand.

TSMC did not simply copy a machine from Taiwan and plug it into the Arizona desert.

A fab is an operating system for matter.

Its performance depends on whether tools arrive and stay calibrated, whether suppliers can meet purity requirements, whether spare parts appear before failures become stoppages, whether engineers can diagnose yield excursions, whether technicians are trained, whether utilities are stable, whether customers design products for the available process, whether packaging capacity exists, and whether the entire economic arrangement runs at enough volume to justify continuing investment.

The difficult word is **ecosystem**.

Industrial policy uses it so often that it can become decorative.

Every factory announcement eventually promises an ecosystem.

The useful meaning is more specific: a set of nearby or reliably connected capabilities whose repeated interaction reduces the time, cost, and risk of making things.

If a tool fails and the specialist who can repair it is three hours away instead of three days, that is ecosystem.

If a materials supplier can run qualification lots with a customer without crossing an ocean, that is ecosystem.

If a community college trains technicians against equipment and procedures that resemble the jobs waiting nearby, that is ecosystem.

If an engineer can move between a device maker, equipment company, packaging operation, and supplier without moving a family across the world, that is ecosystem.

If a customer can prototype, fabricate, package, and test within a region quickly enough to learn faster, that is ecosystem.

It is not the number of logos on a press-release slide.

The United States once had more of this density because it once had more manufacturing.

As production moved, some of the supplier base, tacit knowledge, career paths, and operational learning moved with it. That loss was not always visible in the financial statements of fabless companies, which could become extraordinarily successful by focusing on design while relying on foundries and other partners elsewhere.

The model worked.

It still works.

American-headquartered semiconductor companies remained global revenue leaders even as the U.S. share of manufacturing capacity fell sharply over decades. In 2025, U.S.-headquartered semiconductor companies generated more than half of worldwide semiconductor sales according to the Semiconductor Industry Association. The United States did not stop being a semiconductor power simply because fabrication globalized.

That success is exactly why the weakness was easy to postpone.

A country can be rich in semiconductor intellectual property and poor in manufacturing options.

It can lead design while losing the routine feedback loop between design, process, packaging, and production.

It can dominate profitable layers of the stack while becoming dependent on foreign capacity for the physical realization of its own ideas.

Then a disruption turns the accounting distinction into an operational one.

The right response is not to deny the gains from specialization.

It is to rebuild enough physical depth that the country has meaningful options.

Meaningful is doing important work in that sentence.

Imagine two countries.

Country A manufactures thirty percent of the wafers it consumes but imports nearly all advanced packaging, relies on a single foreign source for a critical material, has little domestic memory production, and cannot service several essential tools without overseas components.

Country B manufactures twenty percent of its wafers but has two qualified allied fabrication paths for critical devices, domestic and allied packaging options, transparent upstream suppliers, spare-tool arrangements, qualified material substitutes, and contracts that let capacity shift during emergencies.

Which country is more resilient?

The answer cannot be found in the wafer percentage alone.

That is why semiconductor policy should stop treating fab capacity as the finish line and start treating it as one layer in a resilience map.

The map begins before the fab.

Electronic design automation software turns functional ideas into manufacturable layouts. Modern chips contain billions of devices and constraints no human design team could coordinate manually. The EDA market is highly concentrated in a small number of firms. That concentration is strategically favorable to the United States in some respects because two of the largest vendors are American, but it is still concentration. A disruption, legal change, cybersecurity event, or compatibility problem in design tooling can affect the ability to produce long before a wafer is exposed.

Then come the tools.

ASML's position in extreme-ultraviolet lithography is famous enough to have entered mainstream geopolitics. In its 2025 annual report, ASML described itself as the world's only manufacturer of EUV lithography systems. It also disclosed another layer of concentration: its production capacity depends on Carl Zeiss SMT as the sole supplier of critical optics including lenses and mirrors for its systems.

The only supplier can have an only supplier.

That sentence should permanently change the way we draw supply-chain diagrams.

A box labeled "ASML" is not one dependency.

It is a door into a network.

The same is true for American equipment companies. Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, and other firms occupy world-leading positions in essential manufacturing processes and inspection. Their strength gives the United States leverage, technology leadership, export-control power, and economic value. It does not make their own upstream dependencies disappear.

Then come materials.

Semiconductor manufacturing uses a periodic table's worth of strategic awkwardness.

Gallium is one example. The U.S. Geological Survey reports that low-purity unrefined gallium is not produced domestically and U.S. demand is met by imports. Gallium compounds matter for radio-frequency devices, optoelectronics, power electronics, and other semiconductor applications. Germanium, tungsten, silicon, hafnium, indium, rare-earth elements, and other materials appear in different portions of the semiconductor and equipment ecosystem.

The volumes can be small compared with steel, oil, or copper.

The consequences can be large.

This is another place where price and tonnage mislead.

A refinery that produces a niche high-purity input may be strategically important even if its annual revenue is tiny beside a leading fab's capital budget. The material may be difficult to substitute because semiconductor processes are unforgiving. A different impurity profile is not "close enough" when yields are measured across millions of devices.

Then come gases and chemicals.

The cleanroom is chemically intense. Deposition, etching, cleaning, lithography, and other steps depend on materials whose names rarely appear in discussions of national technology leadership. Some must be produced at exceptional purity. Some face environmental restrictions. Some depend on feedstocks with concentrated production. Some require specialized storage and delivery infrastructure.

Then vacuum.

In 2025, the U.S. Commerce Department announced CHIPS incentives that included support for Edwards Vacuum, noting that dry vacuum pumps sit beneath fabs and maintain process environments by evacuating toxic fumes and chemicals. The award was small compared with the billions attached to leading-edge fabs.

That is precisely why it matters as an example.

A pump does not make the cover of the semiconductor strategy.

Try running the process without it.

Then come wafers.

The silicon wafer looks almost primitive beside the transistor structures eventually patterned onto it, but its crystal quality, flatness, purity, diameter, and surface condition set the physical stage for everything that follows. Specialty compound-semiconductor wafers create their own supply concerns.

Then come masks and photoresists.

Then metrology.

Then spare parts.

Then software.

Then people.

A fab full of installed equipment is not capacity if it cannot reach acceptable yield.

Yield is where industrial slogans meet reality.

A process can produce wafers and still lose money if too many dies fail. A new facility can own the same nominal equipment as a mature one and perform differently because manufacturing is an accumulation of small corrections. Temperature control, contamination, tool matching, recipe tuning, maintenance timing, material variation, operator judgment, and statistical process control all interact.

This is why manufacturing knowledge cannot be reduced to blueprints.

Tacit knowledge is not mystical.

It is the collection of things people learn because the line has surprised them before.

A technician hears a pump differently. An engineer recognizes a drift pattern. A supplier knows which specification matters more in practice than another. A production team understands how one tool's behavior affects another step three hours later. These are not substitutes for rigorous process control. They are part of rigorous process control.

When a country loses manufacturing, it can lose these loops.

When it rebuilds manufacturing, it has to rebuild them.

Then, after the wafer is finished, the chain keeps going.

For years, assembly, packaging, and test were often described as "back end," which made them sound downstream in both sequence and importance.

That hierarchy is collapsing.

Advanced computing increasingly depends on putting multiple dies, memory stacks, interconnects, and substrates together at extraordinary density. Chiplets let designers build systems from multiple specialized pieces rather than forcing every function onto one enormous monolithic die. High-bandwidth memory stacks memory beside accelerators. The package controls signal integrity, power delivery, thermal behavior, and the ability of those components to communicate.

Packaging has become architecture.

The U.S. government has recognized this explicitly. In early 2025, Commerce finalized $1.4 billion in awards under the National Advanced Packaging Manufacturing Program, with projects involving advanced substrates and materials among the priorities. NIST's program description states the objective directly: establish a self-sustaining, high-volume domestic advanced-packaging industry where advanced-node chips can be both manufactured and packaged in the United States.

Notice what had happened.

The policy had moved from "build fabs" to "build what makes the fab output usable."

That is progress.

The same logic must continue outward.

Packaging requires substrates and materials. Packaging equipment requires components. Memory requires its own fabs and process technologies. Test requires equipment and engineering. Every layer sits inside another set of dependencies.

The supply chain is recursive.

This is why it is dangerous to talk about "onshoring the semiconductor supply chain" as if it were a construction project with a completion date.

There is no final domestic box to check.

There is only a changing map of strategic functions, each with different economics, concentrations, substitution paths, and recovery times.

Some functions should be built domestically.

Some should be diversified across trusted allies.

Some should be stockpiled.

Some cannot sensibly be stockpiled and need standby capacity instead.

Some need second-source qualification.

Some need common interfaces so a future redesign is less painful.

Some need government demand commitments because private markets will not pay for idle resilience.

Some are concentrated for good reasons and should remain concentrated, with contingency plans around them rather than expensive imitation.

The work is analytical before it is patriotic.

That can be uncomfortable because industrial policy likes simple nouns.

Fabs.

Jobs.

Investment.

Factories.

Those nouns are real. They are also easier to count than the verbs that determine whether a supply chain functions.

Qualify.

Maintain.

Repair.

Substitute.

Reroute.

Package.

Test.

Train.

Coordinate.

Recover.

A resilient semiconductor strategy is built around those verbs.

The United States should celebrate every meaningful new fab.

Then somebody should walk out of the celebration, open the dependency map, and ask the impolite question.

What still stops this fab?

The answer is the beginning of the supply chain.
