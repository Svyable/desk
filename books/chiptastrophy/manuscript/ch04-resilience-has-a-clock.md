# Resilience Has a Clock

In a conference room, almost every supply chain has a backup.

There is a second supplier in a spreadsheet. A distributor has another manufacturer in its catalog. A factory in another country makes something similar. Engineers know a redesign is possible. Procurement has an alternate part number. Somebody once ran a qualification sample. A business-continuity deck contains a green box labeled "dual source."

Then the first source disappears.

The green box turns yellow.

The alternate supplier does not have enough capacity. The substitute material changes yield. The part has the same function but a different package. The automotive customer has never qualified it. The tool can be replaced, but the delivery lead time is eighteen months. The factory can run the process, but it needs masks, recipes, test programs, and engineering work. The second source depends on the same upstream supplier. The inventory exists, but only for six weeks. The new design works in the laboratory and has never shipped at volume.

The backup was real in a descriptive sense.

It was not real in time.

This is the problem with most measurements of semiconductor resilience.

They do not include a clock.

A country counts domestic capacity. A company counts suppliers. An agency counts projects. A procurement system counts inventory. An analyst counts market share by geography.

All of those measures tell us something.

None answers the question that arrives during a disruption:

**How long until the system works again?**

That is why this book uses time-to-reroute as its central test.

The phrase does not need to become a federal index with a decimal point and a glossy dashboard. The semiconductor ecosystem is too varied for one fake-precise score to capture reality. A wafer fab, a chemical supplier, a memory device, a photomask, a vacuum pump, and an automotive microcontroller fail differently.

The clock is useful because it forces alternatives to become operational.

Suppose a critical semiconductor input has four possible states.

The preferred source can deliver normally.

A second source exists and is already qualified at production volume.

A second source exists but requires qualification, redesign, or new equipment.

No practical second source exists.

A market-share chart may treat the last three situations as "alternative supply."

A disruption does not.

The first alternative might absorb volume in days or weeks.

The second might take months or years.

The third is not an alternative at all. It is a research program.

Those distinctions should change where the United States spends resilience money.

Consider critical minerals.

In July 2026, the Government Accountability Office examined whether substitution and recycling could reduce U.S. import reliance for minerals used in batteries and semiconductors. Its conclusion was uncomfortable in exactly the right way: for semiconductors, substitution and recycling may not reduce short-term import dependence.

The word "short-term" is doing the work.

A scientist may know that another material can perform a similar function. A company may have a development program. A recycling process may be technically promising. None of that means production can switch next quarter.

Semiconductor manufacturing turns material substitution into a systems problem because the material is embedded in a process.

Change the material and you may change deposition conditions, etch behavior, defect rates, electrical characteristics, reliability, tool contamination, packaging, or downstream qualification. The substitute can be chemically plausible and operationally distant.

Time-to-reroute captures that distance.

The same is true for fabrication.

If a chip is produced in one foundry, the existence of another foundry at the same nominal process node does not guarantee portability. Process-design kits differ. Libraries differ. design rules differ. memory macros differ. analog behavior differs. physical layouts may need work. Timing changes. Verification has to be repeated. Masks may need to be recreated. Production needs to ramp. Yield has to become acceptable.

This is why a "second fab" can mean several different things.

It can mean a second building operated by the same company using a nearly identical process and already qualified for the product.

It can mean a different site that could eventually run the product after engineering work.

It can mean another foundry that advertises a similar node but has never taped out the design.

Those are not equivalent forms of resilience.

One is a route.

One is a construction project.

One is a possibility.

The distinction becomes especially important when policymakers talk about Taiwan.

Taiwan's semiconductor importance cannot be reduced to one percentage because the strategic issue is not merely aggregate wafer output. Taiwan contains dense manufacturing knowledge, leading-edge capacity, advanced packaging, supplier networks, talent, and customer relationships accumulated over decades. TSMC's role in leading-edge foundry manufacturing is unusually important, but the island's value is not located in one corporate logo.

If access to Taiwanese production were interrupted, the relevant question would not be "what percentage of chips disappeared?"

It would be "which exact functions lost their shortest production path, and how long would each take to recreate elsewhere?"

Some production could move relatively quickly.

Some customers would have alternative capacity.

Some products could be redesigned.

Some markets would receive priority while others waited.

Some capacity would be impossible to replace for years.

The disruption would unfold through clocks of different lengths.

That is what national planning has to see.

The same clock applies to America's new fabs.

Construction announcements are usually presented in dollars because dollars are comparable.

Time is harder.

Intel's Ohio project illustrates why. In 2025, the company revised the schedule for its first Ohio fab module, planning construction completion in 2030 and operations between 2030 and 2031, with a second module following later. The company described the change as a financially prudent response to market conditions and customer demand.

There is nothing inherently scandalous about a semiconductor fab taking years.

That is the point.

Leading-edge capacity is not an emergency product.

If the United States discovers a strategic gap after the disruption begins, a groundbreaking ceremony cannot fill the gap next quarter.

The industrial base has to exist before the emergency.

This produces a difficult political problem.

Voters and investors like capacity that is visibly used.

Resilience sometimes requires capacity whose value appears only when normal supply fails.

Electric power systems understand this concept better than many industrial policies do.

A grid does not plan generation solely around average demand. It needs enough capability, reserves, transmission, and operating flexibility to handle peaks, outages, weather, maintenance, and unexpected events. Not every resource runs at full output all the time. Some apparent inefficiency is the price of continuity.

Semiconductor supply chains have their own equivalent of reserve margin, but the mechanism is more complicated.

You cannot simply idle a leading-edge fab and switch it on during war like a backup generator.

Process technology evolves. Equipment requires maintenance. Workers need practice. Customers need qualification. Materials need shelf life. Some product designs become obsolete. A "strategic reserve fab" without commercial demand would quickly become a museum.

The resilience instrument therefore has to match the bottleneck.

For some chips, inventory makes sense.

For others, inventory decays in value too quickly because designs change.

For a specialty material, a government or consortium might support minimum domestic or allied production capacity.

For an automotive microcontroller, the better investment might be funding second-source qualification before a shortage.

For an advanced processor, portability across foundries may be technically and economically unrealistic, so resilience may come from geographically diverse sites operated by the same foundry.

For high-bandwidth memory, the important action may be maintaining multiple competitive suppliers and enough packaging capacity rather than stockpiling finished stacks.

For lithography, reproducing ASML's EUV capability in the United States is not a plausible short-term contingency, so allied continuity, spare parts, supplier visibility, and equipment-support agreements matter more.

The clock tells us which instrument has a chance of working.

If time-to-reroute is three days, normal commercial inventory may be enough.

If it is three months, strategic stock, priority contracts, or qualified alternates may matter.

If it is three years, the work belongs in industrial strategy, not emergency procurement.

If it is ten years, the country is not managing a supply-chain risk. It is managing a technological dependency.

This is where the phrase "before it's too late" in the subtitle earns its place.

Too late does not mean a cinematic date when the semiconductor supply chain collapses.

There is no single collapse.

Too late means the point after a disruption begins when the only remaining solution takes longer than the economy or national-security system can tolerate.

That point arrives quietly.

A material supplier closes because the product line is unprofitable.

A tool component consolidates to one vendor.

A domestic fab ages out of a process because customer volume moves offshore.

A community college closes a technician program because enrollment falls.

A supplier is acquired and a redundant plant is rationalized.

A second source loses qualification because no one has purchased from it in years.

A small company stops making a legacy component because leading-edge products carry better margins.

No emergency occurs that day.

The clock gets longer.

This suggests that the most useful semiconductor resilience review would not begin with catastrophe scenarios.

It would begin with lengthening clocks.

Which critical functions have become slower to replace over the last five years?

Which suppliers have consolidated?

Which skills have fewer training pipelines?

Which process nodes are losing qualified domestic customers?

Which materials have growing import concentration?

Which components have rising lead times?

Which second sources have gone dormant?

Which product designs have become so proprietary that switching suppliers requires a full redesign?

A resilience strategy should notice deterioration before a shortage turns it into news.

Companies already measure versions of these things.

They track lead times, supplier quality, on-time delivery, inventory, yield, utilization, and qualification status. The problem is that each metric lives inside a commercial decision.

National resilience needs to understand the relationship between them.

A long lead time is not automatically strategic.

A single source is not automatically dangerous.

Low inventory is not automatically reckless.

High foreign content is not automatically insecure.

Risk appears in combinations.

Long lead time plus single source plus high consequence plus difficult substitution is dangerous.

Foreign concentration plus reliable allied relations plus multiple sites plus transparent supply may be manageable.

Domestic single-source production in one floodplain may be less resilient than diversified allied production across several regions.

The flag on the factory does not suspend physics.

This is why the book rejects self-sufficiency as the goal.

Self-sufficiency is an inventory of national capabilities.

Resilience is a set of recovery paths.

The difference matters because no country can economically duplicate every specialized layer of the semiconductor stack at world-class quality.

ASML's EUV systems depend on extraordinary optical systems. Those systems depend on specialized suppliers and decades of accumulated knowledge. Japan holds deep positions in semiconductor materials and equipment. South Korea has enormous memory expertise. Taiwan has foundry and packaging depth. The United States leads in design, EDA, key equipment segments, and many device categories. Germany, Singapore, Malaysia, the Philippines, and other countries occupy important manufacturing and packaging roles.

A safer system will still be international.

It should be international on purpose.

That means allied agreements need to become more operational.

Friendship is not capacity allocation.

A joint statement does not answer what happens when two allies want the same scarce chips during a crisis.

A memorandum does not specify whether spare parts for essential manufacturing tools can cross borders under emergency export restrictions.

A shared value system does not automatically harmonize qualification standards, customs treatment, security requirements, or procurement rules.

If allies are part of the recovery path, the path should be exercised before it is needed.

This could involve reciprocal priority arrangements for critical semiconductor inputs, shared visibility into chokepoints, coordinated strategic inventories, common funding for second-source qualification, emergency waivers that preserve safety and security while reducing delay, and procurement rules that recognize trusted allied supply as part of resilience rather than treating every foreign dependency as equal.

The exact instruments will vary.

The principle is simple.

Do not wait until the bridge is out to learn whether the detour can carry a truck.

The United States has already begun paying for capacity.

By April 2026, according to the Government Accountability Office, Commerce had disbursed about $13.1 billion in CHIPS incentives across funded projects, roughly forty-two percent of the direct funding total described in GAO's review. Awardees had met required milestones by their due dates, although some milestones had fallen behind anticipated schedules. At the same time, GAO warned that parts of the CHIPS research-and-development agenda had stalled and recommended clearer plans and timelines.

That is a useful reminder.

Execution has clocks too.

A subsidy program can be authorized and still fail if the associated research institutions, packaging capabilities, infrastructure, workforce, or customer demand arrive too late to reinforce one another.

Industrial policy is itself a supply chain.

Money is an input.

It is not the output.

The output is working capability at the moment it matters.

Time-to-reroute is therefore not merely a risk metric.

It is a discipline for deciding what to build now.

If a bottleneck can be solved during a crisis, do not subsidize it merely because it is foreign.

If a bottleneck cannot be solved during a crisis, decide whether the consequence justifies paying for an alternative before demand exists.

That alternative might look inefficient.

Good insurance often does.

The discipline is to make the inefficiency explicit.

How much does the second source cost each year?

How much faster does it make recovery?

Which industries benefit?

Who should pay?

Can the capability remain commercially useful between disruptions?

Could an allied source provide the same resilience for less cost?

What evidence would show the insurance is no longer necessary?

These questions are harder than promising "semiconductor independence."

They are also answerable.

The United States does not need to predict the exact next shortage.

It needs to know which clocks are unacceptable.

A resilient supply chain is not one that never breaks.

That standard is impossible.

It is one that knows where the next route is before the first route disappears.
