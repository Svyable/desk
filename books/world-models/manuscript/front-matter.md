# World Models

*The Race for 4D Supremacy*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

A language model can tell you what usually comes next in a sentence. A world model is supposed to attempt something more expensive: what comes next here, after this action, under these conditions, with the world allowed to push back.

That difference is where this book begins.

For years, artificial intelligence advanced mainly inside screens. It classified images, ranked feeds, translated text, generated prose, wrote code, and eventually learned to speak with enough fluency that intelligence itself began to look suspiciously like a language problem. Then the frontier started moving outward. Robots needed to know whether a stack would fall before a gripper touched it. Autonomous vehicles needed futures, not captions. Agents needed environments in which mistakes could be made cheaply. Militaries wanted to test plans against changing terrain, sensors, weapons, logistics, weather, and adversaries before discovering the answer in combat.

The phrase that has started collecting these ambitions is *world model*.

It is still an unstable phrase. A learned predictive state can be called a world model. So can a video generator conditioned on actions, an explorable 3D environment, a robotics simulator, or a foundation model trained to generate plausible futures. Nearby sit older technologies with different histories and assumptions: physics engines, digital twins, synthetic training environments, campaign simulations, operational wargames. Some of these systems model causality. Some imitate appearance. Some are useful despite doing neither particularly well, because they make a narrow decision cheaper to test.

The distinctions matter because a race is forming around them anyway.

Silicon Valley is trying to turn video, maps, simulation, robotics, and generative AI into machines that can imagine before they act. China is pushing embodied intelligence into factories, humanoids, navigation systems, and physical-world data collection at industrial scale. Defence organizations are bringing modern AI into a simulation culture that predates the current boom by generations. The actors do not use the same vocabulary, possess the same assets, or optimize for the same thing. But they are converging on one strategic desire: generate enough useful futures that reality no longer gets the first move.

This book calls that contest the race for 4D supremacy. The fourth dimension is time—not as a cinematic effect, but as consequence. A useful model has to survive what happens after an action. The word *supremacy* is intentionally more provocative than settled. It will have to earn its place. The real advantage may belong not to whoever builds the most impressive simulated world, but to whoever can tell when the simulation is wrong and correct it fastest.
