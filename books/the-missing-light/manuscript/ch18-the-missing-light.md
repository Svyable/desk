# The Missing Light

Somewhere in an archive, a star can be alive and dead at the same time.

The old plate says it was there. The modern survey says it is not. Until the cross-match is solved, both statements remain operationally true.

That suspended state is what drew me to disappearing stars. Not because disappearance proves anything exotic. Because absence forces science to reveal how much presence was inferred in the first place.

The Palomar plates were not built to answer questions about extraterrestrial engineering. They were built to map the sky. The plates became more interesting when time turned them into one side of a comparison. Digitization made the comparison scalable. Modern surveys made it deeper. Gaia made it motion-aware. WISE made it thermodynamic. Rubin makes it continuous.

The result is a new kind of telescope assembled from epochs.

VASCO's most provocative observations remain exactly where they should remain: unresolved. Roughly one hundred one-epoch red sources survived an enormous catalogue-cleaning exercise. Nine apparent transients occupied one small 1950 plate region. Three bright point-like sources on a 1952 red exposure vanished before the next exposure and remain absent under much deeper modern imaging.

None is a confirmed technosignature.

The phrase should not feel deflationary by now.

A confirmed technosignature would be one of the largest discoveries in human history. The evidentiary ladder should therefore be annoyingly tall. It should include instrumental provenance, independent detection, motion, spectral behavior, natural-model comparison, repeatability where possible, and quantitative predictions from the technological hypothesis.

The same ladder can discover ordinary astrophysics that was not ordinary before we found it.

N6946-BH1 is the book's favorite corrective. A massive star appears to have faded into black-hole formation without a classical bright supernova. That possibility was theoretically respectable before the candidate, but the observational program had to be redesigned to notice absence. Nature answered an unusual question because somebody asked it operationally.

This is the best defense of strange science.

Not every strange hypothesis deserves belief. Some deserve instruments.

Technosignature astronomy has begun making that transition. NASA has convened workshops. Public surveys are searched for waste heat and anomalous spectral energy distributions. Project Hephaistos has produced candidates consistent with partial Dyson-sphere models while explicitly refusing to call them Dyson spheres. VASCO has turned old photographic archives into a search for extreme temporal behavior.

The field is becoming less about whether extraterrestrial intelligence is a respectable dinner topic and more about whether a query is reproducible.

That is progress.

The recent nuclear-test and UAP correlations around POSS-I transients are a harder test of the same discipline. They are published statistical associations, not causal explanations. Their value now depends on replication, blinded candidate selection, independent plate archives and explicit modeling of observation and reporting processes. If they fail, they should fail cleanly. If they survive, the next question becomes narrower and more interesting.

The sky before Sputnik gives us a unique baseline because one modern contaminant did not yet exist. The sky after Rubin gives us another because the transient denominator is now enormous and explicit. Between those eras lies a century of astronomical memory.

We should use all of it.

There is a temptation at the end of a book like this to decide whether the universe is inhabited.

The data do not permit that ending.

They permit a better one.

We have learned how to ask the sky whether something is missing.
