# The Dirt on the Glass

Every anomaly researcher eventually meets the technician.

The theorist wants to discuss civilizations rearranging stars. The technician wants to know who cleaned the plate holder.

The technician is usually doing the more important work.

Photographic astronomy is physical chemistry performed in an optical system. A plate accumulates latent image through photons altering silver-halide grains. Development amplifies those microscopic changes. Fixing removes unexposed material. Washing, drying, storage and later handling can each alter the plate. Decades afterward, a scanner converts optical density into digital values. Each stage creates its own failure modes.

A point-like mark does not need to be a celestial point source.

Dust can sit in different planes of the optical path and produce structures that vary with focus. Emulsion defects can resemble compact sources. Scratches and pinholes can be transformed by digitization. Internal reflections can create ghosts. Saturated bright stars produce halos, diffraction patterns and nonlinear photographic behavior. Cosmic rays and radioactive particles can affect emulsions, though their morphology may differ from stellar images depending on incidence and development.

The reason VASCO papers spend time on point-spread functions is that stars are shaped by the telescope and atmosphere. A real unresolved celestial source should look statistically like nearby stars of similar brightness on the same plate. An emulsion defect may not.

May not is important.

Some defects can look annoyingly stellar. A morphology test is a filter, not a proof.

The correct procedure is cumulative. Compare the candidate profile with local stars. Inspect the original scan at multiple stretches. Check independent digitizations if available. Look at the emulsion side physically when possible. Search other exposures from the same night. Examine plate corners and regions away from the astronomical field for similar marks. Identify bright stars that could generate optical ghosts. Recover plate metadata and telescope logs.

This is where archive science becomes detective work without needing theatrical language. The chain of custody is literally glass.

There is a broader lesson for every frontier field in this repository. Extraordinary claims are often evaluated downstream, after multiple transformations have hidden the raw measurement. A UAP video has compression, stabilization and sensor metadata. A seismo-electromagnetic anomaly has detector drift and filtering. A biological signal has preprocessing pipelines. A photographic transient has chemistry and scanning. If the anomaly exists only in the processed product, one must reopen every transformation that created it.

The temptation is strongest when the final image is intuitive. A dot looks like a star. Humans trust pictures because pictures appear less mediated than numbers. In plate astronomy the opposite can be true. A digital dot may be the endpoint of seventy years of mediation.

The Palomar plates nevertheless remain unusually strong evidence compared with recollection because the artifact persists. A critic can inspect the same region. Measurements can be repeated. Independent teams can disagree about classification while sharing the underlying object.

That shared object is epistemically precious.

Consider radioactive contamination, one explanation raised for some old transient clusters. Atmospheric nuclear tests released radionuclides and energetic particles into the environment. Photographic materials are sensitive to ionizing radiation. That broad physical statement is established. The hard question is whether fallout or local contamination could create the specific star-like points observed on particular Palomar plates.

One would want controls: plates stored and developed on the same dates, unexposed emulsion from the same batch, geographic and meteorological information, microscopic grain morphology, event rates around known tests compared with matched non-test periods. Without those controls, invoking radioactivity is only a possibility.

The same applies to scanner artifacts. If one scan produces a source and a second independent scan of the same physical plate does not, the source belongs to the scanner. If both scans reproduce the same density feature, the mark is on the plate. That still leaves sky versus emulsion, but one whole branch has been cut away.

Good anomaly research is pruning.

This chapter therefore refuses to apologize for dirt. Dirt is part of the hypothesis space. So are fingerprints, development streaks, plate flaws, catalog transcription errors and alignment mistakes. A field that considers extraterrestrial engineering must also consider the person who dropped a speck into a developer tray.

That is not ridicule. It is respect for the measurement.

If an anomaly survives the technician, it has earned the theorist.
