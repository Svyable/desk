# The Plate from 1952

On July 19, 1952, the Palomar 48-inch Schmidt telescope photographed a field in Cygnus twice.

The first exposure was red-sensitive and lasted fifty minutes. The next was blue-sensitive. On the red plate, three bright point-like sources sat within roughly ten arcseconds of one another. On the blue plate, less than an hour later, they were gone.

Seventy-one years later, Enrique Solano, Geoffrey Marcy, Beatriz Villarroel and colleagues pointed the 10.4-meter Gran Telescopio Canarias at the same coordinates. Their 2023 images reached about magnitude 25.5. The three sources were still absent. The difference between the 1952 detections, around sixteenth magnitude, and the modern limits exceeded ten magnitudes.

The paper reporting this result appeared in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society in 2024. It does not call the three sources aliens. It spends its time doing the less glamorous work required before such a sentence would even become discussable.

First there is morphology. A moving object on a long exposure tends to betray motion by elongation. The most isolated of the three 1952 sources had a profile similar to comparison stars on the plate. That matters because asteroids, aircraft and near-Earth objects do not get a free pass merely because the date predates Sputnik.

Then there is color and timing. The sources appeared on the red-sensitive exposure and not on the blue exposure taken soon afterward. One could imagine intrinsically red objects, but the deep modern nondetections raise a second problem: whatever they were, the sources did not merely change color. They became dramatically fainter or moved somewhere else.

The authors worked through scenarios involving separate nearby sources, gravitational lensing, fast transients and artificial reflections. The geometry becomes strange quickly. If the three apparent sources were physically separate but their brightening or dimming was causally connected within the sub-hour interval, then light-travel time constrains their physical separation. Combined with their angular separation, that argument can push the hypothetical system implausibly close to the solar neighborhood. A lensing explanation moves the strangeness elsewhere: one then needs a background transient fast enough and a foreground mass distribution complicated enough to produce three comparable point images.

There is another possibility that should always remain near the top of a photographic-plate chapter: the emulsion lied.

Not maliciously. Chemistry does not need motive.

Photographic plates record a convolution of sky, telescope, atmosphere, emulsion, handling, development, storage, scanning and later software. A small defect can look stellar. Multiple defects can cluster. A scratch can become a feature after thresholding. Dust can remove light or create a halo. Reflections and internal ghosts can appear at positions determined by bright sources elsewhere in the optical train. If a plate is digitized decades later, scanner behavior joins the chain.

The triple transient is therefore useful precisely because it refuses to sit neatly in one category. Its star-like shape argues against some defects and moving objects. Its clustered geometry argues against three unrelated conventional variables all changing on the same short timescale. Its persistence as a nondetection under much deeper imaging argues against ordinary long-term variability. None of those facts individually or jointly establishes a technological origin.

This is the first discipline of anomaly research: an observation can survive several explanations without earning the explanation you happen to like.

The three sources also belong to a larger family. VASCO investigators had already reported groups of multiple apparent transients on first-epoch Palomar plates, including nine objects in one small field in April 1950. Later analysis identified additional double and multiple events, some with suggestive alignments. That population context cuts both ways. Repetition can imply a real phenomenon. It can also imply a repeatable plate artifact, survey-specific failure mode or selection effect.

The date adds another layer. 1952 is before the first artificial satellite reached orbit. Modern optical transient searches are flooded with satellite glints. A rotating piece of reflective hardware can direct sunlight toward a telescope for a fraction of a second and look like a point source if the flash is short enough. With enough objects in orbit, the sky develops a human-made transient ecology.

That easy explanation is unavailable in 1952 if one means conventional satellites.

It is not unavailable if one means secret human activity more generally, and certainly not if one means plate defects. Pre-Sputnik does not mean pre-technology. Aircraft flew. High-altitude balloons existed. Rockets were being tested. Nuclear weapons were being detonated. Astronomers handled plates by hand. The historical sky had its own contamination.

What 1952 gives us is not purity. It gives us a different contaminant set.

That distinction becomes the architecture of this book. Every extraordinary candidate is a contest among explanations with different historical availability. Modern satellite glints can explain modern flashes but not ordinary orbital glints on a 1950 plate. Fast radio bursts can motivate analogies but cannot be retroactively observed at radio frequencies when no simultaneous receiver existed. Failed supernovae can make stars disappear over months or years, but not easily generate three sixteenth-magnitude point sources within ten arcseconds that turn off between two exposures. Plate defects can generate almost anything once, but a recurrent geometry asks whether the defect mechanism itself is recurrent.

The temptation is to treat unexplained as a destination. It is only a bookkeeping state.

The 1952 plate deserves attention because the bookkeeping is unusually stubborn. The modern follow-up went deep. The sources remain absent. The morphology is not obviously absurd. Several conventional transient classes have the wrong timescale. The historical absence of satellites removes one contemporary nuisance.

So the plate sits there.

Not as evidence of visitors.

As evidence that old data can still surprise a sky that thought it had already been observed.
