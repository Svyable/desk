# A Hundred Red Ghosts

Six hundred million is an excellent number for manufacturing miracles.

That is not an accusation. It is statistics.

The 2020 VASCO paper compared roughly six hundred million USNO-B1.0 catalogue objects with Pan-STARRS DR1. About 150,000 preliminary sources lacked a modern counterpart within the search radius. If those numbers are recited without the intermediate work, the result sounds like a missing-star epidemic.

It was not.

The mismatches were systematically different from ordinary USNO objects. They were redder. They showed larger reported proper motions. Visual examination eliminated the overwhelming majority. In a subset of roughly 24,000 candidates, the team found around one hundred point-like sources visible only on a single old red plate.

Those one hundred are useful because they show how anomaly populations change character as obvious errors are removed.

A single red-plate detection has several plausible natural interpretations. One is a flare from a cool dwarf star. Low-mass stars can brighten rapidly, and their quiescent optical emission may be faint. Another is an extragalactic transient caught by chance. High-redshift supernovae and other events can appear red depending on spectral shift and filter response. Another is a plate artifact sufficiently star-like to survive visual inspection.

The surviving population therefore did not represent one hundred stars that had been present in the 1950s and physically vanished. It represented about one hundred old point-source detections whose modern absence still deserved classification.

That difference should be tattooed onto every anomaly database.

A detection is an event in an instrument. A source is an inferred object. A physical explanation comes later.

The red color itself is instructive. Suppose an analyst began with a fantasy of engineered disappearances. One might expect no particular preference for old red-sensitive plates unless the engineering scenario includes a spectral reason. The observed red enrichment instead points toward astrophysical and survey-selection mechanisms. An honest exotic search should be delighted by that clue even though it makes the exotic story less likely.

This is where the book's title acquires a second meaning. Missing light is not just something gone from the sky. It is information removed by thresholds.

Every survey has a limiting magnitude. The old Palomar plates reached roughly twentieth to twenty-first magnitude depending on band and plate. Pan-STARRS goes deeper in modern imaging, but not identically across filters and observing conditions. A transient caught at magnitude nineteen on one old exposure may have a quiescent counterpart at magnitude twenty-four that is invisible on some comparison images but detectable in deeper follow-up. A candidate can move between categories as the limiting magnitude changes.

Deep follow-up is therefore more than confirmation. It measures how much disappearance occurred.

A five-magnitude change is a factor of one hundred in flux. Ten magnitudes is ten thousand. That arithmetic allows different mechanisms to be tested. Ordinary variability can produce huge amplitudes in some objects but not others. Dust obscuration has spectral consequences. Flares have characteristic colors and durations. Gravitational lensing magnifies existing background sources and usually leaves something behind to be found once the event ends.

The one hundred red sources also introduce a temporal sampling problem. A photographic plate is a long exposure compared with millisecond astronomy but a narrow instant compared with seventy years. It tells us that photons arrived during the exposure. It usually does not tell us whether the source shone steadily for fifty minutes or flashed for a fraction of a second with enough peak brightness to accumulate the same total plate density.

That ambiguity matters enormously for reflected light. A satellite glint lasting milliseconds can be diluted across a long exposure and still produce a point-like detection if bright enough. Before Sputnik, ordinary satellite glints are unavailable, but fast atmospheric or near-Earth phenomena remain possible. The plate integrates time into chemistry and then hides the light curve.

Modern CCD surveys invert that trade. They can measure cadence and repeated detections far better, but the near-Earth sky is more contaminated by technology.

VASCO therefore lives between eras. Old plates have temporal poverty and historical purity with respect to satellites. Modern surveys have temporal richness and technological clutter.

The one hundred red ghosts sit exactly on that seam.

They are not evidence that a hundred engineered stars disappeared. They are evidence that a large, badly matched astronomical archive contains a much smaller subset of transient-like events that survive first-order cleaning. That is still valuable. It tells us how expensive a real disappearance search is in human attention and validation.

It also tells us what the next experiment should do better. Require multiple old detections where possible. Use independent scans of the same plate. Search neighboring plates. Model proper motion with Gaia-quality astrometry. inspect plate defects around candidates. Compare multiple wavelength surveys. Record exact observation dates and exposure metadata. Separate one-epoch transients from persistent-then-vanished stars.

The last distinction may be the most important.

A transient is allowed to disappear. A star is supposed to continue being a star.

The most extraordinary VASCO-style claim would not be another one-plate flash. It would be a well-established persistent source whose later absence cannot be reconciled with known stellar evolution or obscuration.

The hundred red ghosts are not that object.

They are how we learn to look for it.
