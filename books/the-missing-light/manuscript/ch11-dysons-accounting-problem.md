# Dyson's Accounting Problem

Freeman Dyson's famous contribution to SETI was not a blueprint for a sphere.

It was an accounting trick.

A civilization using a substantial fraction of its star's power has to deal with substantial energy. If engineered collectors intercept visible starlight, the energy does not vanish. Unless the civilization has access to unknown thermodynamics, useful work ends in heat. That waste heat can shift a system's apparent spectrum toward the infrared.

This is why modern technosignature searches often look less like science fiction than catalog hygiene. WISE supplies mid-infrared measurements. Gaia supplies exquisite astrometry and optical photometry. 2MASS supplies near-infrared data. Put them together and one can search millions of stars for spectral energy distributions that look unusual under ordinary astrophysics and compatible with partial stellar energy interception.

Project Hephaistos did this at scale. A 2024 Monthly Notices paper searched roughly five million objects using Gaia DR3, 2MASS and WISE data, applying filters and machine-learning tools to remove confounders. The pipeline produced seven M-dwarf candidates with infrared excesses that were compatible with the modeled partial-Dyson-sphere parameter space and not easily dismissed by the natural explanations examined.

The authors immediately stressed that this did not mean they had found Dyson spheres.

That sentence is the field maturing.

Compatibility is cheap. Identification is expensive.

Young stars can have dusty disks. Background galaxies can contaminate low-resolution infrared photometry. Source confusion can manufacture excess. Unusual stellar activity can alter spectra. A candidate list is therefore an invitation to spectroscopy and higher-resolution imaging, not a press release from another civilization.

Disappearance searches and waste-heat searches are natural partners because one constrains what the other loses.

Imagine a star gradually enclosed by opaque collectors. Optical brightness may decline. Infrared emission may rise. The time evolution could become a technosignature if the changes are difficult to match with dust formation or stellar evolution. But the phrase gradually enclosed hides an engineering scale almost too large to say casually. Even a civilization capable of constructing astronomical areas of material must source mass, manage orbits, survive collisions, radiate heat and perhaps maintain stable structures.

The point of Dyson-style SETI is not that such projects are likely. It is that physical consequences can be searched without knowing the engineering culture that produced them.

This is a profound departure from radio SETI. A radio beacon assumes somebody transmits in a way we can receive, perhaps intentionally. Waste heat assumes only energy use. A disappearing-star search assumes only macroscopic change.

The less one assumes about intent, the more one must know about physics.

This chapter also supplies a defense against one of the most dangerous moves in anomaly interpretation: invoking sufficiently advanced technology to avoid constraints. A useful technosignature hypothesis should make more predictions than a natural one, not fewer. If a proposed stellar-scale engineering project blocks visible light, where is the energy? If it beams waste heat away from us, what geometry and purpose make that likely? If it stores energy, for how long and in what capacity? If it converts mass-energy into some unknown channel, what observable side effects remain?

Unknown technology cannot be forbidden from doing surprising things. It can be forbidden from becoming a synonym for anything.

The Hephaistos candidates also show the value of public data. No secret alien telescope was required. The search repurposed surveys built for other astronomy. This mirrors VASCO, which repurposes USNO, Pan-STARRS and old Palomar plates.

Technosignature science increasingly resembles forensic accounting across archives.

That may be its strongest form. A civilization can choose not to send us a greeting. It cannot choose to violate conservation laws merely because we are not looking.

The missing light has to go somewhere.
