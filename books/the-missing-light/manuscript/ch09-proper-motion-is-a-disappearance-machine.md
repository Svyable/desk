# Proper Motion Is a Disappearance Machine

Barnard's Star moves across the sky fast enough that a century matters.

Most stars are so distant that their angular positions seem fixed to casual observers. Nearby stars betray the solar neighborhood by drifting. Their transverse velocity combined with small distance produces measurable proper motion, typically expressed in milliarcseconds per year. Across seventy years, even modest motion accumulates.

This turns a static catalogue comparison into a trap.

Suppose an old source was measured at a coordinate in 1952. A modern pipeline searches a thirty-arcsecond radius around that coordinate and finds nothing. The naive conclusion is disappearance. But if the star moved forty arcseconds, the source is alive just outside the search circle.

Make the radius larger and another problem appears. Crowded fields contain unrelated sources. The pipeline may attach the old star to the wrong modern neighbor, falsely declaring continuity instead of disappearance.

Cross-matching is therefore an inference problem.

VASCO's 2020 candidate population showed larger proper motions than ordinary USNO objects, exactly the pattern one expects if motion contributes to mismatches. The result is a useful internal negative control. The search pipeline was sensitive to a known mechanism for making objects appear missing.

Modern Gaia astrometry changes the problem. Gaia measures positions, parallaxes and proper motions with extraordinary precision for more than a billion sources. For many stars, one can propagate the modern position backward to an old plate epoch and ask whether the old image lands where predicted. This is much stronger than nearest-neighbor matching.

But Gaia cannot solve every historical case. Very faint, very red, blended, transient or unusual objects may be absent from Gaia. A source that existed only briefly in 1952 has no modern proper motion to measure. Old plate centroids can be uncertain. Color-dependent atmospheric refraction and photographic calibration complicate astrometry.

Again, the interesting candidates are those that survive increasing model quality.

Proper motion also illustrates a philosophical problem hidden inside ordinary astronomy. Identity across time is not observed directly. We infer that the point detected here in 1952 and the point detected there in 2026 are the same physical star because their motion, color and brightness fit a coherent trajectory.

A disappearance search is therefore partly a search for failed identity assignments.

This matters when people describe historical sources as gone. The correct question is not simply whether a modern source exists at the old coordinate. It is whether any plausible worldline connects the old detection to a modern object.

The word worldline sounds grand. In this context it is a spreadsheet with motion.

Nearby high-velocity stars are particularly treacherous. Brown dwarfs and ultracool objects can be faint in optical surveys and bright in infrared. They may move significantly between survey epochs and change apparent prominence with bandpass. A red one-plate source should therefore trigger infrared searches and motion-aware cross-matching before anyone mentions disappearance.

Solar-system objects create an even faster version of the same issue. An asteroid can be present in one exposure and absent the next because it has moved, not because it ceased to exist. On a long exposure it may trail, but a faint object near the detection limit or a short flash from a rotating object can complicate morphology.

Motion is one reason multiple transients are interesting. If several points align along a path, a moving reflector with repeated flashes becomes plausible. If they cluster without elongation, the required flash duration can be bounded. Geometry can turn a nuisance into a diagnostic.

The deeper point is that disappearance is always relative to a predicted future position.

That sounds obvious until the prediction itself becomes the source of error.

Technosignature searches should embrace this. An engineered probe in the solar system, if one existed, would likely move. Searching only fixed stellar coordinates would be a poor strategy. Conversely, a civilization-scale modification of a distant star should remain anchored to the stellar system's motion. Different technological hypotheses imply different kinematics.

The better the hypothesis, the more ways it can fail.

Proper motion is not merely something to correct away. It teaches the search to ask what kind of object it thinks it is following.

A star does not vanish because our radius was too small.
