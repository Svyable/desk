# The Missing Light

## Vanishing Stars, Photographic Plates, and the Search for Technology by Absence

**Sven Hardy Benson**

The oldest useful instrument in this book is not a telescope. It is a piece of glass that already did its observing before most of us were born.

In the first Palomar Observatory Sky Survey, astronomers exposed large photographic plates through the 48-inch Schmidt telescope and fixed the northern sky in silver grains. The survey began before Sputnik. Decades later those plates were digitized, catalogued, cross-matched, reprocessed, distrusted, rescanned, and made to answer questions their original observers did not know to ask.

One of those questions sounds childish until the bookkeeping starts: can a star disappear?

Stars vary. They flare. They erupt. They explode. They move. They hide behind dust. They are blended with neighbors in one survey and resolved in another. Catalogues contain mistakes. Photographic emulsions collect scratches, dust, chemical defects, cosmic-ray marks and ghosts. A source can therefore disappear from a modern catalogue for dozens of reasons without anything having happened to the source itself.

But a sufficiently careful search can turn that nuisance into a scientific method. If an object appears star-like on old plates, survives independent checks, has no plausible proper motion, no obvious modern counterpart, no known transient behavior, and remains absent under much deeper follow-up imaging, then the disappearance itself becomes data.

That is the territory entered by the Vanishing and Appearing Sources during a Century of Observations project, VASCO. Its investigators began with a deliberately provocative SETI thought experiment: instead of listening for a message, search old and new sky surveys for physically extreme changes, including objects that appear to have vanished. The work has since produced a trail that is stranger than the original proposal and less conclusive than its most enthusiastic retellings. A 2020 Astronomical Journal paper compared roughly six hundred million USNO-B1.0 objects against Pan-STARRS, reduced about 150,000 preliminary mismatches, visually inspected a subset of about 24,000, and reported roughly one hundred red point sources present in only one old epoch. Later work examined nine apparent transients on a 1950 Palomar plate and a bright triple transient from 1952 that vanished within the interval between consecutive red and blue exposures.

None of those statements is equivalent to extraterrestrial technology.

That is the point of the book.

The interesting thing about a technosignature search is not that it allows an alien explanation into the room. The interesting thing is what happens to scientific reasoning when the hypothesis space includes outcomes nature is not normally expected to produce. The standards should get harsher, not softer. Every plate defect matters more. Every calibration choice matters more. The exact exposure time matters. The catalog matching radius matters. The proper-motion prior matters. The weather log matters. The history of artificial satellites matters. The fact that photographic astronomy was chemistry as much as optics matters.

There is also a natural control case. In 2009 a massive star in NGC 6946 brightened weakly and then faded from optical view. Hubble, Spitzer and the Large Binocular Telescope were used to test whether it had merely become obscured. The surviving source was far fainter than the progenitor, and the event became a leading failed-supernova candidate: a massive star may have collapsed into a black hole without producing the brilliant supernova observers normally expect. A star can, in a practical observational sense, disappear through ordinary astrophysics. That makes the exotic search harder and better.

Another stream enters from the opposite direction. Technosignature astronomy has matured beyond radio messages. NASA convened a technosignatures workshop in 2018. Researchers now search public survey data for infrared waste heat, unusual transit shapes, atmospheric pollutants, artificial illumination, narrowband lasers, anomalous stellar abundances and other possible consequences of technology. Project Hephaistos recently searched millions of Gaia, 2MASS and WISE sources for infrared excesses consistent with partial Dyson-sphere models and reported seven candidates requiring follow-up, while emphasizing that compatibility with a model is not evidence of engineering.

VASCO belongs to the same methodological shift. It asks what civilization-scale engineering might do to the sky, but it does so with one peculiar advantage: old plates contain a control epoch before the modern satellite environment became crowded. The sky before Sputnik is not pristine. It contains meteors, asteroids, aircraft, plate defects and human error. But it does not contain tens of thousands of contemporary satellites producing optical glints.

That historical accident became newly important in February 2026, when the Vera C. Rubin Observatory issued its first scientific alert stream. On its first alert night it generated about 800,000 notices of things that had changed; the system is expected to rise toward millions of alerts per night. Astronomy is moving from a scarcity of transients to a surplus. The future problem is not finding something weird. It is knowing whether weirdness survives contact with denominators.

This book follows that problem backward and forward through time. It begins with the Palomar plates, moves through catalogue construction and failure modes, spends time with the VASCO candidates that deserve the most methodological attention, uses failed supernovae as a natural model for real disappearance, and then places disappearance searches beside Dyson-sphere work and modern technosignature science. It also handles two recent, more controversial statistical claims involving temporal associations between old Palomar transients, nuclear tests, and UAP reports. Those claims are not treated as established links. They are useful because they force us to ask what a correlation means when the event catalogue, exposure schedule, multiple-testing choices and causal model are all contestable.

The evidence posture is explicit. Photographic surveys, VASCO publications, failed-supernova observations, and technosignature search programs are documented. Individual unexplained transients are observations with unresolved causes, not proof of technology. Statistical associations are classed according to design strength and replication. Extraterrestrial engineering remains an open conjecture unless independent evidence earns something stronger.

The sky has always changed.

What changed is that we can now compare its memory against itself.
