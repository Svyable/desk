# History Is Over, Then History Returns

History is over, then history returns.

The first half of the sentence describes the mood.

The second describes the bill.

When the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, the United States entered a period without a peer ideological rival. The Cold War institutions remained—NATO, the Pentagon, intelligence agencies, nuclear forces, alliances—but the organizing enemy had disappeared.

That created an unusual problem for a superpower.

What is power for when nobody comparable is trying to defeat you?

The 1990s supplied one answer: integrate the world.

Trade agreements expanded. The World Trade Organization was created. Capital moved more freely across borders. Supply chains stretched. The European Union deepened. NATO enlarged. The internet commercialized. Personal computing spread. Immigration accelerated. Finance globalized. American entertainment became even more internationally dominant.

The language of the decade was connection.

Markets would connect producers and consumers.

The internet would connect people and information.

Trade would connect economies tightly enough to make conflict less attractive.

Democracy would spread as countries became wealthier and more integrated.

Technology would weaken borders.

The United States would benefit because it sat near the center of the networks being built.

This was not pure fantasy.

Many connections did produce enormous gains.

Hundreds of millions of people around the world entered expanding market economies. New technologies lowered communication costs. Consumers gained access to cheaper goods. American companies reached new markets. Productivity improved in important sectors. The internet created industries that barely existed at the start of the decade. Former communist states held elections, privatized assets, opened trade, and sought membership in Western institutions.

The world did become more connected.

Connection was not the same thing as convergence.

That was the mistake.

The famous intellectual phrase associated with the period was the "end of history," popularized by Francis Fukuyama. The argument was more sophisticated than the caricature that nothing important would ever happen again. Its deeper claim was that liberal democracy had emerged as the final legitimate form of government in the ideological competition among major systems.

The caricature mattered too.

It captured a wider confidence that the direction of development had become obvious.

Countries might move at different speeds.

They might relapse.

They might fight.

But the destination seemed increasingly settled: market-oriented economics, representative institutions, expanding global trade, and integration into a rules-based international order.

History had become implementation.

That confidence affected American policy.

China's rise was increasingly treated through the logic of engagement. Integrate China into global commerce, and economic development might produce a more predictable and perhaps more liberal political system over time. Russia's transition from communism was treated through privatization, elections, and market reform, even as the process generated extraordinary inequality, oligarchic wealth, corruption, and social dislocation.

Markets were expected to do political work.

The previous chapter explains why that confidence existed.

Markets had out-adapted the Soviet system.

The error was turning one historical victory into a universal sequence.

A market can decentralize economic choice without decentralizing political power.

A country can grow richer without becoming liberal.

A state can use markets to strengthen itself.

An authoritarian government can permit entrepreneurship while suppressing opposition.

Economic integration can create interdependence without creating shared values.

Those possibilities were not invisible in the 1990s.

They were easier to discount because the United States was winning.

Power changes perception.

When a system is dominant, its institutions can look like neutral infrastructure rather than one arrangement among several.

The dollar looks like money rather than American power.

The internet looks like open information rather than a system built largely around American technologies and standards.

Trade rules look universal rather than negotiated.

NATO expansion looks like security spreading rather than a strategic boundary moving east.

The dominant country experiences its preferences as common sense.

Other countries may experience them as hierarchy.

This difference in perception accumulates.

The 1990s also exposed the limits of American willingness to use power.

The Gulf War of 1991 seemed to demonstrate overwhelming conventional military superiority. A broad coalition expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait quickly, and President George H. W. Bush stopped short of occupying Baghdad.

The war looked like a model of post-Cold War intervention: limited objective, international coalition, technological dominance, clear exit.

Later conflicts were less clean.

Somalia showed how humanitarian intervention could become military entanglement. The genocide in Rwanda exposed the consequences of nonintervention. The wars in the former Yugoslavia forced the United States and Europe to confront ethnic cleansing, state collapse, and the limits of diplomacy on the continent where NATO had been created to prevent another major war.

History had not ended.

It had fragmented.

The Cold War had organized conflict around two blocs.

Its end released local conflicts from that structure without making them disappear.

Nationalism remained powerful.

Religion remained powerful.

Ethnic identity remained powerful.

Territory remained powerful.

States still failed.

Militias still formed.

People still killed neighbors for reasons that a theory of liberal convergence could not make obsolete.

The United States struggled to decide which conflicts were vital and which were merely tragic.

That distinction would haunt the next decade.

The internet strengthened the optimism because it seemed to have the political structure of the future embedded in its technical architecture.

Information could route around obstacles.

Publishing became cheaper.

Individuals could communicate across borders.

Gatekeepers lost control.

Authoritarian censorship looked increasingly difficult in a network where copying was nearly free.

The early web felt naturally liberal.

That too was only partly true.

Networks lower the cost of democratic organization.

They also lower the cost of propaganda, surveillance, extremism, fraud, conspiracy, harassment, and centralized behavioral targeting.

The same infrastructure can distribute dissent and identify dissidents.

Technology does not carry one politics permanently.

Its politics are built by whoever controls protocols, platforms, capital, law, and data.

The 1990s saw only the opening phase.

Economic globalization carried a similar double structure.

Trade can increase total wealth while concentrating the losses.

A cheaper imported product benefits millions of consumers a little.

A factory closure can devastate one town a lot.

Economists can calculate gains in aggregate.

Politics occurs geographically.

The worker whose job disappears does not live in the aggregate.

Neither does the community whose tax base collapses.

Those local costs accumulated beneath a national story of prosperity.

The United States experienced strong growth, low unemployment by the decade's end, rising technology investment, and a stock-market boom. The benefits were real.

So was the restructuring.

Manufacturing jobs continued moving, automating, and reorganizing. Unions weakened. Executive compensation rose. Finance became more powerful. College education delivered large returns for many workers while people without degrees faced a labor market with fewer stable middle-income pathways.

The national economy could be successful while parts of the country experienced abandonment.

That contradiction will become central to the next sentence.

For now, it matters because post-Cold War confidence made the contradictions easier to treat as adjustment costs rather than political warnings.

If history has a direction, people on the losing side are asked to adapt.

The direction itself is not questioned.

That is how inevitability becomes ideology.

The decade's racial politics also resisted the idea of settled progress.

The beating of Rodney King by Los Angeles police officers in 1991 was captured on video. The acquittal of officers in state court in 1992 helped trigger devastating unrest in Los Angeles. The O.J. Simpson trial exposed starkly different racial perceptions of police and criminal justice. Crime policy hardened through mandatory minimums, expanded incarceration, and the 1994 federal crime law, even as violent crime later declined.

The civil-rights victories of earlier decades had changed law profoundly.

They had not created a single shared account of how institutions treated Americans by race.

History persisted inside the state.

Immigration similarly complicated the national story. The United States became more diverse through migration from Latin America, Asia, Africa, and elsewhere. Cities and suburbs changed. Businesses gained workers and entrepreneurs. Families built transnational lives. Political conflict over borders, citizenship, language, and identity intensified.

Globalization was not only containers and capital.

It was people.

People do not arrive as frictionless economic inputs.

They arrive with cultures, religions, families, memories, aspirations, and claims to belonging.

The country had to decide whether integration meant becoming more alike or learning to live with more difference.

That question remains unresolved.

The 1990s therefore deserve neither nostalgia nor contempt.

The optimism had evidence behind it.

A bipolar nuclear confrontation had ended without direct war between the superpowers.

Germany reunified peacefully.

Eastern European dictatorships fell.

South Africa ended apartheid and elected Nelson Mandela.

Peace negotiations advanced in places once considered permanently trapped in conflict. Technology expanded access to information. International trade helped drive extraordinary development in parts of the world.

People had reasons to believe the circle of liberal modernity was widening.

The failure was extrapolation.

A good decade became a law of history.

Then history returned in forms the theory had discounted.

The return did not happen on one morning.

Al-Qaeda had already attacked American targets before September 11, 2001. The 1993 bombing of the World Trade Center demonstrated that transnational jihadist violence could reach New York. The 1998 bombings of U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania killed hundreds. The USS *Cole* was attacked in Yemen in 2000.

The warning lights were visible.

They did not fit the organizing story.

The dominant story was that globalization was dissolving old forms of conflict.

Al-Qaeda used globalization differently.

Money moved across borders.

People traveled by commercial aviation.

Media transmitted images instantly.

Loose networks coordinated across countries.

Modernity supplied tools to a movement hostile to the political order that modernity was supposed to produce.

The network did not abolish ideology.

It distributed it.

September 11 belongs just outside the 1990s, but the attack reveals the decade's hidden structure.

The same connected world that seemed to make borders less relevant made distant conflict more capable of arriving inside them.

Connection spreads opportunity.

Connection also spreads risk.

That is the second half of the sentence.

History returns because nothing in markets, technology, trade, or institutional design abolishes human conflict permanently.

Interests diverge.

Identities harden.

Power shifts.

Institutions decay.

New technologies create new weapons and new vulnerabilities.

A successful order produces challengers adapted to the order.

That last point is especially important.

The post-Cold War system did not fail simply because old enemies survived.

It generated new forms of power inside itself.

China grew through access to global trade and investment while retaining one-party rule.

Russia's post-Soviet disorder eventually helped produce demand for restored state power.

Digital platforms would later become centers of private authority large enough to influence elections, speech, commerce, and culture.

Global finance would create efficiencies and systemic risks visible in the 2008 crisis.

The order created the conditions for its own next arguments.

That is what history does.

It does not end.

It changes the problem set.

The United States entered the 1990s asking what to do with victory.

It left the decade assuming that the systems associated with victory would continue spreading.

The assumption lasted long enough to reorganize the world.

Then the world began reorganizing the assumption.

History is over, then history returns.

Not because the end was foolish.

Because every ending becomes somebody else's beginning.
