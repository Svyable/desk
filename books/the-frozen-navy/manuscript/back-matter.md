# Back Matter

## A note on the record

Project Habbakuk is unusually well suited to evidentiary reconstruction because its story crosses institutions that wrote for different purposes. British and American military staffs produced classified planning records. The Canadian National Research Council produced technical work and later institutional histories. Max Perutz carried part of the ice-mechanics work into open scientific literature. Canadian official historians reconstructed the wartime policy process. Parks Canada later treated Patricia Lake as an archaeological site. Naval histories reconstruct the strategic crisis that made a floating airfield attractive.

The streams overlap without being interchangeable. A staff minute proves a discussion occurred; it does not prove that a technical claim made in the room was correct. A material paper establishes measured behavior; it does not prove that a fleet-scale system would have been economical. A physical remnant establishes construction; it does not automatically establish the exact mission or performance of the object. A memoir can preserve details absent from official files while also being vulnerable to time, narrative compression, and contamination by later retellings.

The manuscript therefore uses claim-level confidence rather than one global verdict on Habbakuk. The broad program, Canadian research effort, Patricia Lake model, senior Allied consideration, pykrete research, and cancellation are strongly documented. The exact form of the famous pistol demonstration, the bathtub story, some credit claims around pykrete, and several popular numerical specifications require more careful provenance. Full-scale combat performance remains counterfactual because no operational Habbakuk was completed.

## Working chronology

**1942:** The Atlantic air gap and shipping crisis create strong demand for air cover beyond conventional land bases. Geoffrey Pyke develops the broad concept of exploiting enormous frozen structures as floating airfields through Combined Operations.

**Early 1943:** British and Canadian work expands. Canadian institutions begin secret and urgent scientific and engineering investigations. Reinforced ice becomes central as researchers study brittleness, creep, temperature, and fabrication.

**1943:** A large refrigerated experimental structure is built at Patricia Lake in Jasper National Park, Alberta. Canadian work monitors the structure through warmer months and extends into detailed engineering, refrigeration, and construction questions.

**May 1943:** The Battle of the Atlantic shifts sharply. Very-long-range aircraft, escort carriers, improved radar, intelligence, and anti-submarine tactics reduce the strategic value of the mid-ocean air gap that had helped motivate radical floating-airfield schemes.

**August 18, 1943:** A British Chiefs of Staff memorandum presented for the Quebec Conference describes multiple Habbakuk variants and recommends continued development of floating-airfield concepts.

**August 19, 1943:** Lord Louis Mountbatten briefs the Combined Chiefs of Staff on Habbakuks as floating seadromes or giant aircraft carriers with possible use as advance landing grounds and staging bases.

**September 3, 1943:** Roosevelt, Churchill, and military leaders discuss floating-airfield requirements and direct further examination of special Habbakuks for operations in Southeast Asia.

**September 7, 1943:** Admiral Ernest King forwards the report of an ad hoc committee to Roosevelt. The committee favors conventional escort carriers for the immediate requirement while recommending limited experimentation with other artificial floating-airfield concepts.

**September 10, 1943:** Churchill notes in a recorded meeting that improved prospects for the carrier program appear to make other floating artificial landing fields unnecessary for the operation under discussion.

**Late 1943:** Canadian engineering and policy assessment becomes increasingly negative. The official Canadian history records the judgment that there was no purely technical or scientific reason to doubt construction in principle, but schedule, engineering design, construction, cost, steel, manpower, and interference with other war programs made continued Canadian commitment unattractive.

**1944–1945:** Habbakuk does not proceed to operational construction. Conventional aviation, carriers, bases, and the changing strategic map continue to move beyond the problem set that had created the proposal.

**November 28, 1946:** Max Perutz presents work on the iceberg aircraft carrier and frozen wood pulp to the British Glaciological Society.

**1948:** Perutz’s paper appears in the Journal of Glaciology, connecting the wartime material work to questions of glacier flow.

**1992:** Lorne W. Gold’s National Research Council monograph, *The Canadian Habbakuk Project*, reconstructs the Canadian program from institutional and archival material.

**October 22–26, 1992:** Parks Canada’s underwater archaeology team surveys Patricia Lake to inventory and assess the remains of the wartime refrigerated aircraft-carrier prototype.

**2016 and after:** NRC public historical material continues to present Habbakuk as a real secret wartime research project whose reinforced-ice concept proved technically interesting but whose larger system demanded insulation, refrigeration, steel, money, and time as conventional alternatives improved.

## Research still owed

The full 323-page Gold monograph needs systematic annotation against the present prose. The British National Archives and Churchill/Combined Operations collections should be searched for original Habbakuk design memoranda, correspondence, refrigeration studies, and version histories. Canadian NRC archival material should be used to reconstruct the Patricia Lake construction team, exact labor arrangements, material formulations, test apparatus, raw data, and the Montreal Engineering Company’s cost and construction calculations. Perutz’s full paper and later autobiographical accounts should be compared with Alan Brooke and Mountbatten primary records to resolve the pistol-demonstration chronology rather than repeating the most colorful version.

A publication-grade engineering appendix should also rebuild the system quantitatively: freezing energy, steady-state refrigeration load across plausible water/air temperatures, pykrete creep sensitivity, structural thickness, residual steel requirement, propulsion/drag, construction sequence, manpower, and a matched comparison with contemporary escort-carrier production. Any number currently used from a retrospective account should be tied to the design variant that produced it.

## A final note

Habbakuk earns its place in the history of impossible technology because it refuses both easy morals. Governments did not prove their brilliance by funding it. They did not prove their stupidity either. They bought enough evidence to discover that a bizarre material proposition could survive scientific scrutiny while the machine around it became strategically unattractive.

That is a more demanding story than the joke.

The ice melted. The decision survived.
