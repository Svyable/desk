# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*The Decade Makers* asks whether American history becomes more explanatory when organized around the institution that became the switchboard for each decade’s hardest coordination problem.

The strong version — that one institution literally caused or controlled each decade — is false and should not appear in the manuscript.

The version worth testing is narrower:

> An institution can define an era when it creates a repeatable rule, network, or organizational capacity that other institutions must reorganize around. Its importance is visible in what it coordinates, the behavior it changes outside its own walls, the counter-institutions it produces, and the costs that become visible only after it succeeds.

The winner need not be morally admirable, permanent, governmental, or created during the decade it wins. Temporary bodies, private corporations, movements, financial firms, courts, armies, platforms, and hybrid public-private organizations are eligible.

## Selection rules

A decade winner should satisfy most of these tests.

1. **Scarcity test:** What coordination resource was unusually scarce in the decade — legitimacy, credit, information circulation, mobility, labor organization, risk pooling, expertise, standards, attention, compute, or something else?
2. **Switchboard test:** Did actors outside the institution need to route decisions through it, conform to it, bargain with it, or build around it?
3. **External reorganization test:** Did other institutions alter their own behavior because the winner existed?
4. **Repeatability test:** Did the institution make an action routine that previously depended on bespoke relationships or improvisation?
5. **Afterlife test:** Did its methods, categories, standards, or counter-institutions survive the decade even if the organization itself declined?
6. **Counterfactual test:** If the institution had been absent or substantially weaker, would the decade’s key coordination problem have been materially harder to solve?
7. **Distribution test:** Who gained capacity, bargaining power, wealth, access, security, or voice? Who lost them?
8. **Failure test:** Which failure, overreach, exclusion, or unintended consequence is necessary to understand the institution honestly?
9. **Runner-up test:** Can the winner beat the strongest plausible alternative without redefining “importance” opportunistically?

The selection should not be driven by fame. A famous president or invention loses when another institution did more to make the era’s capabilities repeatable.

## Decade boundaries

The chapters use ordinary decade labels: 1770s means 1770–1779, 1780s means 1780–1789, and so on. Institutions may have been created earlier or reach their largest size later. The question is when their role becomes most explanatory.

The 2020s chapter is different because the decade is incomplete. The manuscript must state a dated evidence snapshot and preserve serious rivals. Current selection: **OpenAI, provisional as of September 6, 2026**.

## Falsification discipline

Every chapter should contain or imply the answer to five questions.

**What would make the winner lose?** Name the runner-up and the evidence that would favor it.

**What useful thing did the winner actually accomplish?** Do not confuse power with social value or social value with virtue.

**What cost did the same mechanism create?** A coordination system that creates no tradeoff is probably being described as marketing.

**Who was excluded from the institution’s benefits or subjected to its power without meaningful participation?** Occupational, racial, gender, class, geographic, citizenship, and property exclusions should be treated as causal facts where relevant, not appended as moral disclaimers.

**What institution appeared in response?** Regulation, unionization, litigation, rival standards, governance reform, political parties, new agencies, or technological workarounds are evidence that the original institution changed the field.

## Evidence hierarchy

For concrete claims, prefer in this order when available:

1. statutes, official records, court opinions, agency records, company filings, contemporary institutional documents, archived policies, and original data;
2. original academic research and serious historical scholarship;
3. high-quality contemporaneous journalism for scenes, disputed reactions, and facts not preserved elsewhere;
4. later institutional histories for chronology and context, checked against primary evidence where the claim is consequential.

Do not use a later institution’s own celebratory history as sole proof that the institution was socially beneficial. First-party sources are strong for what an institution said, did, filed, measured, or formally adopted and weaker for causal claims about its own importance.

## Modern evidence checks completed in this pass

### Chapter 21 — EPA

Primary EPA history records confirm the 1973 lead-in-gasoline phase-down action and preserve the agency’s Love Canal chronology. EPA’s current Superfund history explicitly treats late-1970s toxic-waste crises including Love Canal as part of the political path to CERCLA in 1980. The chapter uses these cases to show how diffuse harm becomes an administrative category rather than to claim EPA alone caused later reductions in exposure.

Countercase to keep: environmental regulation imposes real compliance and delay costs; the 1970s may also be more convincingly explained through energy shocks, inflation, Watergate-era oversight, or the Federal Reserve’s eventual Volcker turn.

### Chapter 22 — KKR

A 1989 SEC speech records the roughly $25 billion RJR Nabisco leveraged buyout and the policy debate over shareholder protection and leveraged transactions. Federal Reserve research provides counterevidence to a simple extraction story: samples of LBOs show operating improvements and tighter control of executive perquisites, while other Federal Reserve work documents event-risk losses to bondholders with weak covenant protection.

Countercase to keep: debt can improve capital discipline and still make the company less resilient; the Volcker Fed remains the decade’s strongest macro-institutional rival.

### Chapter 23 — Microsoft

The Justice Department’s archived complaint and the district court’s 1999 findings of fact document Windows’ dominant share, OEM distribution, the “applications barrier to entry,” and the browser/middleware threat. The same findings also acknowledge consumer benefits from browser innovation and distribution, which must remain in the chapter as counterevidence to a one-dimensional monopoly story.

Countercase to keep: open internet protocols may be the more important institution because they constrained proprietary platform power and created cross-platform interoperability.

### Chapter 24 — DHS

DHS records describe the 2003 consolidation of 22 agencies and offices. The 9/11 Commission and post-Katrina federal reviews establish that coordination and information sharing were central reform problems and that Hurricane Katrina exposed major remaining failures of preparedness, intergovernmental coordination, communications, and execution.

Countercase to keep: intelligence reform through the Director of National Intelligence and National Counterterrorism Center targets the information-integration problem more directly; Google may better explain everyday information coordination in the decade.

### Chapter 25 — Facebook

Facebook’s own News Feed documentation describes ranking as a system using inventory, signals, predictions, and relevance scoring, and its 2018 changes explicitly altered the priority given to public content versus interactions among people. FTC records document the Cambridge Analytica allegations and the 2019 Facebook privacy settlement, including the $5 billion penalty and governance requirements.

Countercase to keep: Apple’s iPhone and App Store may have governed a deeper layer of the same attention economy; Google controlled search/discovery and Android distribution; Facebook cannot be credited or blamed for political outcomes without evidence separating platform mechanisms from underlying political causes.

### Chapter 26 — OpenAI

OpenAI’s November 30, 2022 announcement establishes ChatGPT’s public research-preview launch. OpenAI’s current structure page confirms that, after the October 2025 recapitalization, the nonprofit OpenAI Foundation controls OpenAI Group PBC and holds the governance rights to appoint and replace Group directors. Microsoft’s January 2023 announcement documents its multiyear, multibillion-dollar partnership and Azure infrastructure role. OpenAI’s May 2026 Frontier Governance Framework documents the increasing regulatory and internal-governance machinery around frontier model deployment.

Countercase to keep: Nvidia may own the more durable scarcity through compute; the Federal Reserve may have larger economy-wide effects; the COVID-era biomedical network may prove more historically important; open models or distribution through older software platforms may commoditize the assistant layer.

## Editorial guardrails

- No invented dialogue or interior thoughts.
- No quotation unless the original source is verified.
- No “one institution caused the decade” language.
- Preserve ugly side effects in the same causal chain as useful capacity; do not place them in detachable caveat paragraphs.
- Avoid judging an institution only by present-day values while also refusing the opposite error of treating historical norms as exculpatory.
- Keep the human story inside the institution: clerks, organizers, workers, residents, borrowers, examiners, developers, officials, and users should appear where the record supports them.
- Prefer one deep case over a montage of names.
- The ending of a chapter should earn its aphorism from the evidence rather than restating the thesis as a slogan.
