# The Room That Invented a Country

On May 10, 1775, delegates began arriving at the Pennsylvania State House with a problem for which there was no legal procedure.

There had been blood at Lexington and Concord. Massachusetts militia units had surrounded British forces in Boston. The British government still regarded the colonies as subordinate provinces. Many colonists still hoped for reconciliation. No American constitution authorized a national legislature because there was not yet an American nation to authorize one.

Yet the delegates who gathered in Philadelphia had to behave as if some common authority already existed.

That is the first institutional trick in the American story. Before the United States could defend itself, tax itself, borrow, negotiate with foreign powers, or declare independence, somebody had to make decisions on behalf of a political object that had not fully come into being. The Second Continental Congress became that somebody.

It is easy to remember the Congress as a stage on which famous men appeared. John Adams argued. Benjamin Franklin returned from London with little remaining faith in imperial compromise. Thomas Jefferson drafted. George Washington was appointed commander in chief. The names are so familiar that they can obscure the stranger achievement. Congress was not merely a gathering of founders. It was a temporary institution that acquired authority by using it.

The colonies had sent delegates for different reasons and under different instructions. Their economies, religions, local political habits, exposure to war, and dependence on slavery varied widely. Some had powerful assemblies. Some were more commercially connected to Britain. Some had reason to fear frontier warfare. None had elected a national government. Even the word national would have made many delegates uneasy.

Congress therefore began with less power than the municipal government of an ordinary town and more responsibility than almost any legislature in the world.

Its first great act was not the Declaration of Independence. It was administrative improvisation.

The men in Philadelphia had to decide what to do about the armed New Englanders outside Boston. Leaving them as separate provincial forces risked military confusion and political fragmentation. Creating a Continental Army did something more than regularize command. It turned a regional conflict into a common one. On June 14, 1775, Congress moved toward creating that army. The next day it selected Washington to command it.

Washington mattered in part because he was a Virginian. The choice sent a political message before it sent a military one: Massachusetts would not fight alone. Congress was taking ownership of a war it had not started and could not yet finance properly.

That decision reveals why the institution matters more than the biography. Washington could command only because an organization claimed the right to appoint him. Soldiers could increasingly understand themselves as serving a continental cause because the Congress supplied a continental frame. Foreign governments could later negotiate with American commissioners because some body stood behind the commission. A declaration could speak for “the thirteen united States” because a forum had spent more than a year learning, awkwardly, how to make thirteen governments act together.

Congress did not acquire sovereignty in one dramatic moment. It accumulated it task by task.

It authorized paper money. It created committees to handle war, diplomacy, commerce, and correspondence. It sought supplies. It issued instructions. It established a postal system under Benjamin Franklin. It negotiated with Indigenous nations while also participating in a continental expansion that would dispossess them. It appealed to Canada. It debated trade. It tried to influence public opinion. It sent agents abroad. It dealt with prisoners, gunpowder, uniforms, ships, debts, and the endless practical details that separate a political aspiration from a functioning government.

Every one of those tasks contained a constitutional question Congress could not fully answer: Who gave you the right?

The most important answer was that the colonies kept sending delegates and, within limits, obeying.

Institutional authority often looks obvious after it becomes established. A modern Treasury secretary signs under a statute. A court cites jurisdiction. A company points to corporate law and its charter. Congress in 1775 operated in a more primitive loop. It acted because coordination was necessary; the colonies accepted enough of its acts because coordination was necessary; that acceptance made the next act more plausible.

This is not the same as saying its authority was democratic in the modern sense. It was not. The political community represented in Philadelphia excluded most people living in the colonies from formal participation. Women had no vote in choosing the governments that selected or authorized delegates. Enslaved people were property under law in much of the country. Indigenous nations were treated as diplomatic counterparts when useful and obstacles when expansion demanded it. Property qualifications and local election rules narrowed the electorate. Even among free white men, representation was indirect and uneven.

The institution that helped invent the country also carried into the country the exclusions of the societies that created it.

That matters because institutions do not arrive from nowhere. They concentrate the available legitimacy of their time, including its blind spots.

Congress’s weakness was visible in money.

Wars turn political theory into accounts payable. Soldiers need food. Wagons break. Powder runs out. Contracts require somebody willing to accept a promise of payment. Congress could issue bills of credit, request funds from the states, and borrow, but it had no mature tax system under its own control. The paper it emitted depended on confidence in a government whose victory was uncertain. Inflation eventually became severe. The phrase “not worth a Continental” survived because state-building has a way of leaving its failures in ordinary speech.

Yet the financial weakness makes the achievement more interesting. Congress built enough credibility to keep the war politically organized while lacking many of the fiscal tools later governments would consider basic. It learned that a government can declare obligations more easily than it can collect resources. That lesson would drive the institutional fights of the 1780s and 1790s.

The Congress was also a machine for converting disagreement into delay rather than immediate fracture.

The colonies did not enter 1775 with one view of independence. The Olive Branch Petition, approved in July, expressed continued hope that the king might help restore harmony. At roughly the same time, Congress was preparing for war. To later readers this can look inconsistent. Inside an uncertain political coalition, it was functional. The institution permitted incompatible expectations to coexist long enough for events to change the balance.

By early 1776, the political environment had moved. The king had rejected the colonists’ appeals. Fighting had expanded. Thomas Paine’s Common Sense made the case for independence in language that reached far beyond legislative chambers. Colonial and local bodies began giving delegates new instructions.

Then Congress did another institutional thing that myth tends to personalize.

It formed a committee.

The Committee of Five—Jefferson, John Adams, Franklin, Roger Sherman, and Robert R. Livingston—was charged with drafting a declaration. Jefferson produced the initial text, but the document that emerged belonged to a process. Adams and Franklin reviewed it. Congress debated it and made changes. The Declaration’s authority did not come from the literary gifts of its principal drafter. It came from adoption by the delegates acting for the colonies.

The famous sentence about equality has outlived the institution that adopted it and has repeatedly been turned against the country’s own failures to honor it. That too is an institutional effect. An organization can create a text whose future users make claims the original members did not fully intend to satisfy.

On July 2, Congress approved the resolution for independence. On July 4, it approved the Declaration’s text. The date became sacred, but the decision did not magically solve the problems Congress had been handling. The war still had to be won. Foreign recognition still had to be secured. State governments still guarded their powers. Army officers still complained about supply. Congress still lacked reliable revenue.

Its most consequential foreign success grew from the same logic of institutional credibility.

American diplomats in Europe did not simply ask France to admire a rebellion. They sought an alliance that would serve French strategic interests. Victory at Saratoga in 1777 strengthened the American case. The treaties with France in 1778 brought military and financial support that materially changed the war. Diplomacy worked because a foreign government could treat the United States as a political actor with representatives authorized by a continental body.

The Continental Congress was often inefficient, divided, and exhausted. Delegates came and went. Committees overlapped. States ignored requests. Personal rivalries mattered. The army occasionally seemed closer to collapse than later patriotic memory prefers to admit. There was no clean organizational chart for revolution.

That mess is precisely why the institution deserves the decade.

The rival candidate is the Continental Army. Without military survival, no declaration would have mattered. Another candidate is the network of colonial and state legislatures that supplied legitimacy, soldiers, and political participation. The printing press deserves a claim because pamphlets and newspapers created a continental argument. The French alliance may have been indispensable.

But each of those forces required a point of political aggregation.

An army without a political authority risks becoming a regional force or a military regime. State legislatures without a continental forum can coordinate only intermittently. Printers can create opinion but cannot sign a treaty. France needed negotiators who could plausibly bind a new nation. The Congress was the switchboard.

Its deepest innovation was not any single vote. It was the idea that the colonies could become accustomed to doing common business through a common institution before they fully agreed on what their common government should be.

This is one of the recurring patterns of American history. An institution is often most important when it occupies a coordination gap. It does not need to be elegant. It needs to become the place where problems that cannot be solved separately are forced into the same room.

The Second Continental Congress did that for independence.

It did not design a durable federal government. In fact, its weaknesses helped demonstrate the need for one. The Articles of Confederation formalized a system in which Congress remained dependent on the states for crucial powers. After the war, debts, interstate disputes, trade policy, and fiscal weakness would expose the limits of the arrangement.

But a failed final design should not erase the institutional achievement of the emergency.

Congress taught thirteen colonies to speak in the first-person plural.

The plural was contested from the beginning. It would nearly break over slavery. It would expand by conquest, purchase, treaty, migration, and dispossession. It would be redefined by civil war, amendments, immigration, social movements, and struggles over citizenship. There has never been one uncontested answer to who “we” includes.

The important point in the 1770s is more basic. Before Americans could fight over the meaning of the nation, they needed an institution capable of acting as if the nation existed.

For much of the decade, it barely did.

Delegates traveled over poor roads to a city that could be threatened by British forces. They relied on letters that took days or weeks. They represented governments that could revoke their instructions. They had no permanent executive branch, no professional civil service, no reliable national tax collector, and no guarantee that their experiment would survive the season.

Then, gradually, the temporary meeting became the source of commissions, money, diplomatic credentials, military authority, and foundational texts.

The Congress did not discover a country waiting to be governed.

It practiced the country into existence.

That is why the most important American institution of the 1770s was not the army, the press, the statehouse, or even the declaration. It was the room in which enough separate governments kept returning to the same table.

America’s first great institution was an appointment the future kept.
