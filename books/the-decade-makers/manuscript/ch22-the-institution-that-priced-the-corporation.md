# The Institution That Priced the Corporation

In October 1988, the board of RJR Nabisco opened a contest over who should own the company.

The company made cigarettes and food. It also maintained a corporate life elaborate enough to become an argument about what the modern corporation was for. Management had grown comfortable inside an empire assembled through mergers. The company had jets. Executives had discretion. Shareholders owned the legal residue of the enterprise, but the people running it possessed the information, relationships, and daily authority that made ownership abstract.

Then chief executive F. Ross Johnson helped propose taking the company private.

The proposal was not unusual merely because it was large. It was institutionally provocative because management appeared on both sides of the table. Executives paid to run the public company were now participating in an effort to buy it. The offer suggested that insiders believed the market had priced the company too low and that a new ownership structure could extract value the public corporation was leaving unused.

Kohlberg Kravis Roberts & Co., better known as KKR, entered the contest. Banks, bond investors, lawyers, directors, investment bankers, managers, and rival bidders assembled around a transaction whose value reached roughly $25 billion, then an extraordinary figure for a leveraged buyout.

RJR Nabisco became the most famous takeover of the decade because the deal turned a governance argument into an auction.

Who should control the corporation?

The answer became: whoever could finance the price.

That is why KKR is my choice for the most important American institution of the 1980s.

The Federal Reserve is the strongest rival. Paul Volcker’s Fed broke an inflationary regime that had shaped the previous decade, accepting deep recession and high unemployment as the cost of restoring price stability and institutional credibility. The Reagan administration altered expectations around taxation, regulation, organized labor, and the size and purpose of government. The personal-computer industry moved computation from centralized institutions onto desks. Cable television widened the media system. Investment banks and the high-yield bond market transformed corporate finance.

KKR nevertheless represents something institutionally new. It made the redesign of other institutions into a business.

The decade’s scarce resource was not capital in the ordinary sense. America had banks, pension funds, insurers, securities markets, corporate cash, and wealthy investors.

The scarce resource was concentrated ownership capable of disciplining a corporation whose managers had become difficult for dispersed owners to govern.

That problem was the unintended consequence of an earlier institutional success.

The twentieth-century managerial corporation had separated ownership from day-to-day control. Millions of shareholders could own pieces of a company without meeting one another. Professional executives could coordinate factories, product lines, research, distribution, finance, and personnel across enormous enterprises. The structure allowed firms to outlive founders and operate at a scale no partnership could manage through personal relationships alone.

General Motors had helped make that structure ordinary in the 1920s. By the 1980s its weakness was equally ordinary.

If a company had one million shareholders, which shareholder was responsible for noticing that headquarters had become complacent?

Each investor could sell. Few had reason to do the work of reorganizing the whole enterprise. Boards were formally responsible for overseeing management, but directors could be socially and professionally close to the executives they supervised. Managers possessed better information than outside owners. A chief executive could describe a weak division as a long-term investment, an unnecessary acquisition as strategy, or an indulgence as a business requirement.

Some of those explanations were true.

That was the difficulty.

The governance problem did not come with a red light identifying which expense was waste and which was patient investment.

The leveraged buyout created a brutally simple diagnostic.

Could an outsider buy the company at a premium, place much more debt on the business, change incentives and management, sell weak assets, reduce discretionary spending, and still make the economics work?

If the answer was yes, the buyout firm claimed the public corporation had been leaving value on the table.

KKR did not invent leveraged buyouts. Jerome Kohlberg Jr., Henry Kravis, and George Roberts had worked on what were then often called bootstrap acquisitions before founding their own firm in 1976. Other buyout groups, investment banks, corporate raiders, and lawyers contributed to the takeover revolution.

KKR became the defining institution because it turned the model into a repeatable organization at unprecedented scale.

The firm raised equity from investors, identified targets, negotiated with boards, arranged debt, installed governance, worked with or replaced management, sold assets, and eventually exited investments. Its real product was not one security.

It was a temporary constitution for the corporation.

The public company had diffuse owners, a board, professional management, and relatively flexible access to retained earnings.

The leveraged company had concentrated owners, a smaller governing circle, managers with stronger equity incentives, and debt obligations that made cash impossible to ignore.

Debt was not merely financing.

Debt was a management system.

A company carrying large fixed obligations cannot postpone every hard decision. Interest comes due whether the strategic review is finished or not. Free cash that might have funded acquisitions, headquarters expansion, executive privileges, or low-return projects now has a prior claimant.

This was the attraction captured in the era’s academic language about agency costs and free cash flow. Managers might be too comfortable because the public corporation allowed them to retain and spend cash generated by mature businesses. Leverage could force that cash back toward capital providers and make management ownership more meaningful.

There is evidence that this mechanism sometimes produced real operating gains.

Research on 1980s leveraged buyouts found improvements in operating performance in important samples. Later Federal Reserve research examining corporate aircraft found that firms going through buyouts sharply reduced jet fleets, an unusually vivid proxy for discretionary executive consumption. The famous RJR fleet supplied the anecdote: after the buyout and a change in chief executive, most of the company’s jets were sold.

The jet matters because it makes an abstract governance problem visible.

A corporate aircraft can be productive. Executives travel. Plants and customers are distributed. A jet can save time worth more than the cost.

Eight jets can also be a clue.

The institutional value of concentrated ownership was not that private-equity partners possessed a mystical ability to distinguish every justified expense from every indulgence. It was that somebody with a large financial stake now had reason to ask the question in detail.

This is the strongest case for KKR.

The strongest case against it begins in the same balance sheet.

Debt disciplines management because debt removes slack.

Slack is not always waste.

A company with cash and unused borrowing capacity can survive a recession, absorb a failed product, fund research whose payoff is years away, maintain redundant suppliers, train employees, carry more inventory, or invest before demand is certain. A company optimized tightly around debt service has less room to be wrong.

The same structure that can eliminate executive excess can eliminate resilience.

RJR Nabisco illustrated the problem. The price required enormous financing. Federal Reserve records from the period treated the buyout as large enough to affect aggregate borrowing patterns. Soon afterward, RJR had to reduce leverage substantially through asset sales, equity issuance, and debt restructuring. The transaction did not prove that the business had been worthless under public ownership. It proved that a purchase price can create its own operating emergency.

The firm after the deal must earn not only against its business competition but against the capital structure chosen to acquire it.

That changes the definition of success.

A manager before the buyout may ask whether a factory earns an adequate return over ten years.

A highly leveraged owner may also need to ask whether selling the factory this year is the fastest way to meet obligations and protect the equity investment.

Those horizons can align. They can also conflict.

This is where the social argument over leveraged buyouts becomes harder than the financial one.

Shareholders are liquid participants. A pension fund can sell one stock and buy another. A buyout fund can own many companies. A bank can syndicate a loan.

An employee cannot diversify a thirty-year career with one click.

A town cannot sell ten percent of its exposure to the plant that funds its tax base.

A supplier may have built specialized capacity around one customer. A retiree may depend on benefits negotiated under an earlier corporate compact.

The transaction price does not automatically include every relationship the corporation has accumulated.

The 1980s takeover revolution therefore forced a question American corporate law and politics have never fully settled: whose institution is the corporation?

Legally, shareholders possess powerful claims and boards owe duties defined through corporate law. Creditors possess contractual claims. Employees have rights under labor and employment law. Pension promises are governed by another body of law. Communities collect taxes but do not own the firm merely because they depend on it.

Yet an operating company is more than the stack of legally enforceable claims.

It is also a bundle of expectations.

People organize lives around its continuity.

Leveraged buyouts made the gap between legal ownership and social dependence impossible to ignore.

The takeover defenses of the decade were institutions built in response.

Boards adopted poison pills and other mechanisms intended to slow or deter hostile bids. State legislatures considered antitakeover laws. Delaware courts became increasingly important referees of directors’ conduct during takeover battles. Investment bankers built specialized mergers-and-acquisitions practices. Lawyers learned a new grammar of duties, defenses, auctions, fairness opinions, and deal protection.

A market for corporate control had existed before the 1980s.

By the end of the decade, corporate America had reorganized itself around the possibility of that market becoming hostile.

The threat mattered even when no buyout occurred.

Executives sold weak divisions before raiders could point to them. Companies repurchased shares, increased leverage, changed compensation, focused strategy, and defended why unrelated businesses belonged together. Boards became more conscious of shareholder returns. Conglomerates that had been assembled under one theory of management were dismantled under another.

An institution had become powerful enough to alter the behavior of organizations it never purchased.

That is one of my tests for choosing a decade’s winner.

The high-yield bond market made the threat scalable.

Drexel Burnham Lambert and Michael Milken became synonymous with the expansion of below-investment-grade debt as a financing source. The instruments themselves were not inherently corrupt or irrational. Companies outside the investment-grade establishment could use high-yield bonds to access capital markets. In takeover finance, the market helped bidders assemble sums that older financing conventions would have made much harder to raise.

Banks remained central as well. RJR Nabisco required a large consortium of financing commitments. The buyout firm was therefore not a lone predator carrying a checkbook.

It was a coordinator of institutions willing to accept different layers of risk.

This is why the 1980s financial system deserves to be understood as organizational technology.

The innovation was not simply a new kind of bond.

It was the ability to make a temporary coalition behave as if it were one buyer.

Equity investors accepted residual risk. Banks supplied loans. Bond buyers purchased higher-yield claims. Lawyers standardized documents. Boards received offers. advisers produced valuations. Courts established boundaries. Management teams were promised equity or replaced.

The deal was a federation.

KKR’s skill was making the federation close.

The model also produced its own discipline for the buyout firm.

A private-equity partnership does not keep permanent ownership capital in the same way a family holding company might. Funds have investors and investment periods. Portfolio companies are generally bought with an expectation of eventual exit. That gives the ownership structure a clock.

The clock can encourage action.

It can also encourage financial results that mature before institutional consequences do.

This is the deeper reason the leveraged buyout is an institution of the 1980s rather than merely a financing technique.

It changed corporate time.

Managers of the classic postwar corporation could imagine inheriting an enterprise from predecessors and handing it to successors. The company itself possessed continuity.

The buyout owner imagines an entry price and an exit.

What happens between them becomes a value-creation plan.

That language later spread far beyond private equity. Public-company executives increasingly spoke in portfolio terms, evaluated business units against hurdle rates, outsourced functions, linked compensation to equity performance, and faced activist investors demanding explicit catalysts for value.

Corporate boundaries became negotiable.

This did not mean every American company became short-term.

Many firms continued investing patiently. Some private-equity owners supplied capital and strategic discipline that public markets had not. Some public corporations destroyed value through empire building. Some highly leveraged acquisitions failed. Some family-controlled firms took long horizons and tolerated mediocrity. There is no ownership form that eliminates bad judgment.

The 1980s contribution was to make ownership form itself a managerial variable.

Before the decade, a large public corporation could treat its existence as a background fact.

After the takeover revolution, management increasingly had to explain why the existing ownership and asset configuration deserved to continue.

Possession needed a financial defense.

The decade’s labor politics strengthened the shift.

The Reagan administration’s response to the 1981 Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization strike demonstrated that a powerful labor organization could lose a confrontation with the federal government and that replacement of striking workers was politically conceivable on a scale with national symbolic force. Union density continued a longer decline. Manufacturers confronted global competition, especially from Japan and newly industrializing economies. Industrial regions experienced plant closures and restructuring.

The takeover institution entered an economy already questioning the postwar bargain among large companies, organized labor, and stable employment.

It did not create deindustrialization.

It made restructuring an investable strategy.

This distinction is important. It is easy to make KKR a villain because the narrative has recognizable rich people and layoffs have identifiable victims. It is equally easy to make KKR a hero because management excess and bad capital allocation are real.

A serious institutional history has to preserve both.

The buyout is socially valuable when concentrated ownership creates operating improvements larger than the financing costs and does so without merely transferring value from creditors, workers, taxpayers, or future investment.

It is destructive when returns come principally from leverage, fee extraction, favorable tax treatment, asset transfers, pension weakening, or selling resilience that later owners will need.

The difficult part is that the same deal can contain both mechanisms.

RJR Nabisco itself generated new financial protections beyond the company. Research on corporate bonds after the takeover found that bondholders with weak covenant protection could suffer losses from the sudden increase in leverage. The market responded by developing stronger event-risk protections in some bond contracts.

One institution’s innovation became another institution’s clause.

That pattern runs through this entire book.

The railroad created the case for regulation.

The mass corporation created the case for organized labor.

The social platform created the case for content moderation.

Leveraged buyouts created the case for takeover defenses, stronger covenants, and a more explicit debate over fiduciary duty.

Institutions evolve through adversaries.

The Federal Reserve still deserves the final comparison.

Volcker’s Fed changed the price of every takeover by changing the price of money. The recession of the early 1980s devastated industries and communities. The disinflation that followed created the monetary environment in which later expansion and financial innovation occurred. Central-bank credibility may have done more to shape aggregate American life than any buyout firm.

Why not give the decade to the Fed?

Because the Fed was a mature institution performing the most consequential version of a function America had created for it in 1913.

KKR introduced a new institutional category into ordinary corporate governance: an organization whose product was acquiring control, rewriting incentives, and selling the result.

It professionalized the hostile question.

Why should this institution continue to exist in its present form?

That question had once belonged mainly to bankruptcy courts, regulators, or failing owners.

The takeover revolution made it a standing market question for healthy public companies.

Once outsiders could finance an answer, managerial tenure stopped being evidence.

The corporation had learned to fear its owners.

The unfinished question was whether the owners could see everything the corporation had become.
