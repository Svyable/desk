# The Invention of the Permanent Campaign

The most important institution of the 1830s did not have one headquarters, one charter, or one payroll.

It had committees, conventions, newspapers, officeholders, local organizers, slogans, loyalties, and enemies.

It was the Democratic Party.

Political factions had existed from the beginning of the republic. Federalists and Republicans fought bitterly in the 1790s. But many founders regarded party as a pathology—evidence that private interest and faction had contaminated public reason. By the 1830s, that suspicion had become less useful than a practical fact: mass democracy needed durable organizations to recruit candidates, coordinate voters across states, simplify choices, distribute information, and make losing an election survivable.

The party became an institution because it made political conflict repeatable.

Martin Van Buren understood this more clearly than most of his contemporaries. He is remembered as a president whose administration collided with the Panic of 1837. His more consequential work came earlier, as an organizer who helped build political machinery around Andrew Jackson and a national Democratic coalition.

Van Buren’s insight was that party competition could stabilize rather than destroy republican government.

That sounds ordinary now because modern democracy is difficult to imagine without parties. It was not ordinary then.

A political system filled with ambitious individuals, regional interests, and sectional tensions needed an institution capable of turning those forces into recurring teams. Without such an organization, every election threatened to become a new personal coalition. With one, candidates could inherit networks, labels, newspapers, and voter expectations.

Party converted politics from an event into a system.

The expansion of voting rights among white men helped create the market for that system. States reduced or eliminated many property requirements. Presidential campaigning became more popular and organized. Turnout increased. Rallies, parades, partisan newspapers, songs, symbols, and national nominating conventions created a political culture that felt less like elite selection and more like mass participation.

The democratization was real and radically incomplete.

The electorate expanded for white men while Black Americans in many places lost or lacked voting rights, women remained excluded, Indigenous peoples faced removal and dispossession, and slavery expanded across the South and Southwest. A political order could become more democratic within the dominant group while becoming more coercive toward people outside it.

The Democratic Party embodied that contradiction.

Its coalition could unite northern urban voters, southern slaveholders, western settlers, and others around a language of popular government, limited federal privilege, territorial expansion, and hostility to concentrated economic power. But the coalition’s national durability depended in part on avoiding or suppressing conflicts that might split northern and southern Democrats over slavery.

Institutional success can consist of keeping a moral crisis off the agenda.

That is not a compliment.

The party’s greatest political drama of the decade was the Bank War.

Andrew Jackson viewed the Second Bank of the United States as a concentrated privilege inconsistent with democratic government. The Bank’s defenders considered it an important stabilizer of the currency and credit system. Nicholas Biddle, the Bank’s president, was a sophisticated manager who also became a symbol of financial power operating beyond ordinary electoral accountability.

The fight culminated in Jackson’s veto of the Bank’s recharter bill in 1832 and the administration’s removal of federal deposits from the institution.

This was not merely a policy disagreement. It was a demonstration of party politics as institutional combat.

The Democrats presented the struggle as the people against monopoly and privilege. Opponents increasingly organized into the Whig Party, which accused Jackson of executive overreach and defended a different vision of economic development and legislative government.

The Second Party System emerged from conflicts like this.

Parties made abstract constitutional disagreements legible as choices between teams.

That simplification is useful and dangerous.

Voters cannot personally study every tariff schedule, banking rule, land policy, judicial appointment, and diplomatic dispute. Party labels reduce information costs. If you know what a party generally stands for, you can make a bundle decision.

The cost is that bundles discipline thought. A position can become attractive because one’s party holds it. Leaders can suppress an issue that threatens the coalition. Partisans may interpret institutional facts through loyalty before evidence.

The party solves the coordination problem of democracy by creating a new coordination problem inside the mind.

The 1830s also deepened the spoils system, the practice of rewarding political supporters with public offices. Jackson did not invent patronage, but his administration became associated with rotation in office and partisan appointment.

The Post Office was especially valuable because postmasters were distributed across the country. A national administrative network could double as a political network.

This blurred the line between governing institution and party institution.

Modern readers may see corruption in that arrangement, and often rightly. Yet patronage also helped create national organizations in an era before professional campaign staffs, television, polling firms, social media, or large ideological nonprofits. Jobs, newspapers, local committees, and personal relationships were the infrastructure of politics.

Reformers would later try to build a professional civil service precisely because the party institution had penetrated administration so deeply.

Again, one institution creates the demand for the next.

The Democratic Party also standardized presidential nomination through national conventions. The convention was a technology for coalition management. Delegates could negotiate, display unity, adopt positions, and select candidates in a process that was more organized than an informal congressional caucus and more manageable than a direct national popular choice in the communications environment of the time.

Conventions became rituals of legitimacy.

Their rules mattered because rules decide which disagreements count.

Two-thirds requirements, delegation practices, platform language, and procedural control could shape outcomes. What looked like party theater was institutional governance.

The party’s territorial politics were equally consequential.

Jackson’s presidency oversaw the Indian Removal Act of 1830 and the forced removal policies that culminated in the Trail of Tears. The language of white popular democracy coexisted with state and federal actions that dispossessed Native nations from lands white settlers wanted.

This was not an accidental contradiction at the edge of Jacksonian politics. Expansion was central to the political economy of the coalition.

Land created opportunity for white settlers while removal transferred the costs onto Indigenous peoples. In the South, territorial expansion also meant the expansion of slavery.

A party can be an institution of participation and exclusion at once.

The 1830s end with another institutional irony.

After destroying the Second Bank’s federal role, Democrats faced a severe financial crisis. The Panic of 1837 erupted soon after Van Buren became president. Its causes were complex, including international credit conditions, commodity prices, bank behavior, land speculation, and policy choices. It cannot be reduced to one presidential act.

But the crisis damaged the party and exposed the difficulty of governing a national economy without a central financial institution Democrats trusted.

Van Buren responded by supporting an Independent Treasury system that would separate federal funds from private banks.

The organizer who helped create the party as a stabilizer of politics had to confront an economy with fewer stabilizing financial institutions.

Institutional history is full of tradeoffs that refuse to stay in one domain.

The Democratic Party’s rival for the decade is the presidency itself. Jackson expanded the political force of the office, used the veto aggressively, and presented himself as a direct representative of the people. The Supreme Court made consequential decisions on federal-state and Native sovereignty questions. State governments built infrastructure and banks. The abolitionist movement created societies, newspapers, petitions, and moral pressure that would transform national politics.

The abolitionists have a particularly serious claim because they built the institutional network that forced slavery into a political system designed to contain the issue.

Why choose the Democratic Party?

Because the decade’s scarce resource was organized mass legitimacy.

The United States was becoming too democratic, too geographically large, and too politically diverse for presidential politics to remain a contest among loose elite factions. Elections needed machinery.

The Democrats built machinery that could persist from one election to the next.

The Whigs then built their own.

Competition between durable parties created a form of democratic continuity. A losing party did not have to disappear. It could organize for the next election. A politician could build a career inside an institution larger than one patron. Voters could form identities that extended from local to national contests.

This had a stabilizing effect that is easy to underestimate.

Peaceful transfer of power requires more than constitutional rules. Losers need a reason to believe they may win later. Parties create that reason by preserving the organization between elections.

They also make conflict permanent.

There is no final election in a party system. Every governing choice becomes material for the next campaign. Officeholders communicate with supporters while governing. Opponents search constantly for issues that can fracture the coalition in power. Newspapers and later media institutions profit from conflict that never fully ends.

The permanent campaign was not invented by television consultants.

Its institutional ancestor was the mass party.

The party also changed political time. An issue could be delayed to protect the coalition. A promise could be made for the next election. Local leaders could cultivate voters over years. presidential hopefuls could build networks long before nomination.

Politics became less episodic and more professional.

The professionalization did not mean expertise in public policy. It meant expertise in winning and maintaining organization.

Those are different skills.

America has spent much of its history hoping they will overlap.

By the end of the decade, the Democratic Party had survived Jackson’s departure, elected Van Buren, suffered through economic crisis, and faced an increasingly organized Whig opposition. The specific coalitions would change. The Whigs would collapse in the 1850s. Republicans would rise. Democrats would transform repeatedly across two centuries.

The deeper institution remained.

A national party is a private organization that performs public constitutional work without appearing in the Constitution.

It nominates the people who will occupy constitutional offices. It structures legislative coalitions. It helps organize voter attention. It recruits candidates. It raises money. It punishes defectors. It decides which issues are central and which are inconvenient. It gives millions of strangers a common political identity.

The founders wrote a government without parties.

The 1830s showed that voters would build them anyway.

That may be the most American institutional pattern of all: when the formal design leaves out a function people need, an unofficial organization appears to perform it.

Then, after enough elections, the unofficial organization becomes impossible to imagine the formal system without.
