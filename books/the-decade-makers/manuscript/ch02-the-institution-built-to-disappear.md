# The Institution Built to Disappear

The most important American institution of the 1780s lasted less than four months.

That is a strange way to begin a book about institutions. We usually think of institutions as durable organizations: courts with buildings, departments with payrolls, companies that survive founders, universities older than their students. The Constitutional Convention of 1787 was designed to vanish when its work was done.

Its disappearance was part of the design.

The country entered the decade with a government that had been good enough to coordinate a revolution and too weak to manage many of the consequences. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress could conduct diplomacy, wage war, and perform other national functions, but it lacked an independent power to levy ordinary federal taxes. It relied heavily on requisitions from the states. Interstate commercial disputes were difficult. Foreign creditors wanted payment. British troops remained in frontier posts. The states issued their own economic policies and sometimes their own paper money. The national government could promise more easily than it could compel.

This did not mean the Confederation was simply a failure. It completed the peace with Britain, oversaw western land policy, and adopted the Northwest Ordinance in 1787, one of the decade’s most consequential laws. Americans had understandable reasons to distrust concentrated power after a revolution against imperial authority.

The problem was not that the states had power. It was that some problems were now larger than the machinery available to solve them.

A group of reformers spent the decade discovering how difficult it is to redesign a political system from inside that system.

The Annapolis Convention of 1786 offers the small, almost embarrassing prelude. Delegates from only five states attended. The meeting could not accomplish the broad commercial reforms some supporters wanted. Alexander Hamilton helped draft a report calling for a wider convention in Philadelphia the following May.

Failure created the invitation.

When delegates met in Philadelphia in 1787, they had been authorized to consider revisions to the Articles. They soon moved toward something more radical: a new frame of government.

The convention became an institution of constituent power—a temporary body claiming the right to propose rules more fundamental than ordinary legislation. That distinction matters. Congress could pass a measure within an existing system. The convention sought to redesign the system itself.

It therefore faced a legitimacy problem almost as difficult as the Continental Congress had faced in 1775.

Who authorized these men to replace the constitutional arrangement rather than merely repair it?

The answer was procedural as much as philosophical. The delegates would not simply declare their product law. The proposed Constitution would go to special ratifying conventions in the states. The Philadelphia body could write; the people, acting through state conventions, would have to authorize.

That decision made the convention’s temporary character a source of strength. It was not trying to become a permanent sovereign council. It was producing a proposal and then submitting the proposal to another institutional test.

Inside the Pennsylvania State House, the delegates used secrecy to create a protected bargaining space. That secrecy has always carried an uncomfortable democratic implication. Important choices were made away from public scrutiny. Yet it also allowed men who arrived with strong positions to change them without performing each concession for an immediate audience.

The Virginia Plan proposed a national government with stronger powers and representation tied in important ways to population. Smaller states feared being permanently subordinated. The New Jersey Plan preserved a more equal role for states in the national legislature. The conflict threatened the meeting.

The eventual Connecticut, or Great, Compromise created a House apportioned by population and a Senate in which states would receive equal representation.

The arrangement was not deduced from first principles. It was a political settlement. The Senate remains one of the most consequential examples of an institutional bargain becoming almost invisible through familiarity. Wyoming and California today have equal numbers of senators not because anyone would design representation from scratch that way, but because a dispute among states in 1787 produced a compromise difficult to imagine the union without.

The convention’s compromises on slavery are even more important because they show that institutional durability can be purchased at a moral cost that later generations must pay with interest.

The Constitution protected slavery without using the word. The Three-Fifths Clause counted three-fifths of the enslaved population for representation and direct taxation, increasing the political power of slaveholding states while denying enslaved people political rights. The convention allowed the international slave trade to remain protected from congressional prohibition until 1808. The fugitive slave provision further embedded the interests of slaveholders in the constitutional order.

These were not incidental defects around the edges of an otherwise complete democratic design. Slavery was a central political fact of the union the convention was trying to hold together.

Any story that presents the Constitution solely as an ingenious machine for balancing factions turns the human beings reduced to constitutional bargaining units into scenery. The institution of the decade succeeded in creating a more durable federal government while preserving a system of bondage that would eventually require civil war and constitutional reconstruction to destroy.

That contradiction belongs in the middle of the story, not in an apologetic footnote.

The convention also wrestled with a second problem that continues to shape American politics: how much power should be separated, and how much must be coordinated?

The delegates did not create three branches merely because symmetry is pleasing. They were trying to distribute authority so no single actor could easily dominate while still giving the national government enough energy to act. The House would be closer to popular election. The Senate would have longer terms and state equality. The president would execute laws and possess independent electoral legitimacy through a complicated Electoral College. Federal courts would exercise judicial authority. Congress would gain powers over taxation, commerce, borrowing, war, and other national concerns.

The design depended on ambition checking ambition, but it also depended on cooperation. A government of separated powers cannot function if every veto point becomes an absolute refusal to govern.

That tension is not a bug that history later introduced. It is inside the structure.

James Madison’s role in the convention and the ratification debate has made him the intellectual hero of many constitutional histories. He deserves the attention. His notes provide an extraordinary record of the debates. His arguments in The Federalist, especially on faction and extended republican government, remain central to how Americans explain the system to themselves.

But the convention is more interesting when viewed as an institution than as one man’s theory brought to life.

Madison lost important fights. The final Senate did not look like his preferred national design. The delegates came from different states with different interests. Gouverneur Morris shaped the final language. George Washington’s presence supplied credibility. Franklin used prestige and conciliation. Roger Sherman helped protect the position of small states. Delegations bargained, threatened departure, reconsidered, and compromised.

Constitutions are often described as architectures. This one was also a negotiated acquisition.

The convention had to buy enough consent from groups with reasons to fear one another.

Then came ratification.

The Constitution specified that approval by conventions in nine states would be sufficient to establish the new government among the ratifying states. This bypassed the unanimity rule that would have applied to amendments under the Articles. That was legally audacious. It also recognized a basic fact of institutional change: an existing system can make its own reform impossible if every beneficiary of the status quo holds a veto.

The ratification conventions turned abstract constitutional design into public political combat. Federalists defended the proposed government. Anti-Federalists warned about centralized power, distant rulers, taxation, standing armies, and the absence of an explicit bill of rights.

The Anti-Federalists lost the immediate question and won part of the constitutional future.

Pressure for amendments helped produce the Bill of Rights in the First Congress. This is a useful reminder that institutional losers can still redesign the institution. Political defeat does not always mean intellectual disappearance.

By 1788, enough states had ratified. The new government began operating in 1789.

The Philadelphia Convention was finished.

No permanent Convention Department remained. There was no staff to administer its work. The delegates did not become a collective executive. The institution had one purpose: build a mechanism that could outlive the people who designed it.

That is why it wins the 1780s.

The rival candidates are formidable. The Confederation Congress enacted the Northwest Ordinance and handled the fragile postwar nation. State governments were the real administrative engines of American life. The state ratifying conventions possessed the democratic legitimacy that made the proposed Constitution operative. Financial institutions and commercial networks were already tying the states together in ways law sometimes followed rather than led.

But the Constitutional Convention addressed the decade’s scarce resource: legitimate national capacity.

The country had independence. What it lacked was a government that could borrow on credible terms, regulate commerce, collect revenue, manage national policy, and survive ordinary political disagreement without depending on unanimous state cooperation.

The convention did not itself supply those capabilities. It designed the institution that could build them.

Its most radical decision was therefore not the presidency, the Senate, or even the list of congressional powers. It was the decision to create a constitution that contained methods for continuing political conflict after the founders were dead.

There would be elections instead of repeated conventions. Amendments instead of permanent revolution. Courts would interpret. Congresses would legislate. Presidents would press and resist. States would challenge and cooperate. Parties, which many founders distrusted, would emerge almost immediately and make the formal machinery work in ways the text did not specify.

The Constitution did not settle the American argument.

It institutionalized it.

That achievement can sound more flattering than it was. Some people were placed outside the argument. Enslaved Americans had no consent in the constitutional bargain. Indigenous nations were not treated as constituent members of the new polity. Women were excluded from federal political participation through state voting rules. The federal government would become an instrument both for expanding liberty and for enforcing hierarchy.

An institution is not good merely because it lasts.

Durability can preserve injustice as efficiently as it preserves order.

The more interesting test is whether an institution creates peaceful ways to revise the distribution of power it initially embodies. On slavery, the original Constitution proved catastrophically inadequate. Constitutional amendments after the Civil War would abolish slavery, establish birthright citizenship and equal protection, and prohibit racial discrimination in voting rights in law, though enforcement remained contested and incomplete. Later amendments would expand the electorate and alter governmental mechanics.

The institution built in 1787 proved capable of amendment, but not always capable of reform before crisis.

That is a more useful legacy than civic reverence.

The convention also established a pattern that recurs through American history: when ordinary institutions cannot solve a coordination problem, political actors create a temporary institution with unusual permission to redesign the rules.

Bankruptcy reorganizations do this for companies. Constitutional commissions sometimes do it for governments. War boards do it in emergencies. Standards committees do it for technologies. Corporate boards form special committees when conflicts make normal governance suspect. Temporary bodies can sometimes make permanent change precisely because they do not need to administer the world they are creating.

There is danger in that freedom. Temporary institutions can evade accountability. Emergency bodies can acquire powers that never really disappear. Elite gatherings can call their own preferences necessity.

The Philadelphia Convention deserves neither worship nor dismissal. It deserves attention as a successful and compromised piece of institutional engineering.

Its members had no guarantee the states would ratify. They had no guarantee the new government would survive its first contested election. They could not know that their text would govern a continental superpower, be invoked in a civil war, survive assassinations and depressions, structure the abolition of slavery and the entrenchment of segregation, authorize a modern administrative state, and become the object of arguments over everything from guns to abortion to executive power to digital speech.

They were trying to solve a smaller and more immediate problem: the government they had was not working well enough, and the procedure for changing it was itself part of the problem.

The most important institution of the 1780s was therefore an institution with an expiration date.

It met in secret, compromised on matters both practical and unforgivable, proposed a new machine, and submitted the machine to political judgment.

Then it disappeared.

America kept the argument it left behind.
