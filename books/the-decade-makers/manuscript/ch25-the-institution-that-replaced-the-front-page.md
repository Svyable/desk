# The Institution That Replaced the Front Page

The most consequential editorial meeting of the 2010s did not happen once a day in a newsroom.

It happened continuously in software.

A system decided which post should appear above another post, which video should autoplay, which friend’s photograph deserved attention, which article entered a feed, which advertisement matched a profile, and which piece of outrage could travel from one person to millions without an editor ever choosing it for a front page.

Facebook became the most important American institution of the 2010s because it turned social connection into a distribution layer for public attention.

The decade’s scarce resource was not publication.

Publication had become abundant.

The scarce resource was ranking.

The web had already made it cheap for organizations and individuals to publish. Blogs, websites, online news, video platforms, digital storefronts, and social networks expanded the supply of material dramatically. Smartphones then put a networked screen in a pocket all day.

The problem was no longer finding something to read.

It was deciding what would find you.

Facebook’s News Feed became one of the most powerful answers.

The feed had launched before the decade, but the 2010s were when mobile adoption, global scale, algorithmic ranking, targeted advertising, groups, pages, video, and major acquisitions turned Facebook from a social website into infrastructure for attention.

The company’s institutional product was not the profile page.

It was the graph connecting people and the ranking system governing traffic across the graph.

A newspaper editor has a finite front page.

A feed has effectively unlimited inventory and a finite human attention span.

That means every refresh is an allocation decision.

The institution decides which item receives the next second.

Facebook’s own descriptions of News Feed ranking made the process explicit. The system considered available inventory, signals about posts and users, predictions about likely behavior, and a score used to organize material. Signals could include who posted an item, how people interacted with it, how recently it appeared, and numerous other factors.

The exact formula changed continuously.

The institutional fact did not.

A private company had become a governor of visibility.

That governance was powerful precisely because participation was voluntary.

News organizations created pages, optimized headlines and formats, invested in social teams, and depended to varying degrees on referral traffic. Political campaigns built audiences and targeted advertisements. Local businesses used pages instead of websites. activists organized events. Families shared photographs. Neighborhoods formed groups. Hobbyists found one another. Diasporas maintained relationships across continents.

The platform did not need to command anyone to participate.

The audience was the command.

A publisher goes where readers are.

A politician goes where voters are.

An advertiser goes where attention can be measured.

A community goes where the members already have accounts.

Once everyone arrives, the platform’s design choices become rules for institutions that remain legally independent of it.

This is platform power in its clearest form.

The benefits were enormous and should be stated before the harms because otherwise the institution’s rise becomes inexplicable.

Facebook lowered the cost of staying in touch across distance. Small organizations could reach audiences without owning distribution infrastructure. A local fundraiser could spread through friends. A missing-person notice could circulate quickly. Groups could connect people around rare diseases, parenting, immigration, hobbies, schools, neighborhoods, or political causes. Small businesses could advertise to narrow audiences at budgets far below national broadcast campaigns.

The social graph made communication more efficient because people had already supplied the routing information.

The network knew who knew whom.

That knowledge became useful infrastructure.

It also became a commercial asset.

Facebook’s advertising system could combine identity, declared interests, inferred interests, location, device information, activity, and social context into targeting categories. Advertisers did not need to know an individual user’s name to value the ability to reach people likely to care about a product or message.

This was a different model from the old newspaper.

A newspaper sells access to an audience defined broadly by readership and section.

A social platform can sell access to a dynamically selected subset of people predicted to respond.

The institution does not merely distribute attention.

It prices attention at smaller and smaller granularity.

That changed politics as well as commerce.

Campaigns could reach supporters, persuade targeted groups, recruit volunteers, raise money, test messages, and mobilize turnout through digital systems. Political advertising became more individualized and less visible to the wider public than a television commercial or newspaper advertisement.

That creates an accountability problem.

A candidate who airs one television message in a city knows opponents and journalists can see it.

A campaign that delivers thousands of targeted variations can communicate differently to different audiences without any one person seeing the whole campaign.

The institution fragments the public while improving targeting efficiency.

The tradeoff is structural, not partisan.

Any political actor can prefer a system that lets it speak more precisely to persuadable people.

Democratic accountability may prefer a system in which public persuasion is itself public.

The 2010s forced the conflict into view.

Social media also lowered the cost of political organization outside campaigns.

During the Arab uprisings beginning in 2010 and 2011, social platforms including Facebook helped some activists communicate, document events, and mobilize. The phrase “Facebook revolution” became popular because it compressed a complicated social movement into a familiar technology.

The phrase was also misleading.

Platforms did not create authoritarian rule, youth unemployment, corruption, food-price pressures, police violence, opposition networks, or the willingness of people to enter streets under dangerous conditions. Satellite television, mobile phones, face-to-face networks, mosques, unions, professional groups, and preexisting activists all mattered.

Technology can accelerate a movement without supplying the reasons for the movement.

The same caution applies in the United States.

Black Lives Matter emerged from activism around police violence and racial injustice, with social media helping video, testimony, organizing, and commentary travel around traditional gatekeepers. Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, Instagram, and later other platforms each played different roles. A phone video could become public evidence. A hashtag could connect local incidents into a national frame. An organizer could reach supporters without waiting for a television producer.

The front page was no longer the exclusive property of the press.

That was a genuine redistribution of power.

Removing one gatekeeper did not eliminate gatekeeping.

It replaced a visible editorial institution with a partly opaque ranking institution.

The difference matters because a newspaper tells the reader that editors selected the front page.

A social feed feels personal.

The story is next to a cousin’s photograph, a friend’s birthday, a neighborhood complaint, and an advertisement chosen for the user. The institutional selection hides inside intimacy.

That can make ranking feel less like editorial power precisely when it is more pervasive.

Facebook’s own attempts to improve the feed reveal the governance problem.

In January 2018, the company announced major changes intended to prioritize posts that generated “meaningful interactions” among people and to reduce some forms of passive consumption of public content. It later described efforts to prioritize news sources users rated as trustworthy and to demote false news and clickbait.

These were editorial judgments implemented through software.

The company did not write every story.

It decided which properties of stories and social response should affect distribution.

There is no neutral alternative.

Chronological ranking privileges recency.

Engagement ranking privileges predicted reaction.

Friend prioritization privileges social proximity.

Trust scoring privileges some definition of authority.

Local-news promotion privileges geography.

Removing ranking entirely still privileges people who post most often.

Every feed is a theory of relevance.

The theory becomes social policy when a large share of the public inhabits it.

This is why arguments that Facebook should “just be a platform” misunderstand the institution.

The moment there is more content than attention, some mechanism must choose.

The choice can be made by users, by chronology, by algorithms, by editors, or by combinations of them.

There is no ungoverned feed.

The 2016 U.S. presidential election made the political consequences impossible to keep in the product department.

Russian actors used social-media platforms, including Facebook, as part of influence operations aimed at American audiences. Domestic campaigns used sophisticated digital advertising and voter targeting. False and misleading stories circulated through the same distribution infrastructure as journalism and ordinary political speech.

Evidence does not support the simple claim that Facebook determined the election outcome.

Political behavior is not a laboratory variable with one cause. Candidate choices, party loyalties, economic conditions, news coverage, campaign strategy, turnout, geography, email disclosures, polling errors, third-party voting, and other factors all mattered.

The institutional claim is narrower.

A company built to connect friends had become infrastructure through which a foreign intelligence operation and domestic presidential campaigns could reach segmented American publics.

That fact alone changed the company’s constitutional role even though the Constitution did not change.

Facebook is a private actor and can set terms of service under legal rules different from those constraining government. The First Amendment limits government action; it does not ordinarily require a private platform to carry every speaker.

Yet when a private platform becomes a major public forum, users begin demanding constitutional-style legitimacy from corporate policy.

Why was this post removed?

Why was that account suspended?

Why can this advertiser target this audience?

Why did the algorithm recommend this group?

Who appeals the decision?

Which country’s law governs?

How should political speech be treated differently from commercial speech?

What happens when satire looks like misinformation to an automated system?

At sufficient scale, content moderation becomes a court system without judges in robes.

The institution responds by becoming more bureaucratic.

Policies multiply. Appeals procedures appear. Specialized teams emerge for terrorism, child safety, political advertising, coordinated inauthentic behavior, hate speech, misinformation, privacy, and country-specific legal demands. External experts advise. Contractors review content. Automated systems triage enormous volumes.

Scale manufactures bureaucracy.

That is the same trajectory seen in earlier chapters.

The railroad begins by running trains and develops internal government because the network creates disputes.

The food regulator begins with adulteration and develops scientific bureaucracy because products create uncertainty.

The social platform begins with sharing and develops quasi-judicial systems because billions of people create conflicts no founder can adjudicate personally.

The institution becomes less like a product as it succeeds.

The Cambridge Analytica scandal exposed a different layer of power.

A personality-quiz application created by academic Aleksandr Kogan obtained information from users and, under Facebook’s then-existing platform rules, information connected to many of their Facebook friends. Data ultimately became connected to Cambridge Analytica’s political profiling and targeting work. The Federal Trade Commission later alleged deceptive practices by Cambridge Analytica, Kogan, and former chief executive Alexander Nix and described data collected from tens of millions of Facebook users.

The scandal was not simply that a company possessed data.

It was that information supplied in one social context could migrate into another institutional context the user did not anticipate.

A person believed she was taking a quiz or sharing a profile.

The data could become an input into political targeting.

The intuitive boundary between participation and surveillance broke.

Facebook itself entered a major FTC settlement in 2019. The commission imposed a $5 billion penalty and a twenty-year privacy order with governance requirements including a board privacy committee and compliance structures.

The response is institutionally fascinating.

A regulator did not merely fine a platform for past conduct.

It required the platform to build a new internal institution.

The state moved inside the corporation through governance design.

This is a recurring pattern of modern regulation. Instead of reviewing every decision directly, government requires firms to create committees, officers, controls, certifications, audit trails, and procedures that make compliance repeatable.

The regulator governs by requiring the company to govern itself differently.

Facebook’s acquisitions show another way institutions preserve power.

The company bought Instagram in 2012 and WhatsApp in 2014. Both became enormous services. The acquisitions could be defended as investments that supplied capital, infrastructure, technical expertise, and global distribution. They could also be interpreted as a dominant social platform absorbing emerging threats before those threats matured into independent institutions.

That tension later became central to antitrust debates.

The institutional question is not whether every acquisition by a large company is anticompetitive.

It is whether a platform capable of seeing user behavior across an ecosystem has unusual ability to identify and purchase the next source of competition.

A railroad can see traffic on its tracks.

A platform can see attention moving away.

Information advantage becomes acquisition strategy.

Apple is the strongest rival institution of the 2010s.

The iPhone launched in 2007, but the 2010s were the decade in which the smartphone became the universal personal computer for much of American life. Apple’s App Store governed software distribution on iOS. The company’s integration of hardware, software, payments, privacy rules, and developer access gave it institutional control over a critical computing layer.

Google deserves an equally serious claim through Android, search, advertising, maps, YouTube, Gmail, and Chrome.

Amazon reorganized commerce, logistics, cloud computing, and retail expectations. The Federal Reserve reshaped finance through crisis aftermath and unconventional monetary policy. The Supreme Court transformed areas of constitutional law. The Affordable Care Act created an enduring health-insurance institution. Black churches, activist organizations, and decentralized social movements changed politics.

Why Facebook?

Because Apple and Google governed devices and information access while Facebook governed the social distribution of attention.

The decade’s distinctive crisis was not whether people could connect.

It was what happened after connection became abundant and a commercial institution had to rank the consequences.

Facebook also changed journalism’s business environment.

Publishers once controlled content and a significant share of distribution. Social platforms separated the two. A news organization could pay reporters, editors, photographers, foreign correspondents, lawyers, and local bureaus while a platform captured advertising value around the audience’s attention.

Publishers became dependent on algorithms they did not control.

A ranking change could increase or reduce referral traffic quickly. The 2018 shift toward “meaningful interactions” explicitly warned that public content from businesses, brands, and media would receive less emphasis relative to interactions among people.

The company could change the economics of another institution through a product decision.

That is power.

It would be inaccurate to blame Facebook alone for journalism’s financial decline. Craigslist weakened classified advertising. Google transformed search advertising. Consolidation, private-equity ownership, television, local economic decline, changing reader habits, and publisher strategy mattered. Some news organizations built successful subscription models less dependent on platforms.

The stronger point is that distribution and production had separated.

That separation changed editorial incentives.

A story optimized for subscriptions may differ from one optimized for shares. A headline written to represent an article may differ from one written to survive in a feed. Outrage, novelty, identity, fear, and surprise can generate reaction even when they do not generate understanding.

The institution does not need to prefer misinformation ideologically.

It can reward the properties that make misinformation competitive.

Intention is not the mechanism.

Incentive is.

Facebook repeatedly changed ranking and enforcement in attempts to address the side effects. Those changes sometimes created new side effects.

If the platform suppresses sensational content, critics can accuse it of censorship. If it allows sensational content to spread, critics can accuse it of profiting from harm. If it privileges established sources, outsiders can accuse it of entrenching old gatekeepers. If it privileges raw engagement, established institutions can be drowned by material optimized for reaction.

The platform cannot escape governance by refusing to choose because refusal is itself a choice of ranking rule.

This is why Facebook’s institutional legacy is larger than any one scandal.

The company taught the world that attention allocation is governance.

The name of the parent company changed to Meta in 2021 as leadership emphasized ambitions in virtual and augmented reality. The core social institutions did not disappear. Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, advertising infrastructure, moderation systems, and the company’s social graph remained enormous.

The 2010s had already completed the institutional transition.

A private organization could become public infrastructure without being legally public, physically local, or even visible as an organization during use.

The user sees friends.

Behind the friends is a ranking system, ad auction, data architecture, moderation bureaucracy, security organization, developer policy, privacy regime, and corporate governance structure.

The institution hides behind intimacy.

That is why Facebook wins the decade.

The front page told readers that somebody had chosen what mattered.

The feed made the choice feel like it came from everyone you knew.

It did not.

It came from everyone you knew, filtered through an institution deciding which part of everyone reached you next.
