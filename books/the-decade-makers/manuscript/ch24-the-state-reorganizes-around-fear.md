# The State Reorganizes Around Fear

On the morning of September 11, 2001, the United States possessed an intelligence community, an immigration system, customs inspectors, airport-security rules, a Coast Guard, emergency-management agencies, border authorities, federal law-enforcement organizations, public-health agencies, military commands, and a White House staff responsible for national security.

The country did not lack institutions.

It lacked an institutional arrangement capable of making all of them see the same threat in time.

Al-Qaeda operatives turned that weakness into catastrophe. Four commercial airliners were hijacked. Two struck the World Trade Center towers in New York. One struck the Pentagon. A fourth crashed in Pennsylvania after passengers and crew resisted the hijackers. Nearly three thousand people were killed.

The attacks exposed failures across aviation security, intelligence, border processes, information sharing, emergency response, and national-security coordination. The causes were not reducible to one organizational chart. Government agencies held fragments of information. Legal authorities differed. Professional cultures separated foreign intelligence from criminal investigation. Technology systems did not communicate easily. Incentives rewarded protection of sources and missions as well as sharing.

The political response was to redraw the state.

The Department of Homeland Security is the most important American institution of the 2000s because it represented one of the largest reorganizations of the federal executive branch since the creation of the modern Department of Defense.

The decade’s scarce resource was integrated threat awareness.

The Homeland Security Act became law in November 2002. DHS began operations in 2003. Twenty-two federal agencies or offices were brought together under a new cabinet department with a common homeland-security mission.

The list itself reveals the ambition.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency. The U.S. Customs Service. The Transportation Security Administration. The Coast Guard. The Secret Service. Immigration functions previously located in the Immigration and Naturalization Service were reorganized into new entities including Customs and Border Protection, Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and Citizenship and Immigration Services. Other functions related to infrastructure, science, preparedness, information, and border security moved as well.

America tried to solve fragmentation by making a larger institution.

That solution created a new fragmentation problem inside the institution.

A Coast Guard officer, a FEMA disaster specialist, a border inspector, a Secret Service agent, an airport screener, and an immigration adjudicator do not perform the same work, share the same professional culture, or face the same public.

Putting their agencies under one secretary does not automatically create one organization.

A merger on paper is a beginning.

The institution has to build common budgeting, personnel systems, technology, procurement, doctrine, leadership, and a sense of mission without destroying the specialized capabilities that made the components useful before the merger.

That is a corporate integration problem at sovereign scale.

The lesson matters beyond DHS because reorganization is one of the American government’s favorite responses to failure.

An inquiry finds that two agencies did not share information. The political answer is often to create a coordinator, merge offices, establish a new center, or move the boundary upward.

Sometimes that works.

Sometimes it simply changes where information must cross next.

No organizational chart abolishes seams.

It can only decide where the seams live.

September 11 made the old seams politically intolerable.

The 9/11 Commission later described failures not merely of intelligence collection but of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management. The report documented a government in which relevant information did not always reach the people able to assemble the whole picture. Agencies had different missions and legal traditions. Domestic and foreign intelligence were institutionally separated in ways that reflected legitimate civil-liberties concerns as well as operational habits.

The country had built those separations for reasons.

After the attacks, it had reasons to weaken them.

That is one of the most dangerous moments in institutional history.

A safeguard can look like a defect immediately after catastrophe.

A restriction created to prevent domestic surveillance can look like a barrier to intelligence sharing. A warrant requirement can look slow. A divided chain of command can look irresponsible. Privacy can look like a luxury.

Fear compresses the cost side of civil liberty and magnifies the benefit side of security.

The institutional challenge is to respond to a real failure without treating every preexisting boundary as foolish merely because an enemy exploited one.

DHS was one answer.

The creation of the Transportation Security Administration was an even more direct example. Before 9/11, airlines generally contracted passenger screening under federal requirements. Afterward, Congress created TSA and moved screening into a federal workforce and national security framework.

The change was immediately visible.

A traveler did not need to read the Homeland Security Act to know the state had entered the airport differently.

Identification requirements, screening procedures, restricted items, watch lists, explosive detection, security technology, and later rules around shoes and liquids turned aviation security into a recurring federal encounter.

The checkpoint became the most ordinary face of the war on terror.

That ordinariness is institutionally important.

Military action in Afghanistan or Iraq could remain distant from many Americans. Intelligence operations were classified. Airport security reached business travelers, children, grandparents, tourists, soldiers, immigrants, and politicians.

The security state became a queue.

The queue produced real benefits and real costs.

Cockpit doors were strengthened. Screening became more standardized. Aviation security received a direct federal chain of accountability. Intelligence and watch-list systems became more integrated over time. Procedures adapted to attempted attacks.

At the same time, travelers experienced delays, false positives, privacy concerns, intrusive searches, and unequal suspicion. Muslim, Arab, South Asian, Sikh, and other travelers frequently described profiling or disproportionate scrutiny in the broader post-9/11 environment.

Security institutions operate under an asymmetric political incentive.

If an agency prevents an attack, success may be invisible.

If it fails once, the failure can be catastrophic.

That creates pressure to add one more screen, one more watch list, one more prohibited item, one more information-sharing rule, one more database.

The layers accumulate because removing a layer requires someone to accept responsibility for the hypothetical attack that might follow.

Fear is an institutional budget.

It can finance capacities ordinary politics would reject.

It can also protect procedures after their marginal value becomes difficult to prove.

DHS inherited the nation’s borders at the same moment terrorism and immigration were becoming politically linked in ways that would shape the next quarter-century.

Customs and immigration enforcement had always involved sovereignty. After 9/11, those functions entered an explicit homeland-security architecture.

Customs and Border Protection combined inspection and border functions. Immigration and Customs Enforcement took on interior enforcement and investigative roles. Citizenship and Immigration Services handled immigration benefits.

The reorganization separated functions previously housed together and combined functions previously housed apart.

That design reflected a theory.

Border security, customs, immigration enforcement, terrorism prevention, and homeland security belonged to one risk architecture.

The theory created lasting political consequences because organizational placement changes the meaning of a problem.

A visa overstay can be an administrative violation.

Inside a homeland-security institution, it can also appear as a security vulnerability.

An asylum claim can be understood as a humanitarian and legal process.

Inside a border-security institution, the same claim arrives amid questions of screening, detention, capacity, and unauthorized entry.

A cargo container can be a trade object and a security object at once.

Where government places the file influences which of those identities dominates.

Organization is rhetoric made permanent.

The strongest test of the new department in the 2000s came from a threat it had not been primarily created to defeat.

Hurricane Katrina struck the Gulf Coast in August 2005. Storm surge and levee failures produced catastrophic flooding in New Orleans. More than a thousand people died across the disaster. Hundreds of thousands were displaced. Homes, businesses, roads, hospitals, utilities, and public institutions were damaged across a wide region.

The disaster became a national image of government failure.

People waited for rescue on roofs. Thousands gathered at the Superdome and Convention Center under desperate conditions. Communications failed. Responsibilities blurred among city, state, federal, military, law-enforcement, and emergency-management institutions. Logistics were slow relative to need.

FEMA, now inside DHS, became a symbol of incompetence.

The federal government’s own lessons-learned review concluded that significant changes to policy, structure, and mindset were needed. Congressional and Government Accountability Office investigations documented failures of planning, coordination, communications, and execution across multiple levels.

The new institution designed to coordinate catastrophe had encountered catastrophe and still produced seams.

This was not proof that the DHS merger caused the Katrina failure.

The disaster had many causes. The storm was enormous. Levee and flood-protection failures were central. New Orleans contained deep poverty and racial inequality that shaped evacuation capacity. Many residents lacked cars or resources to leave. Local and state decisions mattered. Federal planning assumptions proved inadequate. Military support involved separate authorities and chains of command.

The sharper institutional conclusion is that consolidation did not eliminate coordination failure.

It moved it.

FEMA had a distinct identity and history before being incorporated into DHS. After 9/11, terrorism dominated homeland-security politics and resources. Critics argued that emergency management had lost institutional stature inside a department oriented around counterterrorism and border security.

Whether every claim in that critique is correct matters less than the structural warning.

When a government merges agencies to elevate one mission, it can subordinate missions that do not fit the new hierarchy as neatly.

A department named after homeland security has to decide whether a hurricane is security in the same sense as a terrorist plot.

If the answer is yes, the category becomes enormous.

If the answer is no, FEMA becomes an awkward tenant.

Katrina forced the answer toward yes.

Preparedness, response, resilience, and recovery became more central to the department’s mission. FEMA’s position was later strengthened through statutory and organizational changes.

The institution learned through failure.

That learning is one reason DHS still wins the decade rather than losing it because of Katrina.

An institution becomes important when its mistakes reorganize policy.

The 9/11 Commission produced another reorganization outside DHS that gives this chapter its strongest runner-up.

Congress created the position of Director of National Intelligence and the National Counterterrorism Center in 2004 as part of broader intelligence reform. The purpose was to improve coordination across a community that remained distributed among departments and agencies.

DNI and NCTC attack the decade’s scarce resource more directly than DHS in one sense: they were designed to make intelligence integration somebody’s job.

Why not choose them?

Because their influence, though profound, was concentrated in national-security institutions that most Americans encountered indirectly.

DHS changed the state-citizen interface more broadly.

It reorganized airports, borders, immigration enforcement, disaster response, infrastructure protection, cybersecurity functions, and preparedness under a category that entered ordinary language.

“Homeland security” stopped being a phrase.

It became a jurisdiction.

The creation of a jurisdiction matters because new problems can be fitted inside it.

Cybersecurity is the clearest example.

The department’s founders were responding to a physical terrorist attack, yet digital networks were already becoming critical infrastructure. Over time DHS accumulated larger roles in protecting civilian federal networks and coordinating with owners of privately operated infrastructure. Those responsibilities later evolved into the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, created in 2018.

Election systems, pipelines, hospitals, financial networks, power infrastructure, communications, and industrial control systems could all be understood as homeland-security concerns even though none looked like airport screening.

An institution created around one attack became a platform for threats its designers could only partly anticipate.

This is both institutional strength and mission-creep risk.

Broad categories are durable because they can absorb new problems.

They are dangerous because almost any problem can be redescribed to fit them.

“Defense” can expand beyond armies.

“Environment” can expand beyond visible pollution.

“Homeland security” can expand beyond terrorism.

The broader the category, the more important procedural and legal limits become.

The civil-liberties controversies of the 2000s therefore belong in the chapter even when the most controversial programs were not DHS programs.

The USA PATRIOT Act changed authorities across the federal government. The National Security Agency expanded surveillance under legal frameworks that later became the subject of intense debate. The Central Intelligence Agency operated detention and interrogation programs. The Department of Defense ran wars and detention facilities.

DHS did not own those policies.

That distinction is essential.

The post-9/11 security state was an ecosystem, and attributing the whole ecosystem to one department would reproduce the very coordination error the chapter is trying to explain.

DHS represents the domestic administrative reorganization: the effort to build a department whose institutional identity begins with vulnerability.

That identity altered budget politics.

A proposal to inspect cargo, protect a chemical plant, build a border system, purchase airport scanners, harden a federal network, or fund emergency communications could now be described as homeland security.

The label creates a common purpose across otherwise unrelated programs.

It also creates competition among them.

A dollar spent on aviation security is not spent on disaster preparedness. A cybersecurity position is not a border patrol agent. A detention bed is not a flood map.

The department did not eliminate tradeoffs.

It forced unlike risks into the same institutional family.

That family also had to work with institutions it did not control.

Most critical infrastructure in the United States is not owned by the federal government. State and local police retain authority. Governors command National Guard forces under state status in many circumstances. Airlines operate airports and aircraft inside federal rules. Private technology companies run networks. Hospitals are dispersed among public, nonprofit, and for-profit organizations.

Homeland security is therefore not command.

It is orchestration.

The difference explains why the department can be held responsible for outcomes it cannot directly order.

A successful disaster response may depend on county officials, utility crews, private suppliers, neighboring states, hospitals, volunteers, and federal logistics all moving together.

A cybersecurity defense may depend on a private company patching a system after receiving federal threat information.

An immigration process may depend on courts, consulates, Congress, local governments, and nonprofit legal services outside the department.

The institution’s product is often coordination rather than direct service.

Coordination is difficult to display in a ribbon-cutting ceremony.

Its failure is much easier to film.

That political asymmetry shaped DHS from the start.

The strongest private rival for the decade is Google.

Search became the front door to a rapidly expanding web. Google’s ranking systems, advertising model, maps, email, acquisitions, and browser strategy made the company an institution for organizing information. By the decade’s end, discoverability online was a scarce resource, and Google controlled one of its central gates.

Google may have had more influence on daily cognition than DHS.

Why not give it the decade?

Because the 2000s were politically defined by vulnerability before they were defined by attention.

September 11 reorganized federal authority. The wars that followed reorganized foreign policy. Katrina reorganized disaster expectations. Security became an administrative category broad enough to include border crossings, airports, infrastructure, and eventually digital networks.

The smartphone and social feed would reorganize attention more fully in the next decade.

The sequence matters because institutions inherit the world built by the previous winner.

Facebook’s political problems in the 2010s would eventually become homeland-security problems when foreign influence operations and election infrastructure raised questions about democratic resilience. Cybersecurity would become inseparable from national security. Social platforms would coordinate with federal agencies around threats while also contesting government pressure.

The institutional boundary would move again.

The great lesson of the 2000s is therefore not that America learned how to coordinate security.

Katrina prevents that comforting conclusion.

The lesson is that catastrophic failure can make institutional fragmentation politically intolerable even when consolidation cannot abolish fragmentation.

The United States responded to one morning of catastrophic seams by constructing a department meant to see across them.

Then it spent the rest of the decade discovering that the largest institution in the room still has seams of its own.
