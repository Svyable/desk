# Citizenship as Fieldwork

The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee was created by young people who had already started acting before the adults had finished deciding what to call the movement.

In February 1960, Black college students in Greensboro, North Carolina, sat down at a segregated Woolworth lunch counter and refused to accept the local rules of ordinary commerce. Sit-ins spread rapidly across the South. Students organized demonstrations in city after city, often facing arrest, assault, harassment, and expulsion threats.

Ella Baker, an experienced organizer working with the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, helped bring student activists together at Shaw University in Raleigh that April.

Out of that meeting came SNCC.

The most important American institution of the 1960s was not the presidency, Congress, or the Supreme Court, though each changed the law dramatically.

It was SNCC because the decade’s scarce resource was organized citizenship at the point where law and local power diverged.

A federal court could declare segregation unconstitutional.

Congress could pass the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act.

The Justice Department could sue.

None of those institutions could manufacture local people willing to challenge a courthouse registrar, a segregated bus station, a county sheriff, an employer, a school board, or a white supremacist power structure day after day.

SNCC organized that willingness.

Its institutional style differed from the charismatic model often associated with Martin Luther King Jr. and SCLC. King’s leadership and moral rhetoric were extraordinarily important. SNCC organizers, influenced by Baker, often emphasized group-centered leadership, local participation, and the patient work of building durable power rather than waiting for a famous national figure to arrive.

The difference was not absolute. Organizations collaborated, competed, overlapped, and argued.

But the contrast reveals a fundamental institutional question.

Is a movement strongest when it concentrates attention in a leader or when it distributes capacity among participants?

SNCC pushed hard toward distribution.

That choice made citizenship look less like a status and more like a practice.

In places where Black citizens faced literacy tests, arbitrary registration procedures, economic retaliation, and violence, the legal right to vote existed inside an institutional system designed to make exercise of the right dangerous or impossible.

A person could possess citizenship on paper and lack access to the county office that made citizenship politically effective.

Organizers entered that gap.

They held meetings, taught registration procedures, accompanied applicants, gathered testimony, built relationships, created local projects, communicated with national media, and returned after cameras left.

This was democracy as fieldwork.

The work was slow because fear was rational.

A Black farmer who attempted to register could face eviction, loss of credit, firing, violence, or pressure on family members. A local activist lived with consequences after visiting organizers moved on. White officials could manipulate rules and law enforcement. Federal protection was inconsistent.

The institution’s task was therefore not simply persuasion.

It was risk sharing.

A movement becomes durable when individuals no longer face power alone.

The Freedom Rides of 1961 illustrated both the possibility and the danger. The Congress of Racial Equality initiated the rides to challenge segregation in interstate bus travel. Riders faced brutal attacks. When violence threatened to stop the campaign, student activists associated with the Nashville movement and SNCC continued the rides.

The decision turned federal interstate-commerce rules into a test of whether the national government would protect citizens exercising them.

Again, an institution occupied the distance between law and reality.

The Kennedy administration did not always welcome the timing or tactics of direct action. Federal officials worried about disorder and foreign perceptions during the Cold War. Movement activists worried that delay protected segregation.

This tension recurred throughout the decade.

Government prefers orderly reform.

People suffering under the existing order often experience order as the mechanism of delay.

SNCC’s voting-rights work in Mississippi made the conflict especially stark.

Mississippi’s white political establishment used law, custom, intimidation, and violence to suppress Black political participation. Organizers including Bob Moses and many local activists developed voter-registration projects in some of the most dangerous counties in the South.

The work did not produce instant registration numbers.

It produced evidence.

The difficulty of registering exposed the structure of disenfranchisement. Violence against organizers revealed the cost of challenging it. Local people built political networks.

Failure at the registrar’s office became proof of institutional failure at the national level.

Then the organizers tried something more radical than asking the existing system to admit them.

They built a parallel system.

White officials often defended Mississippi’s tiny Black registration numbers as evidence of apathy. If Black citizens were not on the rolls, the argument went, perhaps they simply did not want to participate.

SNCC and the Council of Federated Organizations attacked that claim experimentally.

In 1963, organizers created a “freedom registration” process in Black communities that removed the hostile courthouse registrar from the transaction. Roughly 80,000 Black Mississippians registered in the movement’s parallel system. A Freedom Vote followed, offering an alternative gubernatorial ticket headed by NAACP leader Aaron Henry with Tougaloo College chaplain Edwin King as the candidate for lieutenant governor.

The ballots had no legal power.

They had evidentiary power.

If tens of thousands of people would register and vote when the threat structure was changed, then low official registration could no longer be plausibly described as mere indifference.

The movement had turned a political claim into a field experiment.

This was one of SNCC’s most consequential institutional inventions.

When an official metric is produced by a hostile system, build a second measurement process.

The new process does not automatically replace the official one.

It can reveal what the official one is measuring besides the thing it claims to measure.

Mississippi’s registration statistics were supposed to measure political participation.

They also measured intimidation.

The Freedom Vote separated the two.

That experience led toward the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party.

In April 1964, Black Mississippians and movement organizers created the MFDP as an interracial political party open to people excluded from meaningful participation in the state’s regular Democratic organization. The strategy was not merely to protest outside the party.

It was to reproduce the party’s own representative machinery.

Over the summer, activists tried to attend the regular party’s precinct meetings. They encountered meetings moved without notice, meetings declared over before they arrived, and outright exclusion. So the Freedom Democrats organized parallel precinct, county, and state processes of their own.

This is what made the later convention challenge more than theater.

The MFDP could say: we did not merely demand seats. We organized constituents, held meetings, selected representatives, and built a delegation through procedures intended to perform the democratic function the official party had denied Black citizens.

The fight had moved from morality to institutional legitimacy.

Which delegation had the better claim to represent Democrats in Mississippi?

The legally recognized one produced by a racially exclusionary state party?

Or the insurgent one built through open participation but lacking formal recognition?

That question forced the national Democratic Party to confront the source of its own authority.

A national convention is not simply a television event.

It is a certification system.

It decides which local organizations count as the party.

The MFDP challenged the input.

The 1964 Freedom Summer project enlarged that challenge. The National Archives describes roughly one thousand out-of-state volunteers working alongside thousands of Black Mississippians in voting-rights organizing, meetings, Freedom Schools, libraries, housing, and other community projects.

The outside volunteers were not the movement.

Their usefulness came from being added to an existing local organizing structure.

They brought labor, education, money, family connections, media attention, and—in a brutally unequal public sphere—the political fact that violence against white northern students might receive attention violence against Black Mississippians had long been denied.

That asymmetry was both strategic resource and moral indictment.

The murders of James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner made it impossible to ignore. The national attention given to their disappearance and deaths exposed a hierarchy of whose suffering the country recognized quickly.

SNCC organizers had to use that attention without allowing it to redefine a movement built by local Black people.

This is another institutional tradeoff.

Outside capacity can increase leverage while displacing authority.

A movement can need allies and still need to prevent the allies from becoming the story.

The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, formed in 1964, challenged the legitimacy of Mississippi’s all-white regular Democratic delegation at the national party convention. The MFDP argued that a party delegation chosen through a racially exclusionary political system could not legitimately represent the state’s Democrats.

This was institutional jujitsu.

Instead of asking only for moral sympathy, activists challenged the rules by which another institution certified representation.

Fannie Lou Hamer’s testimony described the violence and retaliation surrounding Black political participation. The national party offered a compromise that MFDP leaders considered inadequate, and the Freedom Democrats did not win full seating in 1964.

But losing the immediate credential was not the same as losing the institutional argument.

The challenge created pressure for later changes in party rules and delegation standards. The national party could not indefinitely claim broad democratic legitimacy while accepting state delegations built through categorical racial exclusion.

Institutions can lose a vote and still change the rules of the next vote.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 transformed the legal environment by authorizing stronger federal intervention against discriminatory voting practices in covered jurisdictions under its original framework. It was the product of many forces: sustained grassroots organizing, national civil-rights organizations, demonstrations including the Selma campaign led principally by SCLC and local activists, media coverage of state violence, presidential leadership, congressional action, and broader political change.

SNCC does not get sole credit.

That is not how institutions work.

Its role was to make disenfranchisement administratively undeniable through years of organizing before the law arrived.

Then the institution changed.

By the middle and late 1960s, SNCC activists were increasingly frustrated with persistent violence, white liberal paternalism, the limits of federal protection, economic inequality, and the pace of integration. Stokely Carmichael, later known as Kwame Ture, popularized the call for Black Power while serving as SNCC chairman. The organization shifted away from interracial organizing in some contexts and toward Black self-determination, antiwar politics, and a broader critique of American power.

The shift produced support, fear, disagreement, and fragmentation.

Institutions born in one strategic environment can struggle when victory changes the environment.

Sit-ins against legal segregation create a clear target.

Economic inequality, policing, housing, war, and institutional racism are more diffuse.

A movement designed for direct confrontation with explicit exclusion may need different structures to confront systems whose rules are formally equal but outcomes remain unequal.

SNCC’s decline by the end of the decade does not erase its institutional achievement.

Like the Knights of Labor and the Constitutional Convention, it matters partly because it was temporary.

It trained people, created tactics, developed leaders, changed political expectations, and forced older institutions to respond.

Its alumni carried organizing experience into later movements, politics, education, law, and community work.

A temporary institution can be a school for permanent change.

The organization also reveals the costs of distributed leadership.

A decentralized movement can adapt to local conditions and avoid dependence on one charismatic figure. It can also struggle to make national decisions, control messages, resolve ideological conflicts, and maintain funding.

The same structure that empowers local initiative can make strategic coherence difficult.

There is no organizational form that maximizes both autonomy and coordination.

SNCC lived inside that tradeoff.

The 1960s offer perhaps the strongest set of rival institutions in this book.

Congress enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1964, Voting Rights Act of 1965, Medicare and Medicaid, immigration reform, environmental measures, and Great Society programs. The Supreme Court transformed criminal procedure, reapportionment, privacy, and civil rights. The presidency expanded domestic programs and escalated the Vietnam War. Television networks created a national visual public in which images of Birmingham, Selma, assassinations, war, and unrest could reach households rapidly. Universities became centers of research and protest. The Department of Defense managed a global security state.

Why choose a small student-led organization over all of them?

Because the decade’s decisive institutional innovation was not only new law.

It was a method for making institutions obey law they already claimed to respect.

SNCC discovered that citizenship had an operational layer.

A right is not self-executing.

It requires a route to the registrar, a lawyer after arrest, a local meeting place, a ride home after intimidation, a newspaper willing to report, a federal official willing to intervene, and enough people to return tomorrow.

The formal state supplies some of those pieces.

Civil society supplies others.

The American constitutional system depends on institutions not mentioned in the Constitution to make constitutional promises real.

SNCC was one of them.

Its organizers also understood something large institutions often forget: information flows upward only if somebody is willing to sit in the room where the information first appears.

A Washington official can read a report saying registration is low.

An organizer in a county can know which employer threatened which applicant, which registrar changed which test, which family is ready to try again, which church can host a meeting, which road is unsafe after dark.

Local knowledge is not anecdote when the policy problem is local power.

It is the data.

SNCC made that data political.

The institution of the 1960s was therefore not the law that announced equality.

It was the organization that kept asking what equality looked like at 9:00 on Monday morning when the courthouse door opened.
