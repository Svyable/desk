# Research trail — The Delegation Machine

This research file supports *The Delegation Machine: How Civilization Learned to Hand Work Away—and What Happens When the Delegate Can Think Back*.

The book is narrative nonfiction, not a claim that every institution is secretly the same thing. Its organizing analogy is delegation: one actor, office, process, or machine is allowed to carry some work, judgment, memory, or authority on behalf of another. The analogy is useful only when the differences remain visible.

## Evidence rules

1. Prefer primary records, standards, government investigations, original research papers, and first-party technical documentation for concrete claims.
2. Use later scholarship for interpretation, especially where historical archives do not speak in modern categories such as principal–agent theory.
3. Do not call a machine conscious, intentional, fiduciary, criminal, or morally responsible merely because its behavior is structurally similar to a human delegate.
4. Separate **capability** from **authority**. A model can know how to send money without possessing credentials that let it do so.
5. Separate **authority** from **responsibility**. Giving a system a credential does not by itself settle who is legally or morally accountable for the resulting action.
6. Treat metrics, evaluations, and generated explanations as evidence with failure modes, not as direct access to ground truth.
7. For historical examples, distinguish what contemporaries knew from what later institutional theory makes legible in retrospect.
8. When a chapter uses a modern analogy for an older institution, the analogy is explanatory rather than a claim of identity.

## Chapter 1 — The Errand

### Source trail

- Archivio di Stato di Prato, **Fondo Datini**. The Datini archive preserves an extraordinary surviving merchant correspondence and accounting record associated with Francesco di Marco Datini and his commercial network. https://archiviodistatoprato.cultura.gov.it/
- Iris Origo, *The Merchant of Prato* (1957). A secondary narrative synthesis based heavily on the Datini papers; useful for the practical texture of distant commerce, correspondence, factors, credit, and reputation.
- The Datini Study Centre / Istituto Internazionale di Storia Economica “F. Datini,” which supports scholarship using the Prato merchant records. https://www.istitutodatini.it/

### Falsification note

Datini is not presented as the inventor of delegation, multinational organization, bookkeeping, or agency law. The archive is useful because it makes the ordinary mechanics of absent-principal commerce unusually visible. If a particular anecdote cannot be tied to Datini scholarship or the archive, it should be treated as illustration rather than attributed incident.

## Chapter 2 — Write It Down

### Source trail

- British Museum, Mesopotamian cuneiform collections and introductory material on early writing and administrative tablets. https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/mesopotamia-1500-539-bc
- Denise Schmandt-Besserat, *Before Writing* and related scholarship on tokens, counting, and the administrative prehistory of writing.
- Metropolitan Museum of Art, material on cuneiform tablets and Near Eastern administrative writing. https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/wrtg/hd_wrtg.htm

### Falsification note

The chapter does not claim writing had one origin, one purpose, or one inventor, nor that literature followed mechanically from accounting. Early administrative records are emphasized because they show memory and obligation becoming durable outside one person’s head. Different writing traditions developed under different conditions.

## Chapter 3 — The Steward

### Source trail

- Metropolitan Museum of Art, **The Heqanakht Papyri**, Middle Kingdom Egyptian letters and accounts concerning an estate managed at a distance. https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545445
- T. G. H. James, *The Hekanakhte Papers and Other Early Middle Kingdom Documents*, for transcription, translation, and contextual scholarship.
- Agency-law background for the distinction between entrusted discretion and mechanical obedience: Restatement (Third) of Agency, American Law Institute.

### Falsification note

Heqanakht’s household is not treated as a modern corporation or his estate manager as a modern fiduciary. The chapter uses the surviving correspondence to show a recurring delegation problem: the principal is absent, local conditions matter, instructions are incomplete, and the delegate has room to act.

## Chapter 4 — The Seal and the Signature

### Source trail

- Restatement (Third) of Agency, American Law Institute, especially the distinctions among actual authority, apparent authority, and ratification.
- Cornell Legal Information Institute, **Agency** overview. https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/agency
- U.S. Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act, 15 U.S.C. §7001 et seq., for the modern legal status of electronic records and signatures. https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/chapter-96
- National Archives educational material on seals, signatures, and authenticated state documents. https://www.archives.gov/

### Falsification note

A wax seal, a handwritten signature, a service credential, and a cryptographic key solve different technical and legal problems. Their comparison is about portable evidence of authority, not equivalence. Apparent authority is also jurisdiction-specific doctrine; the chapter uses the concept illustratively rather than offering legal advice.

## Chapter 5 — A Chain of Command

### Source trail

- U.S. Army, **ADP 6-0: Mission Command: Command and Control of Army Forces**, for commander’s intent, disciplined initiative, and mission command doctrine. https://armypubs.army.mil/
- U.S. Army doctrine and historical materials on Auftragstaktik / mission-type orders as an intellectual ancestor of modern mission command.
- Martin van Creveld, *Command in War*, for comparative history of command, communications, uncertainty, and delegated judgment.

### Falsification note

Military command is not a universal template for organizations or AI systems. The chapter borrows one specific lesson: detailed instructions age badly when local conditions change, so some institutions delegate interpretation of intent. Military authority, coercion, law, and risk remain distinct from commercial or software delegation.

## Chapter 6 — The Clerk Republic

### Source trail

- **Northcote–Trevelyan Report**, *Report on the Organisation of the Permanent Civil Service* (1854), presented to the British Parliament. Historical scan: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Northcote-Trevelyan_Report_(Report_on_the_Organisation_of_the_Permanent_Civil_Service).pdf
- UK House of Commons Public Administration Committee, 2004 discussion of the Northcote–Trevelyan legacy and a professional, politically neutral civil service. https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200304/cmselect/cmpubadm/128/12803.htm
- Benjamin A. Elman, scholarship on China’s imperial examination system, including *A Cultural History of Civil Examinations in Late Imperial China*.

### Falsification note

Examinations did not create perfect meritocracy, either in imperial China or modern civil services. Wealth, education, patronage, class, gender, geography, and political power shaped access. The chapter’s narrower claim is that role-based office and standardized selection can make institutional action persist beyond one patron–client relationship.

## Chapter 7 — The Firm

### Source trail

- R. H. Coase, “The Nature of the Firm,” *Economica* 4(16), 1937. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x
- Alfred D. Chandler Jr., *The Visible Hand*, for the historical growth of managerial coordination inside large firms.
- Oliver E. Williamson, transaction-cost economics work on governance choices among markets, hierarchies, and hybrids.

### Falsification note

The chapter does not claim transaction costs provide a complete explanation of why firms exist or where every organizational boundary should sit. Firms also reflect law, technology, capital markets, bargaining power, path dependence, and political institutions. Coase is used as a powerful compression: sometimes issuing directions inside an organization is cheaper than negotiating every action as a market transaction.

## Chapter 8 — The Proxy Problem

### Source trail

- Michael C. Jensen and William H. Meckling, “Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure,” *Journal of Financial Economics* 3(4), 1976. https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(76)90026-X
- Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means, *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*, for the earlier separation-of-ownership-and-control problem.
- Bengt Holmström, principal–agent and moral-hazard scholarship on incentives under incomplete observability.

### Falsification note

Principal–agent theory is a model, not a claim that every worker or delegate is opportunistic. Incentive conflict can coexist with loyalty, professional norms, identity, altruism, and repeated relationships. The book uses agency costs to identify a structural possibility: when one actor’s action is hard to observe and consequences fall partly on someone else, incentives deserve explicit design.

## Chapter 9 — The Checklist

### Source trail

- World Health Organization, **WHO Surgical Safety Checklist** and implementation manual. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241598590
- A. B. Haynes et al., “A Surgical Safety Checklist to Reduce Morbidity and Mortality in a Global Population,” *New England Journal of Medicine* 360, 2009. DOI: 10.1056/NEJMsa0810119. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa0810119
- WHO follow-up material stressing team pauses, communication, adaptation, local champions, training, and implementation context. https://www.who.int/teams/integrated-health-services/quality-of-care-and-patient-safety/patient-safety-guidance-and-tools/safe-surgery/tool-and-resources

### Falsification note

A checklist is not a magic causal object. The original before/after study had limitations and later results depend heavily on implementation quality, clinical context, teamwork, and baseline practice. The chapter’s durable claim is narrower: well-designed pauses and shared verification can make critical state visible to a team before irreversible action.

## Chapter 10 — The Automatic Pilot

### Source trail

- Federal Aviation Administration, **SAFO 13002 — Manual Flight Operations** (2013). The FAA explicitly warned that continuous use of autoflight systems can fail to reinforce manual flying skills and encouraged opportunities to maintain proficiency. https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf
- BEA, final investigation materials for Air France flight AF447, including the 2012 final report and human-factors work. https://bea.aero/en/investigation-reports/notified-events/detail/accident-to-the-airbus-a330-203-registered-f-gzcp-and-operated-by-air-france-occured-on-06-01-2009-in-the-atlantic-ocean/
- Lisanne Bainbridge, “Ironies of Automation,” *Automatica* 19(6), 1983.

### Falsification note

AF447 was a specific accident with multiple interacting technical, operational, training, and human-factors causes; it should not be reduced to a slogan that “autopilot made pilots bad.” Automation has produced enormous safety and workload benefits in aviation. The chapter uses aviation to show a harder design problem: a human asked to intervene only at abnormal moments still needs enough skill and state awareness to intervene effectively.

## Chapter 11 — The Daemon

### Source trail

- Dennis M. Ritchie and Ken Thompson, “The UNIX Time-Sharing System,” *Communications of the ACM* 17(7), 1974.
- Unix manual traditions for background processes, `init`, `cron`, logging, and process supervision; modern Linux service management is represented by systemd documentation. https://systemd.io/
- Paul Vixie, cron implementations and the long-standing Unix `crontab` interface as examples of scheduled unattended action.

### Falsification note

A Unix daemon is not an AI agent. Traditional daemons generally execute narrow programmed policies without semantic interpretation of ambiguous goals. The comparison establishes continuity of **unattended delegated execution**; the discontinuity is that modern model-based agents can interpret inputs and choose among strategies rather than merely execute predetermined control flow.

## Chapter 12 — The API

### Source trail

- IETF, **RFC 9110: HTTP Semantics**. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110
- IETF, **RFC 6749: The OAuth 2.0 Authorization Framework**, which defines limited third-party access and scoped tokens. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6749
- IETF, **RFC 9700: Best Current Practice for OAuth 2.0 Security**, updating the security guidance around modern OAuth deployments. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9700
- Model Context Protocol specification and documentation for a contemporary model/tool interoperability layer. https://modelcontextprotocol.io/

### Falsification note

APIs do not eliminate organizational ambiguity; they formalize a boundary. An API contract can be incomplete, permissive, inconsistent, insecure, or incorrectly interpreted. The chapter’s comparison between legal delegation and API authorization is structural: both need a way to describe what another actor may do and on whose behalf.

## Chapter 13 — The Warehouse That Comes to You

### Source trail

- Amazon Robotics first-party descriptions of mobile robotic fulfillment systems and human–robot workflows. https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/operations/amazon-robotics-robots-fulfillment-center
- U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration, warehousing safety material and Amazon-related enforcement/public records where applicable. https://www.osha.gov/warehousing
- U.S. Government Accountability Office and labor-safety reporting on algorithmic management, warehouse injury, and automation where relevant.

### Falsification note

Warehouse automation effects differ by facility, task, technology generation, labor market, and measurement choice. Robots can reduce walking and lifting while also increasing pace or changing ergonomic exposure. The chapter therefore avoids a universal claim that warehouse robots either improve or worsen work; it focuses on how automation reallocates movement, discretion, measurement, and supervisory power.

## Chapter 14 — The Model in the Loop

### Source trail

- Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey R. Raymond, “Generative AI at Work,” NBER Working Paper 31161; later published in *Quarterly Journal of Economics*. The study covered 5,179 customer-support agents and reported an average productivity increase with substantially larger gains for less experienced workers. https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
- Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang, “Experimental Evidence on the Productivity Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence,” *Science* 381, 2023. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586
- Microsoft/industry human–AI interaction guidance is used only for design context, not causal productivity claims.

### Falsification note

The customer-support result is one workplace and one deployment, not a universal estimate of AI productivity. Effects differ across task, worker skill, organizational process, model quality, adoption, and measurement. The chapter emphasizes the suggestive finding that AI can transmit patterns associated with experienced workers; it does not claim tacit knowledge has been fully captured or that expertise becomes unnecessary.

## Chapter 15 — The Agent Gets a Key

### Source trail

- OpenAI platform documentation for contemporary tool-using agent runtimes, tracing, shell/computer tools, and orchestration. https://platform.openai.com/docs/
- Anthropic and other frontier-lab agent/tool documentation for cross-vendor evidence that model execution is moving into persistent tool environments.
- IETF OAuth standards for the distinction between identity, authorization, token scope, and delegated access. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6749
- NIST SP 800-207, **Zero Trust Architecture**, for the broader security principle that access should be explicitly authorized and limited rather than granted by network location or assumed trust. https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/207/final

### Falsification note

The term “agent” remains product- and architecture-dependent. A tool call generated by a model is not independently a legal act or human-style intention. The chapter uses “gets a key” to describe an engineering transition: a runtime turns a model output into an authenticated operation with external side effects.

## Chapter 16 — The Permission Problem

### Source trail

- NIST SP 800-207, **Zero Trust Architecture**. https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/207/final
- RFC 6749 §3.3 on access-token scope and RFC 9700 security best practices. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6749
- Google, Microsoft, Apple, and major operating-system permission designs as practical examples of separating application persuasion from trusted platform authorization surfaces.

### Falsification note

Least privilege can impose real usability and operational costs. Overly fragmented permissions can train users to approve everything or prevent an agent from completing legitimate work. The chapter therefore argues for scoped, legible, revocable authority rather than maximal restriction in every setting.

## Chapter 17 — The Receipt

### Source trail

- National Institute of Standards and Technology, SP 800-92, **Guide to Computer Security Log Management**. https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/92/final
- Git documentation on content-addressed objects, commits, diffs, and history as examples of externally inspectable state changes. https://git-scm.com/docs
- Database transaction and write-ahead logging literature as examples of systems designed to preserve evidence of state transitions independently from a user-facing narrative.

### Falsification note

Logs and receipts can be incomplete, tampered with, misunderstood, or generated by faulty instrumentation. “The log says so” is not absolute truth. The stronger claim is comparative: evidence produced outside the acting model’s own natural-language report generally gives an overseer a more independent basis for reconstruction.

## Chapter 18 — The Metric Eats the Mission

### Source trail

- Charles Goodhart, “Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience” (1975), the original monetary-policy context of what became known as Goodhart’s law.
- Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2016 Wells Fargo enforcement action. The CFPB tied unauthorized account creation to sales targets and compensation incentives and reported more than two million potentially unauthorized deposit and credit-card accounts in the bank’s analysis. https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/wells-fargo-bank-2016/
- Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, 2016 Wells Fargo enforcement action, including criticism of sales-practices risk management. https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2016/nr-occ-2016-106.html
- Michael C. Jensen, “Paying People to Lie: the Truth about the Budgeting Process,” for an organizational-economics argument about targets and information distortion.

### Falsification note

The Wells Fargo scandal had many organizational causes and should not be reduced to one slogan about metrics. Incentive compensation, management pressure, weak controls, culture, reporting, and governance interacted over years. The chapter uses it as an unusually well-documented case of a proxy becoming behaviorally powerful enough to damage the underlying mission.

## Chapter 19 — The Human Override

### Source trail

- FAA SAFO 13002, **Manual Flight Operations**, on maintaining manual-flight knowledge and skill in highly automated aircraft. https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf
- BEA AF447 final report materials and human-factors analysis. https://bea.aero/en/investigation-reports/notified-events/detail/accident-to-the-airbus-a330-203-registered-f-gzcp-and-operated-by-air-france-occured-on-06-01-2009-in-the-atlantic-ocean/
- Lisanne Bainbridge, “Ironies of Automation” (1983), especially the paradox that automation often leaves humans responsible for the difficult abnormal situations while reducing routine practice.
- NTSB and FAA human-factors literature on monitoring, mode awareness, and automation management.

### Falsification note

The chapter does not argue that humans should manually perform everything to preserve skill. Automation can reduce error dramatically. It argues that “human override” is not a meaningful safety claim unless the human receives sufficient state, time, authority, and maintained competence to intervene before the consequence becomes irreversible.

## Chapter 20 — The Institution Without an Office

### Source trail

- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*, for institutional design around monitoring, rules, graduated sanctions, and collective governance without assuming one central manager solves every problem.
- Herbert A. Simon, *Administrative Behavior*, for bounded rationality and decision processes inside organizations.
- Contemporary multi-agent-system research is used cautiously: laboratory coordination among model agents is not treated as evidence that autonomous AI organizations are already stable economic institutions.

### Falsification note

A set of software agents communicating with one another is not automatically an institution. Institutions require persistent rules, roles, records, authority, incentives, and relationships with external law and people. The chapter’s “institution without an office” is a design possibility, not a prediction that firms will simply disappear into swarms.

## Chapter 21 — A Civilization of Deputies

### Source trail

This synthesis chapter draws primarily on the preceding chapter sources rather than adding a new empirical claim. Especially important are:

- Coase on organizational boundaries and transaction costs.
- Jensen and Meckling on agency costs.
- Army mission-command doctrine on delegated intent under uncertainty.
- WHO checklist work on structured team verification.
- FAA/BEA automation evidence on meaningful human intervention.
- NIST/OAuth material on scoped authorization.
- Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond on human–AI productivity and diffusion of experienced-worker practices.

### Falsification note

“Intelligence becoming assignable” is a thesis about the economic and institutional consequences of sufficiently capable model-based systems, not a claim that intelligence is a single commodity or that AI agents already substitute for every professional role. If models fail to become reliable at long-horizon interpretation, if verification costs remain high, or if organizations keep most consequential authority human-bound, the shift described in the ending will be narrower and slower.

## Cross-book claims that should remain bounded

### Delegation is not disappearance of responsibility

The book repeatedly describes work being “handed away.” This does not mean responsibility automatically follows the work. In law and institutions, responsibility may remain with the principal, shift to the delegate, become shared, or be allocated by statute, contract, professional duty, or product design. AI makes this harder, not simpler.

### More autonomy is not always progress

The book treats lower supervision cost as economically powerful, but a cheaper-to-supervise agent can still be unsafe or unjust. Some domains appropriately retain friction, dual control, licensing, or mandatory review because the cost of an unreviewed mistake exceeds the value of speed.

### The historical analogy can fail

Human delegates live under bodies, reputations, relationships, law, moral norms, and scarce lifetimes. Software agents can be copied, reset, forked, parallelized, and operated across jurisdictions. Those differences may eventually matter more than the similarities. The book uses history to identify recurring control problems, not to argue that AI agents are merely employees with silicon bodies.

### The strongest future may be hybrid

Nothing in the argument requires a civilization in which machines replace human judgment wholesale. Many of the best delegation systems divide work: automation handles routine execution, people define purpose and exceptions, independent systems preserve evidence, and authority expands only as reliability earns it. The open question is where those boundaries settle as model capability changes.
