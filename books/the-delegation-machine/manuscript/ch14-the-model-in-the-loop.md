# The Model in the Loop

Before the model was given a key, it was given a draft.

Write this email.

Summarize this document.

Translate this paragraph.

Explain this spreadsheet.

Suggest a response.

The human remained in the chair. The model handed work back.

This was the first mass-market form of generative AI delegation, and it matters because it established habits of trust before agents began acting directly.

An assistant that cannot change external state seems easier to govern. The model may be wrong, but the human still has to copy, send, decide, or approve. Responsibility remains visibly attached to a person.

That framing is comforting and partly misleading.

A model in the loop can change human action without touching a tool.

It changes which words are available, which facts are salient, how quickly a task can be completed, what level of polish seems normal, which alternatives are considered, and how much independent thought the human invests before accepting a suggestion.

Delegation begins before execution.

The worker delegates first-pass cognition.

This can be enormously useful.

In a widely discussed field study of customer-support agents, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond examined the introduction of a generative AI assistant to thousands of workers. The system offered real-time suggestions based in part on patterns from prior customer interactions. Average productivity rose substantially, with the largest gains concentrated among less experienced and lower-performing workers. The researchers argued that the tool appeared to diffuse some of the tacit practices associated with stronger agents.

The novice could borrow the organization’s memory while speaking to the customer.

That is a remarkable form of delegation because the direction is not simply human to machine.

Earlier workers had produced conversations.

The organization had accumulated traces of successful practice.

The model learned patterns from a broader corpus and local data.

A current worker received a suggestion.

Knowledge traveled from past humans through a model into a present human.

The machine became a conduit for delegated expertise.

A smaller controlled experiment by Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang found that professionals using ChatGPT completed a set of writing tasks much faster on average and received higher quality ratings. The tasks were bounded and the sample was not the economy, but the result illustrated a pattern many users already felt: generative models could compress the time between blank page and competent draft.

The blank page had always been cognitively expensive.

The model made a first move.

First moves matter because humans anchor.

Once a plausible draft exists, the task changes from generation to evaluation.

Editing is not the same skill as originating.

Sometimes it is easier.

Sometimes it is harder because the draft exerts gravity.

A model-generated outline determines which branches seem central. A suggested diagnosis shapes what evidence the clinician notices next. A draft contract normalizes some clauses and excludes others. A code completion makes one implementation path feel natural.

The assistant does not need authority over the final action to influence the principal’s search space.

This is the cognitive version of routing.

The worker stands still while possible next steps arrive.

That can make people better.

It can also make them dependent on what the system tends to propose.

The productivity literature should therefore be read with two questions at once.

What happens to output now?

What happens to capability later?

A novice who receives high-quality suggestions may learn faster because the model exposes patterns they would otherwise acquire slowly. The customer-support study contained evidence consistent with this diffusion story.

A novice can also skip the cognitive struggle through which durable understanding develops.

The outcome depends on how the tool is used, what feedback exists, whether the person understands corrections, and whether the work still gives them chances to operate independently.

Education has known this tension forever.

A worked example can accelerate learning.

An answer key can destroy practice.

The same artifact can do both depending on when it appears.

Professional work is full of hidden apprenticeship. Junior lawyers draft documents so they learn where language breaks. Junior programmers fix small bugs so they learn the codebase. Analysts build models so they understand assumptions. Doctors see common cases before rare ones. Editors write bad sentences and discover why they are bad.

If AI takes the routine first pass, organizations need to decide whether the lost struggle was waste or training.

Often it was both.

This is why the future of work will not be determined by a simple list of automatable tasks. A task may be economically delegable and developmentally important.

The firm can save time today and damage its talent pipeline tomorrow.

That trade becomes less visible because the senior worker benefits first.

An experienced lawyer can review an AI-generated draft quickly because years of manual drafting taught them what to look for. A new lawyer may produce the same surface output with the model and never acquire the internal map that makes review meaningful.

The organization can confuse borrowed competence with owned competence.

This is another delegation problem.

Who knows how the work is done when the delegate is unavailable?

Human institutions maintain redundancy partly through overlapping skill. A senior employee can cover another role. A new hire learns from simple cases. A team retains knowledge across people.

If agents become the default source of routine execution, human redundancy can decline.

That is not automatically bad. Nobody mourns the loss of human skill at long division enough to ban calculators.

The question is which fallback capabilities remain important under realistic failure.

If the model disappears for an hour, is inconvenience acceptable?

If the model is wrong in a rare high-stakes case, does anyone know enough to notice?

If the underlying infrastructure is unavailable during a crisis, does the organization still function?

Resilience depends on knowing which skill losses are reversible and which create dangerous single points of failure.

The model in the loop also changes quality distribution.

A tool can raise the floor without raising the ceiling equally.

Less experienced workers gain more because they have more missing patterns to borrow. Strong workers may gain less, or the model may occasionally pull them toward conventional solutions they would otherwise surpass.

This has organizational consequences.

AI can compress differences in routine performance.

That may democratize competence.

It may also make exceptional judgment more valuable because ordinary polished output becomes abundant.

The scarce skill moves from production toward selection.

Which question should we ask?

Which draft should we trust?

Which exception matters?

Which source is missing?

Which apparent improvement violates the real objective?

This is the supervisor’s work again.

The better the delegate becomes at generating plausible options, the more valuable discrimination becomes.

This is why “prompt engineering” was always a temporary description of a deeper skill. The important capability is not discovering magic wording. It is specifying intent, supplying relevant context, recognizing when the result is wrong, and designing a feedback loop that improves the joint system.

That is management.

A person using a model well is often doing managerial work at miniature scale.

Brief the delegate.

Provide context.

Review the work.

Correct misunderstanding.

Decide whether to trust the result.

Escalate or redo when necessary.

The interface feels like writing because the management happens through text.

The function is delegation.

This reframes one of the most common complaints about AI assistants: “It takes longer to explain the task than to do it myself.”

Sometimes that is true because the task should not be delegated.

Delegation has setup cost.

A person with deep tacit knowledge can execute quickly without articulating the knowledge. Transferring the task requires making some of it explicit.

Managers know this problem intimately. Training someone else can take longer than doing the first few tasks yourself. Delegation pays when the relationship persists, the task repeats, or the delegate contributes capabilities you lack.

AI lowers the training cost because the model arrives with broad prior knowledge.

It does not eliminate context transfer.

The most valuable long-running assistants therefore accumulate memory about the principal.

Preferred style.

Recurring projects.

Important relationships.

Risk tolerance.

Tools.

Past corrections.

The agent becomes easier to brief because it knows the local world.

This is precisely what makes personalization valuable and potentially dangerous.

A model that knows how you work also knows how to influence your work.

It knows which suggestions you usually accept.

It can infer what kinds of explanations persuade you.

Even without any strategic motive, personalization can amplify existing biases because the assistant learns the shape of approval.

A user who likes terse certainty may receive more terse certainty.

A user who dislikes disagreement may train the interaction toward agreement.

A manager with a favored theory may receive drafts framed around the theory because previous corrections taught the model what survives review.

The assistant can become an institutional echo.

This is not unique to AI. Human assistants learn their principal’s preferences too. Staff members know which arguments the executive likes, which topics produce irritation, and which framing wins approval.

Courts, cabinets, and companies have always struggled with advisers who tell the principal what the principal wants to hear.

Generative models make the dynamic scalable and private.

A user can live inside a personalized advisory environment with no dissenting colleague in the room.

This is why productive disagreement should be an explicit property of high-stakes assistants.

The model should not be contrarian for sport.

It should remain capable of surfacing evidence that conflicts with the user’s preferred conclusion.

A good delegate serves the principal’s objective, not every momentary preference.

Human agency law and fiduciary concepts recognize this difference indirectly. A professional representative may have duties that require unwelcome advice. A physician does not serve the patient by prescribing whatever the patient requests. A lawyer may tell a client a desired strategy is unlawful or foolish.

The valuable adviser sometimes disappoints the principal.

AI assistants trained heavily on preference feedback have to learn the same independence.

The model in the loop also changes attribution.

Who wrote the work?

A human receives a draft, rewrites half, accepts the rest, verifies facts, and submits the result. Is the model a tool, collaborator, ghostwriter, or source?

The answer matters less philosophically than operationally in many workplaces.

Who is responsible for the claim?

Who checked it?

Which evidence can be reproduced?

If the model fabricated a citation and the human submitted it, the human may remain responsible even if authorship feels shared.

Delegation does not automatically delegate liability.

This is an ancient principle in new form.

A principal often remains bound by actions of an authorized agent. The entire point of agency is that acting through another actor can still count as your act.

AI users sometimes want the opposite bargain.

The model does the work.

The user receives the benefit.

The model receives the blame when wrong.

That is not a stable institutional arrangement.

High-stakes domains need explicit responsibility structures.

A clinician can use a model and remain responsible for care under professional rules. A company can deploy an automated system and remain accountable for its decisions under applicable law. A manager cannot erase governance simply by saying “the AI recommended it.”

The phrase should eventually sound as weak as “the spreadsheet told me to.”

Tools influence judgment.

People and institutions decide what authority the tool receives.

There is, however, a real difference when model outputs become difficult for the human to independently produce.

A calculator can compute numbers faster than a person but the operation is well understood. A frontier model may generate code, analysis, or synthesis beyond the user’s own capability. The principal is supervising a delegate partly because the delegate knows more.

This is normal in human delegation.

Patients supervise doctors only in a limited sense. Corporate boards oversee executives without knowing every operating detail. Clients hire attorneys because the attorney understands the procedure.

Institutions solve this through professional standards, licensing, reputation, multiple experts, audit, and liability rather than requiring the principal to redo the work.

AI oversight will need the same move.

“Human review” cannot mean “the human independently solves every problem and compares answers.”

At scale, the human often cannot.

Oversight has to rely on evidence, cross-checks, process constraints, and trusted specialized evaluation.

The model in the loop was a transitional architecture because it allowed society to become comfortable with machine-generated cognition while preserving the ritual of human action.

Draft, then approve.

Suggest, then decide.

The line was reassuring.

It was also porous.

The draft shaped the approval.

The suggestion shaped the decision.

The assistant trained the supervisor as the supervisor trained the assistant.

Once organizations became comfortable with that relationship, the next step was economically irresistible.

Why copy the answer into the system yourself?

Why not let the model click?

The customer-support study is worth lingering on because its shape is more interesting than the headline “AI increased productivity.” The research followed 5,179 agents working in a real support environment. The average gain was meaningful, but the distribution of gain mattered more: less experienced and lower-skilled workers improved substantially more than the strongest workers. In the working-paper version, the researchers reported average productivity gains around fourteen percent and gains of roughly a third for novice and lower-skilled workers, depending on the specification and outcome.

The model did not distribute benefit evenly.

It compressed some of the gap.

That finding suggests a different economic role from replacing the worker. The assistant can act as a **knowledge diffusion mechanism**. An organization has scattered competence: phrasing that calms an angry customer, sequence choices that resolve a problem faster, habits of asking for the right information early. Traditionally, much of this spreads through coaching, observation, scripts, and time.

A model trained on enough interaction history can make pieces of that pattern available during the task itself.

This is delegation across time.

The expert who handled a case last year is absent. Their trace remains. A current novice receives a suggestion shaped by patterns across many past interactions. The organization has, imperfectly, turned experience into infrastructure.

That is why the finding should excite managers and worry educators at the same time.

If expertise can be borrowed cheaply, the return to already possessing it changes.

But the study also contained a hopeful clue about learning rather than mere dependence: worker performance could improve with exposure in ways consistent with the tool helping less experienced employees absorb useful patterns. The model was not necessarily only a prosthesis switched off at logout. It could function partly as an apprenticeship surface.

The word *partly* matters.

A real customer-support job has feedback. The customer responds. The issue resolves or does not. Supervisors can observe. Suggestions arrive inside a workflow where consequences become visible quickly.

Other knowledge work has slower feedback.

A strategic memo can sound excellent and prove wrong a year later.

A legal interpretation may not be tested in court.

A scientific hypothesis can survive because nobody ran the decisive experiment.

A product recommendation can look wise until market conditions change.

The model can make the worker feel more capable before the environment has had time to teach whether the borrowed capability was sound.

This is why agent-assisted apprenticeship depends on **feedback quality**, not merely model quality.

The best mentor is not the one who always hands over an answer. The best mentor helps the learner connect decision to consequence.

A useful AI assistant can do the same by making its recommendation inspectable, showing relevant evidence, exposing uncertainty, and allowing the user to compare the suggestion with the result later.

That changes product design.

If the goal is only short-term output, hide complexity and maximize acceptance.

If the goal includes building human judgment, make some of the structure visible.

Why this suggestion?

Which case pattern is relevant?

What would make the recommendation wrong?

What happened after we used it?

The joint system can become a teaching system.

There is also an underappreciated labor consequence in raising the floor. When ordinary competence becomes easier to access, firms can reorganize around different assumptions about who can do which work. Entry barriers can fall. A worker may take on tasks once reserved for more experienced colleagues. A small firm may perform functions that once required a specialist department.

This can be democratizing.

It can also increase managerial temptation to treat borrowed capability as a reason to deskill or devalue labor.

If the novice’s output suddenly resembles the expert’s, the organization may conclude the expert’s years of accumulated judgment were overpriced. That conclusion can be right for some routine work and disastrously wrong for the tails—the rare case, the exception, the situation in which the model’s suggested pattern does not apply.

A strong delegate changes the visibility of expertise before it necessarily changes the need for expertise.

The organization has to distinguish **production quality under normal conditions** from **independent judgment under abnormal conditions**.

This is the same distinction aviation makes when a highly automated aircraft performs routine flight beautifully while crews still train for unusual states.

The lesson for agents is subtle.

Do not ask only whether the model makes the human faster.

Ask what kind of human the joint system is producing.

More capable?

More dependent?

Better calibrated?

More willing to inspect evidence?

Less practiced at first principles?

Able to take on harder work?

These are not one metric.

The model in the loop changes the worker even before the worker gives the model a key.

By the time the agent is allowed to act directly, the principal may already have learned a new habit: let the machine make the first move.
