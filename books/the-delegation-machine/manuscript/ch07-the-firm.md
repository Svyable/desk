# The Firm

A company is what happens when asking the market becomes more expensive than telling a colleague.

That sentence is an oversimplification of Ronald Coase, but it points in the right direction.

In his 1937 paper “The Nature of the Firm,” Coase asked a question so basic that economics had mostly stepped around it: if markets coordinate resources through prices, why do firms exist at all?

Why does a factory employ people instead of buying every minute of labor as a separate contract?

Why does a company maintain an accounting department rather than request bids for each ledger entry?

Why does a manager tell an employee to handle a task instead of negotiating a new market transaction every time circumstances change?

Because coordination has costs.

Finding prices takes effort. Negotiating contracts takes effort. Specifying every future contingency is impossible. A firm creates a zone in which some activities are coordinated through authority rather than repeated bargaining.

Inside that zone, delegation becomes cheap enough to be routine.

“Can you take this?” replaces a contract.

The phrase is almost absurdly efficient.

Two coworkers share enough institutional context that a broad request can activate hours of specialized work. Budget, tools, reporting relationships, norms, software, salary, legal obligations, and prior knowledge have already been arranged. The transaction cost was paid in advance by building the organization.

A company is stored coordination.

This is why AI agents may change the boundaries of firms rather than merely the tasks inside them.

If the cost of specifying, discovering, negotiating, executing, and verifying work falls sharply, some activities that once required an employee may become easy to purchase or automate. At the same time, if agents make internal coordination cheaper, firms may absorb work they previously outsourced.

The direction is not obvious.

The important variable is the cost of delegation.

This is a better frame than the familiar question, “Which jobs will AI replace?”

Jobs are bundles created partly because transaction costs made the bundle useful.

A human role often contains ten activities that share a person, office, context, authority, and schedule. Technology can unbundle them. New technology can also rebundle them in stranger combinations.

The history of the factory shows how dramatic rebundling can be.

At Ford’s Highland Park plant, the moving assembly line introduced in 1913 reorganized automobile production around flow. Instead of workers moving around a vehicle performing broad sets of tasks, the product moved and jobs narrowed. Ford’s own history describes the Model T chassis assembly time falling from roughly twelve and a half hours to about an hour and a half. The productivity gain came with a human cost. The work became repetitive, tightly paced, and unpopular enough that turnover contributed to Ford’s famous decision to raise pay.

The machine did not simply replace labor.

It changed what counted as a job.

The worker became a station in a designed sequence.

This is delegation through decomposition.

Management studies the total activity, divides it into steps, standardizes the steps, and assigns each to a worker or machine. The intelligence that once lived in the craftsperson’s integrated practice moves upward into process design.

Frederick Winslow Taylor’s scientific management pushed a similar logic: analyze work, separate planning from execution, measure motions and time, and construct a “one best way” that workers should follow.

The ambition was control through specification.

Modern automation inherited it.

If a task can be decomposed into stable operations, software or machinery can take more of the sequence. The manager’s problem becomes designing the process rather than relying on each worker’s judgment.

Language-model agents arrive from the opposite direction.

They make undecomposition economically interesting again.

Instead of reducing a job into tiny deterministic steps, a company can hand the model a larger bundle and ask it to figure out the steps.

Research this customer and prepare an outreach plan.

Investigate this failure and propose a patch.

Reconcile these documents and identify discrepancies.

Plan the trip within these constraints.

Review the contract and flag unusual terms.

The work starts to look craft-like again, except the craft is performed by a learned system.

This is one reason the language of agents matters. The software is not merely a station on the assembly line. It can become a miniature manager of its own sequence.

The firm now delegates decomposition.

That changes management.

A manager supervising people often decides who should do what, how work should be sequenced, which context should be shared, and when a result is good enough. An agent can absorb some of these coordination decisions. Multi-agent systems can go further by routing tasks among specialists.

The hierarchy begins to automate its own middle.

This does not necessarily make managers disappear.

It changes the level at which management is exercised.

Someone still has to decide what the organization is trying to accomplish, which constraints matter, how risk is allocated, what evidence counts, who has authority, and how local decisions combine into a coherent whole.

The more agents handle execution planning, the more human management moves toward institutional design.

This may be uncomfortable because many managers spend large amounts of time on coordination that looks important precisely because it is scarce.

Meetings. status updates. assignment. follow-up. summarization. escalation. schedule negotiation. information routing.

Agents can make these cheaper.

The organizational question becomes whether the saved attention flows upward into better judgment or merely into more work.

History gives reasons for caution.

Productivity improvements do not automatically reduce workload. The moving assembly line produced more cars. Email made communication cheaper and produced more messages. Spreadsheets made analysis cheaper and increased the amount of analysis expected. Smartphones made people reachable everywhere and expanded the temporal boundaries of work.

When delegation becomes cheaper, principals delegate more.

That may be the defining economic effect of AI agents.

A manager who would never assign a human employee to research fifty minor questions may send fifty agents. A consumer who would not hire a personal assistant may use one constantly. A small company that could not afford specialized departments may instantiate legal, research, design, coding, and operations assistance on demand.

The number of delegated tasks can explode because the minimum economically sensible task size collapses.

This is the opposite of automation scarcity.

We may not run out of work.

We may create oceans of previously uneconomic errands.

Check every invoice.

Compare every contract clause.

Summarize every meeting.

Monitor every competitor.

Personalize every message.

Test every configuration.

Review every account.

The organization gains a new capacity to care about small things.

This can improve quality.

It can also create a surveillance bureaucracy of infinite attention.

A human company ignores many details because attention is expensive. This acts as an accidental civil liberty inside organizations. Managers cannot read every message, score every interaction, and inspect every keystroke because doing so costs time.

Agents reduce the cost.

The firm can delegate observation itself.

That power needs governance.

The problem is not only privacy. Cheap observation changes incentives. Workers behave differently when every action may be scored. Customers behave differently when every sentence is analyzed. Managers can begin optimizing micro-signals whose relationship to actual performance is weak.

The organization becomes more measurable and potentially less sane.

This is another version of Taylorism: intelligence moves into the measurement system.

AI can either deepen that logic or undermine it.

A model can enforce scripts with unprecedented precision.

It can also understand context well enough to permit judgment.

Which direction companies choose will matter more than the raw capability.

Consider customer service.

A traditional call center often decomposes service into scripts, knowledge-base articles, escalation rules, time targets, and quality scores. The structure allows thousands of employees to handle varied requests with bounded training.

Generative AI can be inserted as another script, telling workers exactly what to say.

Or it can act as a knowledge amplifier that gives less experienced workers access to patterns learned from stronger colleagues while leaving the human in control.

Research by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond on thousands of customer-support agents found substantial average productivity gains from an AI assistant, with especially large improvements among less experienced and lower-performing workers. Their interpretation is important: the system appeared to diffuse some of the tacit patterns of high performers.

The model acted like organizational memory.

This is a new form of delegation.

Experts had already done the work.

The system learned from traces of that work.

Novices could then borrow the organization’s accumulated competence in real time.

Firms have always tried to do this through manuals, training, mentoring, templates, and culture. Much of expertise resists documentation because experts do not know exactly what they know. They notice phrasing, patterns, timing, and exceptions implicitly.

Language models can absorb some of these regularities from examples.

That makes tacit knowledge more transferable.

It also raises a question about ownership.

Whose expertise is inside the delegate?

A company fine-tunes or configures a system on successful employee interactions. The model becomes better. New workers receive suggestions distilled from earlier workers. The organization captures knowledge that previously traveled through apprenticeship or individual reputation.

This can democratize skill.

It can also weaken the bargaining power of the people whose skill created the data.

The firm has always tried to make valuable knowledge survive employee departure. Standard operating procedures, documentation, patents, trade secrets, team structures, and succession planning all serve this goal.

AI makes the capture more granular.

A model can learn not only what the employee wrote down as procedure but patterns embedded in thousands of actions.

The delegate becomes a composite ghost of prior delegates.

This is why discussions of AI and labor cannot stop at headcount.

The deeper question is how knowledge, authority, and value move inside the firm.

If an agent makes a junior worker more capable, the worker may gain leverage because one person can do more. Or the firm may need fewer workers. If a model encodes expert behavior, experts may focus on harder cases or lose scarcity rents. If agents make coordination easier, small firms may compete with large ones. Or large firms may exploit scale more aggressively because they can deploy agents across enormous proprietary datasets.

Transaction costs move in several directions at once.

Coase’s question becomes newly alive.

What belongs inside the firm when intelligent coordination is cheap?

One possibility is the tiny giant: a very small human organization with enormous agentic capacity. A handful of people own the mission, capital, relationships, and judgment while models perform much of the research, production, operations, and administration.

Another possibility is the hyper-bureaucracy: large firms become even larger because agents make internal coordination and monitoring cheaper than market contracting.

Another is a fluid market of specialized agents purchased task by task, making the boundary of the company porous.

All are plausible because AI affects both the cost of hierarchy and the cost of markets.

We should expect experimentation rather than one inevitable organizational form.

The most interesting changes may happen below the level of the firm, inside individual roles.

A job is partly a package of delegations received from above and delegations sent downward or outward.

A manager receives an objective and delegates analysis.

An analyst receives a question and delegates computation to software.

A lawyer receives a matter and delegates research to associates.

A doctor receives responsibility for care and delegates tests to laboratories and tasks to a clinical team.

A software engineer receives a feature and delegates compilation, testing, deployment, and monitoring to tools.

AI inserts a new layer into almost every stack.

The human becomes principal and agent at the same time.

This dual role is psychologically important.

Workers often imagine automation as something management does to labor. Agentic tools can also be something labor uses to increase its own reach. The same employee may resist automated monitoring and enthusiastically use an AI assistant.

The politics depend on who controls the delegate.

A worker-owned agent that remembers personal preferences and helps negotiate organizational systems is different from a company-owned agent that supervises the worker.

A customer agent that searches for the best deal is different from a seller agent optimized for conversion.

A citizen agent that navigates government benefits is different from a government agent optimized to reduce improper payments.

Delegation is power, and power changes with the direction of representation.

This is why the term principal should remain visible.

Who is the agent acting for?

The answer may be multiple parties. A corporate assistant serves the employee, employer, software provider, and policy environment at once. A marketplace agent may receive incentives from the platform and instructions from the shopper. Conflicts will be normal.

Firms already manage conflicts through governance structures, imperfectly.

Boards monitor executives. auditors monitor financial reporting. compliance teams constrain sales. procurement separates vendor selection from payment. human resources creates procedure around managers. legal departments review commitments.

These functions exist because the organization does not assume every local incentive aligns automatically with the whole.

Agent-heavy firms will need equivalent internal counterweights.

It may be tempting to consolidate them into one powerful model: the company brain.

One agent sees all data, coordinates all functions, applies all policy, and advises all decisions.

This maximizes context and minimizes handoff cost.

It also creates extraordinary concentration of epistemic and operational power.

Human firms separate functions partly because independence has value. The auditor should not be the salesperson. The person approving payment should not be the same person who created the vendor. Legal review should retain some ability to frustrate a deal.

Friction is organizational redundancy.

Agents make redundancy cheap too.

Instead of one model judging its own work, a company can use separate evaluators, independent logs, deterministic policy engines, and specialized review agents. The future firm may become internally plural by design.

This is where automation could improve governance rather than merely efficiency.

A small organization can afford controls previously available only to large institutions because monitoring and review become cheap.

Every payment can receive anomaly analysis.

Every contract can be checked for conflicts.

Every code change can be independently tested.

Every consequential decision can leave a structured rationale and trace.

The same technology that increases delegated action can increase delegated oversight.

Whether it does depends on incentives.

A company rewarded only for speed will use agents to go faster.

A company that values evidence can use agents to create more of it.

The technology does not decide the institution.

The assembly line is a useful warning here. Ford’s moving line was not simply a machine. It was a philosophy embedded in architecture. The physical arrangement made one kind of work easier and another harder. It dictated pace. It redistributed skill. It changed who could stop the process.

Agentic software will do the same invisibly.

An approval workflow, permission model, model routing strategy, memory system, and performance metric are organizational design choices encoded in software.

The agent stack is the new factory floor.

Workers may never see the architecture that shapes their discretion.

Customers may never know which agent made a decision.

Managers may inherit defaults chosen by platform developers.

The design of delegation becomes political economy.

Coase wanted to understand why some coordination happens inside firms and some through markets.

AI adds another choice.

Some coordination can happen inside models.

The model interprets, plans, routes, checks, and revises without each internal step becoming an explicit transaction at all.

The boundary of the firm acquires an inner boundary: which decisions remain organizationally visible and which disappear inside the agent’s reasoning loop?

That may be the most important management question of all.

A firm is stored coordination.

An agent is compressed coordination.

When the compression becomes too complete, nobody remembers how the company knows what it knows.
