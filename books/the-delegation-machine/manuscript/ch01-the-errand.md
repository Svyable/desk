# The Errand

The first agent in this book is not a machine.

He is carrying a letter.

Somewhere near the end of the fourteenth century, a merchant’s instruction leaves one city because the merchant cannot. The letter may travel by mule, ship, hired courier, acquaintance, employee, or some combination of people whose names matter less than the fact that the paper keeps moving after the author has stopped writing. At the other end, another person reads the instruction and acts.

The merchant has made intention portable.

Francesco di Marco Datini built a commercial life out of this possibility. Datini was born in Prato, near Florence, in 1335 and spent much of his career trading across an expanding network of firms and correspondents. The archive associated with his business is extraordinary not because medieval merchants were uniquely fond of paperwork, but because so much of his paperwork survived. The Datini archive contains hundreds of ledgers and roughly 150,000 letters spanning commercial centers across Europe and the Mediterranean. The geography is the first lesson. Datini’s business extended far beyond the distance one man could personally travel and supervise each day.

He needed other people to be him, temporarily and locally.

They bought, sold, inspected, negotiated, recorded, collected, paid, shipped, waited, warned, and replied. They acted in places where Datini was absent using information he had sent before conditions changed. Sometimes they had to follow instruction. Sometimes they had to interpret it.

That second activity is where delegation begins to become interesting.

If I ask you to place this book on that table, the distance between instruction and execution is tiny. You can see the book, the table, and probably what I mean by place. If a chair blocks the way, you walk around it. I do not need to specify the obstacle because you possess a model of rooms, bodies, books, furniture, and ordinary intention.

Now make the errand longer.

Take this package across town.

The bus is delayed. The address has two entrances. The recipient is not home. It begins raining. A stranger offers to accept the package. The label tears.

The instruction has not changed. The world has.

A useful delegate supplies the missing policy.

That is why delegation is more than assigning labor. Labor is part of it, but the principal is also renting local judgment.

A courier who had to return for instructions at every street corner would extend your legs but not your agency. A factor who could buy cloth only if Datini had already specified the exact seller, quantity, quality, price, exchange rate, shipping date, credit terms, insurance conditions, and response to every possible disruption would be less an agent than a remote-controlled hand.

Historical commerce did not work that way because it could not.

Communication was slow. Markets moved. Ships were delayed or lost. Prices changed. Political conditions shifted. Goods varied in quality. People failed to pay. The principal’s information became stale while the letter was in transit.

The agent’s discretion was not an optional luxury.

It was the thing that made distance economically survivable.

This is still true when the errand takes milliseconds.

A modern delivery platform may calculate a route, but the driver still handles a blocked loading zone. A software process receives structured parameters, but a language-model agent is valuable when the parameters are incomplete. Tell it to find an invoice and reconcile the payment, and it may need to infer which vendor name is a legal entity, which PDF is the final version, whether a credit memo offsets the balance, and whether a mismatch is an error or a currency conversion.

The attraction is exactly the same as Datini’s.

You do not want to travel there yourself.

The dangerous part is exactly the same too.

You are not there.

Distance creates leverage and ignorance together.

A principal delegates because being absent is efficient. The absence also means the principal cannot directly observe every decision. The agent encounters local facts first. The agent decides which facts deserve to travel back. The report is therefore never a perfect copy of the world. It is a compression made by the person whose behavior the principal is trying to understand.

This is the first permanent problem of delegation: the delegate is often both actor and witness.

A merchant asks, what happened to the shipment?

The person who handled the shipment answers.

A manager asks, why did the project slip?

The team that missed the date explains.

A user asks, did you update the file?

The agent that was supposed to update the file reports completion.

There is nothing inherently dishonest about this arrangement. Most human cooperation would collapse if every mundane act required an independent investigator. We accept self-report because it is cheap and usually good enough.

Then consequences rise.

The more valuable the cargo, the stranger the counterparty, the greater the distance, or the more tempting the shortcut, the less comfortable we become with a single narrative.

Commerce answered with documents.

Orders. Invoices. Bills of exchange. Ledgers. Receipts. Marks. Seals. Duplicates. Correspondence. Account books.

These artifacts did more than preserve memory. They created a second version of events that could be compared with the agent’s story.

The invention is easy to overlook because paperwork now feels like bureaucracy. For a delegated system, paperwork is a sensor.

It tells the absent principal something happened.

Not everything. Not perfectly. A ledger can be falsified. A receipt can be forged. A letter can omit. But a transaction that leaves several independent traces is harder to rewrite than a transaction existing only in memory.

Datini’s surviving archive is an accidental monument to this idea. The letters tell one story, account books another, counterparties another. Historians can reconstruct relationships precisely because commercial action generated records across a network rather than one continuous autobiography.

The network also reveals a second problem: delegation creates delegation.

A principal appoints an agent. The agent uses a messenger. The messenger entrusts a packet to a ship’s crew. A local representative hires a broker. A broker consults a warehouse keeper. The original principal may know only the first name in the chain.

This is how real work scales.

No serious organization is one person surrounded by direct reports carrying out atomic commands. Delegates become principals for smaller delegations. A chief executive delegates to a vice president who delegates to a director who delegates to a manager who hires a vendor that subcontracts a specialist who runs software written by someone else.

The chain can become absurdly deep without anyone deciding to make it deep.

Every step exists because local specialization is useful.

Every step also changes the meaning of the original instruction.

Think of the children’s game in which a sentence is whispered from person to person until the final version barely resembles the first. Real delegation is not that simple because competent agents correct errors. They ask questions, preserve context, and consult records. But there is still semantic drift.

“Get this done by Friday” becomes “we need a draft by Thursday so legal can review.”

“Keep costs reasonable” becomes a departmental budget.

“Protect customer data” becomes access-control rules, vendor clauses, encryption settings, retention schedules, employee training, and exception processes.

“Make the customer happy” becomes a refund threshold inside a call-center script.

The principal’s broad intention becomes an institution of local rules.

Some of those rules eventually become more real to the delegate than the original intention.

This is one reason organizations produce absurd behavior without absurd people. A worker follows the metric because that is what reaches them. The company’s mission may be elegant, but the employee interacts with a queue, quota, approval form, or service-level target.

Delegation turns values into interfaces.

What reaches the local actor is the interface.

The same will be true for AI agents.

A company may say its agent should “act in the customer’s best interest.” The model does not receive a mystical beam of corporate intention. It receives system instructions, tool schemas, permissions, retrieved documents, examples, reward signals, and the current task. Those are the operational forms of the value.

If they conflict, the agent has to interpret the institution.

Humans do too.

The difference is that humans arrive with social knowledge accumulated outside the job. A new employee understands that a manager’s “do whatever it takes” is usually constrained by law, safety, morality, and common sense even when those words are omitted. The understanding can fail spectacularly, but civilization leans heavily on unspoken constraints.

Machines are forcing us to notice how much management has always depended on them.

The fantasy of the perfect prompt is partly a fantasy that principals have never needed complete instructions before.

They have not.

They had culture.

They had professional norms.

They had apprenticeship.

They had sanctions.

They had shared physical worlds.

They had the possibility that a subordinate would look at an order and say, you cannot possibly mean that literally.

A capable AI agent may eventually supply similar judgment. The difficulty is knowing which implicit norms it has learned, which it has not, and how they interact with a specific deployment.

This is why the oldest errands remain relevant.

Delegation is not a transfer of a sentence. It is a transfer of a decision boundary.

Inside the boundary, the agent chooses.

Outside it, the agent should return.

When you tell a friend to pick a restaurant, you probably delegate cuisine, location, price within a range, and reservation time. You may not delegate charging dinner to a corporate card, inviting twelve strangers, or booking a flight to Tokyo. The exact boundary is rarely written because the social context makes it obvious.

Computing dislikes invisible boundaries.

If a software agent holds a credential that can spend ten thousand dollars, “I only expected it to buy lunch” is not a permission model.

This is one of the reasons the vocabulary of legal agency becomes unexpectedly useful. Law has spent centuries separating different kinds of authority because third parties need to know when an agent’s action binds the principal. Actual authority is what the principal really granted. Apparent authority concerns what outsiders can reasonably infer from the principal’s conduct.

The distinction has a digital equivalent.

Suppose an employee tells an AI agent, “Coordinate with the vendor.” The agent has access to the employee’s email account and can send messages bearing the employee’s name and company domain. The vendor receives, “We accept your revised terms.”

Was the agent authorized to negotiate logistics or bind the company?

The internal instruction may have been narrow.

The external appearance is broad.

The vendor sees the badge.

This is apparent authority with an API token.

The problem will become common because digital systems make authority easy to copy. A password, session cookie, OAuth token, API key, signing certificate, or service account can allow one process to appear as another actor. Traditional software security treats this as authentication and authorization. Agency law asks the complementary social question: what conclusions are other people entitled to draw from the authenticated act?

An AI agent makes the overlap impossible to ignore because it can speak.

A background process with access to an account usually executes predefined calls. A language-model agent can explain, negotiate, reassure, apologize, and improvise while wearing the account’s identity.

Credentials become rhetoric.

This raises the stakes of a simple design choice: whether the agent speaks as itself or as the principal.

Human organizations have long used titles to manage this. “Assistant to,” “on behalf of,” “authorized representative,” “agent,” “attorney-in-fact,” “buyer,” “broker,” “officer.” The title tells outsiders something about delegated capacity.

AI systems will need equally legible representation.

A message drafted by an agent but approved by a human is one thing.

A message autonomously sent by an agent under a user’s address is another.

A contract generated by an agent but not authorized to bind anyone is another.

If every action arrives in the principal’s voice, outsiders lose the ability to reason about where judgment occurred.

The provenance of action matters.

The oldest commercial networks understood this in physical form. Letters carried names, marks, handwriting, seals, and routes. The trustworthiness of a message depended partly on the chain by which it arrived.

A letter did not merely contain information.

It had a biography.

Digital communication initially seemed to erase biography. A perfect copy has no wear. An email can be forwarded without the recipient seeing every system that processed it. An API call arrives as structured data. Yet secure computing has spent decades rebuilding provenance through logs, signatures, timestamps, certificates, and audit trails.

The more we delegate to agents, the more action will need biography again.

Who requested this?

Which model interpreted it?

Which tools were available?

What did the agent read?

Which human approved the irreversible step?

What changed?

Can the change be reversed?

Who observed the result independently?

These questions may sound heavy for ordinary tasks. They are heavy.

Most delegation should remain light.

Nobody needs a forensic chain of custody for an AI that renames a playlist. The art is matching the control to the consequence.

This was also true for Datini. A merchant did not use the same precautions for every message, shipment, and coin. Trust varied by person, amount, route, and experience. Repeated relationships reduced uncertainty. Reputation served as a distributed memory of past behavior. Formal records became more important when stakes or distance rose.

We can express the principle without inventing a framework.

More delegated consequence requires more independent evidence.

The word independent matters.

If the agent writes the report, creates the receipt, controls the log, and judges the outcome, four documents may still amount to one source.

This is where the errand becomes architecture.

Imagine two ways to design a travel agent.

In the first, the model searches flights, selects one, enters passenger information, pays with the stored card, receives confirmation, and tells the user the trip is booked. The user sees a pleasant summary.

In the second, the model can search and propose. A separate payment service enforces a spending limit. The airline sends confirmation directly to an address the agent cannot edit. The operating system records the purchase. Cancellation remains possible for a period. The user can inspect the underlying itinerary independently.

Both systems may use the same model.

One is easier to trust because the world does not depend on the model’s testimony about itself.

That pattern will recur through the book.

The question “Is the agent trustworthy?” is often too psychologically framed.

The more useful question is “What would have to be true for a mistake, shortcut, or conflicting incentive to become an accepted fact?”

The answer might be: only the agent would have to say so.

That is weak delegation.

Or: the agent’s action must pass an external test, stay within a credential scope, leave an immutable trace, and remain reversible until another actor confirms it.

That is stronger delegation even if the model is identical.

A merchant in the fourteenth century did not possess immutable logs, cryptographic signatures, or machine-readable scopes. He possessed the institutional ancestors: duplicate books, correspondence, countersignatures, trusted intermediaries, accounting conventions, and relationships repeated long enough to build a reputation.

He was solving the same geometry with slower tools.

The point is not to romanticize medieval commerce. The systems were exclusionary, fragile, violent, and dependent on institutions that often protected some people far more than others. Trust networks could reproduce family privilege. Credit could be coercive. Merchants operated inside political and legal orders whose protections varied sharply by status and place.

Delegation has never been neutral.

The person able to delegate gains reach.

The person receiving delegated work may gain opportunity, burden, or both.

An elite who can send instructions across a network lives differently from the person who must carry them.

That social fact remains inside modern automation.

When we say an AI agent “saves time,” whose time?

A customer may save ten minutes while a worker handles the edge cases the automation rejects. A manager may receive instant summaries while employees spend more time structuring data so the model can use it. A company may automate a queue while contractors label the training data. Delegation can make work disappear from the principal’s field of view without making the work disappear.

This is another ancient trick.

The errand feels effortless to the person who stays home.

The road still exists.

The most seductive promise of agentic AI is that the road itself is going away. Tell the system what you want and it will handle the rest. The fewer steps the user sees, the more magical the product feels.

Good products often do hide complexity.

The danger is when hidden complexity becomes hidden accountability.

A person should not need to watch every click an agent makes. That would defeat delegation. But the system should preserve enough structure that someone can reconstruct a consequential decision later.

This is the difference between invisibility and opacity.

A well-run restaurant hides most kitchen work from the diner but can still trace an ingredient when safety demands it. A bank hides settlement machinery from the customer but maintains transaction records. A cloud service automates thousands of processes while producing logs and metrics for operators.

Delegation should remove attention requirements, not evidence.

The errand is successful when you do not have to walk beside the messenger.

It is trustworthy when you can still learn where the messenger went.

This is the first principle the age of agents will have to relearn.

We are building systems whose value comes from not needing us in the room.

The answer cannot be to remain in the room.

The answer is to design absence.

Datini’s letters crossed a world in which every mile increased uncertainty. Our agents may cross a thousand services before a human can blink. The speed changes the engineering but not the basic bargain.

I cannot be there.

You can.

Handle it.

And bring me back something better than your word.
