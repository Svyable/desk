# Chapter 11 evidence note — AI assistance, learning, and transfer

**Checked:** September 6, 2026

Chapter 11’s strongest testable claim is narrower than “AI makes people learn less.” The useful question is whether assistance improves the artifact produced *during assistance* while weakening, preserving, or improving the learner’s ability to perform when the scaffold is removed. Two 2025 randomized studies make that distinction unusually concrete and point away from a simple pro- or anti-AI conclusion.

## Primary study: assistance can improve practice while weakening unassisted performance

Hamsa Bastani, Osbert Bastani, Alp Sungu, Haosen Ge, Özge Kabakcı, and Rei Mariman, “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 122, no. 26 (2025), e2422633122. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122

The preregistered field experiment involved nearly 1,000 high-school students in Turkey and compared ordinary resources with two GPT-4-based tutors during mathematics practice. The less constrained “GPT Base” interface substantially improved assisted practice performance, but students later scored 17% lower than the control group on an unassisted exam. A more structured “GPT Tutor,” designed with teacher-provided solutions and prompts that emphasized hints rather than direct answers, produced much larger gains during assisted practice while largely eliminating the subsequent learning penalty; its unassisted exam performance was statistically indistinguishable from control.

That result maps closely to Chapter 11’s output/formation distinction: a system can improve immediate task completion without improving the capability that remains afterward. It also supports the chapter’s “useful restraint” idea more directly than a generic claim about productive struggle, because the two AI conditions differed partly in whether the system made solution-copying easy or pushed students toward guided engagement.

The boundary matters. This was mathematics, one high school, four review sessions, and short-term transfer to related unassisted exam problems. It does not establish that generative AI generally damages learning, that writing behaves like mathematics, or that withholding answers is always pedagogically superior. The paper’s August 2025 correction concerned an author affiliation, not the reported experimental results.

## Counterevidence: carefully designed AI tutoring can improve learning

Greg Kestin, Kelly Miller, Anna Klales, Timothy Milbourne, et al., “AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: an RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting,” *Scientific Reports* 15 (2025), 17458. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6

In a randomized crossover experiment in an introductory Harvard physics course, 194 students received either an AI-tutored lesson or an in-class active-learning lesson on two topics. The custom tutor was deliberately engineered around pedagogical practices including active engagement, scaffolding, accurate worked material, targeted feedback, and self-pacing. Students in the AI condition showed larger post-test learning gains while typically spending less time on task, and reported higher engagement and motivation.

This is important counterevidence against turning “cognitive bypass” into an anti-assistance doctrine. The relevant variable is not simply whether a machine helps. Tool design, timing, task structure, feedback, prior knowledge, and what the later assessment requires can change the effect substantially. The Harvard study is also narrow: two lessons in one physics course, with expert-crafted instructional content and a purpose-built tutor. It does not show that a generic chatbot will reproduce those gains across subjects or over long periods.

## Strongest chapter-level inference

The paired evidence strengthens a more defensible version of Chapter 11’s argument:

> When formation matters, evaluate assistance by what the learner can still do after the assistance is reduced—not only by how good the assisted output looks.

That standard leaves room for aggressive AI use when the artifact is the objective and for highly capable AI tutoring when the system is designed to teach. It also gives “useful restraint” a falsifiable meaning: restraint is useful only if it improves later independent capability or another explicitly valued learning outcome. Friction that does not improve learning is merely friction.

A future manuscript pass should use these studies to replace some of the chapter’s repeated analogies and symmetrical “give the answer / withhold the answer” scaffolding, not to add an evidence dump. The chapter’s best empirical tension is now concrete: one AI design raised practice scores while degrading unsupported performance; another structured design improved measured learning. The book does not need to choose between them. It needs to explain why they differ.
