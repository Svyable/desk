# Chapter 10 Evidence Notes — The Value of a Life

These notes support the chapter's descriptive claims about VSL, QALYs, and DALYs. The normative argument remains the author's: the measures help compare changes in risk and health but do not, by themselves, determine human worth, fairness, or legitimacy.

## 1. Value of a Statistical Life (VSL)

**Primary sources**

- U.S. Department of Transportation, “Departmental Guidance on Valuation of a Statistical Life in Economic Analysis”: https://www.transportation.gov/office-policy/transportation-policy/revised-departmental-guidance-on-valuation-of-a-statistical-life-in-economic-analysis
- U.S. Department of Transportation, *Treatment of the Value of Preventing Fatalities and Injuries in Preparing Economic Analyses* (March 2021): https://www.transportation.gov/sites/dot.gov/files/2021-03/DOT%20VSL%20Guidance%20-%202021%20Update.pdf

**Claims verified**

DOT defines VSL as a way to value reductions in small mortality risks, not as the value of a person or “life as such.” Its guidance describes willingness to bear costs for safety improvements that, when aggregated, reduce expected fatalities by one. The guidance also applies a single nationwide VSL rather than varying the value by age, income, or other population characteristics. The current DOT web guidance lists a VSL of $14.2 million for analyses with a 2025 base year and an effective date of March 20, 2026.

**Use in chapter**

The chapter uses VSL to distinguish prospective risk valuation from a literal price on an identified person. It also uses DOT's uniform-value policy as an example of an explicit equality judgment layered on top of empirical willingness-to-pay evidence.

**What would make this false?**

Revise the chapter if DOT changes the definition so that VSL is no longer framed as valuation of mortality-risk reductions, or if DOT replaces the single nationwide-value policy with demographic or income-specific values for ordinary departmental analyses. Re-check the current dollar value before publication because the number is periodically updated.

## 2. Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs)

**Primary source**

- National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, *Technology appraisal and highly specialised technologies guidance: the manual*, Chapter 4, Economic evaluation: https://www.nice.org.uk/process/pmg36/chapter/economic-evaluation-2/

**Claims verified**

NICE uses cost-utility analysis and QALYs in health technology evaluation. Its manual describes QALYs as a generic measure of health benefit reflecting both mortality and health-related quality-of-life effects. NICE explicitly links cost-effectiveness analysis to maximising health gains from a fixed NHS and Personal Social Services budget, while also allowing other decision factors and modifiers to affect recommendations. The manual was updated in March 2026.

**Use in chapter**

The chapter uses QALYs to show why health systems need a unit that can compare changes in length and quality of life, then separates that efficiency function from separate judgments about severity, inequality, rights, and other considerations.

**What would make this false?**

Revise if NICE stops using QALYs as its generic reference-case measure of health benefit or abandons the fixed-budget opportunity-cost rationale. Before publication, verify the current manual and avoid freezing any cost-effectiveness threshold in prose unless a dated threshold is essential to the argument.

## 3. Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)

**Primary source**

- World Health Organization, *WHO methods and data sources for global burden of disease estimates 2000–2019* (Technical Paper WHO/DDI/DNA/GHE/2020.3): https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/gho-documents/global-health-estimates/ghe2019_daly-methods.pdf?sfvrsn=31b25009_7

**Claims verified**

WHO describes the DALY as a summary measure combining time lost through premature death with time lived in states of less than optimal health. One DALY can be interpreted as one lost year of healthy life. The technical paper also documents that earlier DALY formulations used time discounting and unequal age weights, while the simplified methods adopted for later Global Burden of Disease work and WHO estimates removed both time discounting and unequal age weighting.

**Use in chapter**

The chapter uses DALYs as an example of a powerful population-health comparison whose result depends on choices about reference life expectancy, health-state weights, discounting, and age weighting. The methodological changes are evidence that these value choices can be debated and revised rather than hidden.

**What would make this false?**

Revise if WHO changes the definition of the DALY or restores time discounting or unequal age weights to its standard reported estimates. Verify against the newest WHO Global Health Estimates methods before release.

## Counter-evidence and cautions

The chapter should not imply that these measures are merely dehumanising abstractions. VSL can make prevention benefits visible when no identifiable victim exists. QALYs can expose opportunity costs and reduce the influence of lobbying, prestige, or vivid anecdotes. DALYs can reveal large non-fatal disease burdens that death counts miss. These are substantive advantages, not concessions.

The chapter also should not imply that one unit is designed to answer every decision. VSL concerns mortality-risk reductions in benefit-cost analysis; QALYs compare health gains in cost-utility analysis; DALYs summarise population health loss. Their meanings, denominators, assumptions, and policy roles differ.
