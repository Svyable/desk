# Chapter 11 — The Commons: evidence and falsification note

## Claims checked

The chapter treats commons problems as incentive and boundary problems rather than as proof that people are inherently selfish. Elinor Ostrom's Nobel interview is useful counterevidence to the crude claim that common-pool resources require either privatization or centralized command: she describes long-lived systems with differing local rules and emphasizes higher-level design principles rather than a universal institutional recipe.

NOAA Fisheries provides a concrete contemporary example of layered commons governance. Federal fisheries use annual catch limits and accountability measures, while catch-share programs allocate portions of an allowable catch to individuals, cooperatives, communities, or other entities. NOAA explicitly notes that catch shares are one management option and are not appropriate for every fishery. That supports the manuscript's narrower claim that governance has to fit the resource rather than proving that catch shares are universally superior.

WHO's 2025 antimicrobial-resistance surveillance and antimicrobial-use materials support the chapter's treatment of antibiotic effectiveness as a shared resource across people and time. WHO reported substantial resistance across common bacterial infections and simultaneously frames stewardship around appropriate access and use. This is important counterevidence to a simplistic 'use fewer antibiotics' conclusion: under-access and inappropriate access can coexist.

## What would make the chapter's argument false or materially weaker?

- Strong evidence that durable common-pool resource systems almost always require either complete privatization or centralized state control would weaken the Ostrom-based institutional pluralism argument.
- Evidence that catch-share systems reliably worsen conservation and fleet safety relative to plausible alternatives across the settings where they are adopted would weaken the fishery illustration. NOAA itself cautions that catch shares are not appropriate everywhere, so the chapter does not claim universality.
- Evidence that antimicrobial resistance is driven almost entirely by unavoidable biological processes rather than materially affected by antimicrobial use would weaken the commons analogy. Current WHO stewardship policy and surveillance practice point the other way, while also recognizing access as a health objective.
- If shared-resource outcomes could generally be corrected by changing individual preferences without changing information, monitoring, incentives, or enforcement, the chapter's institutional emphasis would be too strong.
