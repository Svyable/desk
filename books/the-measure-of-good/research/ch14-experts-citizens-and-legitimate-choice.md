# Chapter 14 — Experts, Citizens, and Legitimate Choice

## Research question

Who should have authority to choose an objective when factual expertise, public preferences, rights, and democratic authorization do not point neatly in the same direction?

## Evidence used

### Ireland’s Citizens’ Assembly and the Eighth Amendment

The 2016–2018 Citizens’ Assembly is useful because it separated informational, deliberative, legislative, and constitutional authority rather than pretending one actor could supply all four. The Assembly heard evidence and arguments over multiple meetings and voted in April 2017 on recommendations concerning abortion law. Its recommendations were not self-executing. A parliamentary process followed, then the legislature placed a constitutional amendment before voters. The referendum was held on 25 May 2018, and more than 66% of those voting approved repeal of the Eighth Amendment; legislation governing termination of pregnancy followed later that year.

Primary/official sources:

- Citizens’ Assembly, meeting and ballot record, 22 April 2017: https://citizensassembly.ie/previous-assemblies/2016-2018-citizens-assembly/meetings/22-april-2017/
- Government of Ireland, referendum polling order, 28 March 2018: https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-housing-local-government-and-heritage/press-releases/referendum-thirty-sixth-amendment-of-the-constitution-bill-2018/
- Government of Ireland, Taoiseach statement following declaration of the referendum result, 28 May 2018: https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-the-taoiseach/speeches/speech-by-an-taoiseach-following-the-declaration-on-the-referendum-on-the-eighth-amendment/
- Government of Ireland, Department of Health, later implementation record noting that more than 66% approved repeal and that the Health (Regulation of Termination of Pregnancy) Act 2018 followed: https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-health/press-releases/minister-donnelly-announces-government-approval-of-the-heads-of-bill-to-legislate-for-termination-of-pregnancy-safe-access-zones/

The chapter uses the case as an institutional example, not as proof that the substantive outcome was morally correct. Its relevance is procedural: expertise informed the process, ordinary citizens deliberated, elected institutions retained legislative responsibility, and the electorate supplied constitutional authorization.

### Representative deliberative processes

OECD guidance defines representative deliberative processes as processes in which a broadly representative group weighs evidence, deliberates, and develops recommendations for public authorities. Its design guidance emphasizes learning from experts and stakeholders, facilitated deliberation, recommendations, and a response by the public authority. Government at a Glance 2025 reports that the OECD tracked 716 such cases across 28 OECD countries between 1979 and 2023, involving more than 80,000 randomly selected citizens, with 148 new cases recorded between 2021 and 2023.

Official sources:

- OECD, *OECD Guidelines for Citizen Participation Processes* (2022): https://doi.org/10.1787/f765caf6-en
- OECD, *Government at a Glance 2025*, section on citizen participation and deliberation: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/government-at-a-glance-2025_70e14c6c/full-report/citizen-participation-and-deliberation_52b90285.html
- OECD, *Evaluation Guidelines for Representative Deliberative Processes* (2021): https://doi.org/10.1787/10ccbfcb-en

The chapter does not treat these processes as automatically legitimate or wise. It explicitly raises selection, agenda-setting, evidence curation, facilitation, representativeness, participation costs, advisory versus binding status, and the risk that a consultation becomes decorative.

## Claims kept deliberately narrow

- Expertise has stronger epistemic authority on technical questions but does not, by itself, determine contested social objectives.
- Democratic authorization does not make false empirical claims true and does not erase rights constraints.
- Public deliberation can be designed to produce more considered judgment than an instantaneous opinion poll, but this does not make small deliberative bodies substitutes for elected institutions.
- Delegation can improve technical consistency and continuity while still requiring bounded mandates, transparency, accountability, and correction mechanisms.
- Legitimate decision systems often distribute authority across stages rather than locating all authority in one actor.

## Counterevidence

The chapter’s easiest conclusion would be that more participation is always better. Evidence and institutional experience do not justify that claim.

Representative deliberative processes are costly, slow, design-sensitive, and vulnerable to agenda control. Participants can misunderstand evidence, facilitators can shape discussion, expert selection can bias the informational environment, and a small randomly selected group has no electoral mandate. Open participation can also overrepresent people with unusually strong preferences, abundant time, or organizational resources.

Expert insulation can be valuable. Some decisions require specialization, continuity, confidentiality, or speed. Technical bodies can protect long-term stability from short-term political pressure. The chapter therefore argues for jurisdiction rather than a universal preference for either experts or citizens.

Majority rule also has limits. Rights and legal constraints may legitimately remove some outcomes from ordinary political choice. Conversely, unelected institutions can overreach by converting a technical mandate into authority over contested values.

## What would make this false?

The chapter’s central claim would weaken if evidence showed that complex public decisions could reliably be divided into purely factual questions answerable by experts and purely value questions answerable by voters, with little overlap or conflict. It would also weaken if representative deliberative processes consistently failed to produce more informed or considered judgments than ordinary public-opinion mechanisms, or if layered authority reliably generated only delay without improving accountability, learning, minority protection, or error correction.

A stronger falsifier would be a broad class of consequential public decisions in which concentrated expert control consistently produced better outcomes, durable public acceptance, and adequate rights protection without meaningful democratic authorization or contestability. The chapter leaves that possibility open in tightly bounded technical domains; it rejects the claim only as a general model of legitimate rule.
