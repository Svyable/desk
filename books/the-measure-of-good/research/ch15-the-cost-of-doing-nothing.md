# Chapter 15 — The Cost of Doing Nothing

## Research question

How should omission, delay, and the status quo enter the moral ledger when inaction changes future costs, risks, and available options?

## Evidence used

### Deferred maintenance

GAO’s 2025 work on federal real property is a clean case because deterioration makes the counterfactual visible. GAO reported that Department of Defense and federal civilian building deferred-maintenance and repair backlogs more than doubled from $171 billion in fiscal year 2017 to $370 billion in fiscal year 2024. It also warned that continued deterioration can force premature replacement that is significantly more expensive than maintenance performed when scheduled.

Official sources:

- U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Federal Real Property: Disposing of Unneeded Facilities Could Help Reduce Maintenance Backlog* (GAO-25-108400), 9 April 2025: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-108400
- U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Federal Real Property: Agencies Should Provide More Information About Increases in Deferred Maintenance and Repair* (GAO-24-105485), 16 November 2023: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-105485

The chapter does not treat the nominal backlog increase as a pure measure of physical deterioration. GAO attributes changes to multiple factors, including funding constraints, rising labor/material costs, age and quantity of assets, and reporting changes. The claim used in the prose is narrower: delay can change the cost and condition of the thing being deferred.

### Lead service-line replacement

EPA’s Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require most water systems to replace lead and certain galvanized service lines under their control within ten years or less. EPA identifies lead service lines as a major source of lead exposure in drinking water and treats full replacement as a way to remove that source.

Official sources:

- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, *Lead and Copper Rule Improvements*: https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/lead-and-copper-rule-improvements
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, *LCRI Questions and Answers*, updated 11 March 2026: https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/lcri-questions-and-answers
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, *Planning and Conducting Lead Service Line Replacement*: https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/planning-and-conducting-lead-service-line-replacement

The chapter uses this example to show that the visible fiscal and construction cost of action can be compared with dispersed health and exposure costs of leaving legacy infrastructure in place. It does not claim that every accelerated replacement schedule is automatically cost-effective or distributively fair.

### Active surveillance and the value of structured waiting

The National Cancer Institute defines active surveillance as close monitoring without immediate treatment, with treatment triggered by changes showing that a condition is worsening. NCI notes that active surveillance is used in some prostate cancers to avoid or delay treatment harms. The USPSTF’s prostate-cancer screening recommendation also emphasizes false positives, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, and treatment complications as reasons that immediate detection-and-treatment is not always an unqualified good.

Official sources:

- National Cancer Institute, definition of *active surveillance*: https://www.cancer.gov/publications/dictionaries/cancer-terms/def/active-surveillance
- National Cancer Institute, *Prostate Cancer Treatment (PDQ®)*: https://www.cancer.gov/types/prostate/hp/prostate-treatment-pdq
- U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, *Final Recommendation Statement: Prostate Cancer: Screening*: https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/RecommendationStatementFinal/prostate-cancer-screening

This evidence supplies the chapter’s main counterexample to action bias. Waiting can be the better strategy when it is monitored, bounded, and linked to explicit intervention triggers.

## Claims kept deliberately narrow

- Inaction is not a zero-cost baseline when underlying conditions continue to change.
- Visible spending can understate costs shifted onto households, health, future budgets, or deteriorating assets.
- Delay can create option loss when future choices become more expensive or unavailable.
- The fact that inaction has costs does not imply that intervention is better; action can create harms, false positives, overtreatment, or irreversible commitments.
- Structured waiting differs from passive postponement when it includes monitoring, review dates, and intervention triggers.

## Counterevidence

The chapter’s easiest error would be to romanticize action. The prostate-cancer material directly cuts against that move. Screening and treatment can generate meaningful harms, and active surveillance can preserve quality of life while reserving treatment for evidence of progression.

Similarly, deferred-maintenance totals do not prove that every listed project should be funded immediately. Agencies may own unneeded assets, and disposal can be preferable to repair. GAO itself frames disposal of unneeded buildings as one tool for reducing the backlog.

Lead service-line replacement likewise has transition costs, funding constraints, construction disruption, and distributional questions. The example is used to expose the non-zero cost of the status quo, not to claim that implementation details are morally trivial.

## What would make this false?

The chapter’s core claim would weaken if delay generally left the relevant system unchanged, preserved all future options, and imposed no meaningful costs outside the decision-maker’s budget. It would also weaken if formal review dates, monitoring, and intervention triggers did not distinguish productive waiting from passive postponement in practice.

A stronger falsifier would be evidence that organizations systematically overestimate rather than underestimate the harms of inaction, and that action bias causes more preventable damage than status-quo bias across the domains examined. The chapter leaves that possibility open and therefore treats “make the baseline visible” as the corrective principle rather than “always act sooner.”
