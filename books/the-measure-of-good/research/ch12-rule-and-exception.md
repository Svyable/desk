# Chapter 12 — The Rule and the Exception: evidence and falsification note

## Claims checked

HRSA/OPTN's current liver-allocation materials provide the chapter's central real-world architecture: lab-calculated MELD/PELD scores establish a standardized priority baseline; standardized exceptions exist for recurring diagnoses that the score represents poorly; and other unusual cases can be reviewed through an exception process. HRSA's current FAQ explicitly says that some medical conditions are not captured well by MELD/PELD and therefore receive exception scores. Current National Liver Review Board materials also document the effort to reduce geographic inconsistency in exception decisions.

The EEOC's ADA guidance supports the chapter's distinction between uniform methods and equal opportunity. Covered employers may need to provide reasonable accommodation to qualified applicants or employees, while not being required to eliminate essential job functions or accept accommodations that impose undue hardship. That makes accommodation a bounded exception mechanism rather than a suspension of standards.

The U.S. Sentencing Commission's current materials support the chapter's sentencing example. After United States v. Booker, federal sentencing guidelines are advisory; courts calculate the guideline range but may impose sentences outside that range under statutory sentencing factors. The Commission's 2025 manual notes the shift from guideline 'departures' toward statutory 'variances,' while preserving the broader tension between consistency and individualized judgment.

## What would make the chapter's argument false or materially weaker?

- Evidence that standardized transplant scores already capture essentially all clinically relevant urgency, with exception processes adding only noise or favoritism, would weaken the main example. Current OPTN policy architecture explicitly assumes the opposite for identifiable categories.
- Evidence that accommodation systems predictably reduce rather than improve equal access because exceptions mostly reward better-resourced claimants would require a much darker treatment of exception mechanisms. The chapter already flags discoverability and advocacy inequality as live risks.
- Evidence that sentencing discretion consistently increases unjustified disparity without correcting meaningful case differences would strengthen the case for rigid rules and weaken the chapter's balanced treatment.
- If repeated exception patterns do not provide useful information about rule defects, the chapter's claim that exceptions can function as institutional sensors would need qualification.
