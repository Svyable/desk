# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*The Measure of Good* asks a practical question: how should people and institutions decide what is better when the things that matter are only partly measurable, the measures themselves change behavior, benefits and harms fall on different people, and the future is uncertain?

The book should not become an argument against quantification. Its stronger claim is that measurement is indispensable but subordinate. Numbers can constrain stories, expose hidden costs, test whether interventions work, and make disagreement more honest. They cannot choose the objective, determine who counts, decide which values may be traded, or supply legitimacy for a contested choice.

The manuscript succeeds only if it can show both sides: the damage caused by bad metrics and the damage caused by refusing measurement in favor of intuition, status, rhetoric, or selective memory.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Proxy versus purpose:** When do commonly used measures track the underlying good well, and when do they drift away from it?
2. **Target effects:** How do incentives change the informational value of a measure once people know rewards or penalties depend on it?
3. **Distribution:** How often do improving totals or averages conceal concentrated harms, excluded populations, or transferred costs?
4. **Uncertainty:** Which decision methods remain useful when probabilities are poorly known, harms are asymmetric, or outcomes are irreversible?
5. **Non-tradable values:** Where do rights, duties, consent, dignity, or prohibitions limit otherwise attractive aggregate gains?
6. **Fairness:** How do equality, need, desert, opportunity, procedure, and priority to the worst-off lead to different judgments from the same facts?
7. **Time:** How should present benefits be compared with future costs, especially when future people cannot participate in the decision?
8. **Valuation of life and health:** What do cost-effectiveness tools illuminate, and what moral judgments remain embedded in their units and thresholds?
9. **Rules and exceptions:** When do standardized rules reduce bias and arbitrariness, and when does judgment need an appeal or exception path?
10. **Algorithmic decisions:** Under what conditions do models improve consistency or accuracy, and when do they hide value choices behind technical outputs?
11. **Legitimacy:** Which decisions require public authorization, contestability, transparency, or appeal even when experts can estimate consequences well?
12. **Inaction and reversibility:** How should decision-makers compare the risks of acting, waiting, experimenting, and preserving the ability to change course?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should actively look for evidence against its easiest conclusion.

- A simple metric may outperform discretionary human judgment when the alternative is favoritism, inconsistency, or memory.
- Strong incentives can improve the underlying outcome rather than merely produce gaming.
- Aggregates can be morally relevant; distribution is not the only thing that matters.
- Cost-benefit and cost-effectiveness analysis can prevent vivid anecdotes from dominating larger but less visible benefits.
- Rules and thresholds can be fairer than case-by-case discretion.
- Algorithms can reduce some forms of bias and error even while introducing others.
- Rights language can conceal tradeoffs or privilege the status quo.
- Precaution can become paralysis; delay and omission also create harms.
- Public preferences can be poorly informed, manipulated, inconsistent, or hostile to minorities.
- Expert judgment can be narrow, self-interested, overconfident, or institutionally captured.

The book should become more precise when counter-evidence is strong, not treat every exception as proof of the thesis.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Metrics, targets, and institutional behavior

Study performance measurement in education, healthcare, policing, finance, research, customer service, public administration, and workplaces. Distinguish honest improvement from relabeling, selection, threshold effects, effort substitution, and transferred costs.

### II. Welfare, distribution, and externalities

Use cases where a total or average improves while gains and losses are distributed differently. Track who is inside the denominator, who is missing, how costs cross organizational or social boundaries, and whether compensation is possible or meaningful.

### III. Risk, uncertainty, and irreversible choices

Separate known probabilities from model uncertainty and genuine ignorance. Compare expected-value reasoning, robust decision-making, precaution, safety margins, option value, and reversible experimentation.

### IV. Rights, duties, fairness, and legitimacy

Treat consequentialist, rights-based, contractual, capability, and procedural perspectives as competing lenses rather than slogans. Use cases in which they point in the same direction and cases in which they conflict.

### V. Valuing life, health, time, and the future

Examine the practical units used in medicine, regulation, insurance, transport, environmental policy, and public budgeting. Record what the units actually measure, what assumptions are needed to compare unlike outcomes, and which moral choices remain outside the arithmetic.

### VI. Rules, models, and appeals

Study the tradeoff between consistency and context in scoring systems, eligibility rules, triage, hiring, lending, benefits administration, and automated decisions. Pay particular attention to uncertainty near cutoffs and to whether affected people can understand, contest, and correct a decision.

### VII. Institutions that judge well

Look for organizations that combine measurement with local knowledge, escalation, audit, appeal, falsification, and the ability to revise objectives. The book should identify institutional practices that preserve judgment rather than merely criticize dashboards.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary sources, original academic papers, official datasets, statutes and regulations, institutional reports, and contemporaneous records for concrete cases.
- Distinguish descriptive findings from normative conclusions. A study can estimate an effect; it cannot by itself determine whether the effect is morally acceptable.
- Record denominators, exclusions, measurement definitions, uncertainty intervals, and material changes in methodology.
- Preserve evidence that a metric improved the underlying outcome, not only evidence of gaming or distortion.
- Do not treat a named law, principle, or philosophical school as proof. Explain the mechanism and test it against cases.
- Do not invent quotations, statistics, motives, or historical scenes.
- For every major empirical claim, keep a note titled “What would make this false?”
- When a case depends on current policy, technology, prices, or institutional rules, record the date and source vintage.
