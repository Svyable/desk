# The Cost of Doing Nothing

Doing nothing has excellent public relations.

It sounds cautious.

It sounds humble.

It sounds like a refusal to make things worse.

Sometimes it is exactly that.

A surgeon declines to operate because the likely harm exceeds the likely benefit. A regulator waits for another round of evidence before banning a useful product. A central bank leaves a rate unchanged because the alternatives look worse. A patient and doctor choose active surveillance rather than immediate treatment.

Restraint can be wisdom.

But “do nothing” is one of the most misleading phrases in decision-making because the world does not stop while we decide not to act.

Pipes corrode.

Buildings deteriorate.

Diseases progress or fail to progress.

Children age.

Debts compound.

Skills decay.

Technology diffuses.

Competitors move.

Habit hardens into infrastructure.

The future keeps arriving.

So the correct comparison is rarely action versus nothing.

It is one path versus another path.

One path contains an intervention.

The other contains whatever happens if the intervention is withheld.

Economists call this a baseline problem. Doctors call versions of it natural history. Engineers see it in degradation curves. Policy analysts see it in the no-action alternative. Ordinary people see it every time a small repair becomes an expensive one.

The moral error is the same.

We treat the status quo as if it were morally weightless.

It is not.

In 2025, the U.S. Government Accountability Office reported that deferred maintenance and repair backlogs for federal civilian and Defense Department buildings had more than doubled, from $171 billion in fiscal year 2017 to $370 billion in fiscal year 2024.

Some of that increase reflected rising costs, aging assets and changes in what agencies counted.

But GAO’s point was not merely that a large number had become larger. It warned that continued deterioration could force premature replacement, which can be substantially more expensive than maintenance performed when scheduled.

A roof ignored is not a roof preserved.

It is a roof moving toward a different and more expensive state.

Deferred maintenance makes the hidden structure of inaction easy to see because physical systems are unforgiving.

Skip painting steel and corrosion continues.

Postpone sealing a roof and water keeps searching for a way in.

Leave a failing chiller in service and the probability of an outage does not remain frozen at today’s level.

The decision to wait changes tomorrow’s options.

That is the first cost of doing nothing: time can alter the thing being decided about.

The second cost is less visible.

Inaction can distribute harm.

Imagine a city with old lead service lines.

Replacing them is expensive, disruptive and logistically difficult. Streets must be opened. Utilities need inventories. Households must be contacted. Crews have to coordinate work on public and private property. Money spent on pipes cannot be spent on something else.

A proposal to accelerate replacement therefore has obvious costs.

Those costs arrive in budgets and construction schedules.

The alternative can look cheap because the pipe is already there.

But the existing pipe is not free.

It is simply paid for differently.

The Environmental Protection Agency’s Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require most water systems to replace lead and certain galvanized service lines under their control within ten years. EPA’s justification rests on a basic asymmetry: lead service lines are a major source of lead exposure in drinking water, and replacing them removes that source.

The cost of replacement is concentrated and visible.

The cost of non-replacement is dispersed across exposure, health risk, household anxiety and future remediation.

This is why budgets can mislead moral judgment even when every line item is accurate.

A budget records what an institution spends.

It does not automatically record what people pay when the institution does not spend.

Delay can shift costs off the balance sheet and onto bodies, households, neighborhoods or future budgets.

The same pattern appears in maintenance, prevention, preparedness and environmental risk.

It also appears in private life.

A dental appointment costs money and time.

Skipping it is free this afternoon.

A difficult conversation costs discomfort.

Avoiding it is free tonight.

Backing up a computer costs minutes.

Not backing it up is free until the drive fails.

The accounting depends on where the time horizon ends.

This makes inaction particularly attractive to institutions judged on short reporting cycles.

The expense of acting is immediate.

The benefit may be a bad thing that never happens.

Prevented events are politically inconvenient successes.

Nobody sees the bridge collapse that did not occur.

Nobody experiences the infection they did not acquire.

Nobody stands beside the floodwater that never reached the house.

Prevention produces missing stories.

Failure produces photographs.

This creates an evidentiary problem.

After a preventive investment works, people can look around and conclude the danger was exaggerated because the feared outcome did not occur.

The better the prevention, the easier it can become to attack as unnecessary.

A fire code is expensive until the fire.

A backup generator is idle until the blackout.

A stockpile looks wasteful until supply fails.

The institution has to compare a visible cost with a counterfactual benefit.

That is difficult even when the probabilities are known.

Often they are not.

This is where people reach for the precautionary principle.

If the downside could be severe, act before certainty arrives.

That can be sensible.

It can also become a slogan for treating every uncertain hazard as a reason to intervene.

The problem is that interventions have counterfactuals too.

A ban can deprive people of a useful product.

A screening program can create false positives and unnecessary procedures.

An evacuation can cause its own injuries.

An aggressive treatment can produce side effects worse than the disease would have produced.

The cost of doing nothing does not imply the virtue of doing something.

It only means that both sides of the comparison need verbs.

“Act” is a verb.

“Wait” is also a verb.

Sometimes waiting is the intervention.

Consider prostate cancer.

The phrase “active surveillance” is almost a philosophical correction to the phrase “do nothing.”

For some patients with lower-risk disease, clinicians may closely monitor the cancer rather than begin surgery or radiation immediately. The National Cancer Institute describes active surveillance as regular monitoring with treatment reserved for signs that the condition is worsening.

This is not abandonment.

It is a decision architecture.

There are tests.

There are intervals.

There are triggers.

There is a plan for what happens if the state changes.

The distinction matters because immediate treatment has costs.

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force has emphasized that prostate cancer screening can lead to overdiagnosis and treatment of cancers that would never have become symptomatic during a patient’s lifetime. Treatment can cause urinary, sexual and bowel harms. For some men, detecting and attacking every abnormality is not obviously better than careful observation.

Here the cost of action can exceed the cost of waiting.

But the waiting is disciplined.

That word—disciplined—does a great deal of work.

There is a difference between postponement and surveillance.

Postponement says: not now.

Surveillance says: not now, because of these reasons; we will watch these indicators; we will reassess at this time; and these changes will cause us to act.

The first can be avoidance.

The second can be strategy.

This gives us a practical test for institutional inaction.

If an organization decides not to act, what information would make it change its mind?

When will the decision be revisited?

Who owns the revisit?

What is being monitored?

What becomes harder or more expensive if action is delayed?

Can the delay be reversed?

If there are no answers, “wait for more evidence” can become a respectable way to never decide.

Evidence gathering has diminishing returns.

At first, another study can materially narrow uncertainty.

Later, another study may simply postpone accountability.

The demand for certainty can be infinite because certainty is usually unavailable.

This is especially dangerous when one option loses value over time.

Suppose a town must decide whether to buy land for a future reservoir.

The town can wait for better population forecasts.

But the land may be developed in the meantime.

The value of information gained by waiting must be compared with the value of the option that may disappear.

That is not an argument to buy every parcel that might someday be useful.

It is an argument to include option loss in the decision.

Delay has a price when choices expire.

We tend to notice irreversible actions.

We should also notice irreversible inaction.

A species not protected before extinction cannot be restored by a later budget increase.

A historic building allowed to collapse cannot be maintained next year.

A child denied an educational intervention at age six cannot literally receive the same year of childhood at age twelve.

A company that waits too long to patch a known vulnerability may not get to choose the timing of the incident.

Some doors close quietly.

This connects the cost of inaction to the problem of the future.

The present has a built-in procedural advantage.

Existing arrangements need no vote to continue.

Existing infrastructure needs no new appropriation merely to remain in use, at least until it fails.

Existing rules can persist without anyone being able to articulate why they were chosen.

New proposals must justify themselves.

The old arrangement often escapes the same burden.

This is status quo bias embedded in procedure.

A new chemical regulation may need extensive analysis.

The old exposure level may continue by default.

A proposed transit line must defend every projected passenger.

The existing highway network is treated as a fact of geography rather than the accumulated result of earlier political choices.

A new benefit must be scored as spending.

An old tax preference may be experienced as property.

The asymmetry does not prove that change is better.

It means the default deserves examination too.

One useful question is simple: if we were designing the system from scratch today, would we choose the current arrangement?

If the answer is yes, the status quo has earned something.

If the answer is no, persistence needs an explanation beyond inertia.

But even that question can mislead because starting from scratch is imaginary.

Transitions have costs.

People have built lives around existing rules.

Assets were purchased under old expectations.

Institutions learned how to operate within the current system.

The current state may be suboptimal and still expensive to change.

So the correct test is not whether we would choose the status quo in a blank world.

It is whether changing from here produces enough benefit to justify transition.

That returns us to measurement.

The cost of doing nothing is not a rhetorical device for inflating the case for action.

It is a missing column.

A serious comparison should include at least four things.

What happens if we act?

What happens if we do not?

How uncertain are both forecasts?

How does waiting change the menu of future choices?

The fourth question is often neglected.

Two policies can have similar expected outcomes next year while leaving very different futures available afterward.

A temporary pilot may preserve options.

A permanent commitment may not.

A delay may preserve information but lose time.

A small preventive investment may buy the right to make a larger decision later.

A large irreversible intervention may eliminate risks while creating new dependencies.

This is why the argument about inaction leads naturally to reversibility.

Before we get there, we need to clear away another moral distortion: omission feels different from commission.

If I push a button and harm follows, my role is vivid.

If I refuse to push a button and the same harm follows, responsibility can feel softer.

Law and ethics sometimes have good reasons to distinguish acts from omissions. Duties differ. Causation differs. We cannot hold every person responsible for every preventable bad outcome in the world.

But institutions can exploit the intuition.

A known failing process continues because nobody authorized a change.

A dangerous backlog grows because each annual budget funds something more visible.

A discriminatory rule stays in place because changing it would be “political.”

The organization tells itself it did not choose the resulting harms.

It inherited them.

Inheritance can explain responsibility.

It does not erase responsibility forever.

At some point, continued use becomes a new decision.

This is one reason review dates matter.

A policy adopted in 1995 may have been reasonable in 1995.

If nobody reevaluates it for thirty years, the organization is not merely respecting the past. It is delegating present judgment to people who never saw the present.

Sunset clauses, periodic reviews, maintenance schedules and trigger thresholds are all ways of refusing to let inaction disguise itself as neutrality.

They force the default back into the room.

Still, there is danger in making action morally glamorous.

Organizations often prefer visible intervention because it demonstrates energy.

Leaders launch programs.

Doctors order tests.

Managers reorganize.

Governments announce initiatives.

A visible response can satisfy the demand that something be done even when the something is poorly targeted.

Action bias is real too.

If a problem is frightening enough, restraint can look like indifference.

That emotional pressure can produce overtreatment, overregulation and theatrical policy.

The prostate cancer example is useful because it breaks the simple story.

A patient with a frightening diagnosis may understandably want the cancer removed immediately.

Yet the disease can be slow-growing, treatment can cause lasting harm, and careful monitoring can sometimes be the better path.

The lesson is not that waiting is safer.

It is that the word “waiting” tells us almost nothing.

We need to know the process underneath it.

Good inaction has structure.

It has a reason.

It has boundaries.

It has monitoring.

It has a clock.

It has triggers.

It preserves the ability to act when evidence changes.

Bad inaction has euphemisms.

“We’re studying the issue.”

“We need stakeholder alignment.”

“We’re monitoring developments.”

“We don’t want to move prematurely.”

Any of these can be true.

They can also be organizational chloroform.

A decision goes to sleep while the underlying system keeps moving.

The moral discipline is to price delay without pretending the price can always be reduced to dollars.

A deferred repair may become a replacement cost.

A delayed health intervention may become disease burden.

A delayed regulation may mean exposure.

A delayed technology decision may mean lost capability.

A delayed apology may mean trust that does not return.

A delayed child-protection decision may mean harm no later intervention can undo.

Some of these can be modeled.

Some can only be described carefully.

All should be placed in the comparison.

And then there is the hardest case: when the costs of action and inaction fall on different people.

A utility pays to replace lead lines.

Households gain reduced exposure.

Ratepayers or taxpayers fund the work.

Construction disrupts streets.

Future residents inherit safer infrastructure.

Workers face project risks.

Suppliers gain contracts.

A single number can summarize some of this.

It cannot decide the distributional question.

“Doing nothing costs more” is not enough if the people who pay for acting are not the people who gain, or if the burden falls unfairly.

The same is true in reverse.

A policy with positive aggregate benefits can impose severe concentrated losses.

The cost of inaction belongs in the ledger.

It does not get to own the ledger.

This is the pattern of the book again.

A corrective principle becomes dangerous when promoted to sovereignty.

Metrics matter, but not alone.

Distribution matters, but not alone.

Rights matter, but not alone.

Legitimacy matters, but not alone.

The cost of doing nothing matters because omission is too often treated as zero.

The correction is not to treat omission as infinite.

It is to make the baseline visible.

Before deciding, ask what the world does while you wait.

Ask what deteriorates.

Ask what heals on its own.

Ask what information arrives.

Ask what opportunities disappear.

Ask what costs migrate to someone else.

Ask whether waiting is monitored or merely comfortable.

Ask when you will look again.

Then compare action not with an empty box labeled “nothing,” but with the actual future that inaction is likely to produce.

Sometimes that comparison will justify urgency.

Sometimes it will justify patience.

The important thing is that patience has finally been made to show its work.
