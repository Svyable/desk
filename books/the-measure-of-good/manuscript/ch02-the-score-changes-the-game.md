# The Score Changes the Game

The moment a measure acquires consequences, it stops merely observing behavior.

It begins changing it.

A sales number becomes a quota. A test becomes a gateway. A customer rating affects a worker's schedule. A response-time target determines a manager's bonus. A fitness streak decides whether someone feels disciplined before breakfast.

The number may have been introduced to describe reality more clearly.

Now reality has a reason to impress the number.

That is a different relationship.

Suppose a company wants its customer-service team to solve problems quickly. It begins measuring the average length of calls.

At first, the measure is diagnostic. Managers look at the number and ask questions. Why is one queue slower? Are employees missing information? Is a product generating unusual confusion? Does a particular problem need a better tool?

The measure is useful because it points back toward the work.

Then the company sets a target.

Calls should be shorter.

Perhaps there is good reason. Customers dislike waiting. Labor is expensive. A queue that never clears is evidence of something broken.

But the target changes the employee's environment.

A long call is no longer merely a long call. It is now a small failure recorded against the person taking it.

The employee has learned something important about the institution.

Whatever the posters say about service, one thing definitely counts.

Minutes.

If the fastest way to reduce minutes is also the best way to help the customer, the system works beautifully.

Often the interests overlap.

Sometimes they do not.

A difficult customer may need patience. A confusing bill may require explanation. A problem may be solved properly in twelve minutes and made somebody else's problem in four.

At the moment those paths diverge, the metric reveals its power.

The employee is not choosing between measurement and no measurement. The employee is choosing between two realities: the customer's experience and the institution's representation of it.

If the representation controls pay, promotion, status or survival, it would be remarkable if nobody learned to serve the representation.

We often call this gaming.

The word is useful, but it can make the problem sound more sinister than it needs to be.

Some gaming is dishonest. People hide failures, manipulate reports, move inconvenient cases out of view or fabricate success.

But much of it is simply adaptation.

People do more of what is rewarded.

They do less of what is punished.

They spend attention where the score can see them.

They discover the edges of the rule.

They learn which kinds of excellence are legible and which kinds disappear.

This is not evidence that human beings are unusually corrupt.

It is evidence that human beings notice incentives.

A well-designed measure uses that fact.

If a factory pays for fewer defects and workers genuinely reduce defects, the metric has helped. If a delivery service rewards safe driving and drivers become safer, good. If a student studies harder because an examination matters, the examination may have done exactly what it was meant to do.

The problem is not that targets influence behavior.

That is why targets exist.

The problem is that the influence is usually wider than the intention.

A target does not say, "Improve the underlying thing in exactly the way we hoped."

It says, "Move this observable result."

Reality supplies the methods.

Some methods improve the thing we care about.

Some improve only the appearance.

Most lie somewhere between.

This is why measurement becomes harder after it succeeds.

Before a metric matters, it can tell you something about the world.

After it matters, it tells you something about the world plus something about how people have adapted to the metric.

Those two effects become difficult to separate.

Imagine a school that is judged heavily on examination results.

Teachers now have an excellent reason to help students perform well on the examination.

That may mean teaching the material better.

It may mean spending more time on the kinds of questions most likely to appear.

It may mean reducing time spent on material that is valuable but untested.

It may mean identifying students near an important threshold and giving them extra attention because moving those students changes the school's reported result more than helping students far above or far below it.

None of these choices requires a dishonest teacher.

They require a finite day.

Attention has to go somewhere.

A metric helps decide where.

This is one of the quiet powers of a target: it creates a curriculum even when nobody calls it one.

The scored work becomes urgent.

The unscored work has to defend itself.

You can see the same pattern almost anywhere an institution must turn a complicated purpose into a manageable signal.

A hospital wants patients treated promptly, so waiting time matters.

A university wants serious scholarship, so publications matter.

A police department wants public safety, so recorded crime matters.

A company wants loyal customers, so satisfaction scores matter.

A government wants prosperity, so economic output matters.

Each measure illuminates something.

Each also creates a surface on which performance can be displayed.

Once reputations and resources attach to that surface, people begin polishing it.

Sometimes the polishing improves the underlying object.

Sometimes it wears the object away.

The distinction can be hard to notice from far above.

This is because numbers travel upward more easily than explanations.

A local manager may know that a target was reached only because three unusual cases were deferred until Monday. A teacher may know that scores rose because the class spent months practicing the exam format. A nurse may know that an apparently efficient process creates extra work somewhere else in the hospital.

By the time the information reaches the top of an organization, the context has often become a footnote.

The number remains.

Green.

On target.

Improved.

This creates a dangerous asymmetry.

Good news is easy to compress.

Bad news often requires a story.

"The target was met" fits in a cell.

"The target was met, but the result may be misleading because people changed how they classify difficult cases" takes a paragraph and threatens somebody's success.

Organizations that love measurement therefore need an unusual virtue: they must make room for information that makes their measurements look worse.

Otherwise the reporting system gradually becomes a public-relations system aimed inward.

The dashboard tells the institution what it wants to hear.

The institution congratulates itself for being data-driven.

There is a second problem with targets, and it begins at the boundary.

Human reality is usually continuous.

Administrative reality prefers categories.

Pass or fail.

Eligible or ineligible.

High risk or low risk.

On time or late.

Meets expectations or does not.

Categories make decisions possible. A scholarship cannot be awarded to 43.7 percent of a person. A train is either counted as on time under a rule or it is not. A regulator eventually has to decide whether a requirement has been satisfied.

But the boundary can acquire more meaning than the underlying difference deserves.

A person just above a cutoff may be treated as fundamentally different from a person just below it even when their actual circumstances are nearly identical.

And once people know the cutoff, effort gathers around it.

A manager who needs eighty percent compliance has a reason to move the organization from seventy-nine to eighty before worrying about the distance between eighty and excellence.

A student who needs a passing mark has a reason to study what converts failure into passage before studying what deepens understanding.

A business facing a regulatory threshold has a reason to organize itself around the line.

This is not irrational.

The threshold changed the value of the next unit of effort.

The world may be smooth.

The rule is not.

Whenever we create a bright line, we should expect behavior to collect around it.

Sometimes that is exactly what we want. Minimum safety standards exist because below some point we are unwilling to accept the risk. Eligibility rules exist because resources are limited and decisions have to be made.

The mistake is forgetting that the line was a decision rather than a discovery.

There may be no natural cliff in reality where the policy draws one.

This matters morally because thresholds can hide responsibility in the same way metrics do.

"The score was below the cutoff" sounds like an explanation.

Often it is only the beginning of one.

Why that score?

Why that cutoff?

How uncertain is the measurement?

What happens to people near the boundary?

Who gets to appeal?

What kinds of error are more damaging?

A system can be perfectly consistent and still be badly designed.

Consistency answers the question, "Did we apply the rule the same way?"

It does not answer, "Was this a good rule?"

The attraction of consistency is understandable.

Human judgment varies. It can be biased, tired, arbitrary and corrupt. Rules protect people from moods and favoritism. A clear standard can be an enormous moral improvement over a powerful person simply deciding whom he likes.

But rules solve one problem by creating another.

They replace the risk of inconsistent judgment with the risk of consistently ignoring what the rule cannot see.

Good systems need both discipline and escape routes.

A rule should usually apply.

An exception should usually require a reason.

And the existence of exceptions should not be treated as proof that the rule has failed.

Sometimes an exception is the mechanism by which a rule remains connected to its purpose.

That requires judgment again.

Judgment keeps returning because the world refuses to stay compressed.

This is frustrating for anyone hoping that enough measurement will make management automatic.

It is even more frustrating now that software can measure more behavior than institutions once imagined possible.

Work can be logged, timed, ranked and compared. Messages can be counted. Tasks can be closed. Keystrokes can be recorded. Customers can rate interactions immediately. Vehicles can report speed and location. Students can produce streams of performance data. Individuals can watch sleep, movement, heart rate, spending and attention with a precision once reserved for laboratories.

More visibility can be useful.

It can reveal patterns that memory misses. It can catch neglect. It can make unfair differences harder to deny. It can show whether an intervention actually works.

But visibility also changes the object being watched.

A worker who knows every visible action is counted may become more visibly active.

A driver who knows speed is monitored may slow down where monitoring occurs.

A student who knows an online system records completion may become excellent at completing.

A manager whose team is ranked on responsiveness may answer quickly and think later.

The measured person begins to develop a second audience.

There is the task itself.

And there is the system watching the task.

When those audiences want the same thing, measurement disappears into good practice.

When they want different things, performance begins.

That performance is not always fake.

People behave differently when observed because observation is part of the environment. A chef cooks differently when a health inspector is present. A speaker talks differently when a microphone is on. A teenager drives differently when a parent is in the passenger seat.

Sometimes this is the entire point.

We want inspection to change behavior.

We want accountability to matter.

The question is what behavior the observation selects for.

Does it make the work better, or merely more defensible?

Does it make people safer, or better at avoiding a recorded violation?

Does it make employees more useful, or more skilled at producing evidence of usefulness?

Those questions become especially important when the people being measured understand the system better than the people reading the reports.

The front line always learns the metric's peculiarities first.

A worker discovers which actions count twice.

A teacher discovers which material moves the score fastest.

A salesperson discovers which customers can be persuaded into a purchase they are likely to regret later.

A manager discovers which problems can be moved into next quarter.

An athlete discovers the exact edge of the rule.

The people closest to a metric become experts in its blind spots.

An institution can respond to this in two ways.

It can treat every workaround as disobedience and add more rules.

Or it can treat workarounds as information.

The second approach is harder but more useful.

If sensible people repeatedly find ways to satisfy a measure without satisfying its purpose, the measure is telling you something about itself.

The gaming is data.

It reveals the gap between the proxy and the goal.

This does not excuse fraud. Lying about a result is still lying.

But an organization that blames every distorted outcome on the moral weakness of the people below may avoid learning why the distortion was predictable.

If you pay for quantity, quantity will rise.

If quality is harder to see, some of it may disappear.

If you punish reported mistakes harshly, reported mistakes may fall faster than actual mistakes.

If promotions depend on projects appearing successful, bad news will develop a long journey upward.

No motivational speech can repeal these pressures.

Culture matters partly because culture changes which pressures people feel able to resist.

A strong culture can tell an employee, "Yes, the target matters, but not enough to lie to a customer."

It can tell a doctor, "Yes, throughput matters, but not enough to rush a dangerous case."

It can tell a researcher, "Yes, publication matters, but not enough to pretend the evidence is stronger than it is."

It can tell a manager, "Yes, your numbers look worse because you surfaced a problem. Thank you for surfacing it."

Those sentences sound obvious.

Their credibility depends on what happens next.

Values become real at the moment they cost something.

If the employee who protects the customer misses the target and is punished, the metric wins.

If the manager who reports the problem loses status while the manager who conceals it advances, the organization has explained its ethics more clearly than any code of conduct could.

People watch consequences.

They learn what the institution actually means by good.

This is why measurement and morality cannot be separated cleanly.

A metric is never only a description once it is attached to power.

It becomes an instruction about attention.

It tells people what deserves to be noticed.

It tells them what can safely be ignored.

It tells them which failures are embarrassing and which are invisible.

It tells them whose experience will become a number and whose will remain anecdotal.

That is moral territory whether the spreadsheet admits it or not.

The same pattern appears in private life, although the stakes are usually smaller.

A person begins tracking steps because movement is good for health.

The count helps. It interrupts a sedentary day. It makes progress visible. It turns an intention into a habit.

Then a strange evening arrives.

The person is tired. It is late. The body would benefit from sleep.

The device says the streak is about to break.

So the person walks circles around the kitchen.

This is funny because the distortion is small enough to see clearly.

The original goal was health.

The measure was steps.

The steps became a target.

The target acquired a life of its own.

The same thing can happen with reading streaks, savings targets, calorie counts, inboxes, workout scores and sleep trackers.

The tools can be genuinely helpful.

But any personal metric can become a tiny bureaucracy if maintaining the record begins to matter more than the life the record was meant to improve.

Sleep measurement provides an almost comic version of the problem.

A person wants to sleep better.

A device produces a score.

The score supplies useful clues.

Then the person wakes feeling rested, sees a disappointing number and begins the day feeling that sleep went badly.

Or lies awake worrying that lying awake will damage the score.

The measurement has entered the phenomenon it was supposed to observe.

This is not an argument against tracking.

It is an argument for keeping the hierarchy visible.

The life is the thing.

The measure is evidence about the thing.

When the evidence begins making the thing worse, it has exceeded its authority.

Institutions need the same discipline, although the design is harder because thousands or millions of people may depend on the result.

A robust measurement system does not merely ask, "What number should we use?"

It asks what behavior the number will create.

What will people do to improve it?

What valuable work might become less attractive because it is not counted?

What happens near the threshold?

Who knows how the measure can be gamed?

Can bad news still travel upward?

Can someone challenge a result that is technically correct but substantively absurd?

What other evidence would tell us that the score and reality are drifting apart?

These are not reasons to avoid targets.

They are part of designing one.

A good measure should remain exposed to contradiction.

If customer satisfaction rises while complaints become more desperate, investigate.

If productivity rises while experienced employees say the work is becoming impossible, investigate.

If a school improves on paper while teachers insist that students understand less, investigate.

The complaints may be wrong.

The metric may be right.

But the disagreement is valuable because it is exactly where the representation meets the world.

Bad systems defend the metric.

Good systems investigate the gap.

This requires a kind of institutional humility that is easy to praise and difficult to reward.

The whole purpose of measurement is to replace some uncertainty with evidence. It feels perverse to build a system and then remain suspicious of its outputs.

But mature measurement includes suspicion.

Not cynicism.

Suspicion.

The willingness to ask whether a number is still telling us what it once told us after everyone has learned how important the number is.

Because people learn.

Markets learn.

Organizations learn.

Students learn.

Algorithms learn.

A measure that worked in a world where nobody cared about it may fail in a world organized around pleasing it.

The score changes the game.

Then the game changes the meaning of the score.

That feedback loop is not an exception to measurement.

It is one of the central facts we have to measure around.

The practical lesson is not to search for a perfect metric that nobody can manipulate.

Such a metric would need to contain the entire purpose of the institution and every relevant feature of reality. At that point it would no longer be a metric. It would be the world itself.

We use measures because compression is necessary.

The discipline is remembering that compression loses information.

When consequences attach to the compressed version, the lost information becomes a place where behavior can hide.

So keep the score.

Use it.

Reward it when it deserves reward.

But keep somebody looking past it.

Keep a route for context to travel upward.

Keep room for an exception that can be explained rather than concealed.

Keep another measure capable of disagreeing with the first.

Keep contact with the people whose lives have been compressed into the number.

And when the score says everything is improving while the world underneath it feels worse, do not congratulate the score.

Go look at the world.
