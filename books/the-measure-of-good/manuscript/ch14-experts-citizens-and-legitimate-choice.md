# Experts, Citizens, and Legitimate Choice

In April 2017, a roomful of people in Ireland voted on abortion law.

They were not judges.

They were not legislators.

They were not a panel of obstetricians, constitutional lawyers or moral philosophers.

They were members of a Citizens’ Assembly: ordinary people selected to resemble the wider population, brought together to hear evidence, question experts and advocates, deliberate, and make recommendations.

The issue before them had occupied Irish politics for decades.

It involved medicine, constitutional law, religion, bodily autonomy, fetal life, family experience, institutional history and competing ideas of moral duty. Expertise mattered everywhere. Medical claims could be true or false. Legal consequences could be understood well or badly. Evidence about travel for abortion, maternal health and clinical practice could be checked.

But no specialist credential could answer the final question by itself.

What should the law permit?

The Assembly eventually recommended substantial change. A referendum followed in May 2018. More than two thirds of those voting approved repeal of the constitutional provision that had sharply constrained abortion law, and legislation followed.

Whatever one thinks of the outcome, the sequence is useful because it separates several kinds of authority that are often collapsed into one another.

Experts supplied knowledge.

Citizens deliberated about values and tradeoffs.

Elected institutions decided what proposal would go forward.

The electorate supplied constitutional authorization.

None of these steps made the others unnecessary.

That is the problem of legitimacy.

When a decision contains both factual questions and value judgments, who gets to decide what counts as good?

The easiest answer is the expert.

Experts know more.

Often they do.

A structural engineer knows more about load paths than a neighborhood meeting. An epidemiologist knows more about disease transmission than a talk-radio caller. A central banker understands the plumbing of monetary policy better than most voters. An oncologist knows more about treatment evidence than a patient encountering a diagnosis for the first time.

Ignoring expertise can be reckless.

Reality does not become democratic merely because everyone has an opinion about it.

A bridge either carries the load or it does not.

A drug either produces certain effects at certain rates under certain conditions or it does not.

A budget constraint remains a constraint even if the room dislikes arithmetic.

The first duty of legitimate decision-making is therefore epistemic humility: admit that some questions have better and worse answers, and that people who have spent years studying them may know things the rest of us do not.

But expertise has a jurisdiction.

The engineer can tell us which bridge designs meet the safety standard and what each is likely to cost.

The engineer cannot, by engineering alone, decide whether the city should build a bridge there rather than a school somewhere else.

The epidemiologist can estimate how an intervention may alter transmission.

The epidemiologist cannot, by epidemiology alone, decide how much restriction of movement is acceptable for a given reduction in risk.

The economist can estimate the distributional effects of a tax.

The economist cannot discover, from the model, which distribution is just.

The oncologist can explain likely survival, side effects and uncertainty.

The oncologist cannot decide what tradeoff between longevity and burden is acceptable to this patient.

Expertise answers questions inside a frame.

Legitimacy partly concerns who gets to choose the frame.

This distinction sounds obvious until institutions become technical.

Then the frame hides.

A committee is asked to maximize cost-effectiveness.

A regulator is asked to minimize expected deaths.

A transportation agency is asked to reduce delay.

A school system is asked to raise achievement.

Once the objective is written into the assignment, expert analysis can become extraordinarily sophisticated. The analysts may do excellent work.

The political choice occurred one step earlier.

Why cost-effectiveness rather than need?

Why expected deaths rather than a broader measure of health or liberty?

Why travel time rather than access, emissions or neighborhood cohesion?

Why test performance rather than a wider account of education?

A technical process can be impeccable while the objective remains contestable.

This is the same problem we met with models.

The model does not choose its target.

Neither does the expert, at least not by virtue of expertise.

There is another easy answer: let the people decide.

Democracy exists because citizens are not merely objects of administration. They are members of the political community. Decisions bind them, tax them, regulate them, expose them to risk, grant and withhold rights, and shape the institutions their children inherit.

Consent matters.

So does equality.

A democracy cannot plausibly say that every adult counts as one person on election day and then treat most adults as unqualified to participate whenever an issue becomes difficult.

Yet public opinion also has a jurisdiction.

A majority can be mistaken about facts.

It can be poorly informed.

It can be manipulated.

It can be frightened.

It can demand mutually incompatible things.

It can support benefits while opposing the taxes that finance them.

It can reward immediate gains and ignore delayed costs.

It can become hostile to a minority.

It can confuse confidence with competence just as easily as an expert can.

Democracy does not turn a false statement into a true one.

Nor does the existence of a majority automatically make every outcome just.

If ninety percent of a town votes to deny the remaining ten percent equal legal protection, the size of the majority does not cure the wrong.

Legitimacy is not simple majoritarianism.

Now we have two dangerous simplifications.

Rule by experts treats knowledge as permission.

Rule by raw preference treats permission as knowledge.

A serious institution needs both knowledge and authorization, while also respecting constraints that neither experts nor majorities should be free to discard casually.

That sounds messy because it is.

Good government often consists of assigning different questions to different forms of judgment.

Consider a medical guideline.

Experts may be best positioned to evaluate clinical trials, adverse events and diagnostic evidence.

Patients may be best positioned to say which burdens, risks and outcomes matter most in lived experience.

Public bodies may have to decide how limited resources are allocated across treatments.

Courts or legislatures may define rights that constrain what the health system can deny.

The treating clinician and patient still have to decide what to do in an individual case.

No single actor possesses all relevant authority.

The mistake is to ask which actor should rule.

The better question is which actor should decide which part.

This is one reason independent institutions exist.

We do not hold a national referendum on the safe load of an aircraft wing.

We do not elect laboratory measurements.

We often delegate decisions to central banks, statistical agencies, courts, utility regulators, medical authorities and standards bodies because some functions benefit from continuity, specialization and insulation from immediate political pressure.

Delegation can improve judgment.

It can also create distance.

The further authority moves from ordinary political contest, the stronger the need to explain what has been delegated and what has not.

An independent agency may have authority to choose the technical means.

Its mandate should not silently expand into authority to choose society’s ends.

That boundary is rarely clean.

Suppose a regulator is told to set an acceptable level of pollution.

Science can estimate exposure and risk.

But “acceptable” is not a scientific unit.

Should the regulator aim for the lowest technically feasible risk?

A risk below one in a million?

A level justified by cost-benefit analysis?

A level that protects especially vulnerable populations?

A level that preserves a particular industry?

The science narrows the space.

It does not pick the point.

This is why claims that a policy simply “follows the science” can be misleading even when the science is excellent.

Evidence can tell us what is likely to happen under different choices.

It cannot erase the choice.

The same caution applies in the other direction.

Politicians sometimes invoke democratic legitimacy as permission to disregard inconvenient expertise.

“We were elected” answers a question about authority.

It does not answer a question about whether the bridge will stand.

A legitimate mandate does not repeal chemistry, accounting or probability.

The discipline runs both ways.

Experts should not smuggle values into facts.

Elected leaders should not smuggle facts into values by pretending evidence is optional.

The interesting institutional designs make that boundary visible.

The Irish Citizens’ Assembly did not ask randomly selected people to improvise constitutional law from memory.

Members heard evidence and arguments over repeated meetings. They could question specialists and advocates. They deliberated before voting. Their recommendation did not itself amend the Constitution. It entered a larger chain involving parliament and, ultimately, a referendum.

The process was not pure direct democracy.

It was not technocracy either.

It tried to create informed citizenship.

That distinction matters.

Many debates assume we must choose between experts and the public as they currently are.

Experts arrive informed.

Citizens arrive busy.

Then we compare the two and announce that one is competent and the other is not.

But institutions can change the conditions under which citizens judge.

Time can be provided.

Evidence can be organized.

Competing witnesses can be heard.

Questions can be answered.

Participants can deliberate with people unlike themselves.

A recommendation can be made after learning rather than before it.

The OECD has documented hundreds of representative deliberative processes used by public authorities, including citizens’ assemblies, juries and panels. Its guidance emphasizes a combination of representative selection, learning, deliberation and an explicit response from the public authority.

This is not evidence that citizens’ assemblies always produce wise outcomes.

It is evidence that “public opinion” need not mean an instantaneous poll.

A poll asks what people think now.

A deliberative process asks what a group thinks after being given some chance to understand the problem and hear one another.

Those are different measurements.

Both can be manipulated.

Both can fail.

The design questions are substantial.

Who selects the experts?

Which evidence is presented?

How are disputed claims framed?

Who facilitates?

How representative is the group?

Who cannot afford to participate?

Are recommendations advisory or binding?

What happens when elected leaders ignore them?

Who decides when an issue is suitable for deliberation?

A citizens’ assembly can become legitimacy theater just as easily as a dashboard can become measurement theater.

Invite ordinary people.

Give them a weekend.

Present a carefully curated set of facts.

Publish a photograph of diverse faces around a table.

Then proceed with the policy already chosen.

The form is participatory.

The authority remains elsewhere.

A credible process therefore needs consequence.

Not necessarily control.

Consequence.

The public authority should say in advance what it will do with the recommendation, and afterward explain what it accepted, rejected or modified and why.

The OECD’s guidance makes this response stage explicit.

That small design choice expresses a large principle: participation without an answer is consultation as decoration.

There is another problem.

Representation by random selection can produce a miniature public more easily than an open meeting can.

Open meetings often reward those with time, confidence, organization and unusually intense preferences.

A lottery can bring in quieter citizens.

But a small sample is still small.

Its members are not elected.

They cannot claim the same mandate as a legislature.

Their value may lie elsewhere: not in replacing elections, but in demonstrating what informed ordinary citizens can do with a difficult question.

Think of the assembly as an instrument.

Its output is evidence about considered public judgment.

That evidence can inform elected institutions and the wider electorate.

It should not automatically overrule them.

This brings us to one of the hardest cases: what if experts and citizens still disagree after everyone has had a fair hearing?

Suppose specialists overwhelmingly believe a policy will produce a net benefit, while a durable public majority rejects it.

Who wins?

There is no universal answer because the nature of the decision matters.

Some questions are properly delegated.

Others require democratic authorization.

Some are constrained by rights.

Some concern emergency powers where delay changes the risk.

Some concern highly local burdens imposed for broad social benefits.

Some concern the management of a system whose technical stability depends on insulation from daily politics.

Legitimacy is partly about matching the decision procedure to the decision.

A useful test is to separate four questions.

What is true?

What is possible?

What is permissible?

What is chosen?

Experts have their strongest claim over the first two, though even there disagreement and uncertainty remain.

Law and rights place boundaries around the third.

Democratic institutions have their strongest claim over the fourth.

Real decisions blur the categories, but the separation exposes hidden moves.

An expert says, “The evidence requires this policy.”

Does it?

Or does the evidence predict consequences that the expert believes justify the policy?

A politician says, “The voters rejected the evidence.”

Did they?

Or did voters understand the prediction and reject the tradeoff?

A court says, “This choice is unavailable to the majority.”

Is it protecting a right?

Or substituting its own policy preference?

The questions are uncomfortable precisely because no actor is entitled to unlimited deference.

This is where trust becomes practical rather than sentimental.

People often say institutions need more trust.

Trust cannot simply be requested.

It is partly produced by procedure.

Was the evidence visible?

Were uncertainties admitted?

Were conflicts of interest disclosed?

Could affected people speak?

Were dissenting experts heard?

Was the decision-maker clear about which judgments were factual and which were moral or political?

Was there an appeal?

Could the policy be revised if predictions failed?

Did the institution explain why some preferences were not followed?

A person may disagree with a decision and still regard the process as legitimate.

That is a crucial achievement.

Democracy cannot promise each person the outcome they prefer.

It can try to provide a reason to live under outcomes one opposed.

Legitimacy therefore has an informational side.

A decision that cannot be explained is harder to contest.

And it has a procedural side.

A decision made by the wrong authority can remain illegitimate even when the outcome happens to be good.

Imagine an unelected health official secretly reallocating a city’s education budget into clinics and, by luck or skill, producing a large improvement in wellbeing.

The outcome does not retroactively grant authority over the school budget.

Good consequences matter.

So does who was entitled to make the choice.

This is why benevolent technocracy is unstable as an ideal.

It asks citizens to accept being ruled well rather than participating in the definition of “well.”

Sometimes delegation is necessary.

Sometimes emergency authority is necessary.

Sometimes a specialized institution should resist popular pressure.

But these arrangements need a source of authorization, a bounded mandate and a path for correction.

Otherwise competence becomes sovereignty.

The opposite failure is plebiscitary innocence: the idea that putting a question to a vote cleanses the process.

A ballot can force a binary choice onto a multidimensional problem.

Campaigns can distort.

Minorities can lose repeatedly.

Complex implementation choices survive after the vote.

A majority may authorize an objective without having settled the means.

A referendum can answer one constitutional question and leave a hundred administrative ones behind.

The vote matters enormously.

It does not end judgment.

The Irish case illustrates this too.

The Citizens’ Assembly recommended.

A parliamentary committee examined the issue.

The legislature acted to put a constitutional amendment before voters.

The electorate authorized change.

Then legislators and health authorities had to write and implement law.

Legitimacy was distributed across stages.

So was expertise.

No stage was sufficient alone.

This layered structure is worth remembering because modern institutions often search for a single final metric.

Approval rating.

Expert consensus.

Vote share.

Cost-benefit ratio.

Judicial ruling.

Public consultation score.

Each can become a token that ends argument.

But legitimacy, like fairness, is plural.

A decision may be scientifically informed but procedurally opaque.

Democratically authorized but factually reckless.

Legally valid but impossible for affected people to contest.

Popular but abusive toward a minority.

Expert-led but outside the institution’s proper mandate.

Participatory but disconnected from consequence.

The measure of a good decision process therefore cannot be reduced to whether the right people were in the room.

It matters what they were entitled to decide.

It matters what they knew.

It matters whose knowledge was missing.

It matters who could challenge them.

It matters what constraints bound the choice.

It matters what happened after the decision met reality.

This may seem inefficient.

It is.

Legitimacy has costs.

Deliberation takes time.

Appeals slow systems.

Consultation can frustrate experts.

Public explanation consumes attention.

Elections introduce discontinuity.

Rights can block policies with attractive aggregate benefits.

Independent agencies can resist majorities.

Legislatures can delay decisions that specialists consider urgent.

There is no reason to romanticize any of this.

The friction is partly the point.

A system that can act instantly because nobody needs to be persuaded, consulted, protected or given reasons may be efficient in the same way a dictatorship is efficient at ending debate.

The relevant comparison is not speed against slowness.

It is the value of speed against the value of consent, error correction, minority protection, public learning and durable cooperation.

Sometimes speed should win.

An air-traffic controller does not convene a citizens’ jury during an emergency descent.

Sometimes deliberation should win.

A constitutional settlement should not be redesigned on the logic of emergency response.

The institution needs to know which situation it is in.

That may be the deepest connection between legitimacy and measurement.

Measurement tempts us to imagine that once enough facts are known, disagreement should disappear.

But some disagreements are not failures of information.

They concern who bears the risk.

Whose freedom is constrained.

What counts as a right.

How much inequality is tolerable.

Which future is worth paying for.

What obligations people owe strangers.

How much authority an institution should possess.

Better evidence can clarify these disagreements.

It can expose impossible promises.

It can narrow the range of defensible claims.

It can reveal that one side has misunderstood the consequences.

Then, sometimes, disagreement remains.

At that point the question is no longer how to calculate the answer.

It is how people who do not agree can still make a decision together.

A good society needs experts who can say, with precision, what they know and what they do not.

It needs citizens willing to learn before judging.

It needs elected officials willing to own value choices rather than hide behind technical advice.

It needs institutions willing to explain why authority belongs where it does.

And it needs procedures that make disagreement survivable.

The purpose of legitimacy is not to make every decision correct.

No process can do that.

It is to make power answerable while giving knowledge somewhere real to stand.
