# Intention, Outcome, Responsibility

Two people make the same bad decision.

They leave the same dinner after drinking more than they should. They drive home along similar roads. They are equally inattentive, equally confident and equally lucky for most of the journey.

One arrives home without incident.

The other reaches an intersection just as a cyclist enters it.

A second later, someone is injured.

The behavior before the intersection was nearly identical.

The moral world after it is not.

One driver wakes the next morning with nothing more than a story he may never tell. The other wakes into consequences that cannot be undone. There may be injury, fear, guilt, expense, legal process, damaged trust and a person whose life has been changed by someone else's decision.

If we judge only the decision, the two drivers look very similar.

If we judge only the outcome, they look radically different.

Neither view is enough.

This is one of the hardest problems in judging what is good because outcomes matter and luck matters at the same time.

We do not want a morality in which consequences are irrelevant. A person lying in a hospital bed is not an accounting error. Harm that actually occurred deserves a response different from harm that did not occur.

But we also resist the idea that chance should determine the entire moral verdict.

The driver who happened not to meet the cyclist was not wiser.

The empty intersection did not make the decision safer.

Luck prevented the harm from becoming visible.

That distinction matters far beyond dramatic accidents.

A manager approves a risky project.

The project succeeds.

Another manager approves an equally risky project under nearly identical conditions.

It fails.

A doctor chooses between two reasonable treatments.

One patient recovers.

Another experiences a rare complication.

A parent gives two teenagers the same freedom.

One encounters no serious trouble.

The other happens to be in the wrong place at the wrong time.

An investor makes a reckless bet and gets rich.

A careful investor suffers an unusual loss.

An engineer accepts a defect that appears harmless in one product and becomes catastrophic in another because of a chain of circumstances nobody predicted exactly.

Outcomes pull our attention because they are concrete.

Once something has happened, it becomes difficult to remember how uncertain the future looked beforehand.

The result begins to rewrite the decision.

A success acquires an aura of wisdom.

A failure acquires an aura of warning signs that suddenly seem obvious.

This is understandable.

The outcome contains information.

It tells us something about the world that was previously unknown.

The mistake is allowing that new information to travel backward in time and pretend it was available earlier.

Good judgment requires a way to keep several questions separate.

What did the person intend?

What could they reasonably have foreseen?

How did they make the decision with the information available?

What actually happened?

The answers can point in different directions.

Intention matters because human action is not merely physical motion.

If I knock a cup from a table while trying to catch a falling child, I have caused a spill.

If I knock the same cup from the table because I want to ruin your work, the movement may be identical.

The act is not.

Purpose changes what the behavior means.

This is obvious in extreme cases.

A surgeon cuts a patient with the intention of healing.

An attacker cuts a person with the intention of harming.

The physical description does not settle the moral description.

But intention becomes less reliable as an excuse when we move away from clear cases.

"I meant well" can become a sentence people use after refusing to notice what their actions were likely to do.

A manager may sincerely intend to improve performance while imposing targets that predictably encourage corner-cutting.

A parent may sincerely intend to protect a child while controlling every decision until the child has little room to develop judgment.

A government may sincerely intend to stop abuse while creating a process so burdensome that people with legitimate claims cannot use it.

Good intention matters.

It does not create immunity from evidence.

An intention is partly a statement about what result a person wanted.

Responsibility also asks what risks the person was willing to create while pursuing it.

That is where foreseeability enters.

We cannot be responsible in the same way for every consequence that can be traced back to us.

Almost any action produces an enormous chain of effects.

You recommend a restaurant.

Your friend takes a different route to get there.

The route delays them by ten minutes.

Because of the delay they meet someone they later marry.

Did you cause the marriage?

In a thin causal sense, perhaps your recommendation belongs somewhere in the chain.

In a useful moral sense, the answer is usually no.

Causation is too generous.

Responsibility needs a narrower boundary.

Foreseeability is one part of that boundary.

Could the person reasonably understand that the action created this kind of risk?

Not the exact sequence.

Not every detail.

The kind of risk.

A person who drives while impaired does not need to predict the cyclist, the intersection, the weather and the precise second of impact.

The relevant question is whether impaired driving creates a foreseeable risk of hurting someone.

A company that stores sensitive information does not need to predict the exact attacker who may one day steal it.

It does need to understand that poor security creates a foreseeable class of harms.

A hospital does not need to know which patient will fall.

It does need to know that certain conditions make falls more likely.

This distinction prevents an impossible standard.

If responsibility required exact prediction, nearly every avoidable harm could be excused as surprising in its details.

Reality is always more specific than foresight.

We usually foresee categories.

The world supplies particulars.

Foreseeability also protects us from the opposite error: blaming people for consequences that no reasonable decision process could have anticipated.

A person can act carefully and still encounter an event outside the range that should have guided the choice.

That does not make the harm unreal.

It does change the moral judgment of the decision.

There is a difference between tragedy and negligence.

The person harmed may experience no such distinction in the pain itself.

The distinction matters for how we assign blame, how we learn and what standard we expect next time.

This is why process deserves its own place.

A decision can be good even when the outcome is bad.

That sentence sounds wrong until we remember uncertainty.

Suppose a treatment has strong evidence in its favor and a small known risk of serious complications.

A doctor explains the tradeoff, checks the relevant conditions, considers alternatives and recommends the treatment for reasons that make sense given the evidence.

The patient suffers the rare complication.

The outcome is bad.

It does not follow that the decision was bad.

If every bad outcome proved a bad decision, rational risk-taking would become impossible.

No surgery could be justified unless success were guaranteed.

No bridge could be built unless failure were impossible.

No child could learn independence because danger could not be eliminated.

No entrepreneur could hire because the business might fail.

No public official could act under uncertainty.

Life does not offer that kind of contract.

The more useful question is whether the risk was understood, proportionate, honestly represented and taken for a defensible reason.

This is also why a good outcome cannot fully redeem a bad process.

A reckless decision can work.

A dishonest forecast can accidentally be right.

A company can ignore a safety warning and encounter no accident.

A trader can confuse leverage with skill for years.

A manager can mistreat people and still deliver a strong quarter.

Success may postpone the discovery of the error.

Sometimes it strengthens it.

The person learns the wrong lesson because reality failed to punish the mistake immediately.

Luck becomes evidence of competence.

That is one of the most dangerous forms of measurement error.

The outcome is real.

The interpretation is false.

Near misses are useful because they expose this difference before catastrophe does.

Imagine a factory in which a heavy object falls from a shelf after workers have complained that the storage system is unstable.

It hits the floor beside someone.

Nobody is injured.

If the organization records only injuries, the event may look like zero.

Zero lost-time incidents.

Zero medical claims.

Zero fatalities.

The measure says nothing happened.

But something important happened.

The system revealed a path to harm.

The near miss contains information about risk without the tragedy required to make the risk undeniable.

A mature institution learns from the empty space where the person happened not to be.

This is difficult because organizations are rewarded for outcomes.

The quarter ended safely.

The project shipped.

The patient recovered.

The forecast was met.

There is little emotional energy behind preventing the disaster that did not happen.

Yet prevention lives almost entirely in that invisible category.

Good judgment has to notice the difference between evidence that a system is safe and evidence that it has been lucky.

The reverse problem appears after a disaster.

Once harm occurs, people search for the moment when it could have been prevented.

This search is necessary.

It is also vulnerable to hindsight.

A warning that looked ambiguous beforehand can look prophetic afterward.

A small anomaly becomes the clue everyone should have understood.

A dissenting voice becomes the person who "knew."

Sometimes that reconstruction is accurate.

Warnings were ignored.

Evidence was suppressed.

People chose convenience over a risk they understood.

But sometimes the signal was one weak piece of information among hundreds of weak pieces.

The fact that it later became important does not prove it was clearly important at the time.

Learning requires resisting both comforts.

We should not excuse every failure as unpredictable.

We should not pretend every failure was predictable because it happened.

The relevant standard is what a careful person or institution could have known before the outcome resolved the uncertainty.

That standard is uncomfortable because it rarely produces the emotional clarity of hindsight.

Responsibility also depends on control.

A person cannot meaningfully be responsible for everything that happens near them.

Authority creates a different relationship.

If you have the power to decide, approve, supervise, maintain, warn, stop or correct, you inherit obligations that bystanders do not have in the same form.

A manager may not personally make the defective part.

The manager may still be responsible for the system that repeatedly rewards speed over quality.

An executive may not write the misleading advertisement.

The executive may still have approved the incentives and review process that made deception predictable.

A public agency may not cause every individual mistake.

It may still be responsible for designing a process in which errors cannot be appealed.

This kind of responsibility is easy to lose in large organizations.

Work is divided because division is useful.

One person sets the target.

Another builds the model.

Another applies the rule.

Another handles the customer.

Another reports the aggregate.

Each person can say, accurately, that they controlled only a small part.

The harm can emerge from the combination.

Nobody intended the whole.

Everybody contributed a piece.

If responsibility follows only the final hand that touched the decision, systems become morally invisible.

That cannot be right.

But the opposite cannot be right either.

We cannot assign full responsibility to everyone who participated in a chain merely because their work was necessary.

The analyst who calculated a forecast does not automatically own every decision made with it.

The employee who followed a lawful ordinary procedure may not possess the authority to redesign the institution.

The junior engineer who raises a concern and is overruled stands differently from the executive who suppresses it.

Control, knowledge and authority matter.

Responsibility should follow them more closely than job titles or proximity to the harm.

This is one reason documentation can be morally important rather than merely bureaucratic.

Who knew what?

When?

What was raised?

What was decided?

What alternatives were available?

Who had authority to stop the action?

What uncertainty remained?

A record cannot answer the moral question by itself.

It protects the question from being rewritten later.

Without a record, successful people tend to remember their confidence as justified.

Unsuccessful people remember their warnings as clearer than they were.

Organizations remember consensus that did not exist and forget objections that were inconvenient.

Memory is not neutral after the outcome.

Documentation gives the earlier uncertainty a chance to survive.

There is another distinction that matters: blame and responsibility are not identical.

Suppose I accidentally damage something you own despite taking reasonable care.

I may have little reason to blame myself morally.

You still have a damaged thing.

The question of who should repair or replace it does not disappear because my intention was innocent.

A person can owe a response without deserving condemnation.

This becomes more important as consequences grow.

When an institution causes harm through a process that was reasonable at the time, it may still owe compensation, explanation, support or reform.

The victim should not have to absorb every cost simply because nobody was wicked.

Fault is one way of assigning burdens.

It is not the only way.

Sometimes responsibility comes from having created the risk.

Sometimes from having benefited from the activity.

Sometimes from having the capacity to repair the damage.

Sometimes from occupying a role that carries obligations even when no individual acted badly.

This does not mean every unfortunate outcome creates unlimited liability.

That would turn responsibility into a general tax on being involved in the world.

It means moral judgment has more than one purpose.

Blame asks what the actor deserves.

Repair asks what the harmed person needs and who should reasonably provide it.

Prevention asks what should change before the next case.

Learning asks whether the decision process was sound.

Those questions can produce different answers from the same event.

We often get into trouble by forcing them into one verdict.

"Guilty" or "not guilty."

"Good decision" or "bad decision."

"Success" or "failure."

Real responsibility is often distributed across categories.

Consider a medication error.

A nurse gives the wrong dose because two packages look almost identical.

The patient is harmed.

One response is to focus entirely on the nurse.

The nurse administered the dose.

That fact matters.

But a serious investigation would want to know more.

Why were the packages so similar?

How were they stored?

Was the workload reasonable?

Did the software make the distinction clear?

Were there independent checks?

Had similar confusions occurred before?

Was the nurse trained?

Did the organization know about the risk?

The point is not to absolve individuals automatically.

It is to avoid stopping at the nearest person.

A system can make errors more likely.

A person can still make a mistake inside that system.

Both can be true.

The desire for a single responsible party is partly the same desire we have encountered throughout this book.

One number.

One score.

One cause.

One answer.

Complex systems often refuse.

Responsibility can be shared without being diluted to nothing.

Several people can each have a real but different obligation.

The engineer may owe candor.

The manager may owe escalation.

The executive may owe resources.

The regulator may owe enforcement.

The organization may owe compensation.

The profession may owe a better standard.

The public may owe enough funding for the standard to be possible.

None of these responsibilities becomes unreal because another also exists.

This is where intention returns in a different form.

Institutions do not have intentions exactly as people do, yet they behave as though they have priorities.

We can see those priorities in budgets, incentives, tolerated exceptions, promotion criteria, staffing, defaults and what happens after someone raises a problem.

An organization may say safety is its highest value.

If every promotion goes to people who deliver faster and nobody advances for preventing failures, the practical intention is more complicated.

A government may say every citizen deserves access.

If the process requires forms, travel, time and documentation that predictable groups cannot provide, declared intention and institutional behavior separate.

A company may say privacy matters.

If the default is to collect everything because the storage is cheap, the statement is not the whole policy.

For institutions, repeated design choices may tell us more than mission language.

This is another reason outcomes alone are insufficient.

An institution may produce acceptable results for years while carrying a structure that will fail under pressure.

We need to judge the arrangements that create outcomes, not merely the latest outcomes themselves.

Still, actual consequence must come back into the picture.

There is a temptation, once we understand moral luck, to become too impressed with process.

The decision was reasonable.

The risk was disclosed.

The procedure was followed.

Everyone acted in good faith.

Fine.

Someone is still hurt.

A world in which excellent process repeatedly produces terrible outcomes is not redeemed by the excellence of the process.

At some point, evidence from outcomes changes what should be believed.

One rare complication may be compatible with a sound treatment.

A pattern of complications is evidence that the treatment, the estimate or the conditions need another look.

One unexpected failure may be bad luck.

Repeated "unexpected" failures are a measurement problem.

The process is not independent of results forever.

It earns trust by surviving contact with them.

This is the balance.

Judge the decision using information that was available when it was made.

Then use the outcome to update what will count as reasonable next time.

Do not punish yesterday's decision for knowledge that arrived today.

Do not waste today's knowledge merely because yesterday's decision was defensible.

That is how responsibility becomes learning rather than theater.

The same distinction applies personally.

A person can make a choice in good faith and discover that it hurt someone.

Defensiveness says: I could not have known.

Self-condemnation says: I caused harm, therefore I was wrong to act.

Neither response is always useful.

The better question may be narrower.

What did I know?

What did I miss?

Was the miss reasonable?

What is visible now that was not visible before?

What do I owe because the harm is real?

What should I do differently next time?

A person can answer those questions without pretending to have been omniscient.

They can also answer them without using uncertainty as an alibi.

This matters because moral judgment is partly prospective.

We judge the past so that the future can improve.

Punishment, praise, compensation, apology, reform and memory all teach.

If praise follows lucky recklessness, people learn recklessness.

If punishment follows every bad outcome regardless of process, people learn to hide risk, avoid difficult decisions or choose only actions whose failures will be invisible.

If organizations blame the nearest individual for systemic problems, people learn not to report mistakes.

If good intentions excuse predictable harm, people learn that sincerity is enough.

If no distinction is made between accident and malice, moral language loses resolution.

A useful system of responsibility should improve the quality of future choices.

That means it must be sensitive to more than consequence.

It must also remain sensitive to consequence.

The two drivers at the beginning of this chapter should not receive identical treatment in every respect.

One person hurt someone.

Repair, attention and perhaps punishment will necessarily differ because the world differs.

But we should be careful about the lesson drawn from the difference.

The driver who reached home safely was not vindicated.

The driver who caused harm did not become reckless only at the instant of impact.

The morally relevant risk existed before either knew how the journey would end.

A fair judgment notices both the shared decision and the different consequence.

It can condemn the risk in both cases.

It can require more from the person whose risk became harm.

It can resist pretending that the outcome alone reveals the quality of the mind that produced it.

That layered judgment is harder than a score.

It is also closer to what responsibility actually asks of us.

What did you mean to do?

What could you reasonably see?

What risk did you impose on other people?

What authority did you have?

How carefully did you decide?

What happened?

What do you owe now?

And what should change because you know more than you did before?

Measurement can help with almost every question.

It can estimate risk.

It can document outcomes.

It can compare failure rates.

It can reveal patterns invisible in individual stories.

It can test whether a reform worked.

It cannot collapse the questions into one number without choosing which kinds of responsibility matter most.

That choice becomes especially difficult when the outcome is life, death, pain or health.

Institutions cannot avoid making such comparisons.

Hospitals allocate limited resources.

Regulators decide how much safety is enough.

Insurers cover some treatments and not others.

Transport systems spend more on some risks than others.

Public budgets move money among interventions that change the length and quality of lives.

Sooner or later, someone asks a question that sounds indecent and is impossible to avoid.

How much is a life worth?

The next chapter begins there.
