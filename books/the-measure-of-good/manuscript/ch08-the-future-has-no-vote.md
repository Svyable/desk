# The Future Has No Vote

Every generation inherits a world it did not choose.

Roads already built.

Debts already issued.

Laws already written.

Rivers already polluted or protected.

Forests already cleared or left standing.

Institutions already trusted or distrusted.

Technologies already normalized.

Risks already accumulated.

The inheritance is not entirely a burden.

Most of what makes an ordinary life possible was created by people who are gone.

We live inside the consequences of decisions made by strangers.

Some of those strangers built systems that outlived them.

Some left problems they did not solve.

Some did both at once.

This creates a peculiar moral relationship.

The future depends on us.

We do not depend on the future in the same way.

People fifty years from now cannot vote in today's election.

They cannot attend today's planning meeting.

They cannot refuse a contract we write on their behalf.

They cannot sue us for choosing a cheaper material that fails after we are dead.

They cannot reward us for preserving something they later value.

They are affected parties with no seat at the table.

That asymmetry is easy to ignore because absence looks like silence.

Nobody objects.

Nobody protests.

Nobody demands compensation.

The decision therefore appears to concern only the people who are present.

But silence from the future is not consent.

It is simply the condition of not having arrived yet.

This matters because many decisions are distributed unevenly through time.

The benefit arrives now.

The cost arrives later.

A project creates jobs this year and maintenance obligations for decades.

A company records revenue now and leaves cleanup for whoever owns the site later.

A government borrows now and assigns repayment to future taxpayers.

A family spends now and leaves fewer options to the next generation.

A technology is adopted now and becomes difficult to reverse after infrastructure, habits and institutions reorganize around it.

The present enjoys a structural advantage in every one of these cases.

It is here.

It can count itself.

The future has to be imagined.

Measurement often amplifies the advantage.

Near-term effects are easier to observe.

They arrive inside budgets, quarterly reports, election cycles, project plans and human attention spans.

Long-term effects are estimated.

They depend on models, assumptions, uncertain probabilities and conditions that may change.

This means the less visible side of the tradeoff is also the less powerful side.

A benefit that arrives next month can be entered as a number.

A harm that may arrive in thirty years often enters as a scenario.

The number feels solid.

The scenario feels speculative.

Sometimes that difference is justified.

Uncertainty grows with distance.

A forecast about next week deserves more confidence than one about the same week fifty years from now.

But uncertainty has two possible moral effects, and we often choose only one.

It can make us less willing to spend resources on a predicted future harm because the prediction may be wrong.

It can also make us less willing to impose an irreversible risk because we may be wrong about our ability to repair it later.

The first response says: do not overreact to uncertain danger.

The second says: do not lock in uncertain danger.

Both can be reasonable.

Which one applies depends partly on what can be reversed.

Suppose a town is deciding whether to sell a piece of public land.

If the sale goes badly, money can perhaps buy another parcel later.

Now suppose the land contains the last local wetland of its kind.

If it is destroyed, no future town council can vote to restore the exact thing that was lost.

The two decisions may have the same present price.

They do not have the same temporal structure.

One spends money.

The other spends an option.

This is one of the most important differences between ordinary tradeoffs and intergenerational ones.

Future people do not merely inherit outcomes.

They inherit the remaining set of choices.

A good inheritance is not only a pile of assets.

It is room to maneuver.

That room can take many forms.

Low debt.

Healthy institutions.

Stable infrastructure.

Knowledge that has been preserved.

Ecosystems that still function.

Laws that can be changed.

Technologies that are not impossible to exit.

Physical systems with spare capacity.

Social trust that has not been exhausted.

The common feature is optionality.

The future can still decide.

This suggests a principle that is easy to state and difficult to apply.

When we do not know what future people will value, preserving their ability to choose can itself be valuable.

The difficulty is that preserving options is not free.

A city that refuses to develop land preserves possibilities but may worsen housing costs today.

A government that refuses to borrow preserves fiscal room but may leave useful infrastructure unbuilt.

A company that overinvests in flexibility may become too expensive to survive.

A family that saves every dollar for descendants may impoverish the life being lived now.

The future can be protected so aggressively that the present becomes merely a custodian.

That cannot be right either.

People alive today are not morally inferior to people who will live later.

A child who needs medicine now should not lose it because the money would earn interest for a hypothetical descendant.

A community facing unemployment now cannot be told that every sacrifice is justified by a distant possibility.

Intergenerational ethics becomes distorted when the future is treated as either weightless or infinitely valuable.

The question is how to count people who are not here without making people who are here disappear.

This is where discounting enters.

The basic idea is familiar from money.

A dollar today and a dollar decades from now are not treated as equivalent.

Money available now can be invested, consumed or used to avoid a cost.

Future money also arrives with uncertainty.

So financial calculations often reduce the present value of distant amounts.

That can be sensible for money.

It becomes more complicated when the thing being discounted is not money.

Suppose a policy produces one preventable injury next year.

Now suppose a different policy produces one otherwise identical preventable injury thirty years from now.

Should the later injury count less simply because it is later?

There are reasons the two situations may differ.

Future people may be richer.

Technology may reduce the severity of the harm.

The prediction may be less certain.

Resources committed today may have valuable alternative uses.

All of those can matter.

But notice that none says the future person's pain matters less because their calendar date is larger.

This distinction is easy to lose inside a model.

There is a difference between discounting future resources and discounting future moral standing.

The first can reflect opportunity cost, growth and uncertainty.

The second would mean that the same harm matters less merely because it happens to someone later.

Those are not the same claim.

A formula can combine them.

Judgment should separate them.

The separation becomes especially important when growth is assumed.

If future people are expected to be wealthier, perhaps they can bear some costs more easily.

That can justify leaving them fewer financial resources in some cases.

But wealth does not make every loss easier to bear.

A richer future person cannot buy an extinct species back from the past.

A technologically advanced society cannot undo every irreversible exposure.

A future city with higher income may still regret an urban design that made some choices impossible.

Money can compensate for many things.

It cannot compensate for everything.

We encountered this problem in Chapter 6.

Some goods are not ordinary entries in an exchange.

Time makes the problem sharper because the person who receives the compensation may not be the person who bears the loss.

Imagine a project that benefits the present generation and imposes a risk on people not yet born.

The project sponsors offer compensation.

To whom?

People alive now can be paid.

Future people cannot negotiate the amount.

A trust can be created for them.

Funds can be set aside.

Insurance can be purchased.

Those mechanisms may help.

But they do not answer the underlying question of authority.

What are we entitled to trade on behalf of people who cannot refuse?

Parents face a smaller version of this question constantly.

Children cannot consent to many decisions made for them.

Parents choose schools, medical care, neighborhoods, diets, routines and countless other conditions.

The absence of consent does not make the decisions illegitimate.

Someone has to decide.

The legitimacy comes from a different standard.

The decision-maker is supposed to act as a steward of interests that are not fully their own.

That idea scales imperfectly, but usefully.

Present institutions are not owners of the future.

They are temporary holders of powers whose consequences may outlast them.

Stewardship sounds gentle.

It is not always.

A steward still has to make tradeoffs.

Some resources should be used.

Some risks should be taken.

Some traditions should end.

Some old structures should be demolished rather than preserved merely because they survived.

The future does not need us to freeze the world in place.

It needs us to distinguish using an inheritance from consuming the conditions that make inheritance possible.

That distinction is difficult because almost every generation changes the world irreversibly.

A city cannot be both built and unbuilt.

A language evolves.

A technology spreads.

A legal precedent changes expectations.

A road alters development.

Children are born into one arrangement rather than another.

There is no policy of leaving the future untouched.

The present always reaches forward.

So the goal cannot be zero influence.

It has to be responsible influence.

One useful question is whether the future receives benefits alongside the constraints we impose.

A bridge may shape development for a century, but future people also use the bridge.

A scientific discovery changes society irreversibly, but it expands knowledge.

A public debt may require repayment, but if it financed durable infrastructure the future also inherits the asset.

The timing of burdens matters.

So does the timing of benefits.

This is why slogans about never burdening future generations are too simple.

Every durable institution is a burden in one sense.

It occupies attention, money, law and physical space.

It also makes action possible.

The relevant question is what is being passed forward.

An obligation without an asset?

A risk without a benefit?

A system that is expensive to maintain but impossible to abandon?

A productive capability?

A genuine option?

A liability concealed as growth?

An inheritance can contain all of these at once.

Debt illustrates the complexity.

Borrowing is often described as taking from the future.

Sometimes it is.

If borrowed money funds current consumption and leaves no durable benefit, future taxpayers may receive mostly the bill.

But borrowing can also move resources across time in the other direction.

A bridge built today may serve people for decades.

A school, water system or research investment can create benefits long after the original debt was issued.

In that case, requiring future beneficiaries to share the cost may be reasonable.

The moral issue is not debt alone.

It is the relationship between what is financed, who benefits, who pays, and how much flexibility remains afterward.

A balanced budget can be irresponsible.

A deficit can be responsible.

The ledger does not decide.

The same is true of maintenance.

Maintenance is one of the least glamorous ways to respect the future.

It creates little spectacle.

It often produces no new ribbon to cut.

Its success looks like nothing happened.

The pipe did not burst.

The bridge did not fail.

The archive was still readable.

The institution still knew how to do its job.

The forest still absorbed rain.

The electrical system still had margin.

Because the benefit is an avoided failure, maintenance is easy to postpone.

The present can consume reliability without recording the consumption.

A machine still works after one missed inspection.

A road still functions after one deferred repair.

An institution still appears competent after experienced staff leave.

The decline can be invisible for years.

Then the future receives a large bill for many small decisions that each looked harmless when taken alone.

This is temporal measurement failure.

The metric captured current output.

It did not capture the depletion of the thing producing the output.

A company can improve this quarter by delaying maintenance.

A government can improve this year's budget by postponing replacement.

A household can feel richer by ignoring a roof that is slowly failing.

The immediate measure improves because the future liability is not yet presenting itself as cash.

Nothing magical happened.

The cost moved through time.

The same structure appears in environmental decisions, infrastructure, health, technical debt and institutional capacity.

The labels change.

The moral pattern does not.

The present can borrow not only money from the future but condition.

Condition is harder to account for.

How much did we consume the reliability of a bridge?

The fertility of soil?

The resilience of an organization?

The credibility of an institution?

The attention of a population?

The ability of a system to absorb one more shock?

These quantities are often measurable only imperfectly.

That does not make them imaginary.

A balance sheet can omit an asset that is still being depleted.

The future receives the omission anyway.

Another complication is that future people may value things differently from us.

We know this because the past valued things differently from us.

Some buildings once considered ugly became treasured.

Some practices once considered normal became intolerable.

Some technologies once considered essential became obsolete.

Some resources once treated as abundant became scarce.

Some forms of scarcity disappeared.

Some new ones emerged.

We should therefore be cautious about claiming to know exactly what future people will want.

But uncertainty about their preferences does not justify giving them nothing.

It strengthens the case for preserving broad capabilities and options.

A future person may not share our taste.

They will still benefit from functioning institutions, knowledge, physical safety and the ability to make choices.

This is the temporal version of humility.

Do not assume the future will want our answer.

Try not to destroy its ability to answer for itself.

The principle becomes harder when future people may not exist under alternative choices.

Suppose a policy changes the economy, which changes where people live, whom they meet and which children are eventually born.

Decades later, the population may consist of different individuals from the population that would have existed under another policy.

Now it becomes strange to say a particular future person was harmed by the policy if, without the policy, that exact person would never have existed.

The philosophical problem is real.

Ordinary judgment does not have to solve every corner of it before acting.

We can still ask whether the future condition we are creating is one we have reason to regard as better or worse.

A child does not need to exist already for us to care whether children in general inherit poisoned water or functioning schools.

Moral concern does not require a name in advance.

This matters because institutions often count only identifiable claimants.

A person with an address can file a complaint.

A future population cannot.

A company can estimate current customer dissatisfaction.

People who will rely on the product years later are absent from the survey.

An election measures current voters.

Future citizens are not missing data in the ordinary sense.

They are outside the sampling frame.

That is a design choice imposed by time.

If future interests matter, institutions need ways to represent them indirectly.

There is no perfect method.

Long-term budgets can expose deferred costs.

Independent review can challenge short political horizons.

Maintenance schedules can make deterioration visible before failure.

Sunset clauses can prevent temporary measures from becoming permanent by inertia.

Environmental and safety standards can place limits on irreversible risks.

Trust funds can reserve resources for obligations that will mature later.

Scenario analysis can show how a choice performs under futures different from the one planners expect.

None of these gives future people a literal vote.

They create friction against spending the future accidentally.

Friction matters because the present already has momentum.

The benefits of a decision usually have advocates.

The project has a sponsor.

The budget line has an owner.

The profit has a recipient.

The convenience has a constituency.

The long-term cost may belong to everyone later and therefore to nobody now.

This is a familiar tragedy of representation.

Diffuse future losses compete against concentrated present gains.

The solution is not to assume the future always wins.

It is to make sure the future appears in the argument before the decision is made.

This requires discipline about horizons.

A one-year measure is not wrong because it covers one year.

It is wrong when a one-year measure is used to judge a decision whose consequences last fifty.

Time horizon is part of the metric.

A school evaluated over a week will look different from a school evaluated over a decade.

A medicine evaluated for immediate relief may look different when long-term effects are included.

A road judged by opening-day traffic may look different after development responds to it.

A business judged by quarterly profit may look different when customer trust, employee skill and maintenance are considered over years.

Every measurement window includes some consequences and excludes others.

The window is therefore a moral choice when the excluded consequences fall on different people.

This does not mean longer is always better.

A horizon can be so long that almost anything becomes speculative.

Immediate suffering can disappear inside grand projections.

People can justify present cruelty with distant promises.

History is full of plans that demanded sacrifice now for a future that never arrived.

Future orientation can become its own form of moral evasion.

The present person is real too.

This is why responsible long-term judgment needs both horizons at once.

What happens now?

What happens later?

Who receives each?

Which effects are reversible?

Which assumptions drive the distant forecast?

What obligations are being created?

What capacity is being consumed?

What capacity is being built?

What happens if the future is richer than expected?

What happens if it is poorer?

What happens if our preferred technology fails?

What happens if our values are not theirs?

These questions do not produce one number.

They improve the terms on which numbers are used.

The temptation is to search for a single discount rate, horizon or sustainability score that will settle the matter.

No universal setting can do that.

Different goods have different temporal properties.

Money compounds.

Concrete deteriorates.

Knowledge can spread without being consumed.

Trust can grow slowly and vanish quickly.

Species do not come back because interest rates fall.

Laws can be repealed, but institutional habits may persist.

Carbon in the atmosphere, toxic waste, public debt, software dependencies, cultural norms and scientific knowledge each travel through time differently.

A general moral method has to notice the difference.

The useful distinction is not simply present versus future.

It is reversible versus irreversible, renewable versus exhaustible, repairable versus unrecoverable, funded versus unfunded, known versus uncertain, and imposed versus chosen.

Those categories tell us more about temporal responsibility than calendar distance alone.

A harm thirty years away but easy to repair may deserve less precaution than a smaller harm ten years away that cannot be undone.

A debt due later may be reasonable if matched by an asset.

A small probability of irreversible catastrophe may deserve more attention than a larger probability of a reversible inconvenience.

This is where uncertainty from Chapter 5 meets constraint from Chapter 6 and fairness from Chapter 7.

The future is not a separate moral topic.

It is where all three become harder.

We do not know exactly what will happen.

Some things, once done, cannot be traded back.

And the people bearing the consequences are excluded from the procedure by definition.

A serious measure of good has to work under those conditions.

That means resisting two easy stories.

The first says future benefits will solve future problems.

Growth, technology or adaptation will make our burdens trivial.

Sometimes they will.

Sometimes they will not.

The story becomes dangerous when hope is treated as an asset already on the balance sheet.

The second story says every long-term risk justifies restraint today.

That can be equally dangerous.

Fear can prevent useful experimentation, infrastructure, medicine and growth.

Future people inherit missed opportunities too.

A world preserved from every risk would also be preserved from many improvements.

The choice is not between protecting the future and changing it.

Change is inevitable.

The question is what kind of change leaves the next decision-maker with something worth inheriting.

One practical test is to ask whether we would consider the arrangement fair if our place in time were unknown.

Imagine you know the policy but not whether you will live now or later.

You might be the person receiving the benefit today.

You might be the person maintaining the infrastructure thirty years from now.

You might be born into the debt.

You might inherit the preserved land.

You might inherit the lost opportunity created by excessive caution.

The thought experiment does not eliminate disagreement.

It changes the perspective.

It makes temporal luck visible.

None of us chose our birth date.

Yet birth date determines which side of many bargains we occupy.

We inherit interest rates, technologies, institutions, wars, debts, discoveries and environmental conditions produced before we could influence them.

A fair system should notice that arbitrary fact.

We cannot make generations equal.

They live in different worlds.

We cannot give future people literal consent.

We cannot predict their preferences perfectly.

We cannot avoid shaping their options.

But we can refuse to treat their absence as zero weight.

We can distinguish uncertainty from permission.

We can separate financial discounting from moral discounting.

We can account for maintenance and depletion.

We can ask what is reversible.

We can pair obligations with assets.

We can preserve options when preferences are uncertain.

We can expose long-term costs before the people who created them have left the room.

And we can remember that every generation stands in both positions.

We are future people relative to the past.

We are present people to ourselves.

We are ancestors to someone else.

That last role is easy to sentimentalize.

It need not be.

It is simply a fact about causation.

Our decisions travel farther than our attention.

The world keeps the parts of our choices that survive us.

That is why the future has no vote.

It also explains why good judgment has to count it anyway.

The next problem is more intimate.

Even within one time period, two people can make the same decision with the same information and produce different outcomes because chance intervenes.

If goodness cannot be judged by outcome alone, what should intention, foresight and responsibility contribute?

That is where we go next.
