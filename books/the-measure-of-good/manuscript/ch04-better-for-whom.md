# Better for Whom?

Suppose ten people each have ten dollars.

Then something changes.

Nine of them still have ten dollars. The tenth now has one hundred and ten.

The group is richer.

The average has doubled.

Nobody has become poorer.

It would be strange to call this a bad outcome simply because the gains were unequal.

Now change the example.

Nine people lose one dollar each. The tenth gains one hundred.

The group is richer again.

The average rises again.

Most people are worse off.

The arithmetic has not lied.

It has answered a question.

The question was not enough.

This is the next difficulty in measuring good.

Even if we choose the right thing to care about, and even if we measure it accurately, we still have to decide how outcomes across different people should be combined.

Better for whom?

Worse for whom?

By how much?

And when gains and losses fall on different people, what makes the total result good?

An average cannot answer those questions because an average is built to forget who had what.

That is its usefulness.

It is also its danger.

An average compresses a population into a single number. The compression lets us compare years, places, schools, hospitals, companies and countries without carrying every individual case into the conversation.

That can be enormously useful.

If average life expectancy rises substantially, something important has probably happened. If average waiting time falls, the system may be improving. If average income grows, there may be more resources available. If average test performance rises, students may be learning more.

But the word *average* performs a small disappearing act.

It removes the distribution.

Two groups can have the same average and almost nothing else in common.

Imagine four households.

In the first town, each household earns fifty thousand dollars.

In the second, three households earn ten thousand and one earns one hundred and seventy thousand.

The average income is the same.

The lived economies are not.

This does not mean equality is automatically better.

It means the average did not contain enough information to decide.

The second town may have more opportunity, more investment, more mobility or some other advantage not visible in the example. The first may have hidden costs of its own.

The point is simpler.

A total can be true while concealing the shape of the truth.

That shape matters whenever people experience outcomes individually.

A country does not feel income.

People do.

A hospital does not wait.

Patients do.

A school district does not learn.

Students do.

A company does not get promoted, exhausted, injured or ignored.

Workers do.

Aggregation is a view from above.

Good and bad are often experienced from below.

The two views need each other.

Without aggregation, we can drown in anecdotes.

Without distribution, we can erase the people inside the aggregate.

This creates a recurring mistake in public and private life: treating improvement in the total as proof of improvement in the parts.

Sometimes that inference is reasonable.

Sometimes it is false.

A business can become more profitable while a crucial department becomes intolerable to work in.

A neighborhood can become wealthier while long-time residents are priced out.

A transport system can move more passengers while becoming much harder to use for people with limited mobility.

A school can raise its average score while the lowest-performing students fall farther behind.

A family can increase its income while losing most of the time that once held it together.

In each case, there may still be a good argument that the change was worthwhile.

But the aggregate cannot make the argument for us.

It can only tell us what happened to the aggregate.

The moral work begins when we ask how the change was distributed.

That question becomes uncomfortable because distribution creates tradeoffs that totals can hide.

Suppose a policy gives a small benefit to a million people and imposes a serious cost on one thousand.

Is that good?

You cannot answer without knowing the size and kind of the benefit, the size and kind of the cost, whether the people bearing the cost had a choice, whether compensation is possible, whether rights are involved, how certain the estimates are, and what alternatives exist.

Even after learning all of that, reasonable people may disagree.

One person may focus on the sum of the benefits.

Another may focus on the severity of the concentrated harm.

Another may ask whether anyone is being used as a means to improve the total.

Another may ask whether the worst-off group is better or worse than before.

Another may care about whether the process was fair even if the outcome looks efficient.

These are not statistical disagreements.

They are disagreements about how people count.

Measurement can make them more honest.

It cannot remove them.

This is why the denominator matters as much as the numerator.

When someone says that an intervention has produced a large benefit, ask: across whom?

When someone says that risk is low, ask: low for whom?

When someone says that a system is efficient, ask: whose costs are included?

When someone says that almost everyone is better off, ask what happened to the people inside the word *almost*.

The questions are not cynical.

They are part of taking the measurement seriously.

A statistic is always defined over a population.

Choosing the population is a decision.

Sometimes the choice is obvious.

If a teacher wants to know how a class performed on an exam, the students in the class are the natural group.

But many important decisions have no boundary that clean.

A factory measures the cost of producing an object.

Does the cost include pollution borne by people nearby?

A city measures the value of a new development.

Does the analysis include renters who may leave because prices rise?

A company measures the productivity created by a new system.

Does it include the unpaid time customers now spend doing work that employees used to do?

A cheap product saves buyers money.

Does the calculation include dangerous conditions somewhere in the supply chain?

A convenient service saves minutes for millions of users.

Does it include the attention it captures from them later?

The boundary of the analysis can determine the answer before the arithmetic begins.

This is one reason externalities are morally important.

An externality is, in one sense, a bookkeeping problem.

A cost or benefit lands outside the transaction being measured.

The buyer and seller complete an exchange. Their accounts balance. Something happens to a third party.

The economic language is useful because it reveals that a private calculation can be internally correct and socially incomplete.

But the same structure appears well beyond markets.

A manager saves time by sending a poorly prepared request to five other people.

The manager's efficiency rises.

The organization's may not.

A department hits its budget by shifting work into another department.

Its costs fall.

The institution's may not.

A parent makes a schedule easier by asking one child to absorb more inconvenience than the others.

The household becomes more orderly.

The burden has moved, not disappeared.

We often mistake transferred costs for eliminated costs because the transfer crosses the boundary of the measurement.

This is especially easy inside large systems.

Every unit can optimize locally while the whole system becomes worse.

The customer-service team reduces call time by transferring complicated cases.

The receiving team becomes slower.

The first dashboard improves.

The second deteriorates.

If the dashboards report to different leaders, each may tell a different story about the same work.

The organization then faces a choice disguised as a data problem.

Which boundary matters?

The team?

The division?

The company?

The customer?

The community?

The answer depends on the purpose of the decision.

But someone has to choose.

There is no universal denominator handed to us by nature.

The moral difficulty becomes sharper when the people excluded from the measurement have less power than the people included.

People who can complain are easier to count.

Customers who stay are easier to survey than customers who quietly leave.

Employees who remain are easier to ask about a workplace than employees who already resigned.

Students who sit an exam are easier to measure than students who stopped attending.

Applicants are easier to count than people who never applied because they believed the door was closed.

Users are easier to study than people for whom the product was inaccessible from the beginning.

This creates a subtle selection problem.

The system observes the people who made it into the system.

Then it may generalize from them to everyone the system was supposed to serve.

The missing people can become invisible precisely because the system failed to reach them.

A restaurant that surveys only diners does not learn why others walked past.

A public program that measures satisfaction among successful applicants does not learn much about people defeated by the application process.

A workplace that asks current employees whether advancement is fair may miss the experience of people who left after deciding it was not.

Again, none of this makes the available data useless.

It changes what the data can support.

A good measurement practice asks not only, "What do we know about the people in the dataset?"

It also asks, "Who had to disappear for this dataset to exist?"

Sometimes the answer is nobody important.

Sometimes it changes the meaning of the result.

There is a related error that appears when an outcome is reported as a percentage.

Percentages feel complete because they contain a denominator.

But the denominator may still be the wrong one.

Suppose ninety percent of customers who contact support have their problem resolved.

That sounds good.

What about customers who could not find the support channel?

What about those who abandoned the process halfway through?

What about people who stopped being customers after an earlier failure?

The ninety percent may be perfectly accurate.

It may also describe a narrower world than the sentence suggests.

Every denominator has a gate.

Someone is inside.

Someone is outside.

The gate deserves attention.

This becomes more than a technical concern when benefits and burdens are distributed unequally.

Consider an improvement that saves everyone the same amount of time.

Ten minutes.

That sounds equal.

But ten minutes does not necessarily have equal value to everyone.

For one person it is trivial.

For another it determines whether a child is collected on time.

For another it determines whether a connecting bus is missed.

For another it means ten fewer minutes of pain.

Equal quantities do not always create equal consequences.

The same is true of money.

A hundred dollars is the same number in every account.

It is not the same event in every life.

To someone with abundant savings, it may barely register.

To someone choosing which bill to postpone, it can change the week.

This is why adding dollars is easier than adding welfare.

Money has a common unit.

Human significance does not.

We can estimate, infer, survey and model.

We should.

But we should not confuse the convenience of a common unit with proof that the unit captures equal moral weight.

The same problem appears with risk.

Suppose a project creates a one-in-a-million risk of serious harm for each of a million people.

Now suppose a different project creates a one-in-ten risk for ten people.

The expected number of harms may be similar.

The situations do not feel morally identical.

The second group lives under a concentrated danger that may shape behavior, fear, insurance, property, family decisions and consent in ways the dispersed risk does not.

Perhaps the expected total should dominate the decision.

Perhaps concentration matters independently.

The arithmetic does not settle the question.

It shows us where the question is.

This is an important distinction.

Measurement is often accused of flattening moral life.

It does not have to.

Good measurement can reveal distribution rather than erase it.

Instead of one average, show the range.

Instead of one success rate, show outcomes across groups.

Instead of one waiting time, show the median and the tail.

Instead of one income figure, show how gains are distributed.

Instead of asking only how many people benefited, ask who bore the largest costs.

The answer does not need to become a dashboard with fifty indicators.

More numbers can create their own fog.

The goal is not maximum measurement.

The goal is enough measurement to prevent a summary statistic from pretending to be the whole moral picture.

There is no perfect rule for how much distribution should matter.

Equality is not the only good.

A society in which everyone has equally little is not made good by its equality.

A company should not necessarily refuse an innovation because one role becomes less valuable.

A family should not divide every resource identically regardless of need.

A hospital should not give every patient equal time when one patient requires urgent attention.

Fairness is not sameness.

But inequality is not automatically harmless either.

A gain can be large enough in total and still leave us uneasy about who paid for it.

That unease is sometimes sentimental.

Sometimes it is the beginning of moral perception.

The hard cases arise when several plausible ideas of fairness point in different directions.

Treat everyone equally.

Give more to those who need more.

Reward contribution.

Protect basic rights.

Prioritize the worst off.

Honor agreements.

Maximize total benefit.

Preserve opportunity.

Avoid imposing severe harm without consent.

Each principle can sound obvious in isolation.

They conflict in practice.

A scholarship awarded entirely by exam score may reward performance and ignore unequal preparation.

A scholarship awarded entirely by need may ignore exceptional achievement.

A hospital that treats patients strictly in order of arrival may be equal in one sense and reckless in another.

A company that pays everyone the same may ignore differences in responsibility, skill or scarcity.

A company that pays only what the labor market will bear may reproduce bargaining differences that have little to do with contribution.

There is no metric that makes these tensions disappear.

There are only better and worse ways to make the underlying choices visible.

This is where the language of weights becomes useful.

Every aggregate gives weight to something.

A simple average gives each observation equal mathematical weight.

A financial analysis may weight outcomes by dollars.

A voting system weights eligible ballots according to its rules.

A triage system may weight urgency.

A family may weight need.

A court may give some rights priority even when violating them would create a larger total benefit elsewhere.

The weights can be explicit or hidden.

Hidden weights are still weights.

If a company optimizes revenue, people who spend more money receive more influence over the objective than people who do not.

If a platform optimizes time spent, heavy users shape the metric more than occasional users.

If a city measures traffic mainly through vehicle speed, drivers receive more representation in the objective than pedestrians whose journeys are not captured by the same measure.

If a school judges success only through test scores, the abilities measured by the test receive more weight than those outside it.

The system may never say, "These people matter more" or "This form of value counts more."

The objective can say it silently.

That is why technical design becomes moral design long before anyone uses moral language.

Choosing what to aggregate is choosing what can trade against what.

Choosing the unit is choosing what differences become comparable.

Choosing the population is choosing whose outcomes enter the account.

Choosing the weights is choosing how strongly those outcomes pull on the result.

Those choices may be defensible.

They should not be invisible.

The question becomes even harder when some of the affected people are not present to speak.

Future people are the clearest example.

A decision made today can create benefits now and costs decades later.

The future people bearing those costs do not vote in today's meeting.

They do not answer today's survey.

They do not appear in current customer satisfaction.

They may not exist yet.

Do they count?

Most of us think they count at least somewhat.

If we knew an action would make life dramatically worse for our grandchildren, the fact that they are not here yet would not make the harm morally irrelevant.

But how much should future welfare weigh against present need?

That is not a measurement problem alone.

We can estimate future costs, probabilities and time horizons.

The moral weight assigned to those future outcomes remains a choice.

Too little weight and the present consumes the future.

Too much weight and current people may be asked to make extreme sacrifices for uncertain possibilities far away.

The correct balance is contested because the values are contested.

The same is true across borders.

A country can make itself richer by shifting environmental damage, dangerous work or extraction elsewhere.

From inside the national accounts, the result may look good.

From a wider boundary, the result may look different.

A company can reduce costs by moving burdens onto suppliers.

A household can save money by buying goods whose true production costs are borne by people it will never meet.

The distance makes exclusion easier.

The people outside the frame do not disappear.

Only their representation does.

This is why "Who counts?" is one of the oldest moral questions and one of the newest measurement questions.

Civilizations have repeatedly expanded the circle of people whose interests are treated as relevant.

Families, tribes, citizens, property owners, workers, women, racial and religious minorities, foreigners, children, disabled people, future generations: the boundary has never been merely statistical.

To count someone is first to recognize that what happens to them belongs in the account.

Only then can arithmetic begin.

This gives the word *count* its double meaning.

We count people by numbering them.

We say people count when their interests matter.

The two meanings should make us cautious.

A person can be present in the dataset and absent from the decision.

A group can be measured constantly and still given little moral weight.

Conversely, a person can matter morally even when measurement is difficult or impossible.

The absence of a number is not the absence of a claim.

This is especially important when the easiest harms to measure are not the deepest ones.

Money lost can be counted.

Humiliation is harder.

Minutes delayed can be counted.

Loss of dignity is harder.

Injuries can be counted.

Fear may be harder.

Transactions can be counted.

Trust is harder.

This does not mean the harder things should dominate every decision.

It means they should not vanish merely because they resist aggregation.

A careful decision can use numbers where numbers are strong and language where language is necessary.

It can distinguish measured facts from moral claims.

It can state who benefits and who pays.

It can admit uncertainty.

It can ask whether the people carrying the largest burdens had any meaningful way to refuse them.

It can ask whether compensation repairs the harm or merely prices it.

And it can recognize that some tradeoffs become unacceptable before the total reaches its maximum.

That last point matters because not every good can be safely aggregated.

Imagine that taking one innocent person's property would create a tiny benefit for ten million others.

At some point the sum can become mathematically large.

Many people would still refuse the trade.

Why?

Because they believe the person has a claim that is not erased by aggregation.

Call it a right, a constraint, a duty, a boundary, a rule of fairness.

Whatever the name, it says that some values do not enter the calculation as ordinary quantities to be traded away whenever enough small benefits appear on the other side.

This is where simple maximizing approaches meet their limit.

If every value can be converted into one common score, then in principle enough of one thing can compensate for any amount of another.

Human moral judgment often refuses that conclusion.

We do not usually believe that enough convenience justifies torture.

We do not believe that enough profit justifies slavery.

We do not believe that enough popularity makes persecution good.

The language is extreme because the principle is easiest to see at the edge.

Some lines are not prices.

They are limits.

A serious measure of good therefore needs more than a total.

It needs a picture of distribution.

It needs boundaries that say who is included.

It needs attention to people who are missing from the data.

It needs an account of which harms can be traded against benefits and which cannot.

It needs a way to notice concentrated suffering inside broad improvement.

And it needs humility about the fact that equal numbers do not always have equal meaning in unequal lives.

None of this makes decision-making simpler.

That is the point.

The simplicity was often purchased by hiding part of the problem.

An average is not immoral.

A total is not cruel.

A cost-benefit calculation is not dehumanizing merely because it calculates.

The failure begins when a compressed representation is allowed to erase the people whose lives produced it.

There is a practical discipline that helps.

Whenever a number claims improvement, ask four questions.

Who gained?

Who lost?

Who is missing?

Who decided the boundary?

The answers will not tell you automatically whether the result is good.

They will make it harder to declare victory too early.

And they reveal something central about moral measurement.

There is no aggregate without a population.

There is no population without a boundary.

There is no boundary without a judgment about whose experience belongs in the account.

Before we decide how much good was created, we have to decide who counts.

Only then do the numbers know whom they are about.
