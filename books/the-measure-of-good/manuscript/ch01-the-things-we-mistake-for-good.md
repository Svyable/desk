# The Things We Mistake for Good

A strange thing happens when a number becomes important.

At first, the number is merely evidence.

A company watches revenue because revenue tells it something about whether customers will pay for what it makes. A school watches test scores because they tell it something about whether students have learned the material. A hospital watches waiting times because waiting can be painful, dangerous and revealing. A person watches a bank balance because money determines which choices are available and which are not.

None of this is foolish.

The trouble arrives gradually. The evidence becomes the objective. The objective becomes the definition.

Revenue stops being one sign that a company is useful and becomes the meaning of success. The score stops being one piece of evidence about learning and becomes what learning is. The waiting-time target becomes more real than the patient who is waiting. The bank balance becomes not a measure of resources but a measure of the person who owns them.

The substitution is attractive because it solves a difficult problem.

Goodness is hard to see.

Numbers are easy to see.

So we begin with the reasonable thought that a measurable thing can help us judge an important thing. Then, without quite noticing, we reverse the relationship. The measurable thing becomes important because we can measure it.

This is not a defect of statistics. It is a habit of mind.

We do it with money first because money is the great common denominator.

Money is one of civilization's most useful inventions precisely because it lets unlike things become comparable. A loaf of bread, an hour of labor, a train ticket and a piece of land can all acquire prices. Once they do, trade becomes easier. Planning becomes easier. Cooperation among strangers becomes easier.

But usefulness creates temptation.

If price lets us compare many things, perhaps price tells us what they are worth.

Sometimes it does.

If two nearly identical umbrellas sit beside each other and one costs twice as much, the difference matters. If a business spends more making a product than customers will pay for it, that matters too. Price carries information about scarcity, demand, alternatives, bargaining power and a great deal else.

It just does not carry all the information we care about.

A wedding ring may have a modest resale price and enormous value to the person wearing it. An hour with a dying parent may produce no income at all. A patch of undeveloped land may be worth more to a builder after it is cleared and worth more to everyone downstream if it continues absorbing rain. A nurse may be paid less than a trader without anyone being required to believe that the trader's work is morally superior.

These examples are easy because the mismatch is obvious.

The harder cases are the ones in which money is relevant but incomplete.

A higher salary really can improve a life. It can buy safety, comfort, time, education, mobility and relief from the humiliations of having too little. It can reduce the number of emergencies that become disasters. Pretending otherwise is often a luxury of people whose own bills are already paid.

But money also travels remarkably well into places where it does not belong.

It becomes a score.

One person earns more than another, so the difference acquires a moral shadow. More disciplined. More capable. More serious. More valuable.

The market did not say any of those things.

The market said that, under a particular set of conditions, one stream of labor or ownership produced more income than another.

We supplied the rest.

This is the first pattern worth noticing: a measure can begin by describing an outcome and end by describing a person.

Income becomes merit.

Weight becomes discipline.

Followers become importance.

Productivity becomes character.

A grade becomes intelligence.

A diagnosis becomes identity.

A ranking becomes destiny.

The mistake is not that these measures contain no information. Most of them contain quite a lot. The mistake is allowing the information they contain to erase the information they do not.

There is another reason we make this error. Measures are socially convenient.

Two people can disagree for hours about who is the better musician. They can settle who sold more records in a sentence.

They can argue about the best university and then point to a ranking.

They can debate whether an employee is doing meaningful work and then open a spreadsheet.

The number does not necessarily resolve the question. It often changes the question into one that can be resolved.

That is a powerful service.

It is also a powerful distortion.

Suppose you are choosing between two jobs.

One pays twenty percent more. The difference is concrete. You can calculate it exactly.

The other gives you a better manager, more interesting work, a shorter commute and the chance to become unusually good at something you care about.

Already the arithmetic has an advantage. The salary arrives as a number. The rest arrives as judgment.

You can put estimates on the other factors, and sometimes that helps. How many hours of commuting will you save? What would you pay for those hours? What is the expected value of the skills you might gain?

But notice what has happened. The measurable option entered the comparison as itself. The less measurable options had to disguise themselves as money before they were allowed to compete.

This is often sensible. Converting things into common units can clarify tradeoffs.

It can also give a false impression that the conversion was neutral.

How much is an hour with your children worth?

The question can be useful if it stops you from taking on three hours of daily commuting for a trivial raise.

It becomes absurd if you forget that the answer was invented for the decision rather than discovered in the world.

A price can be assigned.

That does not mean a price existed.

The distinction matters because modern life rewards whatever can survive translation into a metric.

Institutions need to compare.

A manager with one employee can rely heavily on direct knowledge. A manager with ten thousand cannot. A teacher can know thirty students individually; a ministry of education cannot know three million that way. A small lender may understand a borrower's circumstances. A national bank needs rules, models and categories.

Scale creates distance.

Measurement bridges the distance.

This is one reason metrics spread even when everyone knows they are imperfect. The alternative is often not perfect human judgment. It is inconsistent judgment, inaccessible judgment, favoritism, delay, guesswork or no decision at all.

The problem, then, is not that institutions measure.

The problem is that measurement is usually better at traveling than context.

The score moves upward through the organization. The story stays where it happened.

A dashboard can tell an executive that customer-service calls are getting shorter. It cannot, by itself, tell her whether the calls are shorter because problems are being solved faster or because employees have learned to get people off the phone.

A school system can report that attendance is up. The number alone cannot tell you whether students are more engaged, whether reporting changed, whether absences became harder to record, or whether children are sitting in rooms from which they have mentally disappeared.

A police department can report more arrests or fewer arrests. Neither direction tells you, by itself, whether the public is safer.

Every measure arrives carrying a question.

We get into trouble when we lose the question and keep the measure.

What was revenue supposed to tell us?

What was the test supposed to tell us?

Why did we care about response time?

What human condition was this statistic created to illuminate?

Those questions sound elementary. They are surprisingly difficult to keep alive once incentives attach to the answer.

Imagine that a call center decides that customers are waiting too long. Management begins tracking the average length of each call.

This is not irrational. If employees can solve the same problems faster, more customers can be served.

Then bonuses depend on call length.

The number has changed.

Technically it is still measured in minutes and seconds. Practically it has become a target, and targets exert pressure.

An employee now faces two goals that usually overlap and occasionally conflict: help the customer, and finish the call quickly.

When they conflict, the institution discovers what it really rewards.

Perhaps the employee rushes the explanation. Perhaps a complicated caller gets transferred. Perhaps the problem is closed in the system before it is closed in reality. No villain is required. No conspiracy is required. People adapt to the environment they are given.

This is why the sentence "we measure what matters" is dangerous.

Usually we measure a proxy for what matters.

The difference is not pedantic. It is the whole game.

We want learning, so we measure test performance.

We want health, so we measure biomarkers.

We want prosperity, so we measure economic output.

We want public safety, so we measure crime.

We want excellent research, so we measure publications and citations.

We want useful employees, so we measure output.

We want a good life, so we measure whatever our culture has made visible.

The proxy may be excellent.

It is still a proxy.

Once this is understood, a great many arguments become easier to diagnose.

One side points to the number.

The other side points to what the number misses.

Both may be right.

Consider productivity.

It is difficult to argue against productivity in the abstract. Producing more useful output from the same amount of time or material is one of the basic ways living standards improve. Waste is not noble.

But productivity becomes stranger when applied to a human day.

You answer more messages. Attend more meetings. Complete more tasks. Read more pages. Process more requests.

At five o'clock, the list is impressive.

Did you do the right work?

That question sits outside the count.

A person can become extremely efficient at avoiding the important thing.

This happens partly because completion is pleasurable. Finished tasks give us evidence of motion. Important work is often slower, less legible and more uncertain. It may require an hour of staring at a problem before writing one useful sentence. It may require a conversation whose value appears six months later. It may require saying no to nine reasonable requests so that one difficult project receives enough attention to matter.

A system that counts visible activity will naturally favor visible activity.

Eventually people learn to manufacture it.

Not because they are corrupt. Because they are trainable.

We teach children this lesson early.

A child makes something and looks toward an adult.

"Is this good?"

The adult can discuss effort, choice, technique, imagination, care, improvement, delight.

Or the adult can supply a score.

The score is efficient. It closes the loop.

It may also teach the child to stop looking at the work and start looking at the evaluator.

Adults do the same thing with markets, rankings, awards and applause. We ask the world to tell us whether the thing we made was good because the world has instruments ready to answer nearby questions.

Did it sell?

Did it win?

Did people click?

Did they share it?

Did the boss like it?

Did the audience stand?

These are not meaningless questions. A writer who never considers readers can become self-indulgent. A company that ignores customers can disappear. A scientist whose work persuades nobody may have failed to communicate something important or may simply be wrong.

External response contains information.

The error is granting the response final authority.

Popularity is especially seductive because it looks democratic.

If millions of people choose something, surely that matters.

It does.

It tells us that millions of people chose it.

That may indicate pleasure, usefulness, fashion, habit, availability, addiction, network effects, marketing, price, quality or some combination of them.

The number of choices does not tell us which explanation is doing the work.

Nor does popularity convert desire into virtue.

A thing can be widely desired and bad for the people desiring it. A thing can be unpopular and necessary. A culture can celebrate what it later regrets and neglect what it later treasures.

Human beings are allowed to want things that are not good for them.

Any serious account of good has to survive that fact.

So does any serious account of happiness.

Happiness seems, at first, like a better candidate than money or popularity. If the point of a good life is not to feel good, what could it be?

There is something appealingly humane about putting experience at the center. Pain matters. Joy matters. A philosophy that treats misery as irrelevant has missed something fundamental.

But happiness becomes slippery as soon as we ask it to carry the entire burden.

Would you want your child to be happy because she is loved, capable, curious and free, or happy because she has become extraordinarily skilled at wanting very little from a life that is shrinking around her?

Would you prefer the contentment that follows keeping a difficult promise, or the immediate relief of escaping it?

Is grief evidence of a bad life?

The question sounds offensive because grief often appears where love was deepest.

Some of the states we value most contain pain: devotion, responsibility, courage, mourning, effort, parenthood, loyalty, creation.

A life engineered only to maximize pleasant feeling could become grotesque.

This does not make happiness unimportant.

It makes it insufficient.

The same is true of efficiency.

Efficiency answers a ratio question: how much of one thing did we get for another?

Less fuel for the same journey.

Less time for the same task.

Less waste for the same output.

Useful.

But efficiency cannot tell us whether the journey should be taken, whether the task should exist or whether the output is worth producing.

An efficient fraud is worse than an inefficient one.

An efficient bureaucracy can deny people help faster.

An efficient factory can make useless objects with less waste.

An efficient person can arrive at the wrong destination early.

Efficiency is a property of a process, not a verdict on its purpose.

We repeatedly ask useful measures to become verdicts because verdicts are exhausting.

Judgment asks us to hold several things in mind at once.

A good decision may need to consider consequences, rights, obligations, character, uncertainty, fairness, history, relationships and the distribution of costs. Some of these can be measured. Some can be estimated. Some must be argued about.

Then we have to choose.

No wonder we prefer a score.

The score is not merely convenient. It offers innocence.

If the number decided, perhaps I did not.

If the model rejected the applicant, perhaps I did not.

If the ranking put this school first, perhaps I did not.

If the market set the price, perhaps I did not.

If the policy says no, perhaps I did not.

Measurements can become machines for distributing responsibility until nobody feels fully responsible for the result.

This is unfair to machines and models, which have no need for our excuses.

A metric does not compel obedience.

Someone chose it. Someone decided how to collect it, what to exclude, what threshold matters, how errors should be treated, and what should happen when the number crosses a line.

Even a very good measure sits inside a human decision.

That is why the right response to a flawed metric is rarely to throw away measurement.

The better response is to restore the hierarchy.

First, decide what you are trying to protect or produce.

Then ask what evidence would tell you whether you are succeeding.

Then ask how that evidence could mislead you.

Then keep looking at the world.

This sounds less sophisticated than a dashboard.

It is more demanding.

It requires an institution to remember why the dashboard exists.

It requires a person to admit that some judgments cannot be outsourced.

And it requires us to live with an uncomfortable possibility: two people can possess the same facts, agree on the measurements, understand the tradeoffs and still disagree about what should be done.

At that point there is no better metric waiting behind the curtain.

There is a conflict of values.

We tend to experience such conflicts as failures. If only we had more data, a sharper model, a more intelligent expert, perhaps the disagreement would disappear.

Sometimes it would.

Facts matter enormously. Many moral arguments are partly factual arguments in disguise. People disagree about what a policy will cause, who will bear the cost, whether a risk is real, how large a benefit is, or what alternatives actually exist. Better evidence can change the answer.

But facts do not abolish values.

A perfect map cannot tell you where to go.

This is the uncomfortable limit at the center of measurement.

We need numbers because reality pushes back. Without them, stories become too easy to manipulate. We need judgment because numbers do not contain their own purpose.

A measure earns authority by serving something beyond itself.

The moment it becomes the thing being served, we should ask what disappeared during the substitution.

Often the missing thing is the good we cared about in the first place.
