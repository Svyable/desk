# The Problem of Uncertainty

Most decisions are made before their consequences are known.

This seems too obvious to matter until we notice how often we judge a decision as though the outcome had been available in advance.

A project succeeds, so the choice to begin it looks wise.

A surgery goes badly, so the choice to attempt it looks reckless.

An investment rises, so the investor appears perceptive.

A storm misses the coast, so the evacuation looks excessive.

Once the result exists, it exerts a gravitational pull on the story of the decision.

We forget the fog that surrounded the moment when someone actually had to choose.

That fog is where judgment lives.

Imagine a doctor discussing two treatments with a patient.

One treatment has a high chance of a modest improvement and a small chance of a serious complication.

The other has a lower chance of a much larger improvement and a higher chance of leaving the patient worse off.

No amount of measurement can turn this into a decision about outcomes, because the outcomes do not exist yet.

The measurements describe possibilities.

The choice is made among them.

This introduces a different kind of moral problem from the ones we have considered so far.

A metric can be accurate. The objective can be sensible. The people affected can all be included. And the decision can still be difficult because the world has not told us which future will occur.

We are forced to act on distributions rather than results.

That sounds mathematical, and sometimes it is.

Probabilities are among the most useful tools human beings have developed for thinking about uncertain futures. A probability forces us to separate what is possible from what is likely. It makes confidence visible. It allows different risks to be compared. It can expose the difference between a vivid danger and a common one.

But probabilities create their own temptation.

A number placed beside an uncertain future can make the uncertainty feel domesticated.

Thirty percent.

One in a thousand.

Ninety-five percent confidence.

The precision is reassuring.

The world may not deserve it.

There are situations in which the odds are well understood. A carefully manufactured game of chance can have probabilities built into its rules. A large body of repeated observations can make some risks reasonably estimable. A machine with many similar predecessors may have a failure rate supported by experience.

There are other situations in which the probability is partly a judgment about a model whose weaknesses are themselves uncertain.

Will a new technology create more jobs than it destroys over twenty years?

What is the probability that a political reform weakens an institution rather than strengthening it?

How likely is a new business to discover a profitable market before its financing runs out?

What is the chance that a new medical approach reveals an uncommon harm after widespread adoption?

A percentage can be attached to each question.

The existence of the percentage does not tell us how much knowledge sits behind it.

This distinction matters because uncertain decisions contain at least two separate problems.

The first is ordinary risk: several outcomes are possible and we have some reasonable basis for assigning odds.

The second is uncertainty about the odds themselves.

People often talk about both as if they were the same.

They are not.

If I tell you a machine has a one percent chance of failing because ten million comparable machines have been observed, that number has one kind of authority.

If I tell you a new social policy has a one percent chance of causing a particular long-term effect because a committee debated the matter and settled on one percent, the number has another.

Both may be useful.

Only one should make you feel as though the uncertainty has been measured in the ordinary sense.

This is where false precision becomes dangerous.

A rough estimate can improve a decision.

A rough estimate presented as a measurement can conceal how much judgment entered before the arithmetic began.

There is a simple habit that helps.

Ask not only, "What is the probability?"

Ask, "Where did the probability come from?"

Historical frequency?

A model?

Expert judgment?

A market price?

A simulation?

An analogy?

A guess that has been discussed until it sounds less like a guess?

The source does not automatically discredit the number. Expert judgment can be valuable. Models can be excellent. Simulations can reveal possibilities that experience has not yet produced.

But a probability inherits limits from the process that created it.

A decision should not become more confident than its evidence.

This sounds like a technical warning.

It is also a moral one.

Uncertainty determines who bears the consequences of being wrong.

Suppose a city is deciding whether to reinforce a flood barrier.

The work is expensive. The probability of a severe flood in the next decade is uncertain. Engineers disagree about the tail of the risk. The money could be spent elsewhere.

If the city reinforces the barrier and the flood never comes, the expenditure may look wasteful.

If it does nothing and the flood arrives, the earlier savings may look absurd.

But neither later judgment is enough.

The relevant question at the time of the decision was not whether the flood would happen.

It was whether the combination of probability, consequence, cost, alternatives and uncertainty justified acting before anyone could know.

This is why a good outcome does not prove a good decision.

A reckless driver may arrive safely.

A careful driver may be struck by someone else.

If we judge only by arrival, we train ourselves to learn the wrong lesson from luck.

The same mistake happens at larger scales.

An organization takes an enormous risk and survives. Survival becomes evidence that the risk was acceptable.

Another organization builds redundancy that is never used. The unused capacity becomes evidence that the precaution was unnecessary.

This is a strange asymmetry.

Successful risk-taking produces visible rewards.

Successful prevention often produces nothing.

The disaster did not happen.

The backup was not needed.

The spare capacity sat idle.

The warning turned out to be early.

It is easy to measure the cost of precautions. Their benefit is often a missing event.

That makes uncertainty hospitable to hindsight.

After enough quiet years, people begin to believe that what did not happen could not have happened.

Then the system becomes efficient again.

Spare capacity is cut.

Maintenance is deferred.

Insurance limits rise.

Emergency plans become ceremonial documents.

The numbers improve until the day the missing event arrives.

This does not mean every precaution is justified.

Fear can consume unlimited resources if it is given authority without comparison.

There is always another danger that could be reduced by spending more money, adding another inspection, requiring another approval, storing another backup, delaying another launch.

A society that attempted to eliminate risk would eliminate a great deal of life with it.

Innovation is risky.

Travel is risky.

Medicine is risky.

Trust is risky.

Parenthood is risky.

Freedom permits people to make decisions that may go badly.

The aim cannot be zero uncertainty.

The harder question is which uncertainties deserve which response.

Here the scale of the possible harm matters.

A small reversible mistake should usually require less evidence before action than a large irreversible one.

If you are deciding where to eat tonight, uncertainty is cheap.

Try the restaurant.

If it is bad, eat somewhere else next time.

If you are deciding whether to demolish a building, drain a wetland, approve a drug for millions of people, redesign a city's transport system or deploy software that will make decisions at enormous scale, the cost of discovering that you were wrong can be much larger.

The difference is not simply risk.

It is recoverability.

Some choices teach us cheaply.

Others destroy the conditions needed to learn from the mistake.

This is one reason reversible decisions deserve special attention, and we will return to it later.

For now, notice what reversibility does to the moral burden of uncertainty.

If a decision can be tried on a small scale, observed and changed, we do not need to solve the future in advance.

We can buy information through action.

A pilot program, a staged rollout, a temporary rule, a limited exposure, a smaller bet—these are not merely management techniques.

They are ways of respecting uncertainty.

They allow reality to participate in the decision before the decision becomes difficult to undo.

But not every uncertainty permits experimentation.

Some choices are one-way doors.

Others unfold too slowly for feedback to arrive before the next decision must be made.

Climate, infrastructure, childhood, institutional trust, war, species loss, long-lived technologies: many important domains contain consequences that can outlast the people making the choice.

In such cases the familiar instruction to "test and learn" may arrive too late.

We need another way to think.

One common approach is expected value.

List the possible outcomes.

Estimate their probabilities.

Estimate their value or cost.

Multiply and compare.

This can be extremely clarifying.

It forces rare but severe outcomes into view. It can prevent us from spending enormous resources to avoid vivid but tiny risks while neglecting less dramatic dangers that harm many more people. It can make assumptions explicit enough to argue about.

The method deserves its power.

It also deserves limits.

Expected value compresses different futures into an average before any of them has occurred.

That can be useful when the decision is repeated many times or when gains and losses are reasonably comparable.

It can become morally strange when a small chance of catastrophic harm to one group is balanced against modest benefits to another as though all units were interchangeable.

Return to the question from the previous chapter.

Better for whom?

Under uncertainty we must add another.

Who is being asked to carry the risk?

A project may have positive expected value overall while exposing a small group to a danger they did not choose.

A financial institution may earn attractive returns most years while creating a small probability of losses that others will absorb.

A medical decision may offer a population-level benefit while imposing uncertainty on individual patients whose tolerance for that risk differs sharply.

The expected value does not disappear.

Neither does the distribution of the gamble.

Consent matters partly because risk is not experienced as an average.

A person lives one path.

The spreadsheet sees all of them.

This difference becomes especially important when the downside is concentrated and the upside is dispersed, or the reverse.

A million people may each receive a small benefit from a policy that exposes a thousand people to a significant risk.

Perhaps the policy is justified.

Perhaps it is not.

The arithmetic can tell us the structure of the trade.

It cannot tell us whether the people carrying the risk have a claim that should constrain the trade.

That question belongs to the chapters ahead.

For now, uncertainty complicates measurement in another way: the same evidence can rationally support different choices for people with different capacities to absorb failure.

Imagine two families considering the same investment.

The expected return is identical.

For one family, losing the money would be disappointing.

For the other, it would mean missing rent.

The investment has not changed.

The goodness of taking the risk may have.

This is why risk tolerance cannot be reduced to personality.

Capacity matters.

A wealthy institution can survive experiments that would destroy a fragile one.

A person with savings can recover from a career gamble that another person cannot afford to make.

A country with strong institutions and fiscal space can absorb policy mistakes that a brittle state cannot.

The same probability and the same payoff can occupy different moral landscapes depending on what failure does to the decision-maker and to people who did not make the decision.

The language of averages tends to hide this.

It also hides correlation.

Ten independent small risks can be safer than ten risks that fail together.

A household with five income sources is not diversified if every source disappears in the same recession.

A company with five suppliers is not resilient if all five depend on the same port.

A financial system with many institutions is not necessarily safe if they own the same assets and respond to stress in the same way.

This matters because uncertainty often becomes dangerous at the moment supposedly separate protections fail together.

The average failure rate of each component may look acceptable.

The system-level risk may not be.

Once again, the problem is the boundary of the measurement.

A component can be safe by itself and unsafe in combination.

A local decision can be rational and create collective fragility.

The measure has to be large enough to see the failure that matters.

There is a further difficulty.

Not all uncertainty is symmetrical.

Sometimes being wrong in one direction costs far more than being wrong in the other.

A smoke alarm that occasionally sounds when there is no fire is annoying.

A smoke alarm that remains silent during a fire is worse.

A medical screening system that misses a dangerous condition creates one kind of harm. A system that sends many healthy people through unnecessary procedures creates another.

A court that wrongly convicts and a court that wrongly acquits commit different errors with different consequences.

A benefits system that mistakenly pays someone who was ineligible and one that wrongly denies someone food or housing support may record both events as errors.

The moral weight of the errors need not be equal.

This is why accuracy alone is often an incomplete objective.

Ninety-five percent accurate sounds impressive.

Which five percent?

False positives or false negatives?

Minor inconvenience or irreversible loss?

Randomly distributed or concentrated in one group?

Correctable on appeal or effectively final?

A decision system is partly defined by which mistakes it is designed to fear.

That choice is not technical, even when the resulting system is technical.

We encounter the same question in everyday life.

Should I speak now and risk saying the wrong thing, or remain silent and risk allowing a misunderstanding to grow?

Should I leave a job before I know the next one will work out, or stay until the uncertainty has been reduced?

Should I tell someone what I believe they need to hear, knowing that I may have misread the situation?

There is no calculator for these decisions because the probabilities are weak, the outcomes are not commensurable, and the values are entangled with relationship, obligation and character.

Still, the structure of good judgment is recognizable.

What do I know?

What am I merely assuming?

What would change my mind?

What happens if I am wrong?

Who pays for the error?

Can the decision be reversed?

Can I learn more before committing?

What does waiting cost?

These questions do not solve uncertainty.

They prevent uncertainty from disguising itself as confidence.

That may be the best we can ask from a method.

There is a fashion in decision-making for praising people who are comfortable with uncertainty.

The phrase can mean something admirable: they do not freeze merely because knowledge is incomplete.

It can also become a compliment for recklessness.

Some people appear comfortable with uncertainty because other people bear the downside.

A leader can take bold risks when employees, taxpayers, creditors, patients or future managers absorb the failure.

A trader can look fearless if losses are capped and gains are rewarded.

A policymaker can experiment freely when the affected community has little power to refuse.

Courage and externalized risk can look similar from a distance.

A serious measure of good must notice who owns the downside.

The opposite virtue, caution, has its own disguise.

Caution can protect people from reckless change.

It can also protect incumbents, habits and comfortable arrangements from scrutiny.

"We need more evidence" may be a responsible statement.

It may also be a strategy for making sure nothing happens.

Waiting is not neutral.

A delayed treatment can harm a patient.

A delayed repair can allow damage to spread.

A delayed reform can leave a bad system in place.

A delayed investment can close an opportunity.

The status quo produces outcomes while we debate alternatives.

This means uncertainty cuts in both directions.

Acting under uncertainty can cause harm.

Refusing to act until uncertainty disappears can cause harm too.

The question is not whether we can avoid gambling.

Often we are already in a gamble.

The question is which gamble we are choosing.

This is why the demand for certainty can itself become a bad metric.

An organization says it will act only when evidence reaches a threshold.

The threshold sounds rigorous.

But where did it come from?

Does it reflect the cost of a false alarm, the cost of a missed danger, the reversibility of the action, the quality of the evidence, or merely a cultural preference for delay?

There is no universally correct amount of evidence before action.

The burden of proof should depend partly on the decision.

A reversible low-cost trial can tolerate more uncertainty.

An irreversible high-cost intervention should usually require more.

A dangerous status quo may lower the threshold for change.

A fragile population may raise the threshold for experiments imposed upon it.

A severe but plausible downside may justify safeguards even when its exact probability is unclear.

These are judgments about evidence, but also about value.

They tell us what kinds of mistakes we are more willing to make.

The same is true when experts disagree.

We sometimes respond to disagreement by averaging the forecasts.

Sometimes that is wise.

Sometimes the disagreement contains information that the average destroys.

One expert may be using a different model.

Another may be worried about a failure mode excluded from the main analysis.

A third may agree on the facts and disagree about what counts as an acceptable risk.

Calling all three positions "uncertainty" can hide the actual dispute.

Good decision-making separates them.

What do we disagree about factually?

What do we disagree about probabilistically?

What do we disagree about morally?

More data may help with the first two.

It will not settle the third.

This separation is valuable because uncertainty often becomes an excuse for smuggling values into technical language.

"The risk is unacceptable" may sound like a probability statement.

It is partly a value judgment about acceptable.

"The evidence is insufficient" may sound purely scientific.

It also implies a threshold for action.

"The expected benefit outweighs the expected harm" may sound like arithmetic.

It depends on what counts as benefit, what counts as harm, whose outcomes are included, how they are valued and whether any trade is prohibited before the totals are compared.

None of this means the analysis is fake.

It means analysis becomes more honest when its value choices are visible.

There is a useful discipline here for judging decisions after the fact.

Before asking whether the outcome was good, ask whether the decision process was good given what was knowable at the time.

Were relevant risks considered?

Was inconvenient evidence suppressed?

Were probabilities treated with appropriate confidence?

Were the people bearing the downside visible?

Were alternatives compared?

Was there a way to reduce uncertainty cheaply?

Was reversibility preserved where possible?

Was the cost of waiting included?

Did the decision-maker have incentives that encouraged excessive risk or excessive caution?

A process can answer these questions well and still produce a bad outcome.

That is not a failure of judgment.

It is part of living in a world that contains chance.

The converse is more dangerous.

A bad process can produce a good outcome.

Luck can reward negligence.

If the reward teaches us that the process was sound, the next decision may contain the same flaw without the same luck.

This is one reason institutions should preserve decision records before outcomes are known.

Write down the assumptions.

Record the reasons.

State what evidence would change the choice.

Name the feared failure modes.

Do it before the future arrives and edits everyone's memory.

The purpose is not bureaucracy.

It is to protect learning from hindsight.

A decision record lets us distinguish being wrong from being careless.

It lets us see whether confidence was justified by the information available or borrowed from an outcome nobody could yet know.

Over time, that distinction matters more than any single success.

It allows judgment to improve.

We began this book with the seduction of measurement: the hope that a clean number might relieve us of difficult judgment.

Uncertainty makes that hope impossible to maintain.

The future does not arrive as a column in a spreadsheet.

We build the column ourselves from evidence, models, experience and assumptions.

Then we decide how much trust to place in it.

That decision is not a defect around the edge of measurement.

It is part of the measurement.

The measure of good therefore cannot be a score calculated after all uncertainty has been removed.

There is no such moment.

A mature judgment asks a different question.

Given what we know, what we do not know, what can go wrong, who carries the risk, what can be reversed and what waiting will cost, what choice can we defend before we know whether fortune will agree?

That is a harder standard than being right.

It is also a more useful one.
