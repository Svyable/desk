# Chapter 5 — The First American Abroad

Washington Irving became one of the first American writers to achieve international literary fame by leaving America.

That apparent contradiction is a useful place to begin.

The United States in the decade from 1816 through 1825 had survived another war with Britain and entered an era of rising national confidence. Its territory was expanding. Its politics were reorganizing. Roads, canals, banks, newspapers, migration, and commerce were knitting together a country still marked by enormous regional differences and the deepening power of slavery. Americans could claim political independence. Cultural independence was less secure.

European critics sometimes treated the young republic as provincial, commercial, and artistically derivative. The United States had laws, land, and ambition. Did it have literature anyone elsewhere needed to read?

Irving supplied an answer in a book that was not fully a book at first.

*The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent.* began appearing in parts in New York in 1819. Irving was living in Britain, where a family commercial business had failed after the economic disruptions following the Napoleonic Wars. He had already made a name in America through satire, especially *A History of New York*, published under the invented historian Diedrich Knickerbocker. But *The Sketch Book* reached another level of audience and reputation.

The collection mixed essays, travel observations, literary sketches, and stories. Two pieces became so famous that they eventually floated free of the volume: “Rip Van Winkle” and “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow.” Ichabod Crane, the Headless Horseman, Rip sleeping through the Revolution—these figures entered American folklore so completely that many people know them without having read the book that introduced them.

That afterlife alone would make *The Sketch Book* a candidate. The reason it wins is larger.

Irving helped solve a cultural problem the country could not solve through independence: how to make American material feel old enough to carry legend.

European literature possessed ruins, dynasties, castles, ancient villages, inherited folklore, and landscapes thick with recorded memory. The United States, as a political nation, was new. Indigenous histories reached far deeper than the republic, but white American literary culture often marginalized or appropriated them rather than recognizing them as sovereign traditions. Colonial history was available, but the national mythology was still being assembled.

Irving discovered that recent history could be made to feel legendary if handled with enough narrative distance.

Rip Van Winkle sleeps through the Revolution. That conceit performs more work than the comic plot first suggests. A man leaves a colonial village and wakes in a republic. Portraits, arguments, loyalties, and political language have changed while the mountains remain. The Revolution becomes a transformation a character can literally sleep through and then encounter as strangeness.

Irving was writing only a few decades after independence. In chronological terms, the Revolution was closer to him than the 1990s are to us. Yet his story gave it the haze of folklore.

That was an important literary trick for a young nation. History did not have to be ancient before writers could give it imaginative depth.

“The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” worked differently. Set in a Dutch-influenced community in the Hudson Valley, it turned local geography, old families, superstition, social competition, and a frightening nighttime ride into durable American myth. The setting did not imitate a medieval European castle. It found eeriness in a particular American landscape.

The Dutch past of New York became literary material.

Irving’s use of that past was stylized, selective, and comic rather than documentary. He was creating atmosphere, not conducting ethnography. But the result helped popularize the idea that regions of the United States could possess their own legends, accents, memories, and inherited stories.

That idea would become foundational to American literature.

Later writers would mine New England villages, Southern plantations and towns, the Mississippi River, western settlements, immigrant neighborhoods, industrial cities, reservation and borderlands, Black communities, suburbs, highways, deserts, and countless other American places. The country’s literary power would come partly from refusing to be one place.

Irving arrived early enough to demonstrate the opportunity.

The publication story also matters because American authors faced an international book market structured against them.

There was no effective international copyright agreement protecting American authors in Britain or British authors in the United States. American publishers could reprint British works without paying British authors, making established foreign writers economically attractive compared with domestic writers who expected compensation. American authors therefore competed not only for taste but against a legal-economic system that made imported literature cheap.

Irving’s success in Britain reversed the usual direction of prestige.

The London publisher John Murray issued the book after Irving’s efforts to secure British publication. British reviewers responded warmly. Irving developed relationships with major literary figures and became, for European readers, evidence that the United States could produce elegant prose worthy of attention.

The phrase “first American man of letters” is often attached to him in one form or another, and like all “firsts” it needs care. Earlier American writers had produced important poetry, fiction, political writing, theology, history, and autobiography. Phillis Wheatley had been published in London decades earlier. Charles Brockden Brown had attracted transatlantic interest. Benjamin Franklin had an international literary and intellectual reputation of enormous scale.

Irving’s distinction lies less in being literally first than in the kind of professional literary identity he made visible. He could be known abroad primarily as a writer of imaginative prose associated with an American national culture.

That mattered to readers at home.

Young nations are acutely sensitive to foreign judgment. Political independence can intensify cultural insecurity because the new state wants evidence that separation did not produce inferiority. When European critics praised Irving, the praise could be repatriated as national validation.

An American writer had crossed the Atlantic and come back larger.

There is an irony inside the book itself. Much of *The Sketch Book* is about Britain. Geoffrey Crayon visits English scenes, reflects on customs, literature, rural life, Christmas traditions, Westminster Abbey, and the emotional power of old places. The collection is not a declaration of cultural isolation. Irving became more American by engaging Europe, not by pretending Europe had nothing to offer.

That pattern will recur throughout American culture. National originality often emerges through mixture, borrowing, rejection, translation, argument, and recombination. The dream of a completely self-created culture is usually propaganda. Nations inherit before they invent.

Irving’s achievement was to make exchange feel less like dependency.

He could admire English tradition and still write stories that Europeans recognized as American. He could adapt elements from European folklore and literary models while relocating them into the Hudson Valley. The borrowing did not disappear. The transformation mattered.

This is especially clear in “Rip Van Winkle.” Scholars have long noted European folktale analogues involving supernatural sleep and awakening. Irving did not invent the basic motif from nothing. What he did was attach it to the American Revolution and the Catskills so effectively that the story became an American cultural possession.

Originality often works that way.

A culture takes a durable human pattern and gives it local stakes.

The decade itself strengthens the case for Irving. The War of 1812 had ended in 1815. Americans emerged with a complicated sense of victory, helped by the Battle of New Orleans and by the survival of the republic despite military disasters including the burning of Washington. The following years included the so-called Era of Good Feelings, the Missouri crisis, continued displacement of Native peoples, the acceleration of cotton slavery, and large infrastructure projects such as the Erie Canal, completed in 1825.

The nation was becoming more integrated economically at the same time it was becoming more divided over slavery.

In such a moment, stories about a shared past could perform political work even when they were not overtly political. A national literature gives strangers common references. People who disagree about tariffs or slavery can still know Rip Van Winkle. Cultural cohesion is not the same as political consensus, but symbols can cross boundaries arguments cannot.

The cost is that shared stories also select what belongs inside the nation.

Irving’s fictional Hudson Valley foregrounded Dutch settler history and treated the landscape as material for American legend. Indigenous presence could become background, exotic reference, or absence. This was happening while the actual United States was expanding through Native lands and pressuring Indigenous nations through treaties, warfare, removal policies, settlement, and state power.

The literary making of “American” places often accompanied the political unmaking of other sovereignties.

That contradiction should not be added as a footnote after celebrating national literature. It is part of how national literature works. To tell a place as belonging to a nation is already to make a claim about whose memory anchors it.

Irving’s later writing would engage the American West and Spanish history, sometimes reproducing assumptions of his era and sometimes displaying more curiosity than simple conquest mythology. His long career resists reduction to two ghost stories. He wrote biographies, histories, travel accounts, and diplomatic prose, and served as a U.S. diplomat in Spain.

Yet *The Sketch Book* remains the hinge.

Its title is revealing. A sketch book is provisional, miscellaneous, mobile. It belongs to a traveler. Geoffrey Crayon, the invented authorial persona, can move between places and genres collecting impressions. The form gives Irving permission not to write the Great American Book as a single monumental statement. He assembles a national literary reputation from fragments.

That may be a more accurate image of America than a monument would have been.

The book’s greatest cultural products also thrive because they are adaptable. “Sleepy Hollow” has been retold in theater, illustration, school editions, film, television, Halloween culture, tourist branding, and local commemoration. Each adaptation changes tone and emphasis. The Headless Horseman can be comic, gothic, violent, romantic, or family-friendly. The story survives because it has a strong architecture and enough emptiness for later tellers to occupy.

Rip Van Winkle has become a phrase for someone who sleeps through change.

That linguistic afterlife matters. When fictional names become shorthand, literature has escaped the book. A person need not remember Dame Van Winkle, the Catskill setting, or Irving’s narrative frame to understand the joke. The character becomes a cultural unit.

This kind of endurance is different from a book being assigned in school. Assignment can preserve reading. Myth preserves recognition.

Irving achieved both.

The transatlantic market also reveals an institutional problem that would shape American literature for decades: prestige and payment crossed borders differently.

A British author could be famous in the United States while receiving little from unauthorized American editions. An American author could struggle at home and seek British publication partly because London still functioned as a center of literary certification. Copyright law, shipping, publishing capital, review culture, and bookselling all shaped which national literatures could afford to become professional.

Irving’s career therefore belongs to the economics of authorship, not only the aesthetics of national identity.

The writer who can earn enough to keep writing has a different chance of influence from the writer whose work survives only around another occupation. Professionalization does not guarantee greatness, and many great writers worked under severe economic constraint. But a culture that wants literature eventually has to build markets, patronage, institutions, or rights that make sustained literary labor possible.

Irving was early evidence that an American imaginative writer could become a recognizable professional figure across the Atlantic.

That mattered for writers who followed even when their paths differed.

The international success also changed the meaning of American borrowing. A derivative culture merely copies. A confident culture can borrow so openly that the transformed version becomes exportable in return. European folktale structures move into the Catskills; the Headless Horseman eventually travels back out through translations, illustrations, films, and popular culture as an American icon.

Cultural exchange becomes circular.

That circularity is more honest than the myth of national purity.

The United States itself was already a multilingual, multiethnic, Indigenous, African, European, Caribbean, and increasingly continental society. Its literature would never have one uncontaminated source. The interesting question is which mixtures institutions chose to label “American” and which mixtures they ignored or suppressed.

Irving’s success reveals both the possibility and the boundary. His Dutch New York could become picturesque national inheritance while living Indigenous sovereignty was pushed to the edges of the same national imagination.

The folklore machine can preserve one past while obscuring another.

That makes the afterlife of place important. Sleepy Hollow today is not merely a literary setting. The story can support tourism, local branding, Halloween economies, school traditions, historic interpretation, and visual culture. A fictional place becomes an economic and civic asset attached to a real geography.

This is another job books can acquire after publication.

A story can make a place legible to outsiders.

Sometimes that legibility protects local memory. Sometimes it freezes a place inside the one story outsiders know. The actual Hudson Valley contains histories of Indigenous peoples, Dutch and English colonization, slavery, agriculture, industry, immigration, suburbs, environmental change, and modern communities far beyond Irving’s legends.

The literary brand is powerful precisely because it is simpler.

There were serious alternatives in this decade. The first installments of James Fenimore Cooper’s career arrived near its end, with *The Pioneers* appearing in 1823 and *The Last of the Mohicans* just beyond the boundary in 1826. William Cullen Bryant’s poetry helped establish American literary seriousness. Religious publishing, schoolbooks, political writing, and reform literature circulated widely. The Bible remained a far more consequential text in ordinary American life than any new book.

But the premise asks for the most important book *of* the decade, not the most read book *during* it. *The Sketch Book* is the moment when American imaginative writing visibly crosses from local promise into transatlantic legitimacy while simultaneously manufacturing two of the country’s most durable stories.

A counterfactual keeps the “first” claim from becoming another Weems-like origin myth.

If Irving had never published *The Sketch Book*, American literature would still have developed. Cooper, Bryant, Poe, Hawthorne, Emerson, Melville, Black writers, women writers, Indigenous oral and written traditions, newspapers, sermons, popular theater, and immigrant voices would all continue creating forms that did not require Irving’s permission. European readers would eventually encounter American literature through other writers.

The republic did not need one expatriate to acquire a culture.

What changes is the timing and shape of an early proof.

The United States loses a writer who could show both American and British audiences that local material could travel without being stripped of place. It loses two unusually durable folklore machines. Another author may eventually occupy the role, but the early transatlantic literary market receives a different signal about what “American writing” can mean.

Irving’s distinctive contribution is therefore not creation from nothing.

It is exportability.

He made American memory portable.

The international success also complicates the familiar nationalist demand for self-sufficiency. Irving did not prove America had outgrown Europe. He proved an American writer could enter the larger literary world without disappearing into it.

That difference matters.

Countries often become culturally confident when they stop measuring originality by purity. American music, food, language, religion, technology, and literature are full of imported materials transformed through local conditions. The United States has repeatedly turned cultural mixture into a claim of national distinctiveness.

Irving’s career is an early literary example.

He went to Britain partly because of family business, remained through commercial failure, and found the distance creatively useful. Abroad, home became material. The Hudson Valley could be recollected rather than merely inhabited. The Revolution could be converted from living politics into narrative time. European tradition became something to observe and adapt rather than simply obey.

Sometimes a writer needs to leave a country to see what in it can travel.

The book’s continuing popularity can make its original achievement difficult to feel. “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” now arrives pre-saturated with images. We know there will be a horseman. Rip is already an archetype. The stories can seem inevitable because they have been repeated for two centuries.

They were not inevitable.

Someone had to notice that a young republic had enough past to haunt itself.

That may be Irving’s deepest contribution. America did not need a thousand-year castle before it could produce legend. A village, a war within living memory, old family names, a mountain range, and a writer willing to treat local time as worthy of imaginative inheritance were enough.

The country had spent its first decades proving it could exist politically.

*The Sketch Book* helped prove that it could remember itself artistically.

The proof came, fittingly, from overseas.
