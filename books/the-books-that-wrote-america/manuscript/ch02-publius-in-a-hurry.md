# Chapter 2 — Publius in a Hurry

The Constitution arrived in New York before anyone knew whether it would become the Constitution.

That is easy to forget because the document now sits at the beginning of so many civics lessons that ratification can feel like a ceremonial interval between drafting and government. In the autumn of 1787 it was a proposal. It had powerful supporters, formidable opponents, unresolved compromises, and no authority beyond the process by which the states might choose to accept it.

New York was a particularly difficult audience.

The state mattered economically and geographically. Its politics were divided. Governor George Clinton was skeptical of the proposed federal system, and many New Yorkers feared that the Philadelphia convention had produced a national government too distant and too powerful. The Articles of Confederation were visibly weak, but weakness in one arrangement did not prove that every stronger replacement was safe.

Into that argument came a pseudonym: Publius.

Beginning in October 1787, essays signed with that name appeared in New York newspapers. The pace was punishing. Alexander Hamilton organized the project and wrote most of the essays. James Madison contributed a large group, including several that would become the most famous. John Jay wrote a smaller number before illness interrupted his work. Together they produced eighty-five essays in roughly seven months, explaining and defending the proposed Constitution while ratification remained a live political fight.

The essays were journalism before they were scripture.

That fact should change how we read them. Hamilton, Madison, and Jay were not sitting outside politics creating a neutral manual for future generations. They wanted a result. They were advocates. Their claims were shaped by the objections they needed to answer, the audience they hoped to persuade, and the institutional design they were trying to get adopted. The work’s later authority can hide its original urgency.

The first bound volume appeared in March 1788, the second in May. The collection was titled *The Federalist: A Collection of Essays, Written in Favour of the New Constitution*. By then the essays had already been circulating individually. Turning them into books did something useful: it converted a newspaper campaign into a coherent object that could travel beyond the immediate sequence of publication and survive the ratification battle that created it.

This chapter’s decade, 1786 through 1795, contains the making of the federal government itself. The Constitutional Convention met in 1787. Ratification battles followed. The new government began operations in 1789. A Bill of Rights was proposed and ratified. Hamilton’s financial program began building federal fiscal capacity. Political factions hardened. The Whiskey Rebellion tested federal authority. Washington’s presidency established practices that would outlive him.

Picking one book from those years is almost unfair. The decade generated the operating system of the country.

*The Federalist* wins because it became the most durable book-length explanation of what that operating system was supposed to do—and because Americans have spent more than two centuries using, disputing, and sometimes overusing that explanation.

The immediate political problem was straightforward enough to state: the Articles of Confederation had left the national government weak in taxation, commerce, enforcement, and other basic capacities. The states retained substantial sovereignty. Congress could request money and depend on the states to provide it. Interstate commercial conflicts were difficult to manage. The inability to respond effectively to economic and political instability alarmed many leaders.

Yet stronger central government carried its own risks. Americans had just fought an empire. The proposed Constitution created a president, a federal judiciary, taxing power, military powers, and a national legislature whose authority would reach individuals more directly than Congress under the Articles had. Skepticism was not irrational. The question was how to build power without rebuilding the thing a revolution had rejected.

The Federalist essays tried to answer that question issue by issue.

Some arguments are famous because later generations extracted them from their ratification context and made them part of the American language of government. Federalist No. 10, written by Madison, examines faction and the possibility that a large republic can make dangerous majorities harder to assemble and sustain. Federalist No. 51 discusses the internal structure of government and the need to set power against power. Federalist No. 70 defends energy in the executive. Federalist No. 78, by Hamilton, explains the judiciary and develops an argument for courts refusing to enforce laws contrary to the Constitution.

These essays are now quoted in court opinions, law reviews, political speeches, classrooms, confirmation hearings, and arguments about what the Constitution means. That afterlife was not guaranteed in 1788.

The immediate record of persuasion is mixed. New York eventually ratified the Constitution in July 1788 by a narrow vote, after enough other states had already ratified that the new government was going forward. It would be too neat to credit *The Federalist* for producing the result. Ratification involved conventions, local politics, economic interests, newspaper debates, personal networks, fears of isolation, promises of amendments, and events elsewhere. The essays were one part of a larger campaign.

Their greater triumph came later.

Once the Constitution survived ratification, Americans needed ways to argue about the government they had created. The text of the Constitution is short. Shortness is one source of its endurance, but also of endless conflict. Terms such as executive power, commerce, necessary and proper, due process, and equal protection would accumulate meaning through politics, precedent, institutional practice, and amendment. The founding text does not contain a commentary track.

*The Federalist* became one.

That status brings both value and danger. The authors were close to the drafting and ratification of the Constitution. Madison had played a central role at Philadelphia. Hamilton had been a delegate and a relentless advocate of stronger national authority. Jay brought diplomatic and legal experience. Their explanations illuminate the problems the Constitution was intended to address and the reasoning of influential supporters.

But the essays were not minutes of a single national mind. They were campaign documents written by three men. Other Federalists disagreed with them on particulars. Anti-Federalists read the Constitution differently and identified dangers that mattered enough to produce demands for a Bill of Rights. The ratifying public did not sign a contract stating that every sentence by Publius would govern future interpretation.

Later legal argument sometimes treats *The Federalist* as if it were a privileged answer key hidden behind the Constitution. That can flatten the historical argument the book preserves. Its most interesting quality is not that it eliminates ambiguity. It is that the country was ambiguous about itself from the beginning.

Consider faction.

Madison’s argument in Federalist No. 10 is often taught as a clean piece of institutional design: because the causes of faction cannot be removed without destroying liberty, a well-constructed large republic can limit the effects of faction by multiplying interests and making oppressive majorities harder to coordinate. The argument remains intellectually powerful.

Yet the early republic quickly developed organized political conflict more intense than many founders expected. Hamilton and Madison themselves became central figures on opposing sides of the first party system. Newspapers turned vicious. Foreign policy split coalitions. The Whiskey Rebellion tested federal enforcement. The Alien and Sedition Acts arrived within a decade of the Constitution’s operation, followed by the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions.

The theory did not fail simply because politics remained political. It did reveal that institutional arrangements do not remove the need for political judgment. A structure designed to channel conflict becomes the terrain on which conflict is fought.

That is one reason the book has endured. It is not merely a celebration of constitutional machinery. It contains arguments about recurring problems: scale, representation, ambition, institutional incentives, concentrated power, public opinion, majority rule, minority rights, executive capacity, judicial independence, and the instability of confederations. Those problems are not antique.

The pseudonym matters too.

Publius Valerius Publicola was associated with the founding of the Roman Republic. Eighteenth-century American political writers regularly adopted classical names. The practice could create continuity with republican traditions, protect or partially veil identity, and shift attention toward argument. No one seriously following the New York debate remained forever ignorant of the likely writers, but the collective pseudonym gave the series one voice even though three minds produced it.

That single voice was partly an illusion.

Hamilton and Madison would later disagree profoundly about federal power. Their later political careers can make some passages of *The Federalist* look like messages sent from an earlier universe. They had agreed on ratification before they agreed on what the new government should become in operation.

This is not hypocrisy in the simple sense. Coalitions are built around immediate objectives. The Constitution was a framework capacious enough to hold people with divergent future visions. The act of defending it together did not settle the arguments they would later have inside it.

The book therefore documents a foundational American habit: people can agree on the legitimacy of a system while disagreeing fiercely about the system’s correct use.

The speed of composition amplifies the achievement. Eighty-five essays are substantial by any standard. They addressed military questions, taxation, representation, federalism, separation of powers, the presidency, courts, the Senate, the House, and the defects of earlier confederacies. Hamilton wrote at a rate that still startles editors. Madison was simultaneously participating in political maneuvering around ratification. Jay’s illness reduced his contribution, forcing the others to carry more of the load.

Speed did not prevent the collection from becoming one of the most continuously examined works of American political thought. It may have helped give the prose its argumentative pressure. This was not a retrospective treatise written after the system had settled. The authors were trying to explain an aircraft while asking the passengers to approve its construction.

That urgency creates revealing moments of optimism. Publius repeatedly argues that the proposed structure contains safeguards against dangers that later Americans would experience anyway. The Senate did not end demagoguery. The separation of powers did not prevent every expansion of executive authority. Federalism did not resolve conflict between national and state sovereignty. Judicial independence did not remove courts from political struggle.

None of those observations makes the original reasoning useless. They make the book historical evidence of what intelligent advocates expected institutions to accomplish.

A nation should be wary of treating founders as prophets. They were designers working under uncertainty. *The Federalist* is valuable partly because it allows us to see the design assumptions before two centuries of consequences accumulated around them.

The book’s legal afterlife began early. Courts and political actors cited the essays as evidence about constitutional meaning. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the Supreme Court increasingly drew on *The Federalist*, especially in cases touching federalism, executive power, separation of powers, and judicial authority. Scholars have debated how much interpretive weight those citations should carry. Originalist and non-originalist approaches alike can find useful historical material in the essays while disagreeing about its authority.

This creates an unusual literary condition: the book is simultaneously historical source, political advocacy, constitutional theory, and legal ammunition.

Most books cannot change the practical meaning of later controversies merely by being quoted. *The Federalist* can. A sentence written to persuade New Yorkers in 1788 may reappear in a twenty-first-century Supreme Court opinion about an institution its authors could not have encountered in its modern form. The temporal distance is enormous. The rhetorical bridge remains active.

That is importance of a peculiar kind.

There were strong alternatives for this decade. The Anti-Federalist writings deserve far more attention than the shorthand label often gives them. Mercy Otis Warren’s political work and other republican writings exposed anxieties about consolidated power. Thomas Jefferson’s *Notes on the State of Virginia*, published in the 1780s, combined natural history, politics, agriculture, race, slavery, and self-description in ways that reveal the contradictions of the new nation. The first American novels began testing another form of national imagination.

But the decade from 1786 to 1795 is the decade in which Americans built the federal architecture they still inhabit. The book that most forcefully accompanied that construction became embedded in the architecture itself.

It also supplies a counterweight to the myth that founding was a moment of consensus.

Read the Constitution alone and it can look serene. Read the ratification debate and it becomes noisy again. People worried. They suspected motives. They demanded amendments. They argued about standing armies, taxation, aristocracy, executive power, local autonomy, representation, and the fate of liberty in a large republic. The political system emerged from dispute, not from a room full of demigods speaking in one voice.

*The Federalist* is one side of that dispute, and its dominance in later memory can obscure the other side. A responsible reading therefore requires bringing the opponents back into the room. The Bill of Rights itself is evidence that Anti-Federalist pressure mattered. Ratification was secured in several states amid recommendations or expectations of amendments. The victorious text did not make the losing arguments irrelevant.

The same is true in modern politics. The strongest constitutional traditions are often built from arguments between rival fears: fear of governmental weakness and fear of governmental overreach, fear of majority tyranny and fear of elite veto, fear of fragmentation and fear of consolidation. The Constitution contains compromises among such fears. *The Federalist* teaches one to see institutional design as an attempt to arrange them.

That may be the book’s most durable lesson, although it was not a slogan its authors could have used in the campaign.

A constitution is not self-enforcing wisdom. It is a set of offices, permissions, prohibitions, procedures, incentives, norms, and expectations occupied by human beings. Hamilton and Madison understood enough about ambition to design around it. They could not design away history.

The essays survived because later Americans kept needing to ask what the machine was for.

A counterfactual clarifies what kind of influence that survival represents.

If the essays had never been written, New York might still have ratified. By the time its convention voted, the Constitution had already secured enough state ratifications to take effect, and New Yorkers faced the consequences of remaining outside a new federal union. Hamilton and other supporters would still have campaigned. Anti-Federalists would still have demanded protections. The Bill of Rights would still have had powerful political support from multiple states.

The federal government does not depend on Publius for existence.

What changes is the archive through which later generations interpret the choice.

Without *The Federalist*, judges and scholars lose an unusually systematic set of contemporaneous arguments from prominent supporters. The Constitution remains. Ratification debates remain. Convention records, letters, newspapers, state debates, Anti-Federalist essays, and later practice remain. Interpretation may become less dominated by three advocates and more dependent on that wider, messier record.

That possibility should make modern readers careful about the collection’s authority.

The book’s success at surviving can distort the historical field. Texts preserved, collected, indexed, cited, and assigned become easier to treat as representative than texts scattered across newspapers or less convenient archives. Availability creates interpretive gravity.

Publius is therefore powerful partly because Publius is easy to find.

This is an institutional afterlife every canon creates. A source enters the syllabus. Search tools index it. Courts cite earlier courts citing it. Law students learn which numbers are famous. Political writers reach for the same passages. Repetition converts one historical voice into a default reference point.

The solution is not to stop reading *The Federalist*.

It is to read its rivals beside it.

A constitution born from argument should be interpreted through argument.

There is a final irony. Hamilton hoped the series would help New Yorkers ratify the Constitution. It did that work, if incompletely and alongside many other forces. But the larger victory was one no campaign strategist could safely plan: the argument outlived the election.

Newspapers are written against deadlines. Political persuasion is usually consumed by the event it serves. Yesterday’s editorial becomes packing material. The Federalist essays should have been vulnerable to that fate. The ratification fight ended. The pseudonym could have retired.

Instead Publius became a permanent witness.

The United States has changed almost beyond recognition since 1788. The electorate expanded from a restricted eighteenth-century franchise to mass citizenship. Slavery was abolished through civil war and constitutional amendment. Senators became directly elected. Women gained the vote. Administrative agencies grew. Parties professionalized. The country became continental, industrial, urban, global, digital, and nuclear. The federal budget, military, judiciary, presidency, and bureaucracy acquired scale the ratifiers did not inhabit.

Through all of that, lawyers and citizens kept returning to a hurried newspaper campaign from New York.

The second decade of the American story therefore belongs to a book that began as an effort to get a document approved and became a way of arguing about that document forever.

The country had declared itself in the previous chapter.

Now it needed footnotes.
