# Chapter 15 — The Failure That Became Required Reading

When F. Scott Fitzgerald died in 1940, *The Great Gatsby* was not the book that would have made him confident about immortality.

It had been published fifteen years earlier, in 1925, to respectable reviews but disappointing sales compared with the success Fitzgerald had already experienced. Copies remained unsold. His literary reputation declined during the 1930s. He struggled financially, drank heavily, worked in Hollywood, and died at forty-four while writing another novel.

Then the dead author’s failed book went to war.

During the Second World War, the Council on Books in Wartime distributed special Armed Services Editions—small paperbacks designed to fit into soldiers’ pockets—to American service members. *The Great Gatsby* was among the titles selected. More than 150,000 copies of the novel were distributed in that format.

A book that had failed to become a mass success in peacetime suddenly acquired a vast new audience in uniform.

After the war, Fitzgerald’s reputation revived. Scholars, critics, teachers, publishers, and students turned *Gatsby* into one of the central novels of the American canon. It became a high-school staple, a college staple, a cultural shorthand for wealth, reinvention, doomed aspiration, and something called “the American Dream” that the novel itself never uses as a slogan.

The delayed explosion is why *The Great Gatsby* wins the decade from 1916 through 1925.

Importance does not have to arrive on schedule.

That is one of the book’s larger lessons.

The novel’s plot is famously compact. Nick Carraway, a Midwesterner and Yale graduate, moves to Long Island in 1922 to work in the bond business. His cousin Daisy Buchanan lives across the bay with her wealthy husband Tom. Nick’s mysterious neighbor, Jay Gatsby, throws extravagant parties attended by people who know his house better than they know him.

Gatsby has built the performance for Daisy.

He knew her before the war, when he was a young officer without the wealth to match the world she occupied. She married Tom. Gatsby later acquired money through opaque and criminal channels, remade his identity, purchased a mansion across the water from Daisy, and organized his life around the possibility that enough wealth, display, and will can reverse time.

The green light at the end of Daisy’s dock became one of American literature’s most overloaded objects because it survives every interpretation placed on it.

Desire, distance, money, future, past, America.

The light can carry them all.

The danger is turning the novel into a civics poster about the American Dream. Fitzgerald’s book is stranger and more morally specific than the classroom phrase often allows.

Gatsby’s self-invention is genuinely impressive. Born James Gatz to poor farmers in North Dakota, he creates a new identity and pursues it with enormous discipline. America has always had room in its mythology for the person who refuses inherited position. Benjamin Franklin helped write an early version. Horatio Alger popularized another. Immigration itself repeatedly enacted the possibility of starting over in a different social world.

Gatsby is the dream with fraudulent paperwork.

He becomes rich, but money does not erase origin. Tom Buchanan understands the class hierarchy more clearly than Gatsby does. Old money can treat new money as vulgar even when the balances are comparable. Wealth has dialects.

Daisy is not simply the prize at the end of Gatsby’s climb. She is embedded in a class system he mistakes for a personal romance. He wants the person and the social legitimacy she represents.

That confusion makes the tragedy American.

The 1920s provided ideal conditions for the story. The First World War had disrupted lives and accelerated social change. Consumer credit expanded. Automobiles altered geography and courtship. Radio was creating national mass culture. Advertising grew more sophisticated. Cities attracted migrants and immigrants. Prohibition produced both law enforcement and profitable illegal markets. Stock speculation intensified. Women had gained the constitutional right to vote through the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920, though political and social equality remained far from complete.

The decade called itself modern.

Fitzgerald became one of its public representatives.

His first novel, *This Side of Paradise*, published in 1920, made him famous. He and Zelda Fitzgerald became symbols of youthful glamour and excess. Fitzgerald helped popularize the phrase “Jazz Age” for the era, even though the Black musical innovation behind jazz came from cultural worlds far more complex than the white literary society that consumed it.

His public persona benefited from the very machinery *Gatsby* distrusts: celebrity, luxury, reinvention, attention.

The writer and the book were implicated in their subject.

That may explain some of the novel’s sharpness. Fitzgerald knew the seduction of wealth from close range. He also knew the anxiety of not quite belonging among the people who possessed it without effort.

The Buchanans have the moral insulation of inherited privilege. They can damage other lives and retreat into money. Gatsby, for all his corruption and delusion, still believes in something. Tom believes in possession.

But the novel’s critique of class does not make it socially comprehensive.

Its treatment of race reveals the white anxieties of the period more than it explores Black American life. Tom repeats racist pseudo-intellectual fears about white displacement, drawing on the era’s nativist and eugenic ideas. The novel exposes him as boorish and paranoid, but its Black characters remain largely peripheral or caricatured within Nick’s gaze. The Harlem Renaissance was unfolding at the same time, producing literature and art that Fitzgerald’s fictional world barely understands.

The “American” in *Gatsby* is therefore partial.

That limitation matters because the novel later became so central to national self-description. When a school treats one book as the book about American aspiration, the boundaries of its social world deserve examination.

In 1925, Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Jean Toomer, Countee Cullen, Alain Locke, and other Black writers were helping define the New Negro movement and Harlem Renaissance. Locke’s anthology *The New Negro* appeared the same year as *Gatsby*. Its claim on the decade is serious.

A different canon could choose it and tell a different America.

Why does Gatsby win?

Because after 1945 the novel became one of the most efficient machines Americans have for interpreting success.

Its afterlife entered education at mass scale. Students who knew nothing about bootlegging, Long Island geography, or 1920s finance learned to associate Gatsby with a national tension: the admirable hunger to become more and the destructive belief that money can purchase every form of belonging.

The book made aspiration tragic without making aspiration contemptible.

That balance explains much of its durability.

A pure anticapitalist novel could condemn Gatsby’s wealth. A pure success story could celebrate his ascent. Fitzgerald makes the wealth magnificent enough to desire and hollow enough to fear.

The parties work.

People come.

The house glows.

Daisy is impressed.

None of it can restore the past.

The novel’s revival also exposes how canons are built through institutions rather than pure literary survival.

The Armed Services Editions mattered. Postwar scholars mattered. Biographers mattered. Fitzgerald’s friends and editors helped preserve his work and reputation. University courses incorporated him. High schools assigned the book. Affordable paperback editions kept it in circulation. Film adaptations refreshed public awareness. The copyright status and publishing history shaped availability.

A book does not “stand the test of time” by standing still.

People keep carrying it.

This institutional rescue is particularly striking because Fitzgerald had experienced the opposite process in life. Literary fashion moved away from him. The Depression made Jazz Age glamour feel frivolous. His books went out of print or sold poorly. Critics could treat him as a relic of the twenties.

Then history revised the decade.

After another world war, the 1920s could be seen as a completed era rather than a failed present. Fitzgerald became its witness. *Gatsby*’s shortness made it teachable. Its symbols invited interpretation. Its prose rewarded rereading. Its plot could be summarized without exhausting it.

School systems love books with those properties.

The novel’s teachability became part of its greatness in public life.

This should not be embarrassing. Education is one of the main engines through which a society decides what cultural memory survives. But it should make us skeptical of the mystical idea that classics naturally rise to the top.

No book rises without hands.

The wartime distribution is the most literal example. The Armed Services Editions program shipped millions of books across genres to service members around the world. The books were physically designed for military life: lightweight, portable, disposable. Their purpose included morale, entertainment, education, and connection to civilian culture.

Gatsby found readers in an environment unimaginably different from its mansions.

A soldier in Europe or the Pacific could carry a book about a veteran who returned from war and tried to reinvent himself in a booming America.

The resonance did not need to be planned.

That is how afterlives work.

The novel’s later association with the “American Dream” also deserves skepticism. Historian James Truslow Adams popularized the phrase in *The Epic of America* in 1931, six years after *Gatsby*. Later readers retroactively made Fitzgerald’s novel one of the phrase’s definitive literary treatments.

The book helped define a concept whose famous label arrived afterward.

This is another form of cultural editing. We read the past through language invented later, then forget the sequence.

What Gatsby actually desires is more particular than generic success. He does not simply want a better life. He wants Daisy to erase the intervening years and confirm that the identity he built has conquered the social order that once excluded him.

He wants the past to recognize his future.

That is impossible even before the car crash.

The novel understands a deep feature of status: positional goods cannot be democratized by individual striving. If everyone becomes rich, old money can invent a new distinction. If everyone buys the same luxury, luxury moves. Social hierarchies survive by changing signals.

Gatsby has money and still lacks the thing money was supposed to signify.

That insight is more relevant in an age of social media, personal branding, luxury signaling, and visible wealth than a generic warning about materialism. The tools of performance have expanded. The longing to be recognized by a desired audience has not.

Modern Gatsby figures do not need mansions. They can build profiles.

The book also predicts the instability of attention. Gatsby’s parties are crowded while he is useful as an experience. His funeral is nearly empty. Spectacle generates attendance, not relationship.

Fitzgerald understood something about celebrity before celebrity culture reached industrial scale.

There were major competitors from the decade. Willa Cather published *My Ántonia* in 1918, creating one of the great books of immigration and the American Plains. Sinclair Lewis’s *Main Street* appeared in 1920 and *Babbitt* in 1922, skewering small-city conformity and middle-class boosterism. Theodore Dreiser’s *An American Tragedy* appeared in 1925, another devastating account of ambition and class. Alain Locke’s *The New Negro* helped announce a major Black cultural movement. John Dos Passos and Ernest Hemingway were emerging.

Cather may offer a more humane America. Locke may offer a more historically inclusive one. Lewis had greater immediate commercial force.

Gatsby’s postwar afterlife overwhelms them in national shorthand.

The book became a mirror Americans hold up whenever prosperity starts to look like performance.

Its importance is therefore partly retrospective. That may feel like cheating. A book of 1925 wins because of what happened in the 1940s, 1950s, and after.

But all importance is retrospective.

*Common Sense* looks decisive because independence followed. *The Federalist* matters because the Constitution endured. Weems matters because his myth spread. Darwin matters because science developed through and beyond him. A book’s decade gives us the launch point, not the full measurement period.

Gatsby simply makes the delay visible.

The lesson for any living writer is humbling and useless in equal measure.

You cannot reliably know your afterlife.

Sales can mislead. Reviews can mislead. The cultural mood can mislead. A book may need an institution, a war, a teacher, a technological shift, or an unborn generation before its central metaphor becomes necessary.

Fitzgerald died before the verdict changed.

That fact gives the novel’s final obsession with the unreachable future an additional sadness the author did not write into it.

He spent years believing the book had failed to reach enough readers.

The future was already coming back for it.
