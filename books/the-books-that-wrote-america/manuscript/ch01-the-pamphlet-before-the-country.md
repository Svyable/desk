# Chapter 1 — The Pamphlet Before the Country

On January 10, 1776, there was no United States of America to buy *Common Sense*.

There were colonies in rebellion, an army around Boston, a king who had already declared his American subjects in revolt, a Continental Congress that was behaving more like a government than many of its delegates were prepared to admit, and a great deal of uncertainty about what any of this was ultimately for. Armed conflict had begun. Political separation had not yet become the official destination.

That gap matters. Revolutions acquire inevitability in retrospect. Once the Declaration of Independence exists, it becomes easy to imagine that the colonies had been heading toward it all along. They had not. Many colonists opposed British policies without wanting to become citizens of a new nation. Resistance could be vigorous while loyalty remained psychologically and politically available. Reconciliation was not a ridiculous possibility simply because later events made it impossible.

Then a pamphlet appeared in Philadelphia with no author’s name on the title page.

Thomas Paine had been in America barely more than a year. He was English, in his late thirties, and had already lived enough unsuccessful lives to make a respectable person nervous. He had been a stay-maker, excise officer, schoolteacher, and shopkeeper. His first marriage ended when his wife died. A second marriage ended in separation. He had been dismissed from government service, reinstated, then dismissed again. He came to Philadelphia carrying a letter of introduction from Benjamin Franklin, whom he had met in London.

Nothing in that biography announces the future author of one of the most consequential political texts in American history. That is part of the story. Paine was not an established colonial patriarch explaining independence from a position of inherited authority. He was a recent arrival with a talent for making arguments sound as if they belonged to whoever had just read them.

He found work in Philadelphia journalism, becoming associated with the *Pennsylvania Magazine*. The city was a good place to discover the compressed geography of revolution. Delegates, printers, merchants, artisans, soldiers, intellectuals, loyalists, radicals, and cautious men of property were all arguing within reach of one another. Print moved those arguments outward.

Pamphlets were suited to political uncertainty. They were cheaper and faster than books, more durable than speeches, more portable than newspapers, and capable of being reprinted beyond the control of the original publisher. The years before and during the Revolution produced a dense pamphlet culture. Political thought did not remain in legislatures. It moved through print shops and taverns, was read aloud to people who could not read it themselves, was excerpted, answered, mocked, and forwarded.

Paine entered that system with unusually good timing and unusually direct prose.

*Common Sense* did several things at once. It attacked monarchy as an institution rather than merely complaining about the current king. It argued that an island ruling a continent was absurd. It challenged the emotional idea of Britain as a protective parent. It presented independence not as reckless escalation but as the practical resolution of a relationship already broken by bloodshed. And it tried to move the reader’s frame from a dispute inside the British Empire toward the founding of something new.

The tone was part of the technology. Political theory in the eighteenth century could arrive wrapped in classical references, constitutional history, legalism, and the expectations of an educated audience. Paine could use history, scripture, analogy, ridicule, and plain assertion without making the reader feel that permission from a learned class was required before forming a political judgment. The title itself was a move. Independence was not presented as exotic theory. It was “common sense.”

That rhetorical move irritated opponents for the same reason it empowered supporters. Calling an argument common sense does not merely state a conclusion. It places the burden of embarrassment on the dissenter. Loyalist James Chalmers answered with a pamphlet titled *Plain Truth*. Newspapers printed criticism. The fight over independence became, among other things, a fight over which position deserved to be treated as obvious.

The circulation was extraordinary. The Library of Congress has described more than half a million copies appearing in twenty-five editions in the first year. Exact eighteenth-century circulation numbers are difficult to reconstruct with modern precision, and the colonial population was a fraction of the present United States. Even allowing for uncertainty in historical estimates, the scale relative to the reading public was enormous. Copies also had lives beyond individual purchasers. A pamphlet could be shared, borrowed, read aloud, or reprinted in a newspaper. Reach exceeded sales.

The printing history itself was messy, as important printing histories often are. Robert Bell issued the first edition in Philadelphia. Paine and Bell quarreled over money and accounting. Paine had intended proceeds to support the revolutionary cause, and later editions involved different printers. The work escaped any single commercial channel almost immediately. That loss of control was not a defect in its political effectiveness. It was one reason the argument traveled.

This is an early example of a pattern that will recur throughout this book: a consequential text is rarely just a text. It is an argument plus a distribution network plus a moment in which large numbers of people are deciding what they are willing to believe next.

Paine had not invented the case for independence. Colonial radicals had developed arguments about rights, sovereignty, representation, natural law, and imperial power over years of conflict with Britain. Battles at Lexington and Concord had already occurred. The Continental Army already existed. George III had rejected the Olive Branch Petition. The political weather had changed before *Common Sense* went on sale.

That makes the book more interesting, not less.

A weak version of literary history gives a book magical causation. Someone writes. Everyone reads. History turns. Real political change has more authors than that. Paine’s importance lies in compression. He took forces already gathering and made a destination easier to name. He gave readers a portable case for severing the relationship rather than repairing it.

There is a difference between creating a desire and making a desire socially speakable. Political movements often accelerate when people discover that an idea they considered private, dangerous, or premature has become discussable in public. Print can help create that discovery. A widely circulating pamphlet tells a reader two things at once: here is an argument, and other people are encountering it too.

That second message is difficult to measure but central to mass politics.

Imagine the problem facing an independence-minded colonist early in 1776. The military conflict may already have persuaded you that relations with Britain are collapsing. Yet declaring independence would require a larger leap: abandoning inherited loyalty, accepting uncertainty about foreign alliances and internal government, risking treason, and persuading neighbors that resistance had become nationhood. A private conviction is not enough. Revolutions need expectations about other people’s convictions.

A fast-selling pamphlet is evidence of attention. Reprints are evidence of demand. Public rebuttals are evidence that opponents consider the argument worth fighting. The controversy itself tells readers that the boundary of acceptable discussion has moved.

Paine also understood that emotional inheritance had to be attacked, not merely constitutional arrangements. Britain was commonly imagined through family language: the mother country, the parent protecting its colonies. Paine inverted the metaphor. A parent does not make war on its children. An island had no natural right to rule a continent. Europe, not England, was the parent stock of America. The old language of belonging could be made to sound illogical once repeated in a different frame.

His attack on monarchy went farther than some colonial leaders were initially comfortable going. Grievances could be directed at ministers or Parliament while preserving reverence for the king. Paine made the crown itself part of the problem. That mattered because independence required more than separation from a set of policies. It required Americans to imagine legitimate political order without a monarch at the center.

He was not designing the Constitution that would eventually emerge. Some of his institutional ideas would not become American practice. But political writing can be decisive without furnishing the final blueprint. Sometimes its work is demolition: removing the assumption that an inherited arrangement is the only arrangement possible.

The timing between *Common Sense* and the Declaration is irresistible, and therefore dangerous. The pamphlet appeared in January. By spring, colonial instructions and public opinion were moving. Richard Henry Lee introduced the resolution for independence in June. Congress adopted independence in July. The sequence tempts us to draw a straight arrow.

The safer conclusion is also stronger. *Common Sense* arrived during the decisive months in which independence moved from a radical position toward official policy, and it became one of the most widely circulated arguments for that move. It did not make the war, invent republican thought, or force Congress to vote. It changed the rhetorical environment in which those things were happening.

That is enough.

The first decade of this book, 1776 through 1785, contains formidable competitors. Adam Smith’s *The Wealth of Nations* also appeared in 1776 and would eventually become foundational to economic thought in the new republic. State constitutions were being written. Revolutionary sermons, military accounts, political essays, almanacs, and textbooks circulated through a society trying to govern itself. Noah Webster began publishing educational works that would help make American language more distinctly American.

But this decade begins with a country that does not exist and ends with a country discovering that independence is easier to declare than to govern. The book that wins should be close to the first transformation.

*Common Sense* is.

Its afterlife also reveals something uncomfortable about national memory. Americans celebrate declarations. We are less attentive to the infrastructure that makes declarations politically possible. The famous parchment sits in a museum. The pamphlet looks disposable. Yet disposable media can move faster than monuments.

Paine’s work belonged to a commercial print ecosystem full of competing printers, uneven accounting, unauthorized or loosely controlled reprinting, newspaper excerpting, and personal circulation. There was no centralized communications strategy for “America” because America had not yet become a centralized political fact. The argument propagated through a network whose participants had different motives. Printers wanted sales. activists wanted persuasion. Readers wanted information. opponents wanted rebuttal. Each motive increased visibility.

That is more modern than it first appears.

We tend to imagine virality as an invention of digital networks. The speed is new. The social mechanism is not. A piece of writing enters a contested environment, acquires copies, produces rebuttals, becomes a marker of affiliation, and reaches people partly because other people are talking about it. The medium changes. The recursive attention does not.

There is another reason *Common Sense* deserves the first place: Paine helped establish an American tradition in which outsiders can become unusually forceful interpreters of the country. Recent arrivals often see assumptions that natives have stopped seeing. Their distance can produce simplification, error, gratitude, anger, or clarity. Paine did not inherit colonial deference in the same way men born into the British-American elite did. He arrived late enough to treat some inherited structures as choices.

This tradition will recur. America is repeatedly explained to itself by people crossing boundaries—national, racial, class, regional, religious, educational. Some of the most important books are written by authors who are close enough to understand the system and far enough from its center to describe what insiders normalize.

Paine’s later life complicates any attempt to freeze him as a saint of 1776. He continued into revolutionary politics, controversy, religion, and conflict. He participated intellectually and politically in the age of Atlantic revolutions, supported the French Revolution while opposing the execution of Louis XVI, was imprisoned in France, attacked organized religion in *The Age of Reason*, and eventually returned to the United States with a reputation far more contested than the heroic image attached to *Common Sense*. The new nation did not spend his remaining life in permanent gratitude.

That too is useful. Books often survive their authors’ reputations. The work that becomes foundational may be detached from the later person, simplified into a national story, or remembered by people who would dislike much else the writer believed.

The phrase “common sense” survived even more thoroughly. It remains one of the most powerful claims in American political speech. Policies of every ideology are advertised as common sense. Candidates promise common-sense solutions. Commentators accuse opponents of lacking it. The phrase is attractive because it denies that politics is really politics. If my proposal is common sense, your objection must be ideology, extremism, corruption, or stupidity.

Paine did not invent that human temptation. He demonstrated its political force at a moment when the stakes could hardly have been higher.

There is an irony in choosing *Common Sense* as the first “book” in a history of books. It was a pamphlet, physically modest and meant for immediate argument. It did not need to wait for posterity to decide it was literature. It was useful before it was canonical.

That distinction is central to this project. The important book of a decade is not necessarily the one later critics admire most. It is the one that entered the bloodstream of American life most consequentially.

In 1776, the argument had a practical target. Paine wanted readers to stop treating reconciliation as the default and independence as the dangerous abstraction. The danger was already present. Blood had already been shed. The question was what political structure could honestly describe the reality on the ground.

A pamphlet cannot create a country. It can help create the sentence in which a country becomes imaginable.

Before Americans could declare that “these United Colonies are, and of Right ought to be Free and Independent States,” many of them had to become comfortable saying the word independence without lowering their voices.

*Common Sense* helped make the word ordinary.

That is an astonishing amount of work for forty-odd pages.
