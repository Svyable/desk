# Chapter 16 — China on the American Nightstand

Pearl S. Buck wrote one of the most influential American novels about China before most of her American readers had met a Chinese person.

That imbalance gave the book unusual power.

*The Good Earth* was published in 1931. It follows Wang Lung, a poor farmer, and O-lan, the formerly enslaved woman he marries, through hunger, migration, land ownership, family growth, wealth, desire, and decline. The plot is intimate and elemental. Soil, food, childbirth, work, money, drought, flood, sex, status, and inheritance carry more weight than grand politics.

American readers embraced it on a huge scale. The novel became the best-selling book in the United States for 1931 and 1932, won the Pulitzer Prize, and helped Buck become one of the most famous American writers of her generation. In 1938 she received the Nobel Prize in Literature.

The timing matters.

The United States was deep in the Great Depression. Americans were thinking about land, hunger, debt, displacement, family survival, and economic insecurity with an intensity that made Wang Lung’s world foreign and recognizable at the same time.

The setting was China.

The fear of losing everything was not.

That bridge wins the decade from 1926 through 1935.

Buck’s own biography made her a highly unusual mediator. Born in West Virginia in 1892 to Presbyterian missionary parents, she grew up largely in China. Chinese language, people, customs, politics, and landscapes were not exotic material she first encountered as an adult traveler. They formed much of her childhood environment. She later studied in the United States, returned to China, married agricultural economist John Lossing Buck, taught, and lived through periods of political upheaval.

That experience gave her a depth of familiarity rare among white American writers of China.

It did not make her Chinese.

The distinction is essential because *The Good Earth* became authoritative in a culture that offered relatively few mainstream publishing opportunities to Chinese and Chinese American writers. Buck’s proximity gave her insight. Her identity gave her access to American readers and institutions that did not distribute authority equally.

She became a translator of human experience across an unequal cultural relationship.

The novel’s great achievement is that it makes ordinary Chinese rural life ordinary.

That sentence sounds smaller than it is.

Western representations of China had long oscillated among fascination, missionary concern, commercial ambition, racial fear, Orientalist fantasy, and imperial competition. In the United States, Chinese immigrants had been subjected to severe discrimination and violence. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 had created the first major federal immigration restriction aimed at a specific nationality and remained in force when *The Good Earth* appeared. Anti-Chinese stereotypes were embedded in politics and popular culture.

Buck gave millions of readers a Chinese family whose motives did not require exotic explanation.

Wang Lung wants land, security, sons, respect, sexual satisfaction, and a future for his family. O-lan works, bears children, suffers, calculates, and preserves the household through crises. Their world contains customs unfamiliar to many American readers, but the novel does not present humanity itself as foreign.

This can sound obvious now.

It was political in effect then.

The book’s empathy should not be romanticized. Buck wrote through her own perspective and through conventions of English-language realism. Her representation of Chinese speech is stylized. Critics have debated whether the novel simplifies Chinese culture for Western readers, universalizes peasant life, reproduces certain stereotypes, or gives insufficient depth to characters such as O-lan even while making her labor central.

Chinese and Chinese American responses have never been uniform.

Some readers and scholars have valued Buck for humanizing Chinese people in the American imagination and resisting crude racism. Others have objected that a white American author became the West’s trusted interpreter of China while writers of Chinese descent struggled for equivalent recognition.

Both observations can be true.

Representation can improve the terms of perception and still be unequal in who gets to represent.

The novel’s enormous popularity magnified that tension. If a few thousand people read a book, its simplifications have limited reach. If millions do, the book can become a template through which readers interpret a country of hundreds of millions.

No novel can carry that burden responsibly because no novel can represent China.

*The Good Earth* represented one imagined family in a historical rural world. American culture sometimes asked it to do more.

That overextension is part of the book’s importance.

The Depression-era response reveals another layer. Wang Lung’s attachment to land resonated with American traditions of agrarian virtue. Thomas Jefferson had idealized the independent yeoman farmer. Nineteenth-century settlement mythology linked land ownership to citizenship and self-sufficiency, while ignoring the Native dispossession and unequal access that made much white landownership possible. By the 1930s, Dust Bowl migration and farm foreclosure had exposed the fragility of the agrarian ideal.

Wang Lung’s belief that land is the durable foundation of family wealth felt both distant and familiar.

The novel does not make land sentimental. Land can fail through drought or flood. Work is exhausting. Wealth creates new forms of corruption. Once Wang Lung rises from poverty, his relationship to labor changes. He takes a concubine. Family tensions grow. The sons increasingly view the land as an asset rather than a source of identity.

Economic ascent contains the seed of separation from the thing that enabled it.

That is an American story too.

Immigrant families repeatedly experience a similar generational pattern in different forms. The first generation sacrifices for stability. Children inherit opportunity rather than the memory of precarity. The family’s economic success changes its relationship to the practices that produced success. What looks like ingratitude from one generation can look like freedom to the next.

Buck did not need to set the story in America for American readers to recognize the structure.

The book’s portrayal of O-lan is more difficult.

She is indispensable to the family’s survival and receives relatively little tenderness from Wang Lung. Her labor is constant. Her intelligence is practical. During catastrophe, she does what survival requires. Yet she is judged by standards of beauty and status that help justify Wang Lung’s movement toward another woman once wealth gives him options.

Many readers remember O-lan more powerfully than Wang Lung because the novel’s moral economy depends on her without rewarding her.

That imbalance exposes gender as another system of invisible infrastructure.

Households can appear prosperous because someone’s work is treated as obligation rather than achievement. The pattern is hardly confined to rural China. American women in the Depression likewise performed enormous amounts of unpaid domestic labor, managed scarcity, cared for children and relatives, and absorbed economic shock inside households whose public status was usually attached to men.

O-lan’s foreignness can make the structure visible before readers recognize it at home.

The 1930s would intensify American attention to China for geopolitical reasons. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and expanded war in China later in the decade. American missionaries, journalists, diplomats, businesses, and advocacy networks already linked the countries. As Japanese aggression escalated, sympathetic American views of China became politically significant.

Buck became an outspoken public voice on China, race, imperialism, and later adoption and humanitarian causes.

It would be too strong to say *The Good Earth* determined American China policy or directly caused changes in exclusion law. Foreign policy moved through strategic interests, war, diplomacy, domestic politics, missionary networks, racial ideologies, and the emerging conflict with Japan. The Chinese Exclusion Act was repealed in 1943, during wartime alliance with China, for reasons far larger than a novel published twelve years earlier.

But popular imagination sets conditions for policy.

It is easier to sustain crude exclusion against a population imagined as faceless threat than against people a mass culture has learned to recognize as families, farmers, workers, and individuals. Buck’s contribution was to enlarge that imaginative recognition among white American readers.

That is influence at the level of emotional infrastructure.

It also created backlash.

Critics who disliked Buck’s representation of China could not simply ignore it because the novel was too large. Chinese intellectuals had their own debates about whether her peasants were representative, whether she emphasized backwardness, and how the book’s Western popularity reflected foreign expectations. Some nationalist readers wanted modern China represented through revolution, urban life, intellectual change, or state-building rather than timeless rural poverty.

Every successful cultural bridge is accused, often correctly, of choosing what to leave on each shore.

Buck’s choice was the peasant household.

The novel’s plain style helped. It avoids dense explanation of Chinese history and minimizes the sense that the reader is taking a guided tour. Customs appear through life rather than through footnoted anthropology. This made the book accessible while also allowing readers to mistake narrative fluency for comprehensive knowledge.

Ease is persuasive.

The more smoothly another culture is narrated, the easier it is to forget how much translation has occurred.

That is one reason the book belongs beside Du Bois in this history, though their authority is fundamentally different. Du Bois writes as a Black American describing a racial system he inhabits and studies. Buck writes across cultural identity from long proximity. Both became interpreters for audiences that lacked adequate language for lives beyond their own experience.

The moral risks are different.

So are the rewards.

The decade offers extraordinary competitors. Ernest Hemingway’s *The Sun Also Rises* appeared in 1926, technically at the first edge of this span, and shaped modern American prose and the image of the Lost Generation. William Faulkner published *The Sound and the Fury* in 1929 and *As I Lay Dying* in 1930. John Maynard Keynes’s economic work influenced American policy debates, though *The General Theory* would arrive in 1936, just outside the decade. Dale Carnegie’s *How to Win Friends and Influence People* also appeared in 1936, beyond the line, and would transform self-help and business culture.

Faulkner may be the greater literary innovator. Hemingway may have changed more writers.

Buck changed more Americans’ imagined relationship to another civilization.

That makes her the more consequential choice for the national story.

The book’s success also reveals the power of the Book-of-the-Month-era mass literary market. By the 1930s, American publishing could turn novels into national events through reviews, clubs, serialization, adaptations, awards, and large distribution. Hollywood adapted *The Good Earth* into a major film released in 1937, extending the story’s reach while creating another controversy: leading Chinese characters were played largely by white actors, a common Hollywood practice that exposed the limits of the empathy the story was supposed to produce.

America could admire Chinese characters while denying Chinese actors equal access to representing them.

That contradiction belongs in the same frame as the book’s popularity.

Cultural sympathy does not automatically dismantle institutional discrimination.

It can coexist with it for a long time.

Buck herself later fought against racial prejudice in adoption and public life, and her views evolved through long engagement. Her reputation also shifted. The literary establishment that once honored her with the Nobel Prize later often treated her work as middlebrow or overly popular. Feminist and postcolonial scholarship reopened debates about her significance from different directions.

The rise, fall, and revision of reputation resembles Gatsby’s story in reverse.

Fitzgerald’s modestly received novel became canonical after his death. Buck’s enormous contemporary fame faded in elite literary standing even while *The Good Earth* remained widely read.

Canons do not merely preserve books.

They reorder prestige.

This project is not required to obey that prestige.

Importance asks what a book did.

The translation problem becomes clearest if we imagine the alternatives available to an American reader in 1931.

A reader who wanted to know China could encounter missionary reports, newspaper coverage, travel writing, diplomatic analysis, commercial advertising, political propaganda, and popular fiction. Each medium selected a China useful to its own purpose. Missionary accounts could emphasize souls, suffering, conversion, and institutions. Business writing could emphasize markets. diplomatic writing could emphasize states. Newspapers often emphasized crisis. Racist popular culture could reduce Chinese people to types.

Buck selected a household.

That choice did not make her neutral.

It changed the unit of attention.

Instead of beginning with “China” and moving downward, the reader begins with hunger, work, marriage, birth, land, and family, then encounters larger social forces as they enter those lives. The country is not first an abstraction requiring explanation. It is a place where people need dinner.

This is one reason the novel could generate empathy across enormous cultural distance without requiring the reader to master Chinese politics. The technique also explains its limitation. A household can humanize a population and conceal the diversity of the population at the same time.

The emotional bridge is strongest exactly where the sociological map is weakest.

That paradox belongs to many books in this history. *Uncle Tom’s Cabin* changed antislavery feeling while simplifying Black life. *Roots* later gave family form to slavery’s history while generating serious factual disputes. *Hillbilly Elegy* would make one family legible and then be asked to explain a region and a class. Narrative concentrates attention by reducing complexity.

The ethical question is what happens after the concentration works.

Does the reader treat the story as an opening or a conclusion?

If *The Good Earth* sends a reader toward Chinese history, Chinese writers, immigration history, war, politics, and the lives of Chinese Americans, the bridge has opened a route. If it becomes the reader’s sufficient knowledge of China, the bridge has become a border.

The American publishing system often encouraged the second use because Buck’s authority was unusually marketable. She could be interviewed, reviewed, awarded, adapted, and presented as someone who could explain China. The institutional imbalance that limited Chinese and Chinese American voices made the demand on her larger still.

This is not a reason to deny what she knew.

It is a reason to ask why the market preferred one kind of knower.

White American familiarity with another society was often treated as universal expertise in ways that expertise from people inside that society was not. Publishing, journalism, universities, philanthropy, and government all participated in deciding whose account would be legible to mainstream audiences.

The book’s consequence therefore lies partly in the gate through which it passed.

The Hollywood adaptation makes the mechanism visible. A studio could spend heavily to create a sympathetic story about Chinese characters while casting white stars in central roles. The industry wanted the emotional value of Chinese humanity and the commercial reassurance of performers it believed American audiences already accepted.

Representation was expanded and constrained in the same production.

This is not merely hypocrisy. It is how institutions change unevenly. One assumption becomes negotiable before another one does.

The same unevenness appeared in politics. Wartime alliance with China helped make exclusion law strategically embarrassing, but repeal did not erase the racial architecture of American immigration policy or anti-Asian prejudice. Sympathy toward China could coexist with discrimination against Chinese Americans. Admiration for an overseas ally could coexist with suspicion of neighbors.

National feeling does not move as one block.

A novel can shift one layer without moving the rest.

A counterfactual helps keep the claim disciplined.

If *The Good Earth* had never appeared, American views of China would still have changed dramatically in the 1930s and 1940s. Japanese aggression, missionary networks, diplomacy, wartime alliance, journalists, Chinese political advocacy, and Chinese American communities all supplied powerful reasons for Americans to pay attention. The repeal of exclusion would still have had strategic and political drivers independent of Buck.

Chinese humanity did not require a white novelist to exist, and Chinese American arguments against discrimination did not require her permission.

What changes without the novel is the scale of domestic intimacy delivered through a mass-market book before those geopolitical shifts fully matured.

Millions fewer readers would have entered a Chinese household through the same widely shared story. Another book might eventually have supplied a bridge. It would not necessarily have crossed the class, gender, literary, religious, and commercial audiences Buck reached at once.

This is why immediate sales matter more here than they did for Gatsby.

Gatsby demonstrates delayed canonization.

*The Good Earth* demonstrates simultaneous saturation.

A bestseller, Pulitzer Prize, Nobel Prize, book clubs, reviews, and film adaptation created a reinforcing loop of attention. Each institution certified the book to the next audience. Popularity gave prestige evidence of reach. Prestige gave popularity evidence of seriousness.

That loop made Buck difficult to avoid.

The later decline in her elite literary reputation is therefore instructive. A writer can be culturally central and later fall outside the preferred story of literary innovation. Modernist difficulty, formal experimentation, and influence on other writers became important measures of prestige. Buck’s accessibility could count against her in the very institutions that once celebrated her.

But accessibility had been part of the mechanism of consequence.

A book written only for specialists would not have put Wang Lung and O-lan into so many American homes.

The question of importance cannot simply inherit the taste of later literary departments.

That is one reason this chapter stays.

*The Good Earth* placed a Chinese peasant family on millions of American nightstands during a decade when Americans were frightened about their own land, food, work, and future. It made readers recognize common human stakes across a cultural distance that racism and exclusion had worked hard to maintain.

The recognition was incomplete and mediated through a white American author.

Incomplete recognition can still be historically consequential.

The danger begins when we mistake the bridge for the country on the other side.
