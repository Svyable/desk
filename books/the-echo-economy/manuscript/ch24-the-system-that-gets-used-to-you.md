# The System That Gets Used to You

The first intervention changes the problem.

A city adds a road to reduce congestion. Drivers respond to the faster route. Some switch from other roads, some change departure times, some make trips they previously avoided, some move farther away. Traffic rises. The road that relieved congestion can eventually attract enough use to restore it.

A hospital adds capacity. Physicians refer more patients. A popular emergency room becomes the default. Demand expands.

A company adds customer-support staff. Sales promises more white-glove service. Customers use the channel more. The queue returns.

Capacity invites behavior.

This is a recurring feature of complex systems: the system gets used to the intervention.

The phenomenon is not proof that capacity is useless. More roads can increase mobility. More hospital capacity can treat more people. More support can improve service. The point is that systems adapt, so the first-order effect is not the final effect.

Policy debates often fail because one side calculates the direct effect while the other intuitively anticipates adaptation.

Taxes change incentives. Subsidies change entry. Regulation changes business models. New infrastructure changes land use. Insurance changes risk behavior. Enforcement changes evasion. Benefits change labor or household decisions. Firms and people respond.

Good policy analysis tries to model these responses.

Bad policy analysis treats people as scenery.

The same is true in corporate strategy. A price cut changes competitor behavior. A generous return policy changes customer behavior. Unlimited vacation changes norms about taking vacation. Remote work changes where employees live and what offices mean. A hiring bar changes applicant pools. A new sales commission changes lead ownership.

Every policy creates a new game.

This is why unintended consequences are not always unforeseeable. Some are direct consequences of failing to ask how competent participants will adapt.

A common managerial error is to interpret the first response as the stable outcome.

Launch a bonus. Performance spikes. Success.

Then employees learn the thresholds, easier tasks receive more attention, cooperation changes, and the metric drifts.

Launch a subscription. Revenue becomes more predictable. Success.

Then customers change purchase timing, cancellation behavior, and expectations. Competitors imitate. Accounting changes how the organization values acquisition. Marketing changes. The company becomes a subscription company, with all the incentives that implies.

The policy teaches the organization how to respond to the policy.

This is second-order implementation.

Public institutions confront an even harder version because they cannot choose their population. A company can abandon a market segment. A government must govern the people who adapt.

Tax policy is a clean example. The statutory rate is not the same as effective revenue because households and firms change timing, form, location, reporting, investment, and legal structure. Some responses are socially useful, some neutral, some wasteful, some evasive. The size depends on which margin is easiest to change.

Policy therefore acts on a behavioral elasticity map.

This is why two taxes with the same headline rate can produce different outcomes. The base matters. Enforcement matters. substitution matters. timing matters. international mobility matters.

The echo economy asks a further question: what happens when policymakers themselves respond to the behavioral response?

Revenue misses expectations. Rules tighten. Taxpayers adapt again. Enforcement shifts. Advisors develop new strategies. courts interpret. Legislatures revise.

Tax systems evolve through repeated strategic interaction.

So do regulatory systems.

A regulator writes a rule to prevent a harmful practice. Firms redesign products around the boundary. The regulator closes the loophole. Firms adapt. Compliance industries emerge. Eventually the system may become highly complex not because anyone desired complexity but because each round encodes the previous round's workaround.

The rulebook is a fossil record of adaptation.

Financial regulation is full of such fossils.

Capital rules define risk weights. Banks alter portfolios. New instruments appear. regulators revise treatment. Activities move outside traditional banks. Shadow banking grows. crises reveal gaps. New rules arrive. Financial innovation partly responds to economic opportunity and partly to regulatory topology.

The boundary becomes a business model.

This does not imply regulation is futile. It implies regulation must be designed dynamically. Static rules create stable targets for optimization.

Principles-based regulation tries to preserve flexibility but increases discretion and uncertainty. Rules-based regulation increases clarity but invites boundary gaming. Again, the institution must govern the boundary between rules and judgment.

The deeper issue is complexity accumulation.

Every corrective layer solves a local problem and adds interactions.

Software systems experience this vividly. A bug appears. Engineers add a patch. The patch interacts with another component. A later feature works around the workaround. Years later, nobody remembers why a strange branch exists, but removing it breaks a customer workflow.

Technical debt is institutional memory without explanation.

Organizations accumulate policy debt the same way.

A travel rule exists because someone abused expenses in 2014. An approval step exists because a contract went wrong. A reporting requirement exists because a regulator once asked. A committee exists because a previous leader wanted coordination. Each addition made sense locally. The total system becomes slow.

Then a reformer arrives and deletes friction.

Performance improves until forgotten failure modes return.

This is the oscillation between bureaucracy and disruption.

Young organizations underweight history. Old organizations overweight it.

The young firm says, "Why do we need this rule?" because it has not experienced the event that created it.

The old firm says, "We have always done it this way" because it has forgotten the event too.

Both have lost causal memory.

A good institution stores the reason with the rule.

What failure is this control preventing?

How often has the failure occurred?

What is the cost of the control?

What alternative control now exists?

When should we review it?

This transforms bureaucracy from sediment into a portfolio of hypotheses.

Rules should have owners and reasons, not merely age.

The same principle applies to personal habits. A behavior begins as a solution to a past problem. Working late helped during a startup emergency. Saving aggressively helped during financial insecurity. Avoiding conflict helped in a volatile family. Meticulous preparation protected against early-career inexperience.

Years later the environment changes, but the control remains.

The person has become a legacy system.

Psychological patterns are adaptations too.

This is why character development cannot be reduced to eliminating "bad habits." Some habits were once intelligent responses. Understanding the original function makes change easier because it separates gratitude from permanence.

The system got used to you, and you got used to the system.

Markets contain this reciprocity everywhere.

A successful arbitrage closes the mispricing that made it profitable. A popular strategy attracts capital and compresses returns. A scarce skill commands high wages, attracting students and training programs that increase supply. A profitable industry attracts entrants. Monopoly profit attracts regulation or substitution. The economy continually attacks its own opportunities.

This is one reason capitalism can be dynamically efficient and individually frustrating. Success recruits competitors.

The profit signal coordinates its own erosion.

Joseph Schumpeter emphasized creative destruction: innovation destroys old structures while creating new ones. The recursive addition is that today's innovation changes tomorrow's opportunity landscape. The entrepreneur does not enter a fixed map. Entrepreneurship redraws the map.

A cheap new capability moves scarcity elsewhere.

When transportation gets cheaper, location changes. When communication gets cheaper, attention becomes scarcer. When computation gets cheaper, data and energy matter more. When content creation gets cheaper, trust and distribution become more valuable. When intelligence becomes cheaper, judgment, agency, verification, and physical execution may become more scarce.

Abundance creates new bottlenecks.

The system gets used to progress.

This is why technological forecasts that announce "X becomes free" are incomplete. If X becomes nearly free, behavior changes until some complementary constraint binds.

The constraint is the business opportunity.

Management should therefore track not only costs declining but constraints migrating.

What became newly expensive because something else became cheap?

Who controls the complementary asset?

Which institution cannot scale at the same rate?

Where does waiting time appear?

Where does trust break?

Where does regulation concentrate?

Where does human attention become the scarce input?

These questions turn second-order thinking into strategy.

Complex systems are often governed by feedback around bottlenecks. A queue forms where capacity is scarce. People route around the queue. The workaround creates another queue. Investment expands capacity. Demand responds. The bottleneck moves.

Supply-chain management is a continuous version of this game.

Improve one station and another becomes limiting. Add inventory and working capital rises. Reduce inventory and resilience falls. Increase batch size and waiting changes. Optimize transport and warehousing changes. There is no final globally optimal configuration independent of demand, uncertainty, cost, and service priorities.

Management is constraint migration.

This insight discourages utopian optimization. Every solved problem changes the next problem.

It also discourages despair. A recurring problem may not mean the intervention failed. Congestion returning after a new road can coexist with more total mobility. A support queue returning after hiring can coexist with more customers served. The system expanded into the capacity.

Evaluation must ask what changed, not whether one metric returned.

This is a crucial distinction in policy.

If a city adds transit and ridership rises until trains are crowded again, crowding alone does not prove transit investment failed. More people may be moving. If a hospital expands and beds fill, full beds do not prove capacity added nothing. Demand may have been unmet before.

A feedback system can consume improvement.

The observer needs baseline and throughput, not only endpoint state.

Personal growth is similar. A competent person receives harder problems. Work can feel just as difficult after years of improvement because the difficulty of chosen tasks rises with ability.

The absence of ease is not absence of progress.

Success changes the frontier.

This is a gentler form of the echo economy. Systems adapt not only to exploit us but to meet us at our new capacity.

The challenge is remembering that the target moved because we moved it.
