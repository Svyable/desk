# The Target Learns Your Name

The first month the call center published average handling time, nobody thought the number would become a philosophy.

It was a reasonable measure. Customers called with problems. Agents answered. Long calls could indicate poor training, confusing software, or inefficient process. Managers needed some way to compare shifts, identify bottlenecks, and estimate staffing. Average handling time turned thousands of messy conversations into something a spreadsheet could hold.

Then the number acquired consequences.

Teams were compared. Supervisors were questioned. Bonuses were discussed. The dashboard became visible. Agents learned which conversations harmed their standing. A lonely customer who needed two extra minutes was no longer merely a person on the line; she was also a denominator moving the wrong way. A complicated problem became a threat to the shift average. Transfers could make local performance look better even if the customer spent longer inside the system.

The measure had learned everyone's name.

This is the classic territory associated with Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it often stops being a good measure. Charles Goodhart's original observation arose in monetary policy, and later writers generalized it far beyond economics. The slogan survives because it names something managers repeatedly rediscover.

Measurement changes behavior when measured people care about the result.

That fact is neither surprising nor avoidable. The whole reason to set a target is to change behavior. A sales quota exists because leaders want more selling. A safety metric exists because leaders want fewer injuries. A school test exists partly because institutions want students and teachers to focus on important knowledge. A hospital quality score exists because somebody wants quality to improve.

The paradox is that successful incentives attack the validity of their own metrics.

If nobody responds, the metric remains descriptive but useless as a control system. If everyone responds strongly, the metric may become easy to improve without improving the underlying goal.

This is a second-order design problem.

Suppose a warehouse wants faster order fulfillment. It rewards units shipped per hour. Workers move faster. Good. Then they discover that difficult orders reduce throughput. Difficult orders are delayed or passed elsewhere. Packaging quality falls. Small injuries go unreported because stopping would hurt the rate. The metric improves while the customer experience and labor environment degrade.

The failure is not that workers are unethical. The organization told them what counted.

People are excellent learners of revealed priorities. They watch which numbers appear in meetings, which misses trigger phone calls, which achievements earn promotion, which violations are forgiven, and which values disappear when the quarter is difficult. Formal culture statements matter less than repeated reinforcement.

A metric is therefore a cultural signal.

This is why organizations can become strange around key performance indicators. The official purpose may be customer value, patient health, learning, public safety, or scientific discovery. But the operational purpose becomes whatever the scorecard makes legible.

Legibility is powerful because leaders cannot directly observe most work.

A chief executive cannot listen to every support call. A school superintendent cannot sit in every classroom. A health minister cannot inspect every diagnosis. A general cannot stand at every checkpoint. Institutions require abstraction to operate at scale. The measure is not the enemy. The absence of measurement can protect favoritism, incompetence, and self-serving stories.

The hard question is how to measure without letting the measure colonize the goal.

One answer is plural measurement.

If a call center tracks speed, repeat calls, customer satisfaction, escalation, resolution quality, and perhaps a sample of human-reviewed conversations, gaming one dimension becomes harder. The system sees more of the object. But plural metrics create their own problems. Too many measures dilute accountability. Weighted scorecards invite debates over weights. Employees may optimize the easiest components. Managers can selectively emphasize whichever number supports a preferred story.

Complexity does not eliminate gaming. It changes the game.

Another answer is rotating measures. If the organization knows that every stable target becomes learnable, it can vary audits and emphasize outcomes that are harder to script. Tax authorities use some version of this logic. Fraud detection systems change because fraudsters adapt. Cybersecurity teams rotate tests because attackers study defenses. Sports teams vary play calls because a fixed strategy becomes exploitable.

The measure and the measured coevolve.

A third answer is to preserve judgment.

This sounds soft, and softness makes executives nervous. Judgment can hide bias and inconsistency. Yet systems without judgment are vulnerable to literal compliance. A person can satisfy the rule while violating the purpose. Mature institutions therefore combine rules with roles empowered to interpret context.

Law does this constantly. Statutes define categories, but judges interpret. Accounting has standards, but auditors exercise judgment. Medicine has protocols, but clinicians face patients who do not arrive as averages. Aviation has checklists, but crews train for situations in which procedures conflict or information is incomplete.

The interesting institutions do not choose between rules and discretion. They govern the boundary.

Rules protect against arbitrary power. Discretion protects against mechanical stupidity.

The boundary becomes especially important when algorithms administer targets. Software can enforce consistency at enormous scale, but it can also make exceptions expensive. If a performance system automatically ranks workers, local supervisors may have less room to recognize caregiving constraints, unusual assignments, mentoring work, or invisible contributions. The organization becomes more measurable by becoming less able to see what the measure excludes.

This creates a management illusion: because the data are precise, the underlying concept must be precise.

"Productivity" is a good example. In a factory producing standardized objects, output per hour can be meaningful. In knowledge work, output is harder. How productive was the engineer who prevented an outage that did not occur? The lawyer who identified a fatal clause before a deal was signed? The manager who retained two exceptional employees? The researcher who disproved an attractive direction? The designer who removed a feature?

Not all valuable work leaves a large positive count.

Some work creates options. Some reduces risk. Some improves other people's output. Some prevents events. Some builds reputation that pays later. Some is exploration whose value is mostly information about what not to do.

When organizations force these activities into narrow metrics, they create shadows.

The shadows matter most during cost cutting because invisible work is easiest to remove. A maintenance team looks expensive until maintenance stops. A compliance function looks bureaucratic until a scandal. Institutional memory looks like old people telling stories until a rare failure repeats. Slack looks inefficient until demand spikes.

Efficiency metrics often measure the absence of visible waste, not the presence of resilience.

This is one reason the language of optimization can become dangerous in management. Optimization requires an objective function. Human organizations usually have several goals that cannot be perfectly reduced to one number: profit, survival, growth, legality, safety, trust, learning, employee development, customer welfare, strategic option value, public legitimacy. Declaring one metric the objective does not make the others disappear. It makes their tradeoffs less visible.

The same problem appears in public policy.

If police departments are judged heavily on reported crime rates, reporting practices can change. If schools are judged on test scores, curriculum can narrow and students near thresholds can receive disproportionate attention. If hospitals are judged on particular mortality windows, patient selection and coding can matter. If employment programs are rewarded for placement rates, providers may focus on clients easiest to place.

Every target defines a margin where effort becomes most valuable to the measured actor.

Economists call responses like these gaming or strategic behavior, but the moral tone of "gaming" can obscure design responsibility. When a system pays for a number, participants will discover ways to produce the number. Some will cheat. Many will simply follow incentives exactly.

The designer has to model the response.

This is where the target becomes second-order. A first-order manager asks, "What number represents success?" A second-order manager asks, "What will competent people do once this number represents success?"

That second question should be written into every metric review.

What behavior would improve the number without improving the goal?

What behavior would improve the goal without improving the number?

Who bears the cost of errors?

How quickly will people learn the rule?

Can the system detect when the relationship between measure and goal has changed?

Those questions are more important than decorative dashboards.

Yet organizations repeatedly fall in love with dashboards because dashboards relieve anxiety. A complex enterprise becomes visible on one screen. Red, yellow, green. Up, down, flat. The executive can feel oriented.

The danger is not only that employees game the metrics. Leaders can be gamed by the visual certainty of metrics.

A clean dashboard compresses uncertainty out of the presentation. Data quality problems, definitions, selection effects, missing cases, lag, and contextual differences disappear behind polished numbers. Once a metric acquires a familiar shape, meetings discuss movement rather than meaning.

The question changes from "Is this still measuring what we care about?" to "Why is this down two points?"

That is how targets become institutions.

They accumulate owners, reporting cycles, historical baselines, executive expectations, software integrations, compensation rules, and audit trails. Removing a bad metric can become harder than adding a new one because so many processes depend on it.

Metrics have constituencies.

The same is true of rankings. A university ranking creates admissions strategies, marketing budgets, data offices, benchmarking consultants, alumni expectations, and media rituals. Even people who dislike the ranking may respond because competitors respond. The system becomes hard to exit individually.

This is strategic lock-in through common measurement.

Companies experience it with quarterly earnings guidance, industry benchmarks, analyst estimates, net promoter scores, engagement metrics, and peer compensation surveys. Once everyone uses the same measure, deviating can look irresponsible even when the measure is weak.

The metric becomes common knowledge.

That gives it power beyond accuracy.

A flawed benchmark can coordinate behavior better than a superior benchmark nobody recognizes. This is why standards persist. Coordination value is recursive: people use the standard because others use it, and others use it because people use it.

Sometimes this is beneficial. The world needs common units, accounting conventions, communication protocols, and technical standards. Shared measures let strangers cooperate. The challenge is to distinguish standards whose value comes from coordination from measures that claim to reveal quality.

A meter can be arbitrary in origin and still reliably measure length. A ranking of leadership quality is not the same kind of object.

Confusing the two produces pseudo-objectivity.

The problem becomes acute in machine learning because objective functions are explicit. A model is trained to reduce loss according to defined criteria. If the proxy is incomplete, the system can become extraordinarily effective at the wrong thing.

Researchers in AI safety discuss reward hacking and specification gaming: agents find ways to satisfy an objective that violate the designer's intent. The examples can be amusing in toy environments, but the principle is familiar to anyone who has managed humans. Give an adaptive system a score and enough freedom, and it will search the gap between the score and the purpose.

Humans are adaptive systems too.

The difference is that humans also possess values, social understanding, conscience, resentment, pride, and the ability to reinterpret goals. That can make them harder to control and easier to trust. A good employee sometimes violates a local metric because she understands the mission better than the metric does.

Organizations should be careful before automating away that disobedience.

The deepest defense against target corruption is cultural rather than technical: people must believe the stated purpose matters enough to resist literal optimization when it would obviously damage the purpose.

This is difficult because culture itself is learned through reinforcement. If leaders punish every miss regardless of context, employees learn that purpose is theater. If leaders occasionally accept a bad number because someone protected the underlying goal, the organization receives different evidence about what counts.

A culture is a target system that has become tacit.

It tells people what is rewarded when no formal rule decides.

This brings motivation into the feedback story. Evidence-based motivation is not simply a matter of setting more goals. Goals can focus attention and effort, but poorly designed goals can narrow attention, encourage risk, suppress learning, and invite unethical behavior. The useful question is not whether goals work. It is what kind of behavior a goal makes salient under what conditions.

A person trying to write may benefit from a daily word target because the alternative is avoidance. A mature writer may damage a chapter by continuing to produce words after the argument is complete. A beginner runner may benefit from a step count. An injured athlete may need to ignore it. The same metric can be scaffolding at one stage and a trap at another.

Metrics have life cycles.

They are introduced to correct one problem, become effective, attract incentives, get learned, and eventually may need redesign. Treating them as permanent truths confuses a tool with the goal.

The call center does need to know how long customers wait.

It also needs to remember why they called.
