# The Regime That Changes the Rule

A model can be wrong because a parameter moved.

It can also be wrong because the world stopped being the kind of world the model described.

That difference is the difference between noise and regime change.

Investors learn this painfully. A strategy built for low inflation behaves differently when inflation is high. A business built around cheap capital behaves differently when financing costs rise. A supply chain optimized for efficiency behaves differently when borders close. A labor model built around abundant local workers behaves differently when demographics change. A political coalition built around one media system behaves differently when information channels fragment.

The rule did not merely produce a bad draw. The game changed.

Regime change is especially dangerous in feedback systems because behavior that stabilized the old regime may destabilize the new one.

Consider interest rates. For years, firms can become accustomed to refinancing cheaply. Asset prices, leverage, venture funding, housing affordability, private equity, government budgets, and bank portfolios adapt. Low rates cease to feel like a condition and start to feel like a property of the world.

Then inflation or policy shifts.

Higher rates do more than increase a discount rate in a spreadsheet. They change behavior. Investors demand cash flow sooner. Lenders tighten. Companies reduce hiring. projects are canceled. households refinance less. banks discover duration risk. The new behavior changes economic activity and market prices, which affect future policy.

The regime is a loop, not a setting.

This is why historical averages can become traps. An average combines regimes that participants did not experience as average.

A river can have an average depth of four feet and still drown someone in an eight-foot channel.

Risk management needs distribution and structure, not just mean.

Regime thinking asks what mechanisms hold the current world together.

Why are correlations low?

Why is volatility low?

Why are margins high?

Why is a supply chain cheap?

Why is political cooperation stable?

Why are employees staying?

If the answer is "because it has been that way recently," no mechanism has been identified.

The echo economy encourages this lazy inference because stable outcomes produce behavior that stabilizes outcomes.

Low volatility encourages leverage. Leverage can support asset prices and suppress short-term volatility. Reliable supply encourages lower inventory. Lower inventory reduces cost and reinforces lean practice. Peace encourages lower defense spending and deeper trade integration. Deep trade can make peace more valuable.

Virtuous loops can last long enough to look like laws.

Then the hidden counter-loop activates.

Lean inventories make shortages more consequential. Financial leverage makes small price changes more damaging. Dense trade creates concentrated dependencies. A just-in-time system can be extraordinarily efficient under ordinary transport and brittle when a critical supplier disappears.

The same structure appears in ecology. Fire suppression can reduce small fires while allowing fuel to accumulate, increasing the risk of severe fire under the wrong conditions. Intervention improves the visible metric until the system crosses a threshold.

Success stores risk.

The financial version is sometimes called the paradox of stability: long calm periods can encourage behavior that makes future instability worse. Hyman Minsky's financial instability hypothesis explored how stability can alter financing behavior, with safer-seeming conditions encouraging more speculative structures.

One need not accept every formulation to appreciate the feedback insight. Memories of crisis fade. Risk limits loosen. competition rewards firms willing to stretch. New participants enter who did not experience the last failure. The system learns from calm.

Then calm becomes one cause of fragility.

Institutional memory is the defense, but memory decays because the people who remember retire.

A risk officer says, "We tried this in 2007." A younger executive says the market is different now. Both may be right.

History never repeats with enough precision to settle the argument.

The purpose of historical analogy is not to announce recurrence. It is to identify mechanisms that might recur under different surfaces.

Leverage is one mechanism.

Maturity mismatch is another.

Concentration.

Crowding.

Political legitimacy.

Network dependence.

Single points of failure.

Refinancing risk.

These mechanisms travel across eras even when the assets and technologies change.

This is why sweeping business history is useful when done carefully. It expands the sample of regimes. A manager whose entire career occurred during one macroeconomic environment may confuse biography with economics.

The person who became a successful real-estate investor during decades of falling rates may possess real skill and a tailwind. The technology executive whose career coincided with expanding internet adoption may possess real skill and a secular wave. The manufacturer operating during globalization may have optimized brilliantly for a trade regime that later changes.

Skill and regime are entangled.

Success makes disentangling them harder because the winner gets more capital and authority.

A portfolio manager with strong returns receives more assets. A founder with strong growth receives more control. A country whose development model works expands the model. The larger scale increases the cost of discovering that part of the success belonged to the environment.

This is regime leverage.

The practical defense is scenario diversity.

Instead of asking for one forecast, ask what breaks under several worlds.

Rates high, growth low.

Rates low, inflation high.

Trade fragmented.

Energy cheap.

Energy scarce.

AI labor substitution fast.

AI adoption slow.

Regulation permissive.

Regulation restrictive.

The goal is not to predict every world. It is to discover assumptions shared by all current plans.

A company may discover that every strategy depends on one supplier, one geography, one cloud provider, one customer segment, one financing market, or one regulatory interpretation.

The scenario exercise reveals the hidden common mode.

This is more valuable than assigning false probabilities to every scenario.

Complex systems often fail through correlated assumptions.

A resilient portfolio, company, or state preserves options across regimes. Cash, unused borrowing capacity, multiple suppliers, modular systems, transferable skills, diversified customers, trusted alliances, and adaptable institutions can all function as option value.

Options look inefficient when the regime is stable.

That is why they are hard to maintain.

The echo economy tends to optimize them away. If two suppliers have delivered reliably for years, procurement asks why there should be three. If cloud uptime is excellent, finance asks why local recovery systems are needed. If financing is abundant, cash looks lazy. If one market grows fastest, diversification looks dilutive.

The dashboard rewards concentration before the regime changes.

Resilience therefore requires governance protection. Someone must be authorized to spend current efficiency for future flexibility.

This is a board-level decision because operating managers are often measured on current cost.

The same principle applies personally. A career concentrated in one employer, one tool, one network, or one geography may be efficient until the regime changes. Broad skills, relationships, savings, and reputation create options.

The person with slack can update.

This is not an argument for chronic caution. Too much redundancy prevents compounding. A company that diversifies every risk never builds distinctive strength. An investor holding cash forever avoids drawdowns and misses growth. A professional who refuses specialization never becomes excellent.

Resilience is not the absence of concentration. It is concentration with escape routes.

The distinction is subtle and strategic.

A firm can concentrate on a core product while keeping technical architecture modular. An investor can hold a concentrated thesis with position sizing that prevents ruin. A country can specialize in trade while maintaining strategic reserves. A professional can become expert while sustaining adjacent skills.

The loop can be powerful without becoming closed.

Regime change also interacts with culture. Organizations develop stories that explain success. Those stories become hiring criteria and promotion norms. When the regime changes, the people most aligned with the old story hold the most authority.

The institution selects guardians of the previous regime.

This is why turnaround leadership often feels culturally violent. The new leader is not merely changing strategy. She is challenging the biography through which senior people understand their own success.

"What made us great" is emotionally stronger than "what will work next."

Respect matters here. Treating the old strategy as stupid destroys trust and loses the knowledge embedded in it. The old strategy may have been excellent for its regime.

The mature transition says: this worked, and the conditions changed.

That sentence preserves dignity while opening adaptation.

Societies face the same challenge. Industrial regions, trade systems, welfare states, military alliances, and political parties develop around conditions that later shift. Reform becomes a fight over whether acknowledging change dishonors the past.

Second-order leadership separates identity from mechanism.

We can honor what worked without assuming it still works.

Risk is partly the probability of bad outcomes. It is also the probability that our model of outcomes belongs to a world that has ended.

That probability is impossible to estimate cleanly.

So resilient systems look for early signs that relationships are changing: rising model error, unexplained residuals, new competitor behavior, unusual customer segments, financing stress, supply delays, political realignment, changes in who is entering or leaving.

Anomalies are regime sensors.

The first response should not be panic. Most anomalies are noise.

The second should not be dismissal. Some anomalies are the future arriving badly dressed.

An institution earns its survival by knowing the difference often enough.
