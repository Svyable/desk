# The Strategy Everyone Can Read

Strategy changes when the opponent reads the strategy book.

This sounds obvious. It is also where much business advice quietly fails.

A useful tactic becomes public. Consultants teach it. Business schools analyze it. Investors reward it. Competitors hire people who know it. Suppliers anticipate it. Customers learn how it works. The tactic enters the environment it was designed to exploit.

The playbook becomes part of the game.

A first mover discovers that a certain store format lowers costs. Competitors copy the layout. Landlords learn the economics and raise rents. Suppliers adapt bargaining. Customers begin expecting the format. The original advantage becomes an industry standard.

This is the fate of many best practices.

A practice can remain good after it stops being differentiating. Barcodes, just-in-time logistics, customer relationship systems, cloud infrastructure, search advertising, mobile design, data science, subscription billing, agile development, and countless other methods migrated from advantage to competence as knowledge diffused.

Competition is a feedback system for strategy.

The more successful a strategy becomes, the more information it provides to rivals.

This produces a Red Queen problem. In Lewis Carroll's fiction, the Red Queen tells Alice that in her country one must run very fast to stay in the same place. Evolutionary biologist Leigh Van Valen later used the image for species adapting against other adapting species. Business borrowed the metaphor because competitors also change one another's environments.

A cost reduction is not a permanent gift if competitors match it and prices fall.

A faster product cycle raises customer expectations, forcing everyone to speed up.

A generous loyalty program attracts customers until rivals copy the rewards and consumers come to treat the benefit as baseline.

An AI tool improves productivity until the capability becomes common and the market price of the work adjusts.

Advantage creates imitation; imitation erodes advantage.

This is why strategy cannot be reduced to a list of universally superior practices. If everyone can adopt the practice, it may improve the whole industry without preserving relative position.

The strategic value lies partly in what does not diffuse easily.

Capabilities embedded in culture, tacit knowledge, accumulated data, supplier relationships, physical networks, regulatory trust, brand meaning, learning curves, and organizational routines can persist because copying the visible surface does not reproduce the history that created it.

The distinction between a practice and a capability is crucial.

A practice can be described. A capability has been metabolized.

Competitors can copy a meeting format without the trust that makes candid meetings useful. They can copy a store aesthetic without the procurement system that supports the economics. They can copy an interface without the data and operations behind service quality. They can copy a compensation plan without the labor market reputation that determines who accepts it.

The visible strategy is often the least defensible part.

This helps explain why business history is full of companies imitating leaders and obtaining mediocre results. The imitator sees outputs: store design, product cadence, pricing, organizational chart, slogans. The leader's advantage may sit in a slower loop of accumulated learning.

Path dependence protects what cannot be purchased all at once.

But path dependence can also imprison the leader.

The organization builds routines around the winning model. Employees are selected for fit. Suppliers invest around it. investors learn its economics. Customers develop expectations. Management systems encode the assumptions. The advantage becomes identity.

Then the environment changes.

A rival with a worse business by the old metrics may be building a better business for the new environment.

This is why disruption often feels insulting to incumbents. The entrant appears unsophisticated. Margins are lower. customers are less attractive. Product performance is weaker on dimensions the incumbent has spent years optimizing. The incumbent's rejection is evidence of competence under the existing model.

The loop rewards the wrong confidence.

The same dynamic appears in geopolitics. A military doctrine works in one conflict, becomes institutionalized, shapes procurement and training, and later encounters an adversary designed to exploit it. Victory teaches lessons. Lessons become doctrine. Doctrine becomes a target.

Success is public information.

Strategic actors therefore conceal, misdirect, and randomize. Poker players mix strategies so opponents cannot exploit a deterministic pattern. Militaries use deception. Companies launch stealth projects. Sports teams vary plays. Negotiators preserve uncertainty around reservation prices.

Opacity can be an asset when the other side adapts.

This complicates the modern faith in transparency. Internal transparency can improve coordination. External transparency can reduce strategic room. Publishing every roadmap may reassure customers while teaching competitors. Announcing every negotiation principle may signal consistency while making threats predictable.

The optimal amount of visibility depends on who is watching and how quickly they can respond.

Second-order strategy begins with the opponent's learning rate.

A restaurant can experiment with a menu because rivals cannot instantly copy its location, staff, reputation, and local knowledge. A software feature can be copied quickly, but a distribution network may take years. A manufacturing process may be legally protected, tacitly complex, or reverse engineered. A pricing change can be matched in hours.

The half-life of advantage differs by layer.

This is why moats are not static walls. They are feedback structures that make success reinforce future success faster than rivals can copy it.

Network effects are the purest case. More users make a network more valuable, attracting more users. But network effects vary. Some are local, some global, some one-sided, some multi-sided, some vulnerable to multi-homing. A platform that relies on network effects still must ask what prevents a new network from coordinating elsewhere.

Data can produce another loop. More usage produces more data; more data can improve the product; improvement attracts more usage. Yet data advantages are often overstated because not all data are unique, useful, or hard to replicate. The important question is whether additional data materially improves outcomes and whether competitors can obtain substitutes.

Brand has its own recursion. Recognition reduces customer uncertainty. More customers create more visibility and cultural presence. Distribution improves because retailers or partners prefer proven demand. That presence creates further recognition.

But brands can also overextend. If the name appears on too many weak products, recognition separates from trust. The loop reverses.

Scale economies similarly reinforce position until complexity costs appear. Larger purchasing volumes improve bargaining. Fixed costs spread. Distribution deepens. More revenue funds more investment. Yet large organizations accumulate coordination cost, political behavior, legacy systems, and slower decision cycles.

Every positive loop has a saturation point or counter-loop.

Strategy is the art of finding both.

The mistake is to identify a flywheel and assume it spins forever. Competitive systems answer strength with adaptation.

A company known for premium pricing invites lower-cost entrants. A company known for low cost invites differentiation. A platform with high take rates invites disintermediation. A dominant supplier encourages customers to dual-source. A proprietary ecosystem creates an opening for open standards. A concentrated industry attracts regulators.

Power generates antibodies.

This is one reason the most durable strategies often contain restraint. A platform may leave enough economics to complementors that they continue investing. A supplier may avoid exploiting dependence so customers do not fund alternatives. A market leader may preserve interoperability because a closed system would provoke migration. A country may avoid using a financial sanction tool in every dispute because overuse encourages substitutes.

Strategic restraint protects the loop that creates power.

This is hard for organizations because local incentives favor extraction. The sales team wants price. The finance team wants margin. The product team wants control. The state wants leverage. The immediate gain is measurable. The future response is probabilistic.

Good strategy models the response before taking the gain.

What will the customer do if we raise price?

What will the supplier build if we squeeze margin?

What will competitors copy if we publish this capability?

What substitute becomes attractive if we make dependence painful?

What regulator arrives if market power becomes too visible?

These are second-order questions because they treat other actors as adaptive rather than static.

Game theory formalizes this instinct, but strategy rarely provides clean payoff matrices. Firms do not know all players, payoffs, information, or future technologies. The practical value of game theory is often not numerical solution. It is the discipline of asking what the other side will do after seeing your move.

That discipline should also be applied to internal strategy.

A chief executive announces a reorganization. Employees do not simply occupy the new boxes. They infer which functions gained status, which leaders are rising, which projects are endangered, and where future promotions might sit. Talent moves before formal processes catch up. The org chart becomes a signal about power.

The reorganization changes behavior before it changes reporting lines.

Compensation plans work the same way. A plan says which future the company expects employees to pursue. Employees model the plan. High performers decide whether the upside is credible. Managers allocate opportunities according to what produces recognized output. The plan's incentives shape the talent pool that remains.

Strategy creates a constituency.

Once capital, careers, and identity attach to a direction, changing direction becomes politically difficult. A product line has employees. A factory has a town. A technology has experts. A market has sales teams. A doctrine has senior officers. The strategy is no longer an idea. It is an institution with people who rationally defend it.

This is why strategic pivots are expensive even when the new PowerPoint looks simple.

The old strategy has embedded itself in the organization.

Leaders sometimes respond by glorifying disruption. They praise cannibalization and tell teams to destroy the current business before someone else does. The slogan contains truth and danger. Cannibalization is easier to praise than to govern. A company that attacks every profitable business preemptively can destroy cash flows that fund genuine innovation.

The second-order question is not "Are we willing to cannibalize?" It is "Under what evidence would protecting the current business become more dangerous than attacking it?"

That requires explicit assumptions.

If customer switching costs fall below this level, if the new technology reaches this performance, if a new distribution channel reaches this scale, if regulation changes, if a competitor proves this unit economics—then the decision changes.

Precommitting to evidence can reduce the political power of the existing loop.

It creates an escape hatch before everyone knows who will lose.

This is the strategy version of writing an investment thesis before the price moves. You decide what would change your mind while your identity is less attached to being right.

The best strategic organizations cultivate this habit without becoming indecisive. They commit strongly enough to learn and remain uncertain enough to update.

That balance is character at institutional scale.

Too little commitment and no strategy compounds. Too much and the organization becomes a prisoner of its own success.

The opponent is always reading.

Sometimes the opponent is the market.

Sometimes it is a competitor.

And sometimes it is the company you became by winning the last game.
