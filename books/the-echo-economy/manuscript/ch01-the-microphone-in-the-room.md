# The Microphone in the Room

A microphone is a modest machine until you point it at a loudspeaker.

Then the room discovers recursion.

The sound begins as almost nothing: the scrape of a chair, the exhalation of an amplifier, the soft electrical weather that every system carries inside itself. The microphone turns pressure in the air into current. The amplifier enlarges it. The speaker turns current back into pressure. The microphone hears the speaker. The speaker repeats what the microphone heard. Around the loop the signal goes, each circuit taking the previous circuit as its starting point, until one frequency happens to fit the room especially well. That frequency comes back a little stronger. Then stronger again. Soon the room is no longer amplifying the original sound. It is amplifying its own amplification.

Anyone who has watched a technician walk toward a squealing stage monitor knows the physical awkwardness of this. The technician does not defeat feedback by arguing with the tone. She changes the relationship among the parts. She turns down gain. Moves the microphone. Changes an equalizer. Breaks the loop.

The lesson is so ordinary that it is easy to miss. A system can change character when its output becomes part of its next input.

That sentence is the subject of this book.

Civilization is full of loops that are less audible than a bad sound system. A price changes what buyers want, which changes the price. A ranking changes what people notice, which changes the ranking. A credit rating changes a borrower's cost of capital, which changes the borrower's finances, which later appears to vindicate the rating. A poll changes the stories campaigns tell about themselves. A forecast changes the investment that makes the forecast more or less likely. A school measure changes the teaching that generates the next measure. A recommendation engine changes the tastes from which it later learns. A rumor of insolvency can make a solvent bank illiquid. A model of risk can encourage institutions to hold the same positions and thereby create the correlation the model had treated as unlikely.

These are not merely examples of influence. They are systems with memory of their own descriptions.

The distinction matters because the intellectual habits that work well on a bridge, a falling stone, or a chemical reaction can mislead us when the object being studied can react to the study. If an engineer estimates the weight of a beam, the beam does not become vain. It does not hire a consultant, borrow money, lobby the building code, or change its behavior because somebody published a report about its load capacity. Human systems do these things constantly.

The moment a description enters the world it describes, the description can become an intervention.

Economists have long known versions of this problem. Traders have lived it. Sociologists, cyberneticists, philosophers, military strategists, central bankers, psychologists, artists, and computer scientists have each built vocabularies around pieces of it. Reflexivity. Feedback. Performativity. Self-reference. Second-order observation. Endogeneity. Strategic response. Lucas critique. Goodhart's law. Common knowledge. Signaling. Adaptive expectations. Cybernetics. The names differ because the disciplines differ, and because the mechanisms are not identical. But they share a family resemblance: information does not simply report on the system. It circulates through the system and changes what the system becomes.

A useful way to begin is with three kinds of loop.

The first is physical feedback, the microphone kind. A thermostat senses temperature and changes a furnace, which changes the temperature the thermostat later senses. Negative feedback dampens deviation. Positive feedback enlarges it. The vocabulary came to social science partly because engineers had already learned that loops could stabilize a machine or drive it into oscillation.

The second is behavioral feedback. People see a signal and respond to it. A restaurant with a line attracts more passersby because the line itself becomes evidence. A rising stock draws momentum buyers. A falling currency encourages residents to convert savings, which can accelerate the fall. Here the loop passes through interpretation.

The third is representational feedback. A category, model, target, or story reorganizes the thing it claims merely to describe. An employee evaluated on calls per hour changes how she speaks to customers. A hospital judged on a narrow outcome may alter admissions. A city branded as an innovation hub attracts firms and graduates, which makes the brand less fictional. A child repeatedly described as gifted may be given harder work, more patience, and more opportunities to acquire the very traits the description anticipated.

The boundaries among these types are porous. A bank run is behavioral but also institutional. An algorithmic recommendation loop is computational, commercial, and cultural at once. The point is not to create a taxonomy for its own sake. The point is to notice when a system has crossed from one-way cause and effect into a circuit.

That crossing changes four things.

First, it changes prediction. If people can respond to a forecast, the forecast may invalidate itself or fulfill itself. A warning that a bridge is unsafe may reduce traffic and prevent failure. A warning that a bank is unsafe may produce withdrawals and cause failure. The same abstract structure—information about a possible future—can push the system in opposite directions.

Second, it changes measurement. A measure becomes unreliable when people have strong incentives to improve the number rather than the underlying thing. This is one reason mature organizations accumulate strange rituals around metrics. A metric begins as a window. Then compensation, prestige, fear, and software attach themselves to it. Eventually the window becomes a steering wheel.

Third, it changes power. Whoever controls the representation can sometimes influence the reality. Index providers decide which securities belong in a benchmark. App stores rank software. credit bureaus summarize borrowers. Governments define unemployment, inflation, poverty, disability, nationality, taxable income, accredited investment, and thousands of other categories that distribute resources and obligations. The more a society coordinates through abstractions, the more consequential the custodians of those abstractions become.

Fourth, it changes character. Living inside feedback systems trains people. A manager who is rewarded for the quarter learns to perceive the quarter. A creator who studies engagement statistics begins to feel the audience before the work exists. A politician who lives by polling begins to hear citizens as distributions. A teenager whose photographs are constantly ranked by visible reactions may come to experience attention not merely as attention but as a number attached to the self.

The loop eventually passes through the person.

This is where a book about markets and algorithms becomes a book about culture. The feedback economy is not a sector. It is an environment.

Consider applause. Before electronic measurement, applause was already recursive. People clap partly because others clap. A hesitant audience can tip into a standing ovation when a few people rise and make sitting feel like a judgment. The sound becomes information about the sound. Yet applause is local, fleeting, and hard to store. Digital systems changed those properties. They turn reactions into persistent counts. Counts can be ranked. Rankings can be recommended. Recommendations create exposure. Exposure creates more reactions. What once disappeared into the ceiling of a theater can now return for years as a number shaping distribution.

The decisive technological change is often not that humans became more imitative. Humans have always watched one another. The change is that imitation can now be instrumented, stored, and fed back at machine speed.

Financial markets made this visible early because price is both outcome and signal. If copper rises, the price tells miners to invest, manufacturers to economize, speculators to pay attention, and journalists to ask what changed. Those reactions alter future supply, demand, and expectations. The market is continuously reading its own output. That is part of its genius. It is also part of its instability.

Organizations do something similar with dashboards. A dashboard compresses activity into a few visible measures because no executive can inhabit every warehouse, sales call, service ticket, factory line, and customer account. Compression is necessary. But once the organization sees itself through the dashboard, behavior bends toward what the dashboard can see. The representation becomes an operating environment.

There is no escape from this by refusing measurement. That would be like fixing microphone feedback by banning sound. The difficult work is learning which loops stabilize, which loops distort, which loops can be slowed, and which loops should be broken.

The technician on the stage has several advantages over the executive, regulator, voter, investor, or parent. She can usually hear the oscillation immediately. She can identify the equipment. She knows which knob changes gain. Most social feedback is delayed and distributed. The signal may take years to return. Nobody owns the whole loop. Each participant can behave reasonably while the system behaves absurdly.

That last possibility deserves emphasis because it recurs throughout modern history.

Suppose every bank believes a particular asset is safe because its recent volatility is low. Each bank increases exposure. The growing demand stabilizes the asset and compresses volatility further. The data now provide stronger evidence for the original belief. Risk officers relax limits. More capital enters. The system appears to become safer as it becomes more crowded. No single bank needs to be foolish. The danger lives in the correlation among sensible decisions.

Or suppose every media outlet observes that a certain topic draws attention. Each outlet produces more of it. Audiences encounter the topic more often and infer that it must be important. Interest rises. Editors observe the interest and commission more coverage. The system may be reporting genuine demand, manufacturing demand, or doing both at once. Asking which came first can become less useful than tracing the loop.

Or suppose a company announces that artificial intelligence will transform its industry. Competitors fear being left behind and increase spending. Suppliers build capacity. Investors reward firms with aggressive plans. Engineers migrate toward the field. Customers begin asking for AI features. The original forecast may have been overstated, but the ecosystem created by the forecast can make some version of it true.

A second-order system is difficult to understand because causality becomes circular without becoming meaningless. The temptation is to say that everything causes everything else. That is usually an excuse to stop thinking. Better analysis asks where the gain enters, where the delays sit, which actors can observe which signals, how strong their incentives are, and what limits the loop.

Every loop has friction.

The microphone squeal ends when the amplifier clips, the technician intervenes, the power fails, or the hardware breaks. A speculative boom meets financing constraints, skeptical sellers, regulation, or exhausted buyers. A fashion exhausts novelty. A political narrative collides with an election, a budget, a battlefield, or an institution able to say no. An internal metric runs into a customer who can leave.

Reality has vetoes, but they are not always punctual.

This delay between distortion and correction is one of the most expensive features of feedback systems. A bad measure can guide a company for years before customer loss becomes obvious. A weak credit regime can expand until refinancing conditions change. A prestige hierarchy can reproduce itself across generations before outsiders build an alternative institution. A technical standard can become entrenched because compatibility is valuable even when the standard itself is mediocre.

Positive feedback creates path dependence. Early advantages can become structural advantages because winning changes the conditions of the next round.

This is why small differences sometimes matter too much. A song placed on a prominent playlist receives more listens, which improves the signals used to decide what deserves future placement. A startup that raises a prestigious round can hire faster, attract press, and win customers who interpret funding as evidence of quality. A scientific paper that gains early citations becomes easier to discover and more likely to be cited again. A city with an initial cluster of specialized workers attracts firms that need those workers, which attracts more workers.

The loop does not make the initial difference irrelevant. It makes the initial difference hard to separate from the amplification that followed.

We often confuse the final size of an outcome with the size of the original cause.

The confusion is flattering to winners and cruel to losers. It encourages biographies in which success appears to have been fully visible inside the protagonist from the beginning. The mature company proves the founder was a visionary. The famous artist proves the early reviews were perceptive. The dominant technology proves the design was inevitable. Yet feedback systems can turn contingent leads into durable outcomes. Character matters. So do timing, networks, institutional endorsement, imitation, and luck.

A serious account of achievement has to hold both ideas at once: people make consequential choices, and systems amplify some choices more than others.

That is not a philosophy of helplessness. It is almost the opposite. If outcomes depend partly on loops, then agency includes choosing which loops to enter, which signals to trust, which measures to publish, and which forms of reinforcement to resist.

The practical mistake is to imagine that feedback is inherently bad. Negative feedback keeps aircraft stable, bodies regulated, and organizations from drifting too far from constraints. Social proof can help people find excellent restaurants, trustworthy sellers, useful software, and important research. Market prices coordinate dispersed information. Reputation rewards reliability. Scientific citation links work into a cumulative conversation. Cultural imitation lets good ideas travel farther than their inventors could carry them.

The problem begins when a loop stops importing enough independent reality.

A recommendation system trained mostly on reactions caused by its own recommendations can become an echo chamber in the literal systems sense. A risk model validated on prices shaped by widespread use of similar models can confuse conformity with truth. A political party that listens mainly to activists responding to messages crafted for activists can become exquisitely informed about a narrow feedback circuit and surprisingly ignorant about everyone else.

Closed loops can feel informative because they produce abundant data.

That is one of the central paradoxes of the echo economy. More measurement can mean less contact with the thing being measured.

The rest of this book follows that paradox across domains. We will move from mathematics to music, from brains to bank runs, from management targets to recommendation systems, from credit ratings to military deterrence. The cases are not interchangeable. A theorem is not a TikTok feed; a bank run is not a fugue. The value lies in seeing how similar structural questions recur under different materials.

What is the system observing?

Who can see the observation?

How do they respond?

Does the response alter the next observation?

What keeps the loop connected to something outside itself?

And when the answer to the last question is "very little," who notices first?

A squealing microphone is merciful because it announces the problem.

The more dangerous loops sound like success.
