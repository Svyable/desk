# The Feed Learns Your Taste

A recommendation system faces an innocent problem.

There is too much.

Too many songs, films, products, posts, restaurants, books, jobs, videos, articles, creators, courses, games, and people for anyone to inspect directly. Scarcity once lived mostly in supply. Digital abundance moves scarcity into attention.

The recommender is a machine for deciding what gets a chance to matter.

At first glance, the task is statistical. Infer what this user might like from prior behavior and from patterns among similar users or items. Show likely candidates. Observe response. Update.

That last word changes everything.

Update.

The system learns from behavior caused partly by the system's previous choices.

A listener plays a song because it appeared near the top of a playlist. The play becomes evidence that the listener likes the song. The system becomes more likely to recommend similar songs. The listener encounters more of them, develops familiarity, perhaps develops genuine preference, and supplies additional evidence.

The model learns a person it is helping to create.

This does not make recommendation sinister. It makes recommendation second-order.

Traditional retail had feedback too. A bookstore displayed some titles in the window. Clerks recommended books. Shelf placement affected discovery. Radio programmers shaped music exposure. Television schedules decided what audiences encountered. Newspaper editors chose front pages. Curators selected exhibitions. Teachers built syllabi.

Every distribution system has always filtered abundance.

The difference is that algorithmic systems can personalize, measure, and update at a scale and speed previous gatekeepers could not.

Personalization turns one public shelf into millions of private shelves.

That can be liberating. A listener with obscure interests can discover artists who would never reach local radio. A reader can find a technical essay written for a tiny audience. A shopper can locate a replacement part from a small manufacturer. A patient can find a community around a rare condition. Distribution becomes less dependent on a few mass-market intermediaries.

But personalization also weakens shared observation. Two people can inhabit the same platform and receive different worlds.

This matters because humans use other people's attention as a calibration signal. When everyone sees the same newspaper front page, citizens can at least know what others saw. When feeds diverge, each person observes a social environment partly constructed from his own behavior.

The mirror becomes customized.

A person who clicks on stories about urban crime may receive more crime stories. The increased exposure can make crime feel more prevalent or salient. Concern can increase, producing more clicks. The recommender records stronger interest and continues.

The loop does not need to invent facts. It can change perceived frequency by changing exposure.

Availability becomes worldview.

The same mechanism can be cheerful. A user watches videos about woodworking, receives more, buys tools, practices, joins communities, and becomes a woodworker. The algorithm did not manipulate a false desire. It helped a weak interest find enough reinforcement to become an identity.

This is why the debate over recommendation cannot be reduced to "algorithms manipulate people" versus "algorithms merely give people what they want." Both descriptions can be incomplete.

People have multiple possible interests. Environments develop some and neglect others.

A child exposed to music may become musical. A graduate placed in a strong engineering team may become more technical. A traveler introduced to a cuisine may develop taste. Preference is partly a history of encounters.

Recommendation systems industrialize encounter.

Their objective functions therefore matter enormously.

If a system optimizes for immediate click probability, it will learn different behavior than one optimizing for long-term retention, explicit satisfaction, diversity, purchase value, learning, or some combination. Each objective is a theory of what the platform is for.

The choice is often hidden inside technical language.

"Engagement" sounds neutral until one asks which engagement, over what horizon, from whom, with what external effects.

A user who spends an hour angrily arguing may be highly engaged and deeply dissatisfied. A reader who finds a perfect answer in thirty seconds may be satisfied and leave. A platform rewarded by time spent can misread the difference if its metrics cannot see the goal.

This is Goodhart's law running through code.

The platform optimizes a proxy. Creators learn the proxy. Users adapt to the resulting content. The data change. Engineers retrain. The ecosystem coevolves.

Creators are often the fastest learners in the loop because distribution determines livelihood. They test thumbnails, titles, hooks, formats, posting times, topics, lengths, keywords, pacing, and emotional intensity. Advice industries arise around the platform's apparent preferences. The platform changes its model to reduce spam or improve quality. Creators adapt again.

An algorithmic market develops folklore.

Some folklore is wrong. Creators infer causal rules from noisy outcomes. A post succeeds for reasons nobody fully knows, and the creator attributes success to a specific phrase. Thousands of participants run informal experiments with weak controls. Superstition spreads alongside genuine learning.

This resembles financial markets, where traders build stories about what "the market wants" from imperfect feedback.

In both systems, participants model the model.

A sophisticated creator asks not only what viewers like but what the recommendation system is likely to infer that viewers will like. A sophisticated seller asks not only what customers want but what search ranking rewards. A job applicant asks not only what a recruiter values but what an automated screen recognizes. A website publisher asks not only what readers need but what search engines can discover.

Human communication bends toward machine legibility.

This is one of the quieter transformations of algorithmic culture. People change language, structure, metadata, and behavior so that intermediating systems can classify them.

Restaurants design dishes to photograph well. Musicians consider the opening seconds that appear in previews. Writers think about searchable phrasing. Merchants structure titles around queries. Candidates format résumés for parsing. Influencers stage authenticity in forms platforms distribute.

The machine does not simply understand culture. Culture learns how to be understood by the machine.

That is a recursive relationship.

It can create efficiency. Standardized metadata help discovery. Clear titles help users. Structured résumés can reduce arbitrary screening. Yet machine legibility can crowd out qualities difficult to encode.

A peculiar book may not resemble books a reader previously liked. A candidate from an unusual career path may lack familiar signals. A new musical form may not fit established clusters. A small business may have limited behavioral data. Novelty often begins as a classification problem.

Recommenders therefore face an exploration-exploitation tradeoff.

Exploit known preferences and satisfaction may be high today. Explore unfamiliar options and the system may learn something valuable, but the user may dislike the experiment. Too much exploitation creates a comfortable tunnel. Too much exploration feels random.

This is not merely a technical parameter. It is a cultural policy.

How much surprise should a system impose?

A bookstore clerk can answer with judgment. She may know that a customer who asks for another detective novel would love a history book for reasons not captured in purchase history. Algorithmic systems can model serendipity too, but they must define it.

Serendipity is difficult to optimize because its value is recognized after the deviation.

The user did not ask for the thing she did not know she wanted.

That sentence describes innovation more generally.

The risk of recursive recommendation is not only ideological echo chambers. It is preference overfitting. A system becomes extremely good at predicting the present self and thereby reduces encounters that would have developed another self.

This is an identity problem disguised as a ranking problem.

People are not static customer vectors. They age, move, fall in love, become parents, lose jobs, recover from illness, enter professions, leave religions, learn instruments, change politics, abandon hobbies, and sometimes simply get bored.

A system trained on yesterday can become sticky.

Anyone who has watched recommendations continue serving an interest long after it faded has seen the lag. The model remembers a version of the user the user has already left.

That memory can be annoying. In more consequential domains it can be restrictive. A worker's historical job titles influence future job recommendations. A borrower's past credit behavior affects future terms. A student's prior performance affects opportunities. The digital self becomes a path-dependent archive.

People need a right to surprise their models.

This is not a formal legal phrase here. It is a design principle. Good systems should permit state change that history did not predict.

One way is decay: older signals matter less. Another is exploration. Another is explicit user control. Another is contextual modeling that recognizes a person may have several modes rather than one stable preference. Another is diversity in objectives. Another is allowing users to reset or separate identities.

The principle resembles biological learning. A system that never forgets can be as maladaptive as one that remembers nothing.

Feedback needs forgetting.

Platforms also need independent measures of welfare because behavior alone can be ambiguous.

If a user repeatedly consumes content that leaves her miserable, is repeated consumption evidence of satisfaction? Behavioral data answer what happened, not necessarily whether what happened was good.

This is where product design meets moral philosophy.

A company can reasonably say its users are adults making choices. It can also recognize that its interface shapes those choices. The two claims are compatible. Agency exists inside environments.

Casinos understand this. Grocery stores understand it. Architects understand it. Schools understand it. Choice architecture is not unique to software.

What is new is the feedback resolution. The environment can update itself around the individual.

A store shelf does not rearrange after every glance. A feed can.

That adaptive environment makes causal attribution difficult. Did the user want the content, or did the system create the path by which the content became wanted? Usually both questions are too binary. Desire and exposure interact.

Ethnography helps here because lived use often differs from platform categories. Researchers observing households, teenagers, workers, gamers, or fan communities can see meanings that event logs miss. A "share" may be affection, mockery, obligation, outrage, bookmarking, or group membership. A long viewing session may be delight, insomnia, companionship, avoidance, or autoplay.

The same click can have several human meanings.

This is why quantitative systems need qualitative contact. Without it, a platform can become exquisitely optimized to a behavior it has misinterpreted.

The principle applies far beyond consumer internet companies. Banks need to talk to customers behind risk segments. hospitals need patient narratives behind utilization data. Employers need observation behind productivity dashboards. Governments need fieldwork behind administrative records.

Data tell you what the system can see.

Ethnography tells you what seeing feels like from inside the system.

A recommendation engine cannot escape feedback, and it should not try. Learning from response is the product.

The question is whether the loop contains enough surprise, enough independent welfare measurement, enough forgetting, and enough user agency to remain open.

A good recommendation expands a person's world while learning it.

A bad one becomes so accurate about yesterday that tomorrow has nowhere to enter.
