# The Run on Confidence

A bank can fail because people believe it will fail.

That sentence is so familiar that its strangeness has worn off.

Most objects do not work this way. A bridge does not collapse because commuters suspect it will. A sandwich does not spoil because diners whisper. A mountain does not become shorter when surveyors lose confidence in its height. Banks, by contrast, sit inside a class of institutions whose stability depends partly on shared expectations about stability.

The reason is structural. Banks and many bank-like institutions borrow short and lend long. Depositors expect money to be available on demand. The bank invests much of that money in loans and securities that cannot all be converted to cash instantly without cost. Under ordinary conditions, this maturity transformation is useful. Savers get liquid claims; borrowers get long-term funding. The system works because not everyone demands cash at once.

Confidence is part of the balance sheet even though accountants do not record it as an asset.

If enough depositors expect others to withdraw, withdrawal becomes rational. The depositor need not believe the bank is fundamentally insolvent. She only needs to believe that arriving late is dangerous. The anticipation of a run can create the run.

This is a coordination problem with a queue.

The bank-run literature in economics formalized this intuition in several ways. The Diamond-Dybvig model, published in 1983, showed how demand deposits can provide useful liquidity while also creating multiple possible equilibria, including a run. Later work added information, global games, market funding, deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort policy, and other realities. The technical details evolve, but the core second-order problem remains: my action depends on what I believe others will do, and their actions depend on what they believe people like me will do.

The line outside the bank becomes information about the bank.

This was obvious in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when physical queues formed outside doors. Modern runs can move at the speed of a group chat.

The medium changes the loop gain.

In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank failed after a rapid loss of confidence and deposit outflows. The bank had a concentrated depositor base, substantial exposure to interest-rate risk through securities, and large amounts of uninsured deposits. Rising rates had reduced the market value of long-duration assets. When the bank announced actions to strengthen its balance sheet, the announcement did not calm depositors. It contributed to alarm. Messages spread through investor and founder networks. Withdrawal requests accelerated. Regulators closed the bank.

No single sentence explains that failure. Asset-liability management mattered. Regulation and supervision mattered. Deposit concentration mattered. The economic environment mattered. But the speed of social transmission was impossible to ignore. A network of technologically sophisticated customers could coordinate fear without needing formal coordination.

The bank's customers watched one another watch the bank.

That is the echo economy in concentrated form.

Deposit insurance was invented partly to break this loop. If ordinary depositors know the state guarantees qualifying deposits, they have less reason to run simply because others might run. The guarantee changes expectations before it changes cash flows.

A credible backstop works by making itself unnecessary in many states of the world.

This is one of the peculiarities of institutions built around confidence. The strongest fire extinguisher is the one everyone trusts enough that nobody lights the match.

Central banking contains many similar devices. A lender of last resort can provide liquidity against sound collateral when private funding disappears. Emergency facilities can reassure markets that forced selling will not become universal. Deposit guarantees, capital rules, liquidity requirements, and stress tests all attempt, in different ways, to reduce the chance that beliefs about others' behavior become self-validating collapse.

Yet every stabilizer changes incentives.

If depositors expect rescue, they may pay less attention to bank risk. If creditors expect central-bank support, institutions may take more liquidity risk. If markets assume authorities will intervene after large declines, investors may increase leverage. The policy designed to break one feedback loop can create another.

This is moral hazard not as a moral accusation but as a systems effect.

Insurance changes behavior. That is what insurance is supposed to do. The policy problem is deciding which behavioral changes are worth the stability gained.

Banking therefore reveals a larger truth about institutions: expectations are often an input to institutional performance.

A currency works because people expect others to accept it. A legal system works partly because people expect judgments to be enforced. A government bond market depends on confidence that contractual promises and institutional procedures will survive political disagreement. A university credential has value because employers, students, families, and other institutions expect others to recognize it. A platform marketplace depends on buyers expecting sellers to deliver and sellers expecting buyers to pay.

Institutional trust is recursive recognition.

This does not mean trust is free-floating belief. Durable trust usually rests on enforcement, history, capital, competence, norms, and repeated experience. But once established, trust itself becomes economically productive. Transactions become cheaper because parties do not need to re-prove the institution from zero each time.

The reverse is equally important. Distrust raises transaction costs before any underlying asset disappears.

A company rumored to be in trouble may find suppliers tightening terms. Customers delay commitments. Strong employees take calls from recruiters. Lenders ask harder questions. Each defensive response worsens the company's position. A rumor that began as exaggeration can become causal.

This is why crisis communication is not public relations in the ornamental sense. Information architecture can affect solvency.

But communication faces a paradox. Reassurance can backfire when audiences infer that reassurance itself signals danger.

If a chief executive suddenly announces, without prompting, that liquidity is excellent, investors may wonder why liquidity is being discussed. If a government says there is absolutely no reason to worry about a currency, residents may begin checking exchange rates. The attempt to close a loop opens another: people interpret the fact of communication as information.

Second-order audiences listen to what is said and to why it needed saying.

Diplomacy works this way. Parenting does too. A manager insisting that layoffs are not being considered may accidentally introduce the possibility. A romantic partner saying "you can trust me" can reassure or alarm depending on context. The statement has semantic content and signaling content.

Markets are particularly ruthless readers of signaling content because money is at stake.

A central bank does not simply choose an interest rate. It communicates a reaction function. Traders study wording, forecasts, dissents, press conferences, and sometimes punctuation for clues about how future decisions will respond to future data. The institution then observes market reactions as evidence about financial conditions and expectations. The observers and the observed are in a conversation conducted partly through prices.

This can create awkward circularity.

Officials want to know what markets expect. Markets want to know what officials intend. If officials use market expectations to guide policy while markets derive expectations from officials, information can become endogenous. The apparent consensus may contain less independent judgment than its precision suggests.

A similar problem appears in credit markets. Rating agencies assess borrowers. Investors use ratings to satisfy mandates or regulations. Borrowing costs move. Those costs affect the borrower's finances. The rating agency later observes the changed finances. A rating can therefore be both assessment and intervention.

This became especially consequential when regulations and investment policies embedded ratings into rules. Once a symbol triggers mechanical actions, the symbol carries more power.

The recursive structure should make us cautious about institutional labels that appear binary.

"Safe" and "unsafe" are often state-dependent. A bank that is safe under ordinary withdrawals can become unsafe under universal withdrawal. A market that is liquid for a small seller can be illiquid for everyone selling together. A debt level that is sustainable at low interest rates may become difficult at high rates. A military posture that deters one adversary may provoke another. The quality belongs partly to the surrounding behavior.

Systems have verbs hidden inside their adjectives.

This matters for risk management. Risk is often reported as if it were a property attached to an asset. In reality, risk can depend on who owns the asset, how it is financed, what rules force sales, how crowded the position is, and whether counterparties share the same model.

A Treasury security can be low credit risk and still generate severe losses for a leveraged holder if rates rise and financing becomes stressed. A high-quality mortgage can be part of a dangerous system if underwriting, securitization, leverage, and correlated exposure create a fragile structure. The object alone does not tell you the system risk.

Ask what happens when everyone reacts.

That question is one of the best diagnostic tools in finance.

What happens if every depositor takes the prudent action of withdrawing early?

What happens if every fund obeys the same volatility limit?

What happens if every lender tightens standards during recession?

What happens if every household saves more at once?

What happens if every company cuts investment to protect cash?

The individual action may be sensible. The aggregate result may worsen the condition motivating the action.

Economics contains several names for such situations: paradox of thrift, fire-sale externalities, coordination failures, procyclicality. The language varies because the mechanisms vary. What connects them is that private optimization changes the environment of other optimizers.

The environment answers back.

Bank runs dramatize this because the outcome can be so fast. But slow runs occur too.

A town loses a major employer. Skilled families leave. The tax base shrinks. Public services deteriorate. More families leave. Businesses close because demand weakens. The town becomes less attractive to investment. None of these steps requires panic. A negative feedback loop can unfold over decades.

A company can suffer a talent run. A few respected employees depart. Their departures become evidence for others. Recruiters mention the exits. Internal projects weaken. Stronger employees become more marketable because they are carrying more load. Additional departures confirm the story.

A political party can suffer a donor run. A university can suffer an applicant run. A social network can suffer a user run. Platforms with network effects are especially sensitive because the product's value partly depends on who else is present.

Exit changes the thing being exited.

That makes exit both a right and a system force.

Albert Hirschman famously distinguished exit, voice, and loyalty as responses to decline in organizations and institutions. The framework is valuable here because each response creates different feedback. Exit removes support and sends a signal through departure. Voice stays inside and sends information through complaint or reform. Loyalty delays exit, which can give institutions time to recover but can also protect failure.

A resilient institution needs some balance among them.

If exit is impossible, leaders can ignore feedback. If exit is frictionless, communities can unravel before voice has time to work. If loyalty becomes blind, decline persists. If voice becomes costless theater, nobody changes.

Trust is healthiest when it is neither infinitely fragile nor infinitely forgiving.

This is a difficult cultural achievement.

Organizations often talk about building trust as if trust were an attitude employees should adopt. But trust is an expectation about future behavior. It grows from repeated evidence that commitments survive inconvenience. It weakens when words and actions diverge. Motivational language can amplify existing trust; it cannot substitute for the causal history that produced it.

The same is true of public institutions. Confidence campaigns cannot permanently outrun operational reality.

This is the boundary that saves us from relativism. Belief matters, but belief meets constraints.

A bank cannot survive forever on confidence if its assets are worthless. A currency cannot be maintained by rhetoric if fiscal and monetary institutions destroy purchasing power. A company cannot retain employees with culture slogans while repeatedly violating commitments. Trust can bridge uncertainty. It cannot erase arithmetic.

The art is to recognize that arithmetic and confidence interact.

During calm periods, institutions often underinvest in trust because its value is invisible. The bank's deposit franchise looks cheap. The regulator's credibility looks abstract. The company's reputation looks like marketing. The alliance looks ceremonial. Then stress arrives, and the accumulated expectation determines how quickly people run.

Trust is stored time.

It is the memory of previous loops that ended well.

A resilient system uses that memory to prevent every new shock from becoming a referendum on existence. It gives participants reason to wait for information instead of racing for the door.

That waiting is not passive.

It is one of the most valuable forms of coordination a society can produce.
