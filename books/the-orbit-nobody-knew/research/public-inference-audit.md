# Research audit — public inference before declassification

## Scope

This note stress-tests the opening chapter's strongest secrecy claim: that JUMPSEAT is useful because an observable orbital container remained officially undisclosed until December 2025. The distinction survives, but the current draft should not let **official declassification lag** stand in for **public ignorance**.

## Fresh source check

### NRO, January 28, 2026 — primary institutional source

The NRO announcement establishes the newly declassified baseline: JUMPSEAT was the first-generation U.S. highly elliptical orbit signals-collection satellite; missions 7701–7708 launched from 1971 to 1987; the program operated until 2006; and the December 2025 action was a limited declassification.

Primary: https://www.nro.gov/news-media-featured-stories/news-media-archive/News-Article/Article/4392223/declassifying-jumpseat-an-american-pioneer-in-space/

Use this source for what NRO has now acknowledged. Do not use its heritage language as independent proof of achieved intelligence value.

### Dwayne A. Day, *The Space Review*, February 2, 2026 — specialist historical synthesis

Day's post-declassification reconstruction is useful chiefly because it identifies a direct counterexample to any implication that the **name** remained publicly unknown until 2025: he reports that JUMPSEAT's name appeared in Seymour Hersh's book on KAL 007 in the late 1980s. Day also describes the first launch's conspicuously different Titan fairing and notes that an observer with orbital data could recognize the unusual highly elliptical orbit.

Source: https://www.thespacereview.com/article/5151/1

This is a secondary specialist source, not the endpoint of the provenance chain. The book should locate the exact Hersh edition/page and determine what Hersh actually claimed before treating that publication as the dated public-disclosure boundary.

### Charles P. Vick / GlobalSecurity, 2007 — timestamped pre-declassification public model

A 2007 public page explicitly labeled JUMPSEAT as an NRO/USAF/NSA SIGINT spacecraft series and supplied a proposed launch chronology and technical description. Several particulars on that page now appear inconsistent with, or at least more confident than, the limited NRO release. That is exactly why it is valuable evidence.

Source: https://www.globalsecurity.org/space/systems/jumpseat.htm

The page demonstrates that a fairly specific public model existed at least eighteen years before official declassification. It does **not** establish that every technical particular in that model was correct. Preserve the 2007 page as a frozen prediction and score its claims against newly released records one by one.

## Strongest counterargument

The title and opening can be read as implying that nobody outside the compartments knew what JUMPSEAT was until 2025. The available record already defeats that broad reading. The codeword and SIGINT attribution circulated publicly decades earlier, and observers could reason from launch and orbit.

If that substantially collapses the book's thesis, the book should change rather than defend the title by semantics.

The stronger surviving proposition is narrower and more interesting:

**Official existence, public inference, adversary knowledge, and detailed capability knowledge were different clocks. JUMPSEAT lets the book measure the distance among them.**

The relevant question is therefore not “Was JUMPSEAT secret for fifty-four years?” but “Which facts about JUMPSEAT remained protected, from which audiences, after outsiders had already inferred its name, orbital family, and likely mission category?”

## Editorial consequence

A later surgical prose pass should revise the opening's phrase “whose birth had been classified for more than half a century” and any nearby language that allows declassification date to masquerade as discovery date. The chapter already contains the corrective sentence “Declassification date is not discovery date”; the research now gives that sentence teeth.

The best narrative device is a **dated prediction ledger** rather than another abstract secrecy framework. For each pre-December-2025 source, record publication date and separate claims about:

- object/launch identity;
- JUMPSEAT codeword;
- orbit;
- SIGINT mission category;
- payload architecture;
- targets;
- processing/ground segment;
- performance or intelligence yield.

Then compare each claim with what the declassified record eventually permits us to score. Wrong predictions belong in the chapter. A secrecy comparator becomes much more informative when it measures false positives as well as impressive guesses.

## Evidence boundary

A correct pre-declassification guess about mission category is evidence that one layer of secrecy had failed for that observer. It is not evidence that payload details, tasking, processing, achieved performance, intelligence yield, or vulnerabilities were known. Conversely, a late official declassification date is evidence of classification policy, not proof that outsiders remained ignorant until that date.
