# Exponential Compression

For most of history, the distance between an idea and its execution was measured in people, money, and time.

Want to send a message across an ocean? Find a ship. Want to reproduce a book? Find scribes, then a press, then paper, ink, distribution, and buyers. Want to calculate the orbit of a planet? Find mathematicians. Want to build a company? Hire the people who know the things you do not.

Capability was expensive because capability was attached to scarce machinery, scarce knowledge, scarce labor, or all three.

Then, one by one, technologies began cutting the price.

Not gently.

Photography went from something worth posing for to something we do accidentally with a phone in our pocket. Computation went from a resource governments scheduled to something cheap enough to hide inside toys, thermostats, watches, and greeting cards. Communication went from a journey to a phone call to a text message to a continuous stream of video carried around by billions of people.

Each time, we initially misunderstood what cheaper meant. We imagined using the new technology to do the old thing for less.

Then somebody discovered what became possible when there was no longer much reason to conserve it.

That is the important threshold. A ten-percent reduction in cost usually produces optimization. A tenfold reduction begins to produce new behavior. Another tenfold reduction can make the old behavior hard to recognize. The capability moves from a purchase to a habit, from a habit to infrastructure, and eventually into the background of ordinary life.

Waste is often the signal that the transition has occurred. We take twenty photographs to keep one. Computers perform calculations whose results are discarded. Networks move duplicate data because retransmission is cheaper than perfect coordination. Storage became cheap enough that deleting information sometimes costs more attention than keeping it.

Abundance does not look like efficiency. It often looks profligate.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to push cognition toward the same boundary.

Competent thought has always been expensive in a peculiar way. It could not be purchased independently of a competent human being: educated, awake, available, motivated, and paid. A company that wanted more analysis needed more analysts. A student who wanted more tutoring needed more of a tutor's time. A scientist who wanted more hypotheses needed more researchers, more hours, or both. The cost of thinking was buried inside salaries, institutions, credentials, schedules, and the biological fact that a person can only pay attention to so many things in a day.

Now pieces of cognition can increasingly be bought as computation.

Reading, summarizing, translating, classifying, drafting, checking, programming, searching, comparing, planning, explaining. None of these capabilities has become infinite or infallible. But their marginal cost is falling, and that may matter more than the argument over whether any particular model deserves to be called intelligent.

The first-order effect is substitution. A machine does something a person used to do.

The more interesting effect is consumption.

When light became cheaper, humanity did not preserve the old quantity of illumination and pocket the savings. We lit streets, factories, offices, stadiums, signs, parking lots, homes, screens, and entire cities at night. When bandwidth became cheap, we did not send the same number of messages for less money. We built video calls, streaming services, multiplayer worlds, feeds that refresh continuously, and software that assumes the network is always there.

Cheap cognition is likely to be consumed the same way.

A lawyer who costs hundreds of dollars an hour is reserved for consequential problems. A capable legal system that costs cents might read every contract before it is signed, every policy before it is accepted, every invoice before it is paid. A tutor who is scarce gets scheduled. A tutor who is effectively free can stay present through every exercise. Software that takes a team months to build becomes a capital project. Software that takes a machine minutes to produce can be written for one person, one problem, one afternoon, then thrown away.

The question is therefore not merely which workers artificial intelligence replaces.

It is what civilization does when it can afford vastly more thinking.

More experiments. More simulations. More drafts. More negotiation. More software. More research. More personalization. More checking. More bureaucracy, too. More fraud. More persuasion. More optimization aimed at goals we may later wish we had specified differently.

Falling costs do not abolish scarcity. They move it.

Cheap computation made data valuable. Cheap distribution made attention scarce. Cheap production makes selection harder. Cheap cognition may raise the value of energy, verified information, authority, judgment, trust, taste, physical execution, and the right to act.

That movement is the deeper pattern of technological change. Progress repeatedly compresses one constraint and exposes the one underneath it.

This book is about those compressions: why they accelerate, why demand usually expands into the space they create, and why the world after a capability becomes abundant is rarely a cheaper version of the world before.

For most of history, human beings were defined partly by the enormous expense of making things happen.

We are entering an era in which more things can happen cheaply, quickly, repeatedly, and at enormous scale.

The old question was what we could afford to do.

The next one is what is worth doing when the old excuse of expense begins to disappear.
