# Pigeons in the Loop

There is a metal-and-wood object in the Smithsonian that looks like a prop from a comedy written by someone who dislikes engineers. It is a missile nose cone divided into compartments for pigeons.

That description is accurate and almost useless.

The interesting fact is not that B. F. Skinner once proposed putting birds inside a weapon. Wartime research generated stranger hardware than that. The interesting fact is that the birds were supposed to do several jobs we now assign to a stack of optics, software, processors and control logic. They would see the target, distinguish it from the background, notice when the image drifted away from center, and generate the corrective signal that brought the weapon back toward what they had been trained to follow.

The pigeon was not cargo. It was part of the guidance system.

That makes Project Pigeon and its postwar continuation, ORCON, worth recovering without either ridicule or reverence. The project sits at an awkward border between psychology and engineering, between organism and machine, between demonstrated behavior and military confidence. Skinner later called it a crackpot idea, but the phrase was not a confession that it had never worked. It was partly a description of social classification: an idea could perform on the bench and still arrive in the wrong intellectual costume.

This book asks a narrower question than whether pigeon-guided missiles would have changed the war. They did not enter combat. The question is what the project reveals about technical judgment when a component is alive.

The answer requires refusing two easy stories. One says the military foolishly ignored a brilliant biological computer. The other says sensible engineers briefly humored a ridiculous psychologist until proper electronics arrived. Both are too clean. The historical record instead offers a sequence of prototypes, demonstrations, funding decisions, institutional handoffs, tests, competing guidance systems and retrospective accounts whose evidentiary weight differs sharply.

The pigeons deserve the same discipline we would apply to a radar seeker. What could they discriminate? Under what illumination? At what angular rate? With what latency? How did vibration, novelty, fatigue, satiation and stress affect them? How repeatable was the training? How much of the control loop was actually biological, and how much was mechanical transduction wrapped around the bird? What did the military compare them against? What would have counted as success?

If those questions make the story less funny, good. It becomes stranger instead.
