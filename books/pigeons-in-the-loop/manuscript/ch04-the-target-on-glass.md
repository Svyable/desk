# The Target on Glass

The essential image in Project Pigeon is not a bird inside a bomb. It is a target moving across a small screen.

Skinner's problem was to arrange the world so that pecking became an error signal. In the developed concept, an optical system projected the target scene into the nose. The pigeon had been trained to peck at the target image. If the image stayed centered, the pecks stayed near center. If the missile's line of sight drifted, the image moved and the bird followed it. Mechanical or pneumatic pickup converted the off-center response into a correction.

The elegance is easy to miss because the organism draws attention away from the control loop. The pigeon did not need to know whether the target lay east or west. It did not need range in meters. It did not need a map. It needed a learned local rule: keep pecking the thing that has been made behaviorally salient. The guidance hardware then cared about where those pecks occurred relative to center.

This is closer to pursuit tracking than to navigation.

It also reveals the system's vulnerability. Training is only useful if the image inside the weapon preserves enough of the features the bird learned. A ship seen from one scale becomes another shape as the weapon closes. Aspect changes. The horizon tilts. Whitecaps appear. Smoke can cover the superstructure. Another ship can enter the field. Sun glitter can dominate contrast. The target can maneuver. If the optical path itself shakes or blurs, the animal is being asked to discriminate a moving pattern through an instrument, not to recognize the familiar object in ordinary space.

Skinner and his collaborators therefore had to train on moving and changing visual material, not merely on a painted silhouette nailed to a wall. This is where later archival reconstruction becomes important. Demonstration films and Skinner's retrospective account show that moving-target pursuit was central. They do not by themselves settle the operational envelope.

There is a useful modern temptation here: call the pigeon a classifier. That is partly right, but the project relied on more than classification. The target had to remain a target continuously enough to support control. One correct recognition followed by three seconds of confusion could be fatal to the loop. Guidance performance is temporal.

The bird also generated dense feedback. A peck is discrete, but repeated pecking can produce a stream of directional samples. The engineering question becomes whether that stream is fast and stable enough relative to missile dynamics.

Project Pigeon was therefore a collision between two clocks. The airframe had a time constant. So did trained behavior. The weapon could only work if the animal's loop was comfortably faster than the errors it was meant to correct.

That is a more demanding proposition than teaching a pigeon to recognize a ship.

It is also testable.
