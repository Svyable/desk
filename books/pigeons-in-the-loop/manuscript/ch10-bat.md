# Bat

The pigeons lost to another animal.

Bat was only an animal in name. The U.S. Navy's radar-homing glide bomb was an electronic answer to the same broad problem: release a weapon outside the most dangerous defensive envelope and let it continue toward a target without a human steering every correction.

NIST's institutional history calls Bat the first fully automated guided missile system used successfully in combat. The National Bureau of Standards coordinated the weapon's development and worked on aerodynamics and stabilization; Bell Telephone Laboratories and MIT contributed to guidance technology. The Smithsonian describes a roughly thousand-pound glide bomb with radar homing and autopilot-driven control surfaces, carried by Navy patrol aircraft and used against Japanese targets in 1945.

This is the comparison Project Pigeon cannot avoid.

Radar did not need to recognize a ship the way a pigeon recognized a visual target. It needed enough reflected radio energy, processed through a seeker architecture capable of deriving the steering relation. That brought its own problems: sea clutter, target reflectivity, antenna behavior, electronic noise, stabilization and the fragility of wartime components. But radar offered something procurement organizations value enormously: a development path that looked like engineering all the way down.

The components could be specified, manufactured, measured electrically, redesigned and scaled through industrial processes already expanding at wartime speed. A bad radar set did not require retraining. It required diagnosis.

That difference may be more important than raw accuracy.

Technology competitions are often narrated as if the superior device wins by performance. In practice, systems are selected across production, logistics, maintainability, training, interoperability, growth potential and institutional confidence. A pigeon system could theoretically equal an electronic seeker's terminal accuracy and still lose because every weapon needs live-bird handling and a behavioral qualification pipeline.

The bird has to be fed before launch. Its readiness cannot be checked with a voltmeter. Storage is measured in animal life rather than shelf life. Replacements are grown and trained, not machined. The logistics are not impossible—militaries managed enormous numbers of horses, dogs and messenger pigeons—but guided missiles were moving in the opposite direction, toward compact systems that could wait in storage and be handled by ordnance crews.

Bat also benefited from compounding investment. Radar was not a single-purpose curiosity. It was transforming air defense, navigation, fire control and detection. Improvements in tubes, antennas, signal processing and manufacturing spilled across programs. Project Pigeon's biological tracker did not sit on an industrial curve of comparable breadth.

This creates a counterfactual that should discipline the book. Even if Skinner's system had been granted another year, what would have happened when radar homing improved another year after that? The pigeon did not merely need to work. It needed to remain competitive against a technology receiving massive distributed investment.

The answer may be that the bird occupied a temporary window.

Temporary technologies are not failures. An expedient can be rational if it arrives before the dominant architecture matures. The British used acoustic mirrors before radar. Early computers used relays before electronic switching. A biological target tracker could have been sensible for a few years and still disappear completely once electronics crossed a reliability threshold.

That is a better claim than saying the pigeon was ahead of its time. It may have been exactly of its time.

The Bat record also prevents romanticism about what the military lost by canceling Project Pigeon. The United States did field an autonomous anti-ship guided weapon during the war. There was no empty technological hole that only Skinner could fill. The alternative matured enough to reach combat.

But Bat does not automatically prove the earlier cancellation rational. Timing matters. If Project Pigeon was stopped before Bat's approach had demonstrated comparable maturity, the decision was still made under uncertainty. Historians should not let the eventual winner reach backward and make every earlier judgment obvious.

The fairest reconstruction asks what a committee knew at the decision date, not what the Smithsonian knows now.

Bat won history.

Project Pigeon still gets to cross-examine the verdict.
