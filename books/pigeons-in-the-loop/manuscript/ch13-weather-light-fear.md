# Weather, Light, Fear

A pigeon can see a ship.

That sentence contains almost no engineering information.

Can it see the ship through the optical system in haze? Against sun glitter? When the target is foreshortened? At dawn? Against coastline instead of open water? When smoke crosses the image? When the target becomes only a few pixels' worth of optical detail in the projected field? When the apparent size changes continuously during approach?

These questions turn an animal story into a sensor story.

The pigeon visual system is not a generic camera. Birds have visual capabilities shaped by their ecology, and pigeons in particular are formidable discriminators in laboratory tasks. But a military seeker is judged on the mission image, not on abstract acuity. The relevant environment is whatever reaches the animal after lenses, vibration, illumination and projection have transformed it.

Skinner's team could train across variation, but every variation taught becomes part of the data requirement. If birds learn a white triangle on green, that does not establish ship recognition. If they learn photographs of ships, the photographer has chosen scale and aspect. If they learn moving images, the training set has a motion distribution. The modern vocabulary of dataset shift is tempting here because the structural issue is similar: a system trained under one distribution may fail when deployment supplies another.

The analogy has limits. Pigeons are not static statistical models. They continue learning. That can be an advantage if reinforcement is available, and a liability if the deployment environment teaches the wrong thing. A terminal weapon does not have much time for online adaptation.

Fear is another badly specified variable.

Public retellings love the image of unflappable birds continuing to peck while explosions, gunfire or violent motion occur around them. Skinner himself used demonstrations to attack the intuition that the pigeons would panic. The correct response is neither to scoff nor to accept. It is to ask what disturbance was applied, at what magnitude, for how long, with what controls and with what performance loss.

`The bird kept pecking` is qualitative robustness evidence.

A weapons program needs a curve.

At what acceleration does tracking error increase? Does the animal habituate to noise? Is low pressure relevant to the altitude profile of the guidance compartment? Does temperature affect response rate? Does vibration blur the projected image before it affects the animal? Is the dominant failure biological or optical?

One reason the biological component can be unfairly blamed is that failures upstream appear as animal failures. A pigeon cannot peck a target the optics no longer resolve. A radar receiver cannot detect a target below its signal-to-noise threshold either, but engineers are trained to decompose that failure into antenna, transmitter, receiver, clutter and processing.

Project Pigeon needs the same decomposition.

There is a second unfairness that can run the other way. The bird's ability to compensate for degraded inputs may hide weaknesses in the apparatus during a demonstration. Biological perception performs enormous preprocessing without exposing intermediate signals. A target that a simple photocell could not distinguish may remain obvious to a pigeon. That is genuinely useful, but it makes system characterization harder because the feature set is implicit.

This is why the project belongs in the history of sensors as much as psychology.

The pigeon was a sensor whose transfer function could be trained but not fully derived. It integrated edges, motion, shape and learned salience inside a nervous system the engineers did not model. That power was free in computational terms and expensive in epistemic terms.

The weapon wanted certainty about an organism precisely because the organism was valuable for doing things the designers could not yet reproduce mechanically.

Weather, light and fear are not side anecdotes.

They are the price of borrowing a sensor you did not build.
