# The Nose Cone in the Museum

The Smithsonian lists the object as psychological apparatus.

That is one way to describe it. Another is weapon hardware. Another is a piece of evidence that the boundary between those categories was briefly negotiable.

The surviving apparatus attributed to B. F. Skinner is roughly the size of a large cabinet, built from metal and wood, and designed around a three-pigeon guidance concept. The museum's description is careful about what it knows: during the Second World War, Skinner proposed training pigeons to respond to an image of a target inside a missile nose cone. The object exists. The project existed. The idea was not a newspaper prank reconstructed after the fact. Hardware crossed the line from sketch to artifact.

Museums flatten things by necessity. An object receives a name, maker, date, dimensions, accession number. Project Pigeon resists that flattening because the object is ridiculous only if one silently imports the electronics that later made it obsolete. Stand in 1943 instead. A weapon released from an aircraft has to know where the ship went after release. A human bombardier can no longer correct it. Radio command requires a human operator and a reliable link. Radar homing is possible, but it is still an expensive and demanding engineering problem involving transmitters, receivers, stabilization, power, antennas and signal interpretation. Television guidance exists as a developing possibility, but a camera does not by itself decide what to follow.

A pigeon already possesses a compact visual system, target-following behavior, motor output and a metabolism that provides its own power. It is insensitive to vacuum-tube supply chains because it contains no vacuum tubes. It can be trained to respond selectively to shapes. None of that proves it belongs in a weapon. It does mean the proposal should be evaluated as an engineering trade rather than a zoological joke.

The museum object also creates an evidentiary problem that will recur throughout this book. The artifact proves that a guidance apparatus was built. It does not prove combat readiness. Skinner's later account proves that he remembered experiments, demonstrations and institutional resistance. It does not transform memory into a complete test report. Later historians reconstruct contracts and organizational decisions from archives, but those records do not automatically tell us what a particular bird did on a particular run.

So the nose cone is a good place to begin because it is solid and incomplete.

Three compartments imply redundancy. Windows imply an optical path. A movable plate or transduction mechanism implies that peck location was meant to become a steering command. Every physical feature narrows the space of possible stories. The apparatus does not ask us to believe Skinner. It asks us to explain why someone went to the trouble of building it.

The answer begins before the nose cone, with a bird keeping pace beside a train.
