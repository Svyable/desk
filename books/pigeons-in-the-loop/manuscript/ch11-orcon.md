# ORCON

The project came back after the war.

That fact alone complicates the standard story in which sober engineers finally killed an embarrassing wartime idea. In 1948 the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory reopened the basic question under a name that sounded more respectable and somehow stranger: ORCON, for organic control.

The Navy did not need Skinner's original enthusiasm in order to resurrect it. The existence of a postwar program means that at least some technical people considered the biological tracking problem unresolved rather than ridiculous beyond further study.

The best source still waiting to be fully recovered for this manuscript is an NRL progress report published after declassification, cited as `Project ORCON: The Use of Pigeons to Guide Missiles` in the laboratory's 1959 progress reporting. Skinner's own 1960 article states that both Project Pigeon and ORCON had been declassified and treats ORCON as a continuation of the wartime work. Later histories say he served as a consultant.

The chronology matters. By 1948, wartime urgency had disappeared but electronic guidance had not yet become trivial. Radar and missile technology were advancing quickly. The Navy had operational experience with Bat and strong reasons to investigate homing against ships. If pigeons were tested again in that environment, the evaluation should have been less vulnerable to the accusation that nobody understood the engineering problem.

ORCON is therefore the book's most important control case.

If the Navy replicated the behavioral capability and still walked away, why?

One possibility is simple technological displacement. Electronics became reliable enough, compact enough and easier to integrate. Another is that ORCON exposed variability that Skinner's demonstrations had concealed. Later summaries describe the birds as capable but not sufficiently dependable under all conditions. Those statements need to be traced to the actual NRL report rather than repeated as final truth.

Reliability is not a yes-or-no property. What target set was used? How many birds? What illumination? How was tracking error defined? Did the program test target recognition across changing aspect and scale? What happened when more than one candidate target appeared? How were missed pecks and spurious pecks translated through the control mechanism? How was performance affected by training age, food deprivation, long delay before launch, temperature or motion?

A military laboratory can discover that an organism works and still decide that it is not weaponizable.

That distinction is especially important because Skinner's later account had an intellectual stake in the project. Project Pigeon became part of the origin story for his confidence in behavioral technology. He was not inventing the story from nothing; the apparatus and program are documented. But memoir is never neutral instrumentation. ORCON lets us compare the advocate's narrative with an institution that had no need to vindicate Skinner's broader philosophy.

The revival also complicates the role of ridicule. An idea that returns five years later under a naval research program has survived the first laugh. By then, cancellation can more plausibly be read as a technical and logistical judgment rather than immediate aesthetic rejection.

Unless, of course, the same category problem returned in a different uniform.

That is why the NRL documents are essential. A clean retrospective sentence—electronics got better—can conceal the engineering details that decide whether Project Pigeon was a near miss or merely an interesting laboratory demonstration.

ORCON asks us to stop arguing about whether the birds were smart.

The Navy already knew they could be trained.

The remaining question was whether trained behavior could be purchased as reliability.
