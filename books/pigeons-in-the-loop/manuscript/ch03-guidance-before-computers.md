# Guidance Before Computers

Precision weapons did not wait for digital computers.

By the early 1940s the problem had already become urgent enough to produce several competing answers. German weapons such as Fritz X used radio command guidance against ships. American programs explored television, radar homing, radio control and aerodynamic stabilization. The U.S. National Defense Research Committee issued contracts for guided weapons, and the National Bureau of Standards became deeply involved in what would become the Bat, an autonomous radar-homing glide bomb.

The word autonomous needs period discipline. The Bat did not contain anything resembling a modern general-purpose computer. Its autonomy came from purpose-built electronics and control machinery. It emitted radar energy, received reflections, derived a steering relation and drove control surfaces through an autopilot system. NIST now describes it as the first fully automated guided missile system used successfully in combat. The Smithsonian records a 1,000-pound weapon released from patrol aircraft at substantial stand-off distance.

That is the technological neighborhood into which the pigeon arrived.

The comparison matters because Project Pigeon was not competing against dumb gravity. Nor was it competing against a 2026 optical seeker. It was competing inside a transitional engineering ecology where every method carried ugly constraints. Radio command exposed the guidance link to interference and depended on an operator. Radar systems required specialized components, careful stabilization and enough reflected signal from the target. Television could transmit an image but left the operator in the loop. Optical contrast systems could be fooled by background and illumination. The closer we get to the actual period, the less absurd it becomes to ask whether an animal visual system might temporarily occupy part of that stack.

The pigeon proposal also separated sensing from stabilization in a way that later summaries blur. The bird did not have to fly the missile through direct muscular control. It only had to generate directional information. Mechanical and pneumatic devices could translate pecks or plate displacement into servo commands while the airframe handled the rest. The bird's job was more like target tracker than pilot.

This distinction changes what would count as evidence. A pigeon flapping skillfully proves nothing. A pigeon repeatedly holding a target image near the center of a screen while the projected scene moves would be relevant. Better evidence would quantify angular error, response latency, target-loss rate and recovery under realistic acceleration and vibration. Better still would compare those numbers with the electronic alternatives available under the same mission assumptions.

Most popular accounts stop before that comparison because the premise is enough entertainment. A serious history has to do the opposite. The premise is the least interesting part.

Guidance was an engineering market full of half-mature solutions. Project Pigeon deserves to be priced in that market.
