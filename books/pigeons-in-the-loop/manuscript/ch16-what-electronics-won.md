# What Electronics Won

The ending is usually one sentence: electronic guidance became reliable, so the Navy canceled ORCON.

The sentence may be broadly correct and still hide the important part.

Electronics did not merely become more accurate. They became an ecosystem.

Radar had manufacturing plants, laboratories, military schools, maintenance doctrine, standardized components and enormous wartime investment behind it. Servomechanisms were becoming a discipline. Vacuum tubes improved. Gyroscopes improved. Antennas, power supplies and signal-processing techniques accumulated across unrelated military systems. The marginal guidance problem could borrow from all of them.

Pigeon guidance could borrow from behavioral science, optics and mechanical engineering, but its central capability did not ride a comparable industrial learning curve. A pigeon in 1953 was not ten times smaller than a pigeon in 1943. Its visual system did not become easier to stock in a depot. Training could improve, selection could improve and interfaces could improve, but biology imposed a floor under certain logistical costs.

Electronics also won documentation.

An electronic seeker can be represented in schematics, tolerances and test procedures. A trained pigeon can be represented in a protocol and a performance distribution, but not fully reproduced from a drawing. The knowledge required to make the bird work is partly procedural. Trainers matter. Small differences in contingency matter. The component carries tacit knowledge in both the animal and the staff.

That complicates transfer.

A weapon program wants to move from an inventor's laboratory to production facilities and operational units. Every dependency on gifted handling or local technique becomes a risk. Skinner's own ability to shape behavior may have been part of the prototype's success. ORCON's importance lies partly in whether the Navy could reproduce the performance without turning every missile unit into a behavior laboratory.

If it could, the biological system was more mature than critics admit. If it could not, the crackpot label was hiding a genuine scalability problem.

Electronics also promised multi-mission adaptation through engineering rather than retraining organisms. Change the seeker frequency, the filter, the antenna or the signal logic. That process was difficult in early hardware but legible to the industrial system. Biological retasking required new stimuli and new conditioning, which could be quick in some cases and unpredictable in others.

The victory of electronics therefore included social infrastructure.

This is common in technology history. The `best` technology is often the one embedded in the strongest complementary system. QWERTY stories are overused and frequently simplified, but the general principle survives: performance cannot be detached from the world that produces, maintains and improves the artifact.

Project Pigeon had to win twice. The bird had to track. The institution had to want a future in which birds remained part of guidance.

By the early 1950s, the second proposition was becoming harder to defend even if the first remained experimentally interesting.

This is why ORCON's cancellation should not be narrated as the moment nature lost to machine. Electronics had already been borrowing from nature metaphorically—Bat being the obvious name—while replacing biological sensing physically. The system that won did so because it could be standardized, stored, scaled and improved across programs.

The pigeons may have had enough intelligence.

What they lacked was an industry.
