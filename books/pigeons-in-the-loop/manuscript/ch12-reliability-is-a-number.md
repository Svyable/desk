# Reliability Is a Number

The word reliable is where this story usually goes to die.

Advocates say the pigeons were reliable. Skeptics say they were not. Both statements can be true under different denominators.

A pigeon may reliably peck a trained target in a laboratory and unreliably maintain track through a terminal dive. A group of birds may reliably outperform naïve human expectations while still falling short of weapons requirements. An electronic seeker may be unreliable by peacetime standards and still preferable because its failure modes are easier to characterize.

The book therefore needs numbers the public history does not yet provide in one place.

At minimum, a reliability reconstruction should identify bird-level acquisition success, target-hold duration, tracking error, target-loss frequency, reacquisition time, false-target selection, response-rate distribution, day-to-day drift, retraining burden, and failure under disturbance. Those values should be indexed by target type, apparent size, contrast, image speed, illumination, vibration and whatever other test conditions survive in the record.

Without that table, `reliable` is rhetoric.

Skinner's demonstrations may have been excellent. The Navy's ORCON tests may have been more systematic. But even a good laboratory success rate must be propagated through the rest of the weapon. If the bird tracks correctly 99 percent of the time, the optics function 98 percent, the transducer 97 percent, the servo 97 percent and the airframe 99 percent, the total mission probability is lower than any individual component figure. Real systems have dependencies, so multiplication is only a first approximation, but it reminds us that impressive component performance can disappear inside a chain.

The pigeon system also contains unusual readiness variables. A bird has a motivational state. Skinner could manipulate food deprivation and reinforcement history to make responding vigorous, but military logistics would then have to convert that laboratory procedure into a repeatable prelaunch condition. Too little motivation might reduce pecking. Too much physiological stress might create different problems. The right state could depend on the delay between preparation and release.

That sounds hopeless until one compares it with the machinery of the period. Vacuum tubes warm up and fail. Radar sets require calibration. Gyroscopes need spin-up and can drift. Batteries self-discharge. Mechanical linkages bind. Wartime production introduces unit variation. Reliability engineering was not choosing between a messy bird and a perfect box. It was learning how to count failure in both.

The deeper institutional advantage of electronics was that their failure could be turned into standardized acceptance tests. Voltage here. Signal strength there. Servo travel within tolerance. An individual missile could be checked without reconstructing its development history.

Can a pigeon be acceptance-tested the same way?

In principle, yes. Put the bird in a standardized apparatus, present target trajectories, measure peck distribution, accept or reject. That turns behavioral variability into quality control. But it adds time and biological handling, and the test itself may alter the animal through reinforcement. The measurement process is not neutral in the same way an electrical continuity check can be.

This is where Skinner's approach was strongest and weakest simultaneously. Behaviorism gave him tools for producing repeatable performance in individual organisms. Military procurement wanted interchangeability across units.

A flock can be standardized statistically without any two pigeons being identical. Agriculture already does this with animals. Militaries did it with horses and messenger pigeons. The real question is whether the cost and uncertainty are acceptable for a disposable precision weapon.

ORCON reportedly ended in 1953, by which time electronic guidance had improved dramatically. If the NRL report shows that biological performance was still respectable, then the cancellation may reveal that weapons reliability had become an industrial systems problem the pigeons could no longer win even if their target tracking remained good.

That would be a more interesting ending than failure.

The pigeons may have lost because the definition of reliability moved.
