# A Bird Beside the Train

Skinner's retrospective account begins with observation rather than doctrine. He watched birds in flight and became interested in their capacity to maneuver and hold relation to moving objects. The later story has been compressed into a neat origin myth: psychologist sees pigeon, psychologist imagines missile. Origin myths are usually too tidy, but the underlying engineering intuition matters.

The bird could already solve a pursuit problem.

A guidance system needs some way to convert the world outside the vehicle into an error signal inside it. The target is not a coordinate written by God. It is a visual or electromagnetic pattern arriving through noise. The system must decide which part of the pattern matters and whether the weapon is pointing too far left, right, high or low. Then it has to correct without overcorrecting. Skinner did not need the pigeon to understand naval warfare. He needed a reproducible mapping between image displacement and pecking behavior.

That mapping was plausible because Skinner's laboratory work had already made behavior an object that could be shaped by consequences. The pigeon could be reinforced for pecking a target and for continuing to peck as the target moved. The important intellectual move was to stop treating the animal's behavior as personality and start treating it as a controllable relation between stimulus, history and response.

This is where the story can become grander than the evidence warrants. It is tempting to say Project Pigeon invented a biological computer or anticipated machine vision. Those phrases are useful only if we keep their limits visible. A pigeon does not run an algorithm in the ordinary engineering sense. Its visual system is not a camera feeding a programmable classifier. Training produces behavior without giving the designer full access to the internal representation. The animal is powerful partly because it arrives preassembled by evolution, and troublesome for the same reason. The engineer inherits capabilities and hidden state together.

That hidden state is not a philosophical nuisance. It is a maintenance problem. How hungry is the bird? What has it learned from the last session? Has reinforcement history drifted? Does a new background texture change discrimination? Will the response survive vibration? Does fatigue appear suddenly or gradually? A vacuum tube also has failure modes, but the categories are different and better understood by the engineers who built around it.

Skinner's proposal therefore crossed two unfamiliarities at once. The component was alive, and the discipline claiming to control it was psychology. In wartime institutions already juggling radar, radio, servomechanisms, aerodynamics and explosives, a psychologist asking to place three pigeons in the nose may have sounded as if he had confused a laboratory demonstration with weapons engineering.

Maybe he had. That is one of the questions the book has to test.

But the first mistake would be to assume the answer from the species of the component.
