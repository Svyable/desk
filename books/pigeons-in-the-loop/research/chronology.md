# Chronology

## 1940–1942 — Idea formation

Skinner's later account places the origin of the guidance idea in observations of bird flight and the recognition that trained visual behavior might be coupled to a weapon. Exact day-by-day chronology remains retrospective until notebooks/correspondence are recovered.

## 1943 — Organized wartime development

Later histories place General Mills involvement and government-backed development in 1943. Recover the primary contract and OSRD/NDRC file before freezing exact contract date, amount or formal title sequence.

## Late 1943–1944 — Demonstration and termination sequence

Secondary/scholarly accounts describe a late-1943 demonstration and subsequent decisions against continued funding; public summaries disagree about the date to call `cancellation`. Treat January/March/October 1944 references as potentially different administrative events until NDRC minutes resolve them.

## 1944–1945 — Electronic guided weapons advance

The U.S. Bat radar-homing glide bomb matures through National Bureau of Standards and partner work.

## 1945 — Bat combat use

Official NIST and Smithsonian histories document Bat use in the Pacific in 1945. This establishes that autonomous radar homing crossed the operational threshold during the same war.

## 1948 — ORCON revival

The U.S. Naval Research Laboratory revives biological guidance research under Project ORCON (`organic control`). Full program documentation remains to be recovered.

## 1953 — ORCON ends

Later histories place ORCON termination in 1953 amid improving electronic guidance. The exact termination rationale must be taken from NRL records rather than shorthand.

## 1959 — Declassification/public technical reporting

The NRL progress report `Project ORCON: The Use of Pigeons to Guide Missiles` is cited as August 1959. Skinner later wrote that Pigeon/ORCON had been declassified by this period.

## 1960 — Skinner publishes retrospective

`Pigeons in a Pelican` appears in *American Psychologist*, providing the central first-person technical/historical narrative.

## 1981 — Smithsonian accession

The National Museum of American History records accession of Skinner's pigeon-guidance apparatus as a gift from B. F. Skinner.

## 1993 — Historical reconstruction

James H. Capshew publishes `Engineering Behavior`, situating Project Pigeon in wartime science and Skinner's development as a behavioral engineer.

## 2024 — Cultural afterlife

Skinner receives a posthumous Ig Nobel Peace Prize for the pigeon-guidance work. This is cultural afterlife, not evidence of wartime technical efficacy.

## 2025–2026 — Current research context

Modern scholarship continues to review the unusual centrality of pigeons in experimental behavior research. The historical Project Pigeon record remains sufficiently fragmented that exact technical comparisons still require archival reconstruction.
