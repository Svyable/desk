# Defeat Conditions

The manuscript's central thesis should materially weaken if primary records show any of the following:

1. Project Pigeon's target tracking was largely a staged demonstration effect that did not survive ordinary repeat testing.
2. ORCON failed to replicate the core trained tracking behavior under controlled conditions.
3. The surviving nose-cone apparatus was primarily demonstrational and no serious integration work connected biological output to a plausible weapon servo.
4. Pigeon performance degraded so sharply under realistic image motion, vibration, illumination or acceleration that biological sensing was never technically competitive even at the proof-of-concept stage.
5. Wartime committee records show that cancellation followed a well-supported quantitative comparison in which electronic alternatives were already decisively superior across the relevant mission at the actual decision date.
6. The servo/interface problem was not a tractable integration issue but a fundamental inability to extract a stable control signal from peck behavior.
7. The three-bird architecture did not function as meaningful redundancy and was mainly a demonstration flourish.
8. ORCON ended because quantified biological performance was inadequate, rather than because electronics crossed a broader logistics/reliability threshold.

Evidence for these outcomes would not make the project uninteresting. It would shift the book away from `temporarily plausible biological guidance` toward a history of how convincing laboratory behavior can outrun systems integration.
