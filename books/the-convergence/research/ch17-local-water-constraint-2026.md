# Chapter 17 — Local water constraint: research and counterevidence note

## Purpose

Chapter 17 argues that national water averages can conceal the physical constraint that matters to a power plant: the water available in a particular watershed, at a particular time, under a particular cooling design and set of environmental rules. This note strengthens that claim with newer U.S. Geological Survey evidence while also preserving the strongest counterargument: thermoelectric water use has been falling in the United States, so the water-energy nexus should not be presented as a mechanically worsening national constraint.

## Primary evidence

### USGS / Environmental Science & Technology: Water, 2025

Kenneth D. Skinner and coauthors evaluated freshwater-using utility-scale thermoelectric plants across the conterminous United States from 2008 through 2020. They report that both withdrawals and consumption declined over the period. The authors attribute much of the decline to the shift away from coal-fired generation and to changes in cooling-system technology, especially reductions in once-through cooling.

That is important counterevidence for the chapter. A larger or more capable electricity system does not require thermoelectric water use to rise in lockstep. Generation mix, plant efficiency, and cooling design can materially change the relationship.

But the same study also shows why a falling national total does not erase local exposure. USGS reports that some thermoelectric plants with recirculating-tower or once-through cooling withdraw volumes that are, on average, close to or greater than simulated streamflow. The authors flag possible consequences including competition among water users, ecosystem impacts from thermal pollution, and constraints on electricity generation.

This is a more useful boundary for the manuscript than a generic claim that "power needs water." The relevant risk is not the national gallons-per-day number by itself. It is the ratio between a facility's requirements and the water actually available in the basin and season where that facility operates.

### USGS National Water Availability Assessment data companion, 2026

USGS's June 12, 2026 thermoelectric-water-use map presents monthly average modeled withdrawals and consumptive use for 2009–2020 by watershed. The underlying model links heat and water budgets and accounts for plant generation, cooling technology, fuel use, air and water temperature, wind speed, and elevation.

Book use: this is strong support for Chapter 17's insistence that water-energy comparisons are spatial and temporal rather than purely national. It also reinforces the chapter's broader methodological point: a single technology label or annual average can hide the conditions under which a local constraint actually binds.

The map and model should not be used as a forecast of current 2026 plant operations. They are a 2026 publication surface for historical modeled data through 2020. Their value here is methodological and spatial: they show how USGS itself treats water use at subwatershed scale rather than assuming one national water balance is sufficient.

## Sharper claim for manuscript use

A defensible formulation is:

> Thermoelectric water use can decline nationally while remaining locally binding. The relevant question is not only how much water the fleet uses, but whether a particular plant's withdrawal and consumption are large relative to the water available in its watershed when the plant is needed.

This improves on either extreme:

- "Thermal power is inherently water constrained" is too broad because fleet composition and cooling technology can reduce water dependence substantially.
- "Thermoelectric water use is falling, therefore water is no longer a meaningful power constraint" is also too broad because basin-level scarcity, water temperature, ecological limits, and competing demand can still bind individual plants.

## Counterargument and disconfirming evidence

The strongest counterargument is the observed U.S. trend itself. From 2008 through 2020, thermoelectric withdrawals and consumption declined, driven largely by coal retirements or lower coal utilization and changes in cooling systems. Solar PV and wind generally avoid large operational cooling-water requirements, and continued deployment of those technologies can further weaken the coupling between electricity output and water withdrawals.

That counterevidence should stay visible because it prevents Chapter 17 from treating today's water-energy relationships as technologically fixed. The surviving claim is narrower: water constraints are conditional, geographically uneven, and design-dependent. They can shrink, migrate, or reappear as the generation mix, climate, basin conditions, and cooling choices change.

## Factual boundary

For the U.S. Geological Survey's national water-use assessment, model estimates for water year 2020 were 61,399 million gallons per day of freshwater withdrawals and 19,033 million gallons per day of saline-water withdrawals for thermoelectric power, totaling 80,432 million gallons per day across the conterminous United States. Consumptive use was 2,382 million gallons per day, overwhelmingly freshwater. USGS describes thermoelectric consumptive use as about 4 percent of freshwater withdrawals.

These figures should not be turned into a universal technology ranking. They aggregate many plants, fuels, cooling systems, climates, and water sources. Their strongest use in the book is to demonstrate how dramatically withdrawal and consumption can differ and why the unit of analysis must eventually move from national totals to actual watersheds.
