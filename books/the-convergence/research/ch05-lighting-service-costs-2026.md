# Chapter 5 — Lighting service costs: 2026 research update

This note sharpens the quantitative backbone of Chapter 5, “Energy Becomes Invisible.” The chapter’s central move—from counting fuels to counting the service people actually obtain—is well supported, but the most useful evidence is stronger when three different claims are kept separate: technical efficiency, the real price of a unit of light, and the amount of light consumed.

## Primary evidence already underlying the chapter

Roger Fouquet and Peter J. G. Pearson, “Seven Centuries of Energy Services: The Price and Use of Light in the United Kingdom (1300–2000),” *The Energy Journal* 27(1), 2006. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5547/ISSN0195-6574-EJ-Vol27-No1-8

Their reconstruction estimates that by 2000 the real price of lighting services in the United Kingdom was less than one three-thousandth of its 1800 level, while per-capita consumption of lighting services was about 6,500 times higher and total consumption about 25,000 times higher. Those are different quantities. The first is a service-price result; the second and third are demand results. The paper itself warns against mechanically projecting the historical relationship because rebound, saturation, new technologies, and new fuels can change long-run demand.

William D. Nordhaus, “Do Real-Output and Real-Wage Measures Capture Reality? The History of Lighting Suggests Not,” Cowles Foundation Discussion Paper 1078, 1994; later published in *The Economics of New Goods*. Yale record: https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cowles-discussion-paper-series/1321/

Nordhaus gives Chapter 5 an especially concrete way to express the change in service cost. His table of the labor price of light estimates that obtaining 1,000 lumen-hours required about 5.37 hours of work with tallow lighting around 1800, 0.22 hours around 1900, and roughly 0.00012 hours with compact fluorescent lighting in 1992. The exact values depend on reconstructed wages, fuel prices, and device performance, so they should be treated as order-of-magnitude historical estimates rather than metrology. Their value is that they put the reader’s unit of account in human terms: hours of work for the same illumination.

Nordhaus’s larger measurement claim is stronger and more contestable than the physical comparison. He argues that conventional price indexes substantially understate quality-adjusted progress when products change radically. Chapter 5 need not adopt a general claim about GDP measurement to use the narrower lighting example.

## 2026 extension of the lighting-price series

A 2026 chapter by Roger Fouquet, “A Historical Perspective on Energy Innovation: Energy Transitions, Efficiency Improvements, Rebound Effects and Consumer Welfare Impacts,” appears in the *Handbook of Energy Innovation* (Edward Elgar, 2026). Publisher record: https://www.e-elgar.com/shop/gbp/handbook-of-energy-innovation-9781035310401.html

Our World in Data’s current lighting dataset combines Fouquet’s updated material with Fouquet and Pearson (2006). It extends the UK service-price series through 2023. The dataset reports a 2023 price of £2.15 in constant 2000 pounds per million lumen-hours. Dataset and processing notes: https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/the-price-for-lighting-per-million-lumen-hours-in-the-uk-in-british-pound

This is useful current corroboration, but it should not silently replace the 2006 estimates in the manuscript. OWID describes the series as having “major processing,” and the 2026 handbook chapter is a synthesis rather than a new peer-reviewed reconstruction focused solely on lighting. If Chapter 5 mentions the extension, attribution should make that provenance explicit: Fouquet’s updated series as processed and published by Our World in Data.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming considerations

The lighting example can become too perfect if it is allowed to carry more than it proves.

First, a collapse in the price per lumen-hour does not by itself establish an equal welfare gain. Artificial light has diminishing marginal value, can create nuisance and ecological harms, and eventually encounters saturation. Fouquet and Pearson explicitly flag saturation as one reason not to extrapolate the historical demand response indefinitely.

Second, service-price accounting can hide shifted burdens. Electric lighting is clean at the point of use, but generation, grids, mining, manufacturing, and disposal remain physical. Chapter 5 already makes this point well; the quantitative lighting passage should not outrun it.

Third, Nordhaus’s labor-price series combines technological performance with wage changes. It is therefore an economic accessibility measure, not a pure engineering-efficiency measure. The distinction is useful rather than fatal: a reader should simply not be told that the 45,000-fold fall from 1800 to 1992 is an efficiency improvement alone.

Finally, historical reconstructions depend on assumptions about representative devices, prices, wages, utilization, and luminous output. The orders of magnitude are robust enough to make the chapter’s point; the last decimal place is not.

## Recommended manuscript use

The highest-value future manuscript edit is surgical: replace the current generic sentence that Nordhaus “found declines far larger than ordinary price measures suggested” with one short concrete comparison—roughly 5.4 labor-hours per 1,000 lumen-hours in 1800, 0.22 in 1900, and 0.00012 in 1992—followed immediately by the qualification that these are reconstructed economic estimates, not pure measures of lamp efficiency.

That change would make the chapter more vivid without adding a new framework or duplicating Chapter 6’s rebound discussion. The 2023 extension is better kept as corroborating research unless the chapter is otherwise being updated to carry current end dates consistently.
