# Chapter 11 — Renewable cost versus firm service (2026)

## Why this note exists

Chapter 11 uses the low cost of newly commissioned solar and wind, together with rapid battery deployment, as evidence that energy abundance can emerge when one input becomes cheap enough for another constraint to matter more. The current numbers are supportable. The important guardrail is that plant-level levelized cost, battery deployment, and dependable system service are different quantities.

## Current cost claims verified

IRENA's July 2026 *Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2025* reports that the global weighted-average levelized cost of newly commissioned utility-scale solar PV was USD 44/MWh in 2025 and onshore wind was USD 33/MWh. More than 90% of utility-scale renewable projects commissioned in 2025 delivered power below the cost of the cheapest new fossil-fuel plant built in their market under IRENA's methodology.

Source: International Renewable Energy Agency, *Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2025* (July 2026): https://www.irena.org/Publications/2026/Jul/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2025

These figures support the manuscript as written. They are generation-cost comparisons for newly commissioned plants, not estimates of a complete retail bill or of the cost of supplying every hour reliably.

## Battery deployment claims verified

The IEA's *Global Energy Review 2026* reports that 108 GW of battery storage was deployed worldwide in 2025, 40% more than in 2024, bringing installed capacity to eleven times its 2021 level. Around 80% of 2025 additions were utility-scale. Lithium-iron-phosphate batteries accounted for around 90% of storage deployments, and most projects still clustered around roughly two hours of duration while an increasing number reached four hours or more.

Source: International Energy Agency, *Global Energy Review 2026 — Technology: Battery storage*: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-battery-storage

The chemistry point in the manuscript is therefore well grounded: stationary storage rewards cost, cycle life, safety, and manufacturability differently from applications in which mass and volume are dominant constraints.

## Strongest counterevidence: cheap generation is not firm service

IRENA's May 2026 *24/7 Renewables: The Economics of Firm Solar and Wind* is the strongest current counterweight to reading the USD 33–44/MWh figures as the cost of reliable electricity. Its modeled solar-plus-storage systems in high-quality resource regions reach roughly USD 54–82/MWh at high reliability, while firm onshore-wind costs vary substantially by location. In the United States, IRENA estimates minimum firm solar costs around USD 63/MWh at the best-resourced sites under long-run assumptions, while warning that near-term project outcomes can be worse because financing costs, interconnection charges, and other structural costs are not fully captured in the modeled benchmark.

Source: International Renewable Energy Agency, *24/7 Renewables: The Economics of Firm Solar and Wind* (May 2026): https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2026/May/IRENA_TEC_24-7_renewables_2026.pdf

This is not evidence against the chapter's abundance thesis. It makes the thesis more precise. The cost of an intermittent generator and the cost of a dependable electricity service diverge because reliability requires some combination of storage, transmission, complementary generation, flexible demand, geographic diversity, overbuilding, curtailment, and reserve capacity.

The difference is also endogenous. As cheap solar becomes common, midday electricity can lose marginal value while evening and weather-correlated scarcity becomes more important. A technology can therefore keep getting cheaper as hardware while the value of the next unit depends increasingly on the system around it.

## Strongest formulation for the chapter

The defensible claim is not that solar or wind at USD 33–44/MWh makes electricity itself a USD 33–44/MWh product. It is that very low generation costs create room to buy complementary reliability services and can move the binding constraint away from fuel toward networks, storage, timing, financing, siting, and firm capacity.

That is a stronger fit with the book's recurring argument that scarcity migrates. Cheap generation matters precisely because it exposes the price of the complements required to turn generation into capability.

## What would change the argument?

Revisit this chapter if:

- reliability-adjusted renewable costs stop falling despite continued declines in plant-level solar, wind, and battery costs;
- storage duration or firm-renewables economics improve enough that the present distinction between cheap generation and expensive reliability becomes materially smaller across ordinary markets;
- grid, interconnection, financing, or siting costs rise enough that falling equipment costs no longer lower the delivered cost of adding renewable capability;
- battery deployment growth proves temporary or installed batteries systematically fail to provide the capacity, shifting, congestion, and balancing value assumed in current system analyses.

The chapter should continue to keep plant cost, system cost, and service value separate. That accounting discipline is more persuasive than treating any one falling cost curve as proof that the rest of the electricity system has been solved.
