# Chapter 15 — The Cost of Reliability: research and falsification note

## Purpose

This note supports the chapter’s distinction among energy, capacity, flexibility, and resilience. The chapter argues that low average electricity cost is not equivalent to reliable service, and that storage should be evaluated by the specific time-scale and grid service it provides rather than treated as a universal substitute for firm capacity.

## Primary evidence used

### International Energy Agency, Batteries and Secure Energy Transitions (2024)

The IEA reports that power-sector battery storage was the fastest-growing commercially available energy technology in 2023, with more than 40 GW added globally. It emphasizes that battery storage provides short-term energy shifting, ancillary services, congestion relief, peak capacity, and fast grid response. The report identifies batteries as especially well suited to roughly 1–8 hour flexibility needs while also stressing supply-chain concentration and the need for other storage technologies and system resources.

Book use: supports the claims that batteries already provide multiple reliability services and that their strongest established role is short-term flexibility rather than an all-duration reliability solution.

Limits: the report’s future deployment numbers are scenario-dependent. Rapid cost declines and deployment do not prove that current lithium-ion designs are the least-cost solution for multi-day or seasonal shortages.

### International Energy Agency, Electricity 2026 — Flexibility

IEA’s 2026 electricity analysis reports continued rapid growth in utility-scale battery storage. It notes that batteries can shift renewable output, provide balancing and grid-support services, contribute to capacity, and help integrate correlated new loads. It also shows that typical project duration in major markets remains measured in hours even as average duration rises.

Book use: supports the chapter’s claim that the current battery fleet is primarily an hourly flexibility resource and that installed power capacity alone does not describe how long a system can sustain output.

Limits: market averages conceal large variation among individual projects. Battery durations are changing quickly, so any specific current duration should be treated as a snapshot rather than a technology ceiling.

## Distinctions preserved

- **Energy versus capacity.** Annual megawatt-hours do not establish what a resource contributes in the highest-risk hour.
- **Capacity versus duration.** A storage resource’s MW rating and MWh inventory answer different questions.
- **Reliability versus resilience.** Adequacy during expected peak conditions is not identical to surviving and recovering from extreme failures.
- **Technical capability versus system value.** The value of solar, storage, transmission, or firm generation changes with the composition of the rest of the system.
- **Average conditions versus tail events.** Planning should not optimize only around typical weather, but neither should it overbuild for arbitrarily remote events without considering cost.

## Counterevidence and uncertainty

The chapter could overstate the need for firm generation if long-duration storage costs fall rapidly, transmission expands enough to diversify weather risk, flexible demand becomes much larger than current practice suggests, or vehicle-to-grid systems provide substantial distributed capacity. It could understate reliability risk if electrification creates more correlated peak demand than planners expect, if extreme weather correlation broadens across regions, or if supply-chain/interconnection constraints slow storage and transmission deployment.

The discussion of capacity markets is deliberately non-prescriptive. Different jurisdictions use energy-only markets, capacity mechanisms, regulated procurement, reserve requirements, or hybrids. The chapter does not claim one design is universally superior.

The statement that no resource is perfectly firm is conceptual, not an argument that all outage risks are equal. Forced-outage rates, fuel constraints, weather sensitivity, and duration differ materially by technology and location.

## Falsification triggers for later revision

Revisit this chapter if:

- commercially deployed storage in major power systems shifts decisively from hours to multi-day average duration;
- authoritative evidence shows flexible demand providing a much larger share of resource adequacy than current planning practice assumes;
- market-design evidence establishes a clearly dominant approach to pricing reliability across diverse power systems;
- new reliability standards materially change the definitions or planning practices used for capacity contribution;
- long-duration storage, advanced geothermal, hydrogen-to-power, or another firming technology reaches broad commercial deployment and changes the portfolio logic described here.
