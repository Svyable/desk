# Chapter 2 — Health costs of early farming

## Claim under review

Chapter 2 argues that the transition to farming could increase population and collective capability without making individual lives easier, and notes that early farmers were sometimes shorter, sicker, or under greater physiological stress than foragers.

That claim is directionally supported, but it is easy to overstate. Adult stature reflects both environment and inherited height potential, skeletal stress markers are imperfect proxies, and the archaeological record is geographically uneven. A useful source should therefore distinguish biological inheritance from achieved growth rather than simply compare average skeletons across periods.

## Strong primary evidence

Stephanie Marciniak et al., “An integrative skeletal and paleogenomic analysis of stature variation suggests relatively reduced health for early European farmers,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 119, no. 15 (2022), e2106743119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2106743119

The study links osteological and genome-wide ancient-DNA data for the same 167 prehistoric European individuals, spanning the Upper Paleolithic through the Iron Age. That design lets the authors compare achieved adult stature with an estimate of each individual’s inherited height potential.

Their headline result is useful but should be stated carefully. Neolithic individuals were, on average, 3.82 cm shorter relative to their genetic height expectation than Upper Paleolithic and Mesolithic individuals (P = 0.040). The Neolithic group was also 2.21 cm shorter relative to post-Neolithic individuals, though that comparison was weaker statistically (P = 0.068).

The study also reports that 14 of 18 Neolithic individuals with suitable preservation showed at least one nonspecific skeletal stress indicator. That result is suggestive rather than uniquely Neolithic: the Bronze Age subsample had an even higher prevalence, which cautions against turning “farming caused poor health” into a universal temporal law.

## Strongest counterevidence / limitation

The ancestry-adjusted model materially weakens the headline stature result. After accounting for genome-wide ancestry variation, Neolithic individuals were still estimated to be 2.82 cm shorter than expected relative to pre-Neolithic individuals, but the comparison was no longer conventionally significant (P = 0.120).

That matters for the manuscript. The safer claim is not that agriculture mechanically made people shorter. It is that the early farming transition often coincided with biological stress consistent with poorer nutrition and/or higher disease burdens, while migration, ancestry, local ecology, crop mix, settlement density, and later technological adaptation complicate any simple causal story.

The authors themselves note that the pattern is not universal and that stature is only a proxy for health during growth. Their evidence is European, not global.

## Recommended manuscript use

The strongest revision would sharpen the existing paragraph beginning “The early agricultural record gives plenty of reason for caution.” Instead of listing narrower diets, disease, and bodily stress generically, it could anchor one sentence in this study: even after partially accounting for inherited height potential, the sampled Neolithic Europeans were shorter than expected on average, consistent with nutritional and disease stress.

The next sentence should preserve the ancestry-adjustment result so the book does not turn a suggestive biosocial pattern into deterministic evidence against farming.

A compact formulation could carry the argument without adding a detour:

> In one study matching ancient DNA to skeletons from 167 prehistoric Europeans, Neolithic farmers were shorter than their genetic height estimates would predict relative to earlier hunter-gatherers. The gap weakened after broader ancestry adjustment, so the result is evidence of physiological stress, not a universal verdict on agriculture.

That formulation should be treated as a paraphrase, not a quotation from the paper.

## Why this improves Chapter 2

Chapter 2’s most interesting argument is already that population growth, stored surplus, and individual welfare can move in different directions. This source makes that distinction empirical rather than moralizing. It also supplies its own disconfirming evidence, which fits the chapter’s broader refusal to treat the agricultural transition as either a triumphal “first abundance” story or a simple historical mistake.
