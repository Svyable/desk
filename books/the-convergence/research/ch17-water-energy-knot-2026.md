# Chapter 17 — The Water-Energy Knot: research and falsification note

## Purpose

This note supports the chapter’s treatment of water and energy as coupled infrastructure. The chapter distinguishes withdrawals from consumption, emphasizes local watershed context, and argues that energy can relax some water constraints while simultaneously creating new electricity, capital, ecological, and infrastructure dependencies.

## Primary evidence used

### U.S. Geological Survey, Water use across the conterminous United States, water years 2010–20

USGS estimates thermoelectric power withdrawals in water year 2020 at 61,399 million gallons per day from freshwater and 19,033 million gallons per day from saline water, for a total of 80,432 million gallons per day across the conterminous United States. Estimated thermoelectric consumptive use was much lower, at 2,382 million gallons per day, illustrating the importance of distinguishing withdrawals from consumption.

Book use: supports the chapter’s central accounting distinction and the claim that thermal electricity remains deeply coupled to water systems even when most withdrawn cooling water is returned.

Limits: the estimates are modeled historical values for the conterminous United States, not a current global average. Cooling technologies, water sources, climates, and plant fleets vary widely, so the numbers should not be transferred mechanically to other countries or technologies.

### U.S. Geological Survey, Water withdrawal and consumption trends for thermoelectric-power plants in the conterminous United States, 2008–2020

USGS reports declining thermoelectric water withdrawal and consumption trends over the 2008–2020 period. The study attributes much of the decline to shifts away from coal-fired generation and changes in cooling technology, with especially large withdrawal declines associated with once-through freshwater cooling systems.

Book use: provides counterevidence against a deterministic claim that more electricity necessarily means steadily rising water use. Generation mix and cooling design can materially change the water profile of the power system.

Limits: the trend ends in 2020 and reflects U.S.-specific fleet changes. It does not establish that water constraints are disappearing, particularly in locally water-stressed basins or under future climate conditions.

### International Energy Agency, Energy and Water

The IEA reports that global energy demand for desalination has nearly doubled since 2010 and, under current trends, is poised to double again by 2030. It reports around 21,000 desalination plants operating across roughly 150 countries, with about half of installed capacity in the Middle East and North Africa.

Book use: supports the reciprocal side of the chapter’s argument: modern water supply can depend heavily on energy, and desalination is a concrete example of energy transforming a natural freshwater constraint into an infrastructure and cost problem.

Limits: desalination does not make all water scarcity economically irrelevant. Location, brine disposal, capital cost, network delivery, water quality, and end-use value remain important. The IEA’s forward-looking energy-demand statement is scenario-based rather than a guaranteed outcome.

### International Energy Agency, The Future of Electricity in the Middle East and North Africa

The IEA describes MENA as an extremely water-stressed region and reports 12 billion cubic metres of desalinated-water production in 2024, with production projected to triple by 2035. It expects future desalination growth to be met by electricity-powered high-efficiency reverse osmosis rather than new thermal desalination capacity.

Book use: supports the chapter’s discussion of infrastructure convergence: as desalination electrifies, reliable electricity planning becomes more directly tied to water security, while water storage and flexible pumping can create opportunities for demand flexibility.

Limits: the regional projection is not a universal model. MENA has unusual water stress, climate, fuel systems, and desalination experience; different regions may rely more heavily on conservation, reuse, surface water, groundwater, or transfers.

## Distinctions preserved

- **Withdrawal versus consumption.** Water taken from a source is not necessarily water removed from near-term availability.
- **Volume versus impact.** Timing, temperature, water quality, ecosystem condition, and local scarcity determine impact alongside volume.
- **National average versus watershed constraint.** Water stress is local and seasonal; national totals can conceal binding basin-level limits.
- **Technical possibility versus economic allocation.** Desalination or transfer may be technically possible without being economical for every end use.
- **Firm generation versus unconditional generation.** Thermal plants can remain exposed to cooling-water availability and temperature even when fuel and machinery are available.
- **Nexus versus determinism.** Water-energy coupling creates dependencies but does not imply water use must rise in lockstep with energy output.

## Counterevidence and uncertainty

The chapter could overstate water as a future electricity constraint if generation continues shifting toward low-operational-water technologies such as solar PV and wind, if dry cooling improves, or if wastewater and saline-water cooling expand. It could understate the constraint if drought, warming water temperatures, competing agricultural and municipal demand, or ecological flow requirements become more binding in major generation regions.

Desalination costs and energy intensity can continue falling, making energy a more powerful substitute for freshwater scarcity in coastal urban and industrial systems. Conversely, cheap desalination may increase dependence on electricity, coastal infrastructure, membranes, and brine management rather than eliminating system risk.

The chapter deliberately avoids one universal gallons-per-kWh ranking because results depend heavily on whether withdrawal or consumption is measured, cooling design, facility efficiency, climate, and system boundaries.

## Falsification triggers for later revision

Revisit this chapter if:

- USGS materially revises the thermoelectric withdrawal/consumption distinction or newer national data show the 2008–2020 trend reversing sharply;
- desalination energy demand departs materially from the IEA trajectory because of major efficiency improvements, slower construction, or alternative water strategies;
- MENA desalination growth shifts back toward thermal processes rather than predominantly electricity-driven reverse osmosis;
- large-scale deployment of dry cooling or other technologies substantially removes cooling-water availability as a constraint for thermal generation;
- evidence establishes that flexible operation of desalination, pumping, or wastewater systems provides material grid balancing at broad scale, warranting a stronger claim than the chapter currently makes.
