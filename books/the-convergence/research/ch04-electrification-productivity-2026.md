# Chapter 4 — electrification, productivity, and the network

## Research question

How much of electricity's industrial effect should be attributed to the energy carrier itself, how much to complementary organizational change, and how slowly did the gains actually arrive?

Chapter 4 already makes a strong systems argument: the grid mattered because electricity was easier to route, divide, meter, control, and convert at the point of use than shaft power or a local fuel pile. The evidence supports that claim, but it does **not** support the simplest version of the familiar general-purpose-technology story in which electrification produced little measurable productivity benefit for decades and only paid off after factories were completely redesigned.

The cleaner distinction is between **diffusion of electrical infrastructure**, **reorganization around electricity**, and the **timing of measured productivity effects**. Those processes overlapped. They should not be forced into one tidy lag story.

## Pearl Street: a system demonstration, not just a lamp story

The chapter's September 4, 1882 Pearl Street opening is well grounded.

The Thomas A. Edison Papers chronology records Edison's Holborn Viaduct central station beginning operation in London on January 12, 1882, followed by the Pearl Street central station in New York on September 4. The Edison Papers' company history says the Edison Electric Illuminating Company of New York was organized on December 17, 1880 specifically to construct generating stations in New York City, with Pearl Street as its first central station.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History adds the engineering and business constraint that makes the scene useful for the chapter. Edison's 100-volt direct-current system limited customers to roughly half a mile from the generator, so the station had to solve a local-density problem as well as an electrical one. The Smithsonian also notes that Pearl Street did not become profitable for about five years even though it succeeded as a technical demonstration and helped support subsequent central-station licensing.

That is useful counterevidence against writing infrastructure history as instant commercial triumph. The system could be technically important before its business model was mature.

## From shafts to wires: why the carrier changed factory geometry

Warren Devine's 1983 *Journal of Economic History* article remains the foundational source for the chapter's shaft-and-belt discussion. His historical reconstruction describes the 1880–1930 shift from steam and water prime movers feeding mechanical drive trains toward electric motors located closer to individual machines.

The important part of Devine's argument is not merely that electrical drive reduced mechanical-transmission losses. He argues that the transition allowed more output per unit of capital and labor because factories no longer had to organize production around the geometry and operating constraints of a central shaft system.

That supports the chapter's governing idea: electricity changed the feasible arrangement of production. The gain was partly energetic and partly organizational.

Source:

- Warren D. Devine Jr. (1983), “From Shafts to Wires: Historical Perspective on Electrification,” *The Journal of Economic History* 43(2): 347–372: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/from-shafts-to-wires-historical-perspective-on-electrification/500078D9B4764BA1109A7967437CF226

## The strongest correction to the classic long-delay story

A popular interpretation of U.S. electrification treats electricity as a general-purpose technology whose productivity benefits remained weak for a long period because firms first had to rebuild factories, reorganize workflow, and accumulate complementary capital.

The April 2024 revision of Martin Fiszbein, Jeanne Lafortune, Ethan Lewis, and José Tessada's NBER paper complicates that story materially.

Using U.S. sector-county manufacturing data from 1890 to 1940, pre-electrification energy intensity, and proximity to early hydroelectric plants, they find **rapid and persistent labor-productivity gains** from electrification. Their abstract explicitly says this result is contrary to established narratives of long-delayed productivity benefits.

But the paper does not imply that organization was irrelevant. The authors also find that electrification was accompanied quickly by capital deepening and organizational change. Effects differed with initial market structure: in sector-county cells with larger firms, productivity increased without the same employment expansion seen in cells where firms were initially smaller.

The resulting causal boundary is useful for Chapter 4:

- do not say electrification was “just” a better power source;
- do not say the gains required decades before showing up in measured productivity;
- do say that electricity and complementary capital/organization moved together, and that the effects were heterogeneous across industrial structures.

This is a better fit with the chapter's actual thesis than the neat lag story. Electricity mattered partly because it made reorganization easier, not because the reorganization can be cleanly separated and credited after the fact.

Source:

- Martin Fiszbein, Jeanne Lafortune, Ethan G. Lewis, and José Tessada, *Powering Up Productivity: The Effects of Electrification on U.S. Manufacturing*, NBER Working Paper 28076, revised April 2024: https://www.nber.org/papers/w28076

## Essential infrastructure is not always the binding development constraint

The chapter already carries a modern countercase from Jonathan Colmer, David Lagakos, and Martin Shu. Their NBER working paper was revised in April 2026, so the current version should be the one cited.

They assemble electricity inputs and outputs for 100 countries and estimate electricity-sector total factor productivity. Their central empirical result is awkward for a strong “electricity is the weak link” development claim: electricity-sector productivity varies considerably less between low- and high-income countries than aggregate productivity does.

They then use a multi-sector growth model in which electricity is strongly complementary to other inputs and the electricity sector faces distortions. The model predicts only modest long-run GDP gains from raising electricity-sector productivity or removing electricity-market distortions alone.

This is not evidence that reliable electricity is unimportant. It is evidence against confusing **necessary infrastructure** with the **marginal binding constraint** on national income.

The distinction strengthens the book's broader constraint-migration argument. A grid can be foundational while capital, management, transport, skills, demand, governance, or other productive inputs bind first.

Source:

- Jonathan M. Colmer, David Lagakos, and Martin Shu, *Is the Electricity Sector a Weak Link in Development?*, NBER Working Paper 32041, revised April 2026: https://www.nber.org/papers/w32041

## Census evidence: when novelty becomes an industry

The chapter's point that U.S. statistical bureaucracy eventually treated electric power as a distinct industry is also well grounded.

The Census Bureau's historical publication archive includes dedicated reports for **Central Electric Light and Power Stations** in 1902 and 1907 and a combined **Central Electric Light and Power Stations and Street and Electric Railways** report for 1912. The third edition of the 1900 Census abstract, published in 1904, explicitly added tables for central electric light and power stations along with other electrical industries.

The bureaucratic observation in the manuscript should remain an observation, not a causal claim. Still, it is a vivid marker of institutionalization: the network had become economically large and standardized enough to require a recurring statistical category.

## Strongest counterargument to Chapter 4's likely thesis

A chapter centered on the grid can overstate the autonomy of infrastructure.

Electricity's industrial value arrived inside a bundle of changing machinery, firms, management, capital markets, urban growth, transport, labor organization, and demand. A network that makes flexible power available does not tell firms what to produce, how to reorganize, or whether the complementary investment is profitable. Modern cross-country evidence also shows that an electricity sector can be imperfect without being the single weak link holding back aggregate productivity.

The strongest version of the chapter should therefore resist two claims:

1. **Technological determinism:** electricity by itself reorganized industry.
2. **Pure complementarity:** electricity was merely one incidental ingredient among many.

The evidence supports a narrower middle position. Electricity changed the feasible geometry and control of production, and those physical properties made certain organizational changes cheaper or possible. The size and timing of the economic payoff still depended on complementary capital, firm structure, skills, markets, and institutions.

## What would make this claim false?

The chapter's argument would weaken if better-identified historical evidence showed that access to electricity had little independent effect on productivity once pre-existing industrial trends were controlled, or that the measured gains attributed to electrification were entirely explained by contemporaneous capital deepening and market changes unrelated to electrical access.

It would also weaken if the supposed physical advantages of distributed electric drive—independent machine control, flexible placement, lower mechanical-transmission burden, modular expansion—did not produce observable differences in factory organization or output where adoption occurred.

The current evidence does not establish electricity as an autonomous cause. It does support the more defensible claim that electricity altered the production possibility set in ways that firms could exploit differently depending on their complementary capabilities.

## Recommended future prose move

No broad rewrite is needed. Chapter 4 already contains the right caveats. A later surgical pass could tighten the paragraph that introduces the general-purpose-technology delay story so the sequence is less textbook-neat: present the classic complementarity explanation, then immediately note that the April 2024 Fiszbein et al. results find rapid measurable productivity effects alongside fast organizational change.

That preserves the chapter's central image—wires changing the geometry of work—while making the empirical timing more precise.
