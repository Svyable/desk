# Chapter 13 — Material constraints

## Purpose

This note supports Chapter 13's move from energy abundance to material-system constraints. The chapter's claim is deliberately narrower than a prediction of mineral shortage: scaling energy infrastructure shifts pressure into extraction, refining, fabrication, recycling, substitution, permitting, and supply-chain concentration.

## Sources and uses

### International Energy Agency, Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 and 2026

The IEA's 2025 copper outlook estimates total copper demand rising from 26.7 Mt in 2024 to 31.3 Mt in 2030 and 34.1 Mt in 2040 under stated policies, with clean-energy demand contributing much of the growth. Its 2025 outlook also identifies copper as an important case where announced mine projects may not match projected requirements. The 2026 Critical Minerals Data Explorer updates the scenario data and makes clear that these are projections conditional on energy, policy, technology, and behavior assumptions.

Book use: establishes that electrification can increase demand for particular material supply chains while preserving uncertainty around the size and timing of that demand.

URLs:
- https://www.iea.org/reports/copper-2
- https://www.iea.org/data-and-statistics/data-tools/critical-minerals-data-explorer

### U.S. Geological Survey, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026

USGS's 2026 summary is the first comprehensive U.S. government source for 2025 mineral production and trade data. The copper sheet reports U.S. mine production and shows the diversity of copper end uses: building construction, electrical/electronic products, transportation, consumer/general products, and industrial machinery.

Book use: prevents the chapter from treating copper as a clean-energy-only input and grounds the argument that new energy demand competes with established uses.

URL: https://doi.org/10.3133/mcs2026

## Falsification / counterevidence

The chapter should be revised materially if evidence shows that projected clean-energy mineral demand can be met at scale without meaningful expansion or adaptation in mining, refining, recycling, substitution, or inventories. It should also be revised if material intensity falls so rapidly across major technologies that minerals cease to be a relevant pacing constraint in energy-system buildout.

Current evidence does not support a simple depletion story. Prices induce exploration, substitution, efficiency, recycling, and new supply. Reserves are economic and technological quantities rather than a fixed countdown to exhaustion. The IEA's own scenario structure and technology cases show that demand is sensitive to chemistry, policy, and consumer behavior. The chapter therefore avoids claims that the world is 'running out' of copper, graphite, rare earths, or other minerals.

Supply concentration is also not identical to physical scarcity. A material may be geologically widespread while processing is geographically concentrated; conversely, a concentrated supply chain may remain reliable for long periods. The policy implication is resilience and diagnosis of the actual bottleneck, not automatic self-sufficiency.

## Claim boundaries

- IEA projections are scenarios, not forecasts guaranteed to occur.
- 'Critical mineral' is a policy/supply-risk category, not a synonym for geological rarity.
- Recycling can become a major supply source but cannot instantly furnish material that is still locked in a rapidly expanding installed stock.
- Substitution can reduce exposure to one material while increasing cost, volume, energy use, or dependence on another.
- Mining and processing impacts vary greatly by commodity, geology, technology, jurisdiction, and operating practice; the chapter does not assign a universal environmental burden per tonne.
