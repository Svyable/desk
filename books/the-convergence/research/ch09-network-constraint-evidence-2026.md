# Chapter 9 — Network Constraint Evidence, 2026

## Purpose

Chapter 9 argues that as generation and end-use technologies improve, the binding energy-system constraint can migrate into the network: interconnection studies, transmission capacity, transformers, permitting, cost allocation, and the sequencing of physical upgrades.

The claim is useful only if it survives a harder test than citing a very large interconnection queue. Queue capacity is not future generation, queue duration is not pure utility processing time, and congestion is not evenly distributed across hours or geography. This note separates those measurements and records the strongest current counterevidence.

## 1. The 2026 interconnection queue is large, but it is not a build forecast

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's *Queued Up: 2026 Edition* uses data through the end of 2025 from all seven U.S. ISOs/RTOs and 50 non-ISO utilities, collectively representing about 98% of installed U.S. generating capacity.

At the end of 2025:

- about 8,200 projects were actively seeking transmission interconnection;
- they represented 1,312 GW of proposed generation and about 749 GW of storage;
- total active generation-plus-storage queue volume was about 2,061 GW;
- only 13% of capacity entering queues from 2000 through 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025;
- 75% had withdrawn, while 10% remained active;
- for regions with usable duration data, projects reaching commercial operation in 2025 had spent a median of more than five years between interconnection request and commercial operation.

Primary source:

- Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, *Queued Up: Characteristics of Power Plants Seeking Transmission Interconnection*, 2026 edition, data updated May 2026 and report published June 2026: https://emp.lbl.gov/queues

### What the chapter may say

The queue is evidence of heavy demand for a scarce interconnection interface and of long project-development timelines. It is not evidence that roughly two terawatts of generation and storage are simply waiting behind paperwork.

The 13% completion figure is especially important because it blocks the easiest rhetorical abuse of the dataset. A queue is a population of proposals at different stages and with different probabilities of completion. Developers withdraw because of network-upgrade costs, but also because of financing, permitting, equipment prices, power-purchase agreements, site strategy, tax policy, project duplication, and ordinary commercial failure.

### What the chapter should not imply

Do not attribute the full request-to-operation interval to utility study delay. Interconnection, permitting, procurement, financing, construction, and project development overlap. The LBNL measure is a duration from request to commercial operation, not a stopwatch measuring regulator or utility inactivity.

## 2. Strong counterevidence: the backlog eased in 2025

The network-constraint story is not a monotonic deterioration story.

LBNL reports that active U.S. queue volume fell about 10% from 2024 to 2025, after peaking near 2,600 GW at the end of 2023. It also reports rising interconnection-agreement processing in several regions: CAISO, ERCOT, SPP, and MISO each signed record amounts of interconnection agreements in 2025, with CAISO and ERCOT each exceeding 30 GW.

LBNL nevertheless reports persistent friction. More than 40% of proposed capacity has historically withdrawn even after signing an interconnection agreement, and median 2025 timelines remained above three years from request to agreement and above five years from request to commercial operation for the regions with sufficient data.

Primary source:

- Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, “Backlog of power plants seeking transmission grid connection eased somewhat in 2025 amidst high withdrawals,” July 1, 2026: https://emp.lbl.gov/news/backlog-power-plants-seeking-transmission-grid-connection-eased-somewhat-2025-amidst

### Why this matters for the argument

A bottleneck can be real without worsening every year. Falling queue volume and faster agreement processing are evidence that institutions and procedures can adapt. That strengthens the book's broader constraint-migration thesis because it makes the network constraint contingent rather than metaphysical: it can be relieved, after which some other constraint may become binding.

If future queue duration falls materially, completion rates rise, and connection costs cease to dominate otherwise viable projects, Chapter 9 should say that plainly rather than preserving a crisis narrative after the evidence changes.

## 3. Congestion is concentrated in time; the exact statistic matters

The U.S. Department of Energy released its draft 2026 National Transmission Needs Study on July 9, 2026. The draft remains open for public comment through September 7, 2026, so its findings should be identified as draft findings rather than settled final results.

The draft's detailed discussion says that **half of transmission congestion value occurs in 5% of hours**. Those high-value periods are associated especially with unexpected or mis-forecast conditions roughly 12 to 36 hours before operation, with high net load and cold weather as important additional drivers. DOE's public summary describes the majority of congestion as concentrated in 5% of hours, but the report text is more precise about the value measure.

Primary sources:

- U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Electricity, *National Transmission Needs Study Draft for Consultation and Public Comment*, July 2026: https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-needs-study
- DOE release, July 9, 2026: https://www.energy.gov/oe/articles/does-office-electricity-publishes-2026-draft-national-transmission-needs-study

### Editorial implication

When the manuscript next receives a prose pass, prefer the report-level formulation: roughly half of estimated transmission congestion **value** is concentrated in 5% of hours. “Value” matters. The statistic is not saying that the physical grid is uncongested for exactly 95% of the year, nor that every congestion metric follows the same distribution.

The concentration also cuts both ways. It supports the value of transmission during stressed hours, but it is counterevidence against assuming that every recurring constraint requires the same remedy. Storage, demand flexibility, generation siting, reconductoring, grid-enhancing technologies, or operational reforms may compete with new lines in particular cases.

## 4. Transformer scarcity is partly a manufacturing problem and partly a coordination problem

DOE's Office of Electricity continues to report that distribution-transformer lead times rose from roughly three to six months in 2019 to 12 to 30 months in 2023, the latest lead-time data it publishes on its current supply-chain page.

DOE also reports more than 80,000 distribution-transformer varieties nationwide. Following a March 2023 industry workshop, DOE identified inconsistent utility specifications and legacy requirements as one contributor to long production times and convened a utility-manufacturer working group to identify interoperability and standardization opportunities. The working group presented progress in a March 5, 2026 webinar.

Primary source:

- U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Electricity, “Supply Chain and Market Analysis,” current page accessed August 2026: https://www.energy.gov/oe/supply-chain-and-market-analysis

### What this supports

The manuscript's transformer example is stronger when treated as a coordination case, not merely a shortage anecdote. Manufacturing capacity matters, but the number of bespoke specifications can fragment demand and complicate production. Standardization can therefore create effective supply-chain capacity without being equivalent to building another factory.

### Counterargument

Specification diversity is not automatically waste. Utilities operate different voltages, climates, network configurations, mounting systems, accessories, maintenance practices, and reliability requirements. The defensible target is unnecessary variation, not universal sameness.

## 5. The strongest counterargument to “the constraint is the network”

The strongest disconfirming case is a project that has timely, affordable network access and still fails because the binding constraint lies elsewhere. Financing can collapse. A permit can fail. Equipment can arrive late. Customer demand can disappear. A data center can obtain a connection and still be constrained by chips, cooling equipment, construction labor, or its own economics.

Likewise, high withdrawal rates make queue statistics endogenous. A costly or slow interconnection process can kill projects, but the queue also contains projects that would not have been built even under an ideal connection process. The data do not cleanly identify the causal fraction attributable to the network.

This means Chapter 9 should make a conditional claim:

> Networks become binding when generation or load can be built faster than the system can connect, transfer, transform, and reliably operate the resulting power at the required place and time.

That formulation can be falsified. It does not turn every failed energy project into evidence for the chapter.

## 6. What would make the chapter's network thesis false or materially weaker?

Evidence that would weaken the chapter includes:

1. Interconnection timelines falling toward historical norms while generation and large-load deployment remain slow for unrelated reasons.
2. Completion rates rising without material network expansion, showing that queue reform or developer selection—not physical network capacity—was the dominant issue.
3. Congestion costs falling persistently even as load and generation grow, suggesting existing capacity plus operational improvements are absorbing the change.
4. Transformer lead times normalizing while project schedules remain unchanged, implying the transformer was vivid but not actually binding.
5. Empirical project-level work showing that financing, permitting, equipment, or customer-demand failures explain substantially more delay than interconnection and network upgrades.

None of these would show that networks are unimportant. They would show that Chapter 9 had promoted one moving bottleneck into a permanent explanation—the exact error the book argues against elsewhere.

## 7. Recommended manuscript boundary

Keep the chapter's central distinction between generation and delivery. Keep the transformer opening and the interconnection-queue example. Keep the emphasis on geography and time.

But preserve three boundaries whenever the prose is revised:

- **Queue capacity is not future capacity.** It measures proposals seeking access to an interface.
- **Queue duration is not pure network delay.** It contains overlapping development processes.
- **Congestion value is concentrated, not uniform.** A small number of hours can carry a large share of system value, which makes diagnosis more important than a universal prescription.

The most durable version of the argument is not that the grid is always the bottleneck. It is that once generation becomes cheap or fast enough, the ability to connect and coordinate it can become more scarce than the energy-producing machine itself.
