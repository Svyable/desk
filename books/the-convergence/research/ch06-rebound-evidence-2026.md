# Chapter 6 — Rebound evidence update, 2026

## Research question

How much of an engineering energy-efficiency gain is typically lost when households, firms, and the wider economy respond to lower effective service costs?

Chapter 6 is directionally sound, but the rebound literature is unusually vulnerable to false precision because studies estimate different objects. Direct rebound for one household service, total microeconomic rebound, indirect re-spending, producer rebound, and economy-wide rebound are not interchangeable. The cleanest revision rule is therefore to report the object being estimated beside the number and to treat large estimates as evidence that economy-wide response can matter, not as a universal coefficient.

## What the older evidence actually supports

Sorrell, Dimitropoulos, and Sommerville’s 2009 review remains useful for the narrow household-service claim already in the chapter. It concluded that direct rebound for common OECD household energy services was generally likely to be below 30 percent. That is a claim about direct rebound, not total household rebound and not the economy-wide response.

Source:

- Steve Sorrell, John Dimitropoulos, and Matt Sommerville (2009), “Empirical estimates of the direct rebound effect: A review,” *Energy Policy* 37(4), 1356–1371. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2008.11.026

## A newer household-level synthesis is larger — and measures more

A 2025 meta-regression by Marvin Schütt, Anke Jacksohn, Tobias Möllney, and Katrin Rehdanz assembled 1,118 rebound estimates from 43 studies. Their estimated average **total microeconomic rebound** is roughly 41–52 percent.

That does not straightforwardly overturn the older “below 30 percent” direct-rebound result. The object is broader. The meta-regression combines estimates across services and study designs and explicitly finds that methodology, data type, scenario construction, and estimation choices explain a substantial share of the dispersion.

The useful manuscript implication is not “the accepted rebound number is now 50 percent.” It is that direct rebound and total micro rebound should not be placed on the same numerical ladder without naming the difference.

Source:

- Marvin Schütt, Anke Jacksohn, Tobias Möllney, and Katrin Rehdanz (2025), “How to Explain the Huge Differences in Rebound Estimates: A Meta-Regression Analysis of the Literature,” *The Energy Journal* 46(2). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/01956574241289004

## Economy-wide evidence remains both large and method-sensitive

Bruns, Moneta, and Stern’s 2021 structural-vector-autoregression estimate for the United States remains one of the strongest empirical challenges to a small-rebound story. Their preferred estimates put economy-wide rebound around 100 percent after four years, implying that the initial aggregate energy saving is eventually offset.

The chapter is right not to generalize that result. Identification of an “energy-efficiency shock” at the economy level is difficult because efficiency changes alongside prices, capital, trade, regulation, sector mix, supply, and other technologies. A structural VAR imposes a different set of assumptions from a computable general-equilibrium model or household demand study.

Source:

- Stephan B. Bruns, Alessio Moneta, and David I. Stern (2021), “Estimating the economy-wide rebound effect using empirically identified structural vector autoregressions,” *Energy Economics* 97, 105158. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eneco.2021.105158

Brockway, Sorrell, Semieniuk, Heun, and Court’s 2021 review helps put that estimate in context. Thirteen of the 21 computable general-equilibrium studies they reviewed estimated economy-wide rebound of at least 50 percent, as did 10 of 12 studies using other methodologies. Their conclusion was deliberately qualified: the evidence suggests economy-wide rebound **may erode more than half** of expected savings, while methodological diversity and omitted mechanisms limit confidence in any single point estimate.

Source:

- Paul E. Brockway, Steve Sorrell, Gregor Semieniuk, Matthew Kuperus Heun, and Victor Court (2021), “Energy efficiency and economy-wide rebound effects: A review of the evidence and its implications,” *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews* 141, 110781. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2021.110781

## The measurement problem is still active research

A 2025 methodological paper by Heun, Semieniuk, and Brockway is valuable precisely because it does **not** present rebound measurement as settled. The authors argue that the field still lacks sufficiently clear analytical foundations for consistently determining rebound size. Their framework separates energy, expenditure, and consumption effects; operational energy from embodied and “emplacement” effects; and microeconomic from macroeconomic responses.

This is strong counterevidence against writing the chapter as though competing rebound estimates are merely noisy observations of one agreed quantity. Some disagreement is definitional and structural, not only statistical.

Source:

- Matthew Kuperus Heun, Gregor Semieniuk, and Paul E. Brockway (2025), “Energetic and Economic Aspects of Rebound, Part I: Foundations of a Rigorous Analytical Framework,” *The Energy Journal* 46(5). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/01956574251331969

## A new 2026 global estimate should be treated as a model result, not a new universal fact

Freire-González, Chen, and Bordón-Lesme published a global multiregional input-output analysis in June 2026. Their model covers 43 countries plus five rest-of-world regions and estimates household re-consumption after hypothetical efficiency improvements. Under their setup, backfire occurs in a majority of economies for several energy categories; for an all-energy efficiency improvement, 56.25 percent of modeled economies show rebound above 100 percent.

That is striking evidence that indirect re-spending and supply-chain effects can be large in a globally connected accounting framework. It is **not** a direct observation that most real-world efficiency policies increase total energy use. The result depends on the model’s re-spending assumptions, input-output structure, efficiency shock, and the distinction between counterfactual potential savings and realized consumption.

The chapter should use this paper, if at all, as evidence that the high-rebound side of the literature remains live — not as a replacement for the more cautious synthesis already in the manuscript.

Source:

- Jaume Freire-González, Qian Chen, and Martín Bordón-Lesme (2026), “Worldwide rebound effects: a multiregional input–output analysis,” *Journal of Industrial Ecology* 30, 1851–1867, published 20 June 2026. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44498-026-00126-9

## Strongest counterarguments to both easy stories

The strongest challenge to a **small-rebound** story is that producer, indirect, macroeconomic, and general-equilibrium channels can be much larger than direct household response. Looking only at driving miles or thermostat settings can miss investment, price, income, sectoral, and supply-chain effects.

The strongest challenge to a **Jevons-always-wins** story is equally serious. Rebound varies by service, income, saturation, time budget, market structure, policy regime, and what the study calls potential savings. A model can generate backfire without demonstrating that a particular historical efficiency improvement caused aggregate energy use to rise. Direct rebound for many mature household services remains well below 100 percent.

A defensible chapter claim is therefore conditional: efficiency reliably reduces energy required per unit of service, but the fraction of those engineering savings that survives at household or economy scale is an empirical question whose answer depends on what becomes cheaper, for whom, and what other constraints bind.

## Suggested surgical manuscript change

The existing Chapter 6 evidence section should eventually distinguish three levels in one short passage rather than presenting the estimates as a progression toward a single “true” rebound number:

1. direct household rebound is often modest;
2. broader microeconomic rebound can be materially larger;
3. economy-wide estimates can reach or exceed 100 percent, but are more model- and identification-sensitive.

That hierarchy would make the chapter more precise without changing its conclusion: efficiency is a genuine technological improvement, but conservation is an outcome of an entire system rather than a property of the machine alone.
