# Chapter 10 — Nuclear buildout economics, 2026

## Research question

What does the current nuclear buildout actually support about the role of fission in a high-electricity system, and what does it *not* support about cost, speed, or repeatability?

The useful argument is narrower than “nuclear is back.” Fission can supply large amounts of firm, low-carbon electricity from a small physical footprint, but the present construction record is geographically concentrated and unusually sensitive to financing, project execution, standardization, regulation, and supply-chain capability. The physics travels better than the institutions do.

## Current buildout: large, but concentrated

The IAEA Power Reactor Information System (PRIS) is the cleanest current statistical spine. Its public dashboard, last updated June 23, 2026 when checked for this note, listed 415 reactors in operation with 379.4 GW(e) of net capacity, 23 reactors in suspended operation, and 73 reactors under construction totaling 75.8 GW(e). PRIS is continually maintained with information confirmed through national contact points.

That is substantial construction momentum, but it is not geographically neutral. The IEA's *Global Energy Review 2026*, using PRIS data accessed March 25, reports 78 GW under construction in 15 countries and says half of that capacity is in China. Ten reactor construction starts occurred in 2025, nine in China and one in Russia. More strikingly, the IEA reports that 94% of reactors that began construction over the preceding decade used Chinese or Russian designs.

This concentration matters more than the headline reactor count. It suggests that a Chapter 10 explanation should ask what capabilities are being reproduced where construction is active: standardized designs, experienced contractors, regulator familiarity, supply chains, financing structures, serial ordering, and a political willingness to sustain long projects. The evidence does **not** establish which of those variables is causal by itself.

## A useful 2024 comparison: same milestone, different histories

PRIS records six reactors connecting to the grid in 2024, including Barakah 4 in the United Arab Emirates, Vogtle 4 in the United States, and Flamanville 3 in France. Putting those three names in the same sentence is more useful than treating “nuclear construction cost” as one global number. They reached the same broad milestone under very different national, regulatory, industrial, contractual, and financing environments.

For the United States, the NRC records Vogtle Unit 4's combined license as issued on February 10, 2012. The IAEA records Vogtle 4 among the reactors first connected to the grid in 2024. The elapsed calendar time is therefore a real project fact; it should not be silently converted into a claim that reactor technology intrinsically requires that duration. The project carried first-of-a-kind U.S. AP1000 execution problems, contractor and supply-chain disruptions, and a particular financing and regulatory history.

The chapter should eventually choose one or two case studies and reconstruct them from regulator, utility, and project records rather than from advocacy summaries. The point of the comparison is institutional: similar underlying fission physics can produce sharply different delivery outcomes.

## Cost: do not confuse the plant with the financing of the plant

The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency's 2025 report on financing new nuclear builds is especially useful because it identifies a mechanism often hidden by arguments over levelized cost. New nuclear projects are capital-intensive and expose lenders and owners to construction, price, and political risk for long periods. The NEA argues that the cost of capital can be reduced when those risks are identified, allocated, and managed more effectively. The IEA makes a similar point: predictable cash flows and some mechanism for bearing construction risk are often prerequisites for commercial debt financing of new large reactors.

This is not a semantic distinction. Two plants with comparable engineering costs can produce very different delivered electricity costs if one takes longer to build or is financed at a much higher weighted cost of capital. Conversely, a low financing rate does not make an inefficient construction program efficient; it can merely make delay cheaper to carry.

- OECD Nuclear Energy Agency, *New Perspectives for Financing Nuclear New Build* (2025): https://oecd-nea.org/jcms/pl_76525/new-perspectives-for-financing-nuclear-new-build-financing-new-nuclear-power-plants-minimising-the-cost-of-capital-by-optimising-risk-management

## The historical-cost literature is contested

The planned Chapter 10 brief already cites Lovering, Yip, and Nordhaus (2016), which assembled overnight construction-cost data for more than half of reactors built worldwide and emphasized strong differences across countries and eras. That paper is useful precisely because it resists a single universal nuclear learning curve.

But it should not stand alone. Koomey, Hultman, and Grübler published a direct critique arguing that overnight cost omits financing and construction-duration effects that are economically central to nuclear projects, and disputing some dataset and classification choices. Lovering, Nordhaus, and Yip replied that mixing financing costs across countries and decades can obscure the construction-cost signal they were trying to isolate.

The disagreement gives the chapter a better methodological rule than picking a side: **state what cost is being measured before comparing projects.** Overnight construction cost, all-in capital cost, financing cost, and delivered electricity cost answer different questions. Construction duration belongs beside all of them.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter's likely thesis

If Chapter 10 argues that nuclear's mixed record is mainly an institutional-capability problem, the strongest challenge is that this framing can become unfalsifiable. Every expensive or delayed project can be blamed on “institutions,” while every successful project is credited to standardization and competence after the fact. Reactor scale, engineering complexity, quality-assurance requirements, long construction periods, and severe downside risk are themselves technological characteristics that shape the institutions and financing the technology requires.

The chapter should therefore avoid saying that nuclear is cheap *if only* institutions are good. A more defensible claim is conditional: the observed range of outcomes shows that project delivery is not determined by reactor physics alone, while the persistence of large-project risk shows that institutional competence cannot simply wish away the technology's capital intensity and complexity.

A useful falsification test for any proposed explanation is: does it predict, before completion, which projects should be faster or cheaper? If “standardization” is the mechanism, specify what is repeated — design, contractor team, regulator review, supply chain, site work, or financing structure — and look for the result across successive units.

## Current trajectory and what not to overclaim

The IEA expects nuclear output to rise through 2030, driven especially by China, where it expects almost 30 GW of new capacity to come online during 2026–2030. In the United States and European Union, by contrast, the IEA expects nuclear output to remain broadly stable over that forecast period. That is a better description of the present “return” than a global renaissance slogan: expansion is real, but its pace and geography are uneven.

SMRs should remain a prospective section rather than proof of a solved construction model. The IEA explicitly makes favorable SMR deployment contingent on future cost reductions, regulatory progress, financing, and project execution. PRIS did record construction start of Canada's 300 MW Darlington SMR 1 on April 22, 2026, which is a concrete milestone, but a construction start is not a demonstrated commercial cost curve.

## Recommended Chapter 10 narrative move

Do not open with a generic cooling tower or a debate over whether nuclear is good or bad. Open with three 2024 grid connections — Barakah 4, Vogtle 4, Flamanville 3 — and the awkward fact that a reactor crossing the same finish line can embody very different histories of repetition, delay, financing, regulation, and industrial capability. Then widen to the current construction map, where much of the momentum is concentrated in countries that are building repeatedly.

That structure matches the broader Benson corpus visible in this repository: begin with a concrete system under pressure, keep the costs and uncertainty in frame, and only then name the governing idea. It also keeps Chapter 10 from becoming a referendum on nuclear power. The more interesting subject is what it takes for a civilization to turn difficult physics into repeatable infrastructure.
