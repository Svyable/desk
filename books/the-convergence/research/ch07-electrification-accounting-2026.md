# Chapter 7 — Electrification accounting and substitution, 2026

## Research question

Does the current global shift toward electricity support the chapter's claim that electricity is becoming a more general-purpose carrier across transport, buildings, industry, and computation, and what accounting choices can make that transition look faster or cleaner than it is?

The strongest version of the chapter is not “everything becomes electric.” It is that electricity's share of final consumption is rising, electricity demand is growing faster than total energy demand, and several major end uses can deliver the same service with less final energy when they move from combustion to electric technologies. That claim survives current evidence, but it needs three boundaries: electricity is still a minority of final energy, final-energy accounting is not the same thing as useful work or welfare, and electrification can shift rather than eliminate upstream burdens.

## The long-run share: real change, slow change

IEA's *Key World Energy Statistics 2021* reports electricity at 9.5% of world total final consumption in 1973. The IEA's *Electricity 2026* report puts electricity at 21% of total final consumption in 2025 and projects 24% in 2030.

That comparison supports the manuscript's current half-century framing. Electricity's share has more than doubled, but the change took fifty-two years. It is therefore evidence against both complacency and technological inevitability: the system is meaningfully more electric, but most final energy still arrives in other forms.

A statistical caution belongs beside the comparison. The 1973 and 2025 figures come from different IEA publication vintages and should not be presented as if they were one frozen historical series assembled under a single vintage. The broad change is robust; false decimal precision is not useful.

Sources:

- IEA, *Key World Energy Statistics 2021 — Final consumption*: https://www.iea.org/reports/key-world-energy-statistics-2021/final-consumption
- IEA, *Electricity 2026 — Demand*: https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/demand

## 2025 demand: broad-based, but not uniformly “electrification”

The IEA's *Global Energy Review 2026* estimates global electricity demand grew around 3% in 2025 while total global energy demand grew 1.3%. Electricity therefore grew roughly 2.3 times faster than total energy demand that year.

The composition matters. Buildings accounted for nearly 45% of the increase in electricity demand. Transport contributed more than 10%, compared with about 4% on average over the preceding decade. Data-centre electricity consumption rose around 17%, adding roughly 70 TWh, but total global electricity demand rose by around 800 TWh.

These numbers support the chapter's argument that the shift is not reducible to AI or EVs. They also warn against calling every additional kilowatt-hour “electrification.” Some of the 2025 increase came from rising incomes, appliance ownership, cooling demand, weather, and conventional industrial growth rather than the direct substitution of an electric technology for a fossil end use.

That distinction matters causally. Faster electricity-demand growth is consistent with electrification, but the demand statistic alone does not identify how much combustion was displaced.

Source:

- IEA, *Global Energy Review 2026 — Electricity demand*: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/electricity-demand

## Electric vehicles: a cleaner substitution example

The IEA's *Global EV Outlook 2026* estimates that the global EV fleet consumed around 250 TWh of electricity in 2025, approximately 1% of worldwide final electricity demand. The manuscript already uses the companion IEA estimate that EVs displaced roughly 1.7 million barrels of oil per day in 2025.

This is a stronger test of the chapter's physical argument than aggregate electricity growth because the end-use substitution is visible. An electric drivetrain can deliver useful motion with substantially less final energy than an internal-combustion drivetrain because much less input energy is rejected as waste heat.

But final-energy reduction should not be silently converted into a claim about total system burden. Vehicle manufacturing, battery materials, charging infrastructure, road use, electricity generation, upstream fuel and mineral supply, and lifecycle emissions remain relevant. The appropriate claim is that electrification can eliminate a large conversion loss at the vehicle, not that the physical system disappears.

Source:

- IEA, *Global EV Outlook 2026 — Outlook for electric mobility*: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/outlook-for-electric-mobility-chap-9-11

## Heat pumps: favorable physics, stubborn adoption

The IEA's *Global Energy Review 2026* reports global heat-pump sales fell about 2% in 2025. Europe returned to growth, with sales up 11%, while U.S. sales fell around 13% and China and Japan were broadly flat.

The newer *Heat Pump Monitor 2026* adds an important system-level counterpoint. It estimates heat pumps supplied around 5% of global heating needs in 2024 and around 12% of global space-heating needs in buildings. The report also emphasizes retrofit complexity, installer constraints, local electricity and gas prices, building-stock heterogeneity, and policy stability.

This is exactly the kind of evidence Chapter 7 needs. Heat pumps can move several units of heat for each unit of electricity under suitable conditions, but favorable thermodynamics does not guarantee rapid diffusion. Installation is a construction project, not a physics demonstration.

The IEA also estimates that in 2024 heat pumps represented 2–16% of annual peak electricity demand across major markets, depending on climate and adoption. That range reinforces the chapter's next-step argument: successful electrification creates network and peak-load questions even when it reduces final-energy requirements.

Sources:

- IEA, *Global Energy Review 2026 — Technology: Heat pumps*: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-heat-pumps
- IEA, *Heat Pump Monitor 2026 — Key findings*: https://www.iea.org/reports/heat-pump-monitor-2026/key-findings

## Renewable capacity: do not confuse the stock of machines with delivered electricity

IRENA's *Renewable Capacity Statistics 2026* records 692 GW of renewable capacity added in 2025, taking global renewable capacity to 5,149 GW. Renewables represented 85.6% of net power-capacity additions and 49% of installed global power capacity at year end.

These are remarkable industrial-buildout numbers. They still measure capacity, not annual generation, hourly availability, system value, or reliable supply. IRENA itself notes that approaching half of installed capacity raises planning questions around grids, flexibility, and variable renewable output.

The distinction is useful for the manuscript because it prevents a common rhetorical slide: “most new capacity is renewable” is not equivalent to “most electricity is renewable,” much less “reliable electricity is solved.” Capacity factors, curtailment, transmission, storage, hydro conditions, maintenance, and dispatch all intervene between nameplate capacity and delivered service.

Source:

- IRENA, *Renewable Capacity Statistics 2026*: https://www.irena.org/Publications/2026/Mar/Renewable-capacity-statistics-2026

## Primary energy, final energy, and the appearance of efficiency

Electrification changes energy accounting because combustion technologies and non-combustion generation enter statistical balances differently. Replacing an internal-combustion engine with an electric drivetrain can reduce final energy because the electric motor is more efficient. Replacing a thermal power plant with wind or solar can also reduce measured primary energy under methods that count the thermal input to combustion generation but do not assign an analogous rejected-heat term to wind and solar.

Neither effect is fictitious. The conversion losses are physically real. The danger is interpretation. Falling primary energy can coexist with rising useful service, and that can be genuine progress. But primary-energy decline alone does not show lower material use, lower system cost, lower emissions, or higher welfare.

For Chapter 7, the clean hierarchy is:

- electricity share of final consumption measures the carrier's penetration;
- final-energy savings can capture real end-use efficiency gains;
- primary-energy changes depend partly on accounting convention and conversion technology;
- useful work and delivered service are closer to the book's capability thesis;
- welfare and external costs require additional evidence.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter's likely thesis

The “Age of Electricity” framing can become self-confirming if every rise in electricity use counts as evidence while every stalled electric technology is treated as temporary friction. Electricity still supplied only about one-fifth of final energy in 2025. Aviation, shipping, high-temperature industry, chemical feedstocks, legacy heating systems, and much road transport remain molecule-heavy. Even where electric alternatives are technically feasible, capital turnover, relative energy prices, financing, building constraints, infrastructure, consumer preferences, and policy can slow or reverse adoption.

There is also a denominator problem. Electricity demand can rise rapidly because economies are richer, hotter, more digital, and more appliance-intensive even without direct fuel substitution. Faster electricity growth therefore does not prove that electricity is displacing fossil energy at the same rate.

The chapter's defensible claim is narrower: electricity is becoming a more important and unusually general-purpose final-energy carrier, and in several major end uses it can deliver the same service with less final energy. Whether that transition reduces total cost and environmental burden depends on generation mix, networks, equipment turnover, materials, and institutions.

## What would make this false?

The chapter's convergence claim would weaken if electricity's share of final consumption stopped rising for a sustained period; if major electric substitutes consistently failed to reduce system-level cost or useful-energy requirements; if end uses thought to be electrifiable proved broadly resistant for physical rather than temporary economic reasons; or if electricity-demand growth remained high while measurable substitution away from direct fossil use stagnated.

A useful future check is therefore not merely the growth rate of electricity demand. Track electricity's share of final consumption alongside displaced fuel use in transport and heating, industrial electrification, generation emissions, network investment, and the cost of delivered energy services.
