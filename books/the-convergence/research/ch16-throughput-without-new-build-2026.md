# Chapter 16 — Throughput without new build (2026)

## Claim under test

Chapter 16 argues that the physical economy moves at the speed of deployment rather than discovery, and that build capability can become more important than further declines in equipment cost. That survives current evidence, but the chapter needs one important boundary: **a system can sometimes raise effective build throughput without constructing a proportionate amount of new infrastructure.**

The distinction matters because otherwise “build rate” risks becoming a synonym for pouring more concrete, adding more steel, or authorizing more capital. In networked infrastructure, operational rules, connection agreements, reconductoring, dynamic ratings, power-flow control, and better use of existing assets can increase the amount of useful capacity delivered before a new corridor is finished.

## Fresh primary-source check: IEA *Electricity 2026*

The IEA’s February 2026 grid chapter supports the manuscript’s underlying timing mismatch. It reports more than 2,500 GW of renewable generation, storage, and large-load projects in grid connection queues worldwide. It also gives indicative lead times of roughly 5–15 years for new grid infrastructure, compared with about 1–5 years for solar and wind projects, 1–3 years for data centers, and 1–2 years for EV charging infrastructure.

That is strong evidence for Chapter 16’s point that different layers of the system run on different clocks.

But the same source contains the strongest useful counterargument to a purely construction-centered reading. The IEA estimates that **1,200–1,600 GW of advanced-stage queued projects could potentially be connected by using existing grids more efficiently**, assuming other constraints are unchanged. Its estimate includes roughly 750–900 GW enabled through conditional non-firm connection agreements, with the rest coming from grid-enhancing technologies and upgrades such as dynamic line ratings, advanced power-flow control, reconductoring, and voltage uprating.

The IEA separately estimates that grid-enhancing technologies and related upgrades could unlock about 450–700 GW of advanced-stage projects. These are modeled estimates, not completed connections, and should not be presented as guaranteed capacity. Still, they matter conceptually: some bottlenecks that look like a shortage of physical network can partly be a shortage of information, operating flexibility, regulatory permission, or willingness to accept conditional service.

## Why this sharpens the chapter

The chapter’s strongest formulation is therefore not “civilization advances at the rate it can build new assets.” It is closer to:

> Civilization advances at the rate it can turn available technology, capital, institutions, and existing physical assets into reliable service.

Sometimes that requires new factories, lines, substations, reactors, mines, or crews. Sometimes the higher-value intervention is to extract more throughput from what already exists.

That distinction reinforces, rather than weakens, the chapter’s emphasis on diagnosis. A ten-year transmission line may genuinely be the binding constraint. In another region, the binding constraint may be a queue rule that assumes every project needs firm access at all hours. Treating both problems as “build more grid” can waste years and capital.

## Counterargument and disconfirming evidence

The strongest counterargument to the build-rate thesis is that modular technologies and operational improvements can scale much faster than traditional infrastructure, allowing systems to adapt before slow assets are replaced. Solar, batteries, flexible demand, advanced conductors, and software-assisted grid operation all support that objection.

The evidence does not falsify the chapter, but it narrows it. Build rate should be understood as **system throughput**, not only new construction. If a regulatory change or grid-enhancing technology connects useful capacity faster than a new line would, that is a build-rate improvement in the economically relevant sense even though little new right-of-way was created.

## Manuscript implications

A future prose pass can strengthen Chapter 16 by making three distinctions explicit:

- **new construction vs. effective capacity:** useful throughput can rise through operation and upgrades as well as new assets;
- **queued capacity vs. physically impossible capacity:** a queue records projects awaiting connection, not proof that every queued project requires major new network construction;
- **modeled unlock potential vs. realized deployment:** the IEA’s 1,200–1,600 GW figure is an estimate under stated assumptions, not a forecast of completed connections.

This preserves the chapter’s central insight while making it harder to read “the build rate” as a one-dimensional measure of concrete poured or dollars spent.
