# Chapter 10 research note — nuclear buildability, concentration, and cost accounting

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 argues that nuclear fission's physical advantages do not translate automatically into cheap or rapid deployment. The binding variable can be the production system around the reactor: standardization, supplier continuity, regulatory sequencing, project management, financing, and the ability to repeat difficult work without rebuilding institutional memory from scratch.

The claim should remain narrower than “institutions explain nuclear cost.” Reactor design, safety requirements, civil works, site conditions, labor productivity, financing, and policy all matter. The useful question is whether observed variation in build outcomes is large enough that construction capability must be treated as part of the technology rather than as an external footnote.

## Current global construction evidence

The International Energy Agency's *Global Energy Review 2026*, using IAEA PRIS data accessed March 25, 2026, reports 420 GW of nuclear capacity in operation at the end of 2025 and 78 GW under construction in fifteen countries. Half of the capacity under construction was in China. Ten reactor projects began construction in 2025; nine were in China and one in Russia. Across the preceding decade, 94% of reactors that started construction used Chinese or Russian designs.

Source: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-nuclear

This supports the manuscript's claim that the present nuclear expansion is real but geographically and institutionally concentrated. It does **not** show that Chinese or Russian reactor designs are inherently superior, nor that Western countries are incapable of new construction. The evidence is about where repeat construction is occurring, not a clean causal estimate of why.

A stronger formulation for the chapter is therefore:

> Nuclear construction is expanding, but repeat build capability is concentrated. That concentration is evidence that deployment depends on more than reactor physics; it is not proof that one national model universally minimizes cost or risk.

## Do not collapse construction starts, grid connection, commercial operation, and full-power testing

The IAEA's 2024 reactor record is useful because it distinguishes project milestones that public discussion often compresses into “completed.” Barakah Unit 4 connected to the grid on March 23, 2024 and began commercial operation on September 5, 2024. Vogtle Unit 4 connected on March 6, 2024 and entered commercial operation on April 29, 2024. Flamanville 3 connected to the grid on December 21, 2024.

Source: https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/RDS-2-45_web.pdf

EDF then reported that Flamanville 3 reached 100% nuclear thermal power for the first time on December 14, 2025, while startup testing continued afterward. That means first grid connection and full-power operation were separated by almost a year.

Source: https://www.edf.fr/en/the-edf-group/dedicated-sections/journalists/all-press-releases/update-on-the-flamanville-epr-the-reactor-has-reached-100-of-nuclear-thermal-power

This distinction matters narratively as well as statistically. “The plant opened” is too blunt for a technology whose startup sequence contains fuel loading, first criticality, low-power tests, synchronization, staged power ascension, inspections, trips, and further tests. Chapter 10's existing line that “a giant machine has to prove itself while becoming itself” is directionally right; the milestone data give it an empirical spine.

## Vogtle: cost is not one number

Georgia's Public Service Commission approved a December 19, 2023 agreement capping the share of Vogtle Units 3 and 4 capital construction costs recoverable from Georgia Power ratepayers at $7.562 billion. The same PSC release says Georgia Power expected to have spent about $10.75 billion to bring the project to fruition.

Source: https://psc.ga.gov/site/assets/files/7846/media_advisory_12_19_23_vogtle_prudency_agreement.pdf

Those figures refer to Georgia Power's portion and regulatory recovery treatment, not the all-in cost of both reactors across every co-owner and financing category. Georgia Power owns 45.7% of Vogtle; Oglethorpe Power, MEAG Power, and Dalton Utilities own the remainder.

Source: https://www.georgiapower.com/about/energy/plants/plant-vogtle.html

The manuscript is right to resist a single headline “Vogtle cost,” but it should keep the accounting boundary explicit whenever the number appears. At minimum distinguish:

- overnight or construction cost before financing;
- owner-specific capital cost;
- total project cost across co-owners;
- financing / allowance-for-funds-used-during-construction;
- amount judged recoverable from a particular customer base;
- lifetime cost of delivered electricity.

A cost figure that moves between those categories without warning can make almost any nuclear argument look stronger than it is.

## Flamanville: delay changes the economics even before generation begins

EDF's project accounting put Flamanville 3's completion cost at €13.2 billion in 2015 euros, excluding interim interest. That exclusion matters. For a capital-intensive project, a long construction period creates financing costs before the plant earns revenue; schedule delay is therefore an economic variable rather than only an engineering inconvenience.

Source: https://www.edf.fr/sites/groupe/files/2024-10/2024-10-24-half-year-results-financial-report-30-june-2024.pdf

This is the cleanest reason to preserve the chapter's distinction between the **operating plant** and the **building project**. A completed reactor can have low fuel costs, high capacity factors, and a long operating life while the preceding construction project was financially punishing. Both statements can be true without contradiction.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest counterargument to the chapter's institutional thesis is that nuclear's difficulty is not primarily institutional at all. Large reactors are intrinsically complex, safety-critical megaprojects. Heavy civil works, nuclear-grade components, redundant systems, containment, seismic requirements, quality assurance, and long commissioning sequences create unavoidable opportunities for error and delay. A country cannot regulate or standardize those physical demands away.

The evidence supports taking this counterargument seriously. The chapter should not imply that better management can make nuclear construction resemble solar-module manufacturing.

But the international construction map and within-program repetition still make a pure “physics determines buildability” account insufficient. If outcomes differ sharply across countries, eras, and repeated programs, then institutional continuity and industrial learning are part of the causal system even when physical complexity remains irreducible.

The strongest surviving claim is therefore:

> Nuclear's physical complexity sets a demanding floor. Institutions determine how often a project adds avoidable complexity on top of it.

## What would make the chapter's argument false?

The institutional-production argument would weaken if reactor construction outcomes converged strongly across countries and programs after controlling for design and site conditions; if repeated builds showed no durable improvement in schedules, labor requirements, or financing terms; or if countries with long pauses in construction routinely restarted large-reactor programs without first-of-a-kind penalties.

It would strengthen if repeated standardized builds systematically reduced schedule variance and labor hours, if regulatory reuse shortened licensing without degrading safety outcomes, and if financing costs fell measurably as execution became more predictable.

Small modular reactors should be treated as a prospective test of this mechanism, not as proof of it. Their promise is partly to move work from site-specific megaprojects toward repeat fabrication. The relevant evidence will be successive-unit labor hours, factory throughput, schedule variance, financing cost, and actual design repetition. Until those series exist at commercial scale, the production-model claim remains a hypothesis.

## Editorial implication

Chapter 10's core idea survives the evidence and becomes more precise when three boundaries stay visible:

1. **Expansion is not uniform.** Global nuclear construction is substantial but unusually concentrated in a few repeat-building systems.
2. **Milestones are not interchangeable.** Construction start, grid connection, commercial operation, and full-power testing describe different states of the project.
3. **Cost is an accounting boundary.** Plant cost, financing cost, owner exposure, regulated recovery, and lifetime electricity cost answer different questions.

That precision improves the book's larger thesis. Energy abundance is never only the discovery of a dense source. It also depends on whether a society can turn demanding physical systems into repeatable infrastructure.
