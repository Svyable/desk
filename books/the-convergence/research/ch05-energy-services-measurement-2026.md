# Chapter 5 — Energy services, measurement, and the lighting example

## Research question

How strong is the empirical basis for Chapter 5's claim that civilization is better understood through the cost and quantity of energy services than through raw fuel consumption alone, and where can that framing overreach?

The chapter's lighting example is unusually useful because it lets the book compare a service — visible light — across technologies whose fuel inputs, efficiencies, prices, convenience, and operating characteristics differ radically. The empirical point survives scrutiny, but the historical series should be described as a reconstruction rather than as a directly observed seven-century meter reading.

## Fouquet and Pearson: the chapter's main quantitative spine

Roger Fouquet and Peter Pearson's 2006 *Energy Journal* paper reconstructs the price and use of artificial lighting in the United Kingdom from 1300 to 2000. Their headline result is the one already used in the manuscript: by 2000, UK lighting services cost less than one three-thousandth of their 1800 real price, while per-capita use of lighting services was about 6,500 times greater.

Those are not simply fuel-price observations. The authors combine historical evidence on fuel and appliance prices, wages, technology, luminous efficacy, and estimated consumption to translate changing systems — candles, oil, gas, electricity — into a common service measure. That is precisely why the series is valuable, and also why its numbers should retain the language of estimation.

The paper itself warns against projecting the historical trend mechanically into the future because technology shifts, rebound, and saturation can change demand behavior. That warning belongs beside the spectacular cost decline rather than after it.

Source:

- Roger Fouquet and Peter J. G. Pearson (2006), “Seven Centuries of Energy Services: The Price and Use of Light in the United Kingdom (1300–2000),” *The Energy Journal* 27(1), DOI 10.5547/ISSN0195-6574-EJ-Vol27-No1-8.

## Nordhaus: why ordinary price indexes miss technological change

William Nordhaus's lighting history makes a related but distinct argument. He asks how much labor was required to purchase a standardized quantity of light and shows enormous long-run declines in that labor cost. Chapter 5 already uses his estimates of roughly 5.37 labor-hours per 1,000 lumen-hours around 1800, 0.22 around 1900, and 0.00012 by 1992.

The important methodological boundary is that these figures are quality-adjusted historical estimates. They depend on reconstructed wages, lamp and fuel prices, and assumptions about the light output of historical devices. Their force comes from making quality change visible, not from eliminating uncertainty.

This is a good place for the manuscript to keep its present qualification: the series is not a pure measure of lamp efficiency. It is an economic reconstruction of the labor cost of a lighting service.

Source:

- William D. Nordhaus (1997), “Do Real-Output and Real-Wage Measures Capture Reality? The History of Lighting Suggests Not,” in *The Economics of New Goods*, University of Chicago Press / NBER.

## Rebound is part of the lighting history, not a contradiction to it

Fouquet and Pearson's later work on long-run lighting demand strengthens the bridge from Chapter 5 into Chapter 6. Their estimates show that the responsiveness of lighting demand to falling service prices changed sharply across development phases rather than remaining constant.

In their long-run UK estimates, price and income elasticities became very large during parts of the nineteenth century and then declined substantially during the twentieth century. That pattern matters because it shows both sides of the book's argument at once: cheaper and better lighting generated enormous additional use, but demand did not remain infinitely elastic as illumination became commonplace.

The right inference is therefore not “efficiency causes more consumption” in every setting. It is that service demand can expand dramatically while a formerly scarce service is being adopted, then become less responsive as saturation and changing preferences matter more.

Source:

- Roger Fouquet and Peter J. G. Pearson (2012), “The Long Run Demand for Lighting: Elasticities and Rebound Effects in Different Phases of Economic Development,” *Economics of Energy & Environmental Policy* 1(1); working-paper version available from the Basque Centre for Climate Change / University of the Basque Country repository.

## Modern LED evidence supports the service/input distinction

The same distinction remains visible in current lighting technology. U.S. Department of Energy guidance describes LED lighting as the most energy-efficient widely deployed lighting technology and reports specialized source efficacies around 150 lumens per watt or more, while commercial and industrial procurement guidance sets minimum luminaire efficacies that commonly exceed 120 lumens per watt and reach 175 lumens per watt for some high-bay applications.

DOE's current framing is useful because it explicitly separates source efficacy from luminaire efficacy: the performance of the LED device is not identical to the performance of the complete fixture, power supply, optics, controls, and application. That is the modern version of Chapter 5's central measurement problem. A watt at the wall, a lumen from the source, useful illuminance on a work surface, and the value of the activity performed under that light are different layers.

Sources:

- U.S. Department of Energy, Solid-State Lighting, “LED Basics,” current program guidance checked 2026-08-30.
- U.S. Department of Energy Federal Energy Management Program, “Purchasing Energy-Efficient Commercial and Industrial LED Luminaires,” current procurement guidance checked 2026-08-30.

## Strongest counterargument: a service metric can hide burdens too

The service framework is better than a raw fuel ledger for many questions, but it is not automatically a welfare metric.

A lumen-hour records visible-light output. It does not say whether the light was useful, badly placed, intrusive, ecologically damaging, or unwanted. A kilometer of travel does not distinguish an emergency trip from congestion. A degree-hour of cooling does not reveal who could afford the equipment or whether the electricity came from a polluting marginal generator.

Energy-service accounting can therefore hide externalities and distribution just as raw energy accounting can hide efficiency. The chapter already moves in the right direction when it separates primary energy, final energy, useful work, service, and welfare. That sequence should remain explicit.

The strongest form of the counterargument is that a service measure can become another denominator that makes progress look cleaner than the system really is. Outsourced production, embodied energy, pollution, land use, and infrastructure costs can disappear from the point-of-use service even though they remain part of the physical chain.

The defensible claim is narrower: energy-service measures are often better for understanding capability and technological change, but they must be paired with system boundaries, external costs, and distributional evidence before they can support welfare claims.

## Implications for a future surgical prose pass

1. Keep the 3,000-fold and 6,500-fold lighting figures, but consistently attribute them as Fouquet and Pearson's estimates.
2. Keep Nordhaus's labor-cost series, but retain the manuscript's existing qualification that wages, historical prices, and device assumptions enter the reconstruction.
3. Use lighting as a bridge into rebound without implying that one elasticity governs all stages of adoption.
4. Preserve the hierarchy already present in the chapter: primary energy → final energy → useful work → energy service → welfare.
5. Do not let “energy becomes invisible” become “energy becomes irrelevant.” Modern LED systems reinforce the opposite point: the interface can get simpler while the physical and institutional stack underneath remains complex.

## Falsification / boundary test

The chapter's service-centered argument would weaken if quality-adjusted service measures failed to explain major technology transitions better than raw energy inputs, or if apparent service-cost declines proved to be artifacts of implausible assumptions about historical output or prices. The lighting literature does not show that. It does show that the exact magnitude of long-run improvement is reconstructed and that service demand changes with income, price, saturation, and technology.
