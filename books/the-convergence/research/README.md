# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

The Convergence asks whether energy is the most physically fundamental expression of human advance.

The strong form of the thesis — that more raw energy consumption simply means more progress — is too crude to survive contact with the evidence. The version worth testing is narrower and more interesting:

> Civilization advances as it becomes able to command more useful energy, at higher quality and with greater precision, reliability, reach, and efficiency, while reducing the human and environmental cost of obtaining each unit of useful work.

That formulation makes energy a substrate of capability rather than a moral score. It also creates measurable ways for the thesis to fail.

## What “advance” will mean in the book

Do not use a single proxy. Test the energy thesis against several dimensions:

- material capability: useful work, transport, industrial output, computation, heating, cooling, lighting, and communications
- economic capability: GDP per capita and productivity
- human capability: life expectancy, time freed from subsistence labor, mobility, access to information, and resilience
- system capability: reliability, controllability, storage, transmission, and the ability to move energy across time and space
- cost of capability: energy-service prices, conversion losses, capital intensity, pollution, land, materials, and risk

## Core empirical tests

1. **Long-run scale:** Does the rise of complex civilization track greater energy capture per person, total societal power, or both?
2. **Energy quality:** Does useful work or exergy explain economic growth better than raw primary-energy quantities?
3. **Energy services:** Have the real costs of light, motion, heat, and computation fallen as civilization improved conversion technologies?
4. **Electrification:** Is the present shift toward electricity a qualitative transition because electricity is a high-control carrier that can serve nearly every end use?
5. **Intelligence:** Does AI make computation visibly energy-bound again, turning electricity supply into a direct input to frontier cognition?
6. **Constraint migration:** As generation gets cheaper, do bottlenecks move into grids, storage, interconnection, permitting, materials, and firm power?
7. **Abundance:** If clean electricity, nuclear power, storage, and eventually fusion expand the energy frontier, which previously scarce capabilities become cheap enough to change civilization?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should actively look for evidence against the thesis.

- Mature economies can increase GDP while primary energy use stagnates or falls.
- Efficiency can reduce the amount of energy required for a given service; raw consumption can therefore understate progress.
- Energy access is unevenly distributed. A society can consume enormous energy while leaving large groups with little capability.
- External costs matter. Pollution, climate damage, land use, safety risk, and material extraction can turn additional energy into negative welfare.
- Energy is necessary for many capabilities but not sufficient. Institutions, knowledge, capital, law, trust, and coordination determine what energy can become.
- Different energy forms are not interchangeable. A joule of low-temperature heat, gasoline, electricity, and shaft work have different usefulness.
- Rebound effects can turn efficiency gains into higher total use; this is evidence about demand for services, not proof that efficiency failed.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Deep history

Use archaeological and historical work to trace the sequence from metabolic energy and fire through agriculture, draft animals, wind, water, coal, steam, oil, gas, and electricity. Keep reconstructed estimates separate from directly measured modern statistics.

### II. Energy and complexity

Start with Alfred Lotka’s 1922 energetics of evolution, Leslie White’s 1943 energy theory of culture, Kardashev’s 1964 power-based civilization scale, and later energy-rate-density work. Treat these as hypotheses and intellectual lineage, not settled laws.

### III. Energy and economic growth

Pair long-run GDP and population data with energy series. Give special attention to the useful-work/exergy literature because it challenges simple energy-input models while preserving a central role for energy services.

### IV. The modern electricity transition

Track total demand, electricity demand, generation mix, investment, renewable capacity, storage, grids, nuclear, and data centers. The key contemporary claim to test is whether electrification is causing energy to become more universal, controllable, and tightly coupled to frontier technologies.

### V. Frontier abundance

Separate demonstrated technologies from projections. Nuclear fission, batteries, solar, wind, geothermal, transmission, and demand flexibility belong in the empirical core. Fusion and space-based energy belong in a frontier section until sustained commercial performance exists.

## Planned figures

1. **Powering the United States, 1776–2025** — EIA historical primary-energy series by source.
2. **GDP per capita vs. energy-service capability** — Maddison Project plus long-run UK energy-service research.
3. **The changing quality of energy** — biomass → coal → oil/gas → electricity, with conversion efficiency and end-use examples.
4. **The Age of Electricity** — global electricity-demand growth vs. total energy-demand growth, 2014–2030.
5. **Where new energy capital goes** — global energy investment by fossil supply, clean supply, grids, storage, efficiency, and electrification.
6. **AI’s power curve** — data-center electricity use, 2014–2028/2035 scenarios.
7. **Low-carbon generation crossing the old system** — renewable and nuclear growth vs. fossil generation growth.
8. **The bottleneck moves** — generation additions compared with grid/interconnection buildout and storage.

## Source discipline

- Prefer original datasets, government or intergovernmental statistical releases, original journal papers, and laboratory reports.
- Use secondary histories to locate primary material, not as the sole support for quantitative claims when an underlying dataset exists.
- Record the vintage of every dataset. Do not silently combine projections with observations.
- Distinguish primary energy, final energy, electricity, exergy, and useful work.
- Preserve uncertainty ranges and scenario labels.
- Never quote a historical figure without verifying the original text or a reliable scan.
- For every major claim, keep a note titled “What would make this false?”
