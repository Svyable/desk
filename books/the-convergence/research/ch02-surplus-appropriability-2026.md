# Chapter 2 — Surplus, appropriability, and state formation

## Research question

How strong is the evidence that the physical properties of cereal crops helped make agricultural surplus easier for elites to appropriate, and how much weight should Chapter 2 place on that mechanism?

The chapter’s useful claim is narrower than “grain created the state.” Storage, visibility, divisibility, transportability, and harvest timing can make some forms of surplus easier to measure and seize. That mechanism is plausible and empirically interesting. The current literature does not justify treating it as a universal law of political development.

## The original appropriability result

Mayshar, Moav, and Pascali (2022) challenge the older surplus-productivity story in which farming raises land productivity, surplus follows, and tax-levying elites then emerge. Their alternative is that dependence on crops whose output is readily appropriable—especially cereals—matters more for hierarchy than land productivity by itself.

Their empirical work combines several datasets. One central test uses nearly 1,000 preindustrial societies from the Ethnographic Atlas and an instrumental-variable strategy based on the caloric-yield advantage of cereals relative to roots and tubers. The published paper reports a causal effect of cereal cultivation on jurisdictional hierarchy and no corresponding effect of land productivity. It also uses historical and geographic evidence beyond the Ethnographic Atlas.

The paper’s mechanism is directly relevant to Chapter 2 because it focuses on traits already present in the manuscript’s granary argument: cereal harvests are relatively visible in time, divisible, storable, transportable, and therefore potentially easier to assess and appropriate than crops that can remain underground and be harvested less conspicuously.

Source:

- Joram Mayshar, Omer Moav, and Luigi Pascali, “The Origin of the State: Land Productivity or Appropriability?”, *Journal of Political Economy* 130(4), published online March 8, 2022, DOI 10.1086/718372: https://doi.org/10.1086/718372

## The 2026 replication challenge

Cook, Duprey, Heyes, and Pelli published a direct comment in the *Journal of Political Economy* in 2026. Their critique targets the Ethnographic Atlas component of the original identification strategy rather than every piece of evidence in Mayshar et al.

They report two main results:

1. The cereal-appropriability relationship appears in the transition from tribe-chiefdom societies to states, not as a general relationship across all levels of jurisdictional hierarchy.
2. The instrumental-variable result is sensitive to a small number of extreme observations. In their reanalysis, statistical significance at the 10 percent level disappears after winsorizing 2.1 percent—or trimming 1.2 percent—of locations by cereal advantage.

The comment was published online May 8, 2026 and appears in the July 2026 issue of the journal. This is therefore not an informal objection or secondary summary; it is a published replication comment in the same journal as the original paper.

Source:

- Nikolai Cook, Thibaut Duprey, Anthony Heyes, and Martino Pelli, “The Origin of the State: Land Productivity or Appropriability? A Comment,” *Journal of Political Economy* 134(7), published online May 8, 2026, DOI 10.1086/740225: https://doi.org/10.1086/740225

## What survives the critique

The 2026 comment weakens the strongest reading of the original result. It does not show that crop appropriability is irrelevant.

Most importantly for the manuscript, Cook et al. still find the relationship in the institutional transition Chapter 2 cares most about: movement from tribe-chiefdom organization toward state organization. Their objection is that the evidence does not support a broader monotonic theory in which more cereal advantage predicts more hierarchy at every level.

The outlier sensitivity also matters. A mechanism can be plausible while a particular causal estimate is fragile. The chapter should therefore avoid language suggesting that the paper settled why states formed. The defensible use is as evidence that crop properties can alter the administrative and coercive possibilities surrounding stored surplus.

That distinction matches the manuscript’s own logic. A granary changes what can be counted, guarded, rationed, inherited, stolen, or taxed. None of those physical affordances decides whether the institution that emerges is communal insurance, household property, tribute, taxation, or something else.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest counterargument is not simply that one instrumental-variable estimate is fragile. It is that state formation is multiply determined and that “appropriability” can become an after-the-fact label for many features correlated with cereal agriculture.

Cereal-growing regions may differ in ecology, settlement density, transport, disease environment, military technology, labor organization, trade, and the returns to fixed infrastructure. Those variables can interact with crop choice and with political hierarchy. A broad cereal-versus-tuber contrast may therefore capture more than ease of seizure.

There is also a chronology problem for any simple causal story. Storage and cultivation appear long before states in some regions. Dhra′ itself is useful precisely because purpose-built storage predates fully domesticated cereals and appears in a social setting without palace-scale political architecture. The existence of a storable surplus is therefore not sufficient for state formation.

A better causal question is conditional: when a community already has concentrated production, settlement, and stored surplus, do crop properties change the cost of measuring and appropriating that surplus? The literature supports keeping that mechanism in play. It does not support making it the sole or universal explanation.

## Implication for the manuscript

Chapter 2 is already close to the right level of confidence. Its current language says that Mayshar et al. provide evidence “consistent with” the appropriability mechanism, then immediately presents the 2026 comment and narrows the conclusion to the ease of extraction rather than political destiny.

A future prose edit, if one is made at all, should preserve that structure. The best sentence-level emphasis is not that cereals caused states, but that the physical form of surplus can change the administrative price of coercion.

The chapter’s broader energy argument becomes stronger when it allows that distinction. Agriculture created more persistent and governable stocks of biological energy. Political systems then competed over what those stocks meant and who could claim them.

## What would make this false?

The appropriability mechanism should lose weight if stronger reanalyses show that cereal advantage has no robust relationship even with the tribe-chiefdom-to-state transition, or if comparable crop-property measures fail to predict differences in taxation, storage, administrative visibility, or hierarchy once settlement, ecology, transport, and other confounders are handled credibly.

Conversely, stronger evidence would require more than repeating the same cross-sectional association. It would show, prospectively or through cleaner historical variation, that changes in the visibility, storability, divisibility, or seizure cost of surplus alter the feasible scale of extraction while holding alternative mechanisms in view.
