# Chapter 3 — Coal, land constraints, and causality

## Research question

How strongly does the historical evidence support Chapter 3’s claim that coal relaxed a land-and-biomass constraint without becoming a sufficient explanation for the Industrial Revolution?

The chapter’s qualitative argument is good, but this is exactly the kind of thesis that can become too comfortable. An energy-centered history can point to Britain’s coal use after industrialization and then quietly treat correlation, physical possibility, and causation as the same thing. The strongest version of Chapter 3 should instead carry three pieces of evidence at once: coal represented a physically enormous escape from the annual biomass budget; proximity to coal became economically consequential after coal-using technologies diffused; and serious economic-history work still disputes whether Britain’s coal endowment itself explains much of the initial income gain.

That combination strengthens the chapter because it makes the causal claim narrower, not weaker.

## The land-counterfactual is physically large

E. A. Wrigley’s organic-economy argument gives the clearest physical interpretation of coal’s importance. In an economy relying heavily on contemporary photosynthesis, land supplies food, fodder, fibers, timber, and much industrial heat. Expanding one use therefore competes with others.

Wrigley’s often-cited calculation puts the energy in English coal output around 1800 at an amount that would have required roughly 11 million acres of woodland to supply from wood on a sustained-yield basis. England’s total land area was roughly 32 million acres. The point is not that Britain literally faced a choice between exactly those 11 million acres and coal; the calculation depends on assumptions about woodland yield, fuel substitution, conversion efficiency, and what forms of coal use wood could practically replace. Its value is order-of-magnitude. Fossil fuel allowed energy throughput to rise without requiring a proportional expansion of contemporary biological production.

This is the strongest physical evidence for the chapter’s “compression of time” idea. Coal did not create energy. It allowed accumulated geological stocks to be drawn down much faster than living landscapes could regenerate equivalent biomass.

Useful sources:

- E. A. Wrigley, *Energy and the English Industrial Revolution* (Cambridge University Press, 2010), especially the organic-economy framework and land-equivalent calculations.
- Alan Fernihough and Kevin Hjortshøj O’Rourke, NBER Working Paper 19802 (2014), which summarizes Wrigley’s calculation and the broader historiographic dispute before presenting its own empirical test: https://www.nber.org/papers/w19802

## Coal proximity appears to matter after coal-using technology becomes useful

Fernihough and O’Rourke provide an unusually useful causal test because they do not simply compare “coal countries” with “non-coal countries.” They use a panel of European city populations from 1300 to 1900 and exploit distance to coalfields, instrumenting coal proximity with proximity to Carboniferous-era rock strata.

Their published 2021 *Economic Journal* paper finds no relationship between coal proximity and city growth before 1750. After 1750, cities nearer coalfields grew substantially faster. Their counterfactual estimates attribute around 60 percent of European urban population growth from 1750 to 1900 to the coal effect under their specification.

The timing matters at least as much as the size. Coal lying underground was not automatically an economic advantage in 1400. Its value increased when technologies and industries capable of using large quantities of coal spread. That supports Chapter 3’s complementarity argument: geology becomes economically important through machinery, transport, demand, and institutions.

The 60 percent estimate should not be translated into “coal caused 60 percent of the Industrial Revolution.” The outcome is urban population growth, not GDP, productivity, welfare, or industrial output; the result depends on the identifying assumptions and spatial model. It is better used as evidence that coal proximity had a large post-1750 effect on urban development than as a universal causal share.

Source:

- Alan Fernihough and Kevin Hjortshøj O’Rourke (2021), “Coal and the European Industrial Revolution,” *The Economic Journal* 131(635), 1135–1149. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueaa117
- Earlier open working-paper version: https://www.nber.org/papers/w19802

## A serious countercase: coal may have followed demand more than it led growth

Gregory Clark and David Jacks provide the strongest direct challenge to a coal-centered explanation that should sit beside Wrigley rather than in a footnote. Their 2007 study reconstructs coal rents, pithead and market prices, and firewood prices for Britain between 1700 and 1869.

Their conclusion is deliberately uncomfortable for a simple energy thesis. They argue that coal output expanded during the Industrial Revolution mainly because demand rose, not because a major technological transformation suddenly made coal mining cheap. They further estimate that possession of English coal reserves made a negligible direct contribution to Industrial Revolution incomes through resource rents.

That does not refute the land-constraint argument. It attacks a different causal claim. Wrigley asks whether an organic energy system could indefinitely support industrial-scale throughput; Clark and Jacks ask how much Britain’s coal endowment and coal-mining productivity directly raised incomes during the observed transition. A resource can be physically enabling without generating a large measured rent contribution to GDP.

This distinction is worth making explicit in any later Chapter 3 prose revision. Otherwise “coal mattered” can slide between three different statements:

- coal relieved a physical biomass/land constraint;
- cheap coal changed the profitability and geography of particular technologies;
- coal endowment directly caused a large share of early British income growth.

The evidence is strongest for the first, substantial but context-dependent for the second, and contested for the third.

Source:

- Gregory Clark and David Jacks (2007), “Coal and the Industrial Revolution, 1700–1869,” *European Review of Economic History* 11(1), 39–72. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S1361491606001870

## Steam reinforces the delayed-complementarity story

Nicholas Crafts’s growth-accounting work remains a useful guardrail against an instant-Watt narrative. He finds that steam contributed little to British growth before 1830 and reached its peak impact roughly a century after Watt’s famous invention; high-pressure steam after 1850 was especially important in realizing the technology’s wider potential.

The chapter already uses Crafts well. The added causal point is that this pattern is consistent with coal’s value being mediated by complementary capital and networks. A cheap fuel stock and an efficient engine are not enough. Railways, steamships, factories, mines, iron, finance, repair skill, and demand have to accumulate before the macroeconomic effect becomes large.

Source:

- Nicholas Crafts (2004), “Steam as a General Purpose Technology: A Growth Accounting Perspective,” *The Economic Journal* 114(495), 338–351. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0297.2003.00200.x

## Current factual check in the chapter

The chapter says the official British historical coal series begins in 1853 and that the latest release extends through 2025. This remains correct. The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero dataset was updated on 30 July 2026 and is described as “Historical coal data: coal production, availability and consumption 1853 to 2025.”

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter’s likely thesis

The strongest objection is not that coal was unimportant. It is that an energy-centered explanation risks confusing an enabling condition with the cause of takeoff. Britain had coal before it industrialized. Other regions had coal without following the same trajectory. Demand, high wages, engineering knowledge, trade, institutions, capital, transport, and market size can explain why coal became valuable when and where it did.

Robert Allen’s induced-innovation account makes this especially concrete: eighteenth-century Britain combined unusually high wages with cheap energy, creating incentives to invent and adopt technologies that substituted capital and energy for labor. On that view, cheap coal is one price inside a larger economic configuration rather than an autonomous prime mover.

Source:

- Robert C. Allen (2011), “Why the Industrial Revolution was British: commerce, induced invention, and the scientific revolution,” *The Economic History Review* 64(2), 357–384. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0289.2010.00532.x

The defensible Chapter 3 claim is therefore conditional: coal relaxed a severe physical constraint and became increasingly valuable as complementary technologies and institutions made concentrated fossil energy useful. That is stronger than saying Britain industrialized because it had coal, and more historically interesting than treating energy as scenery.

## Suggested later manuscript move

A future surgical prose pass could add one compact paragraph after the existing discussion of land constraints. It should pair the Wrigley woodland-equivalent calculation with Clark and Jacks’s counterevidence, then let Fernihough and O’Rourke supply the bridge: coal proximity becomes strongly associated with urban growth only after coal-using technologies spread.

That would make the chapter’s causal structure visible in the evidence itself: **physical relief, economic complementarity, and contested macro causation are different claims.**
