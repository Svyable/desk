# Chapter 18 — The Last Mile Is a System: research and falsification note

## Purpose

This note supports the chapter’s claim that an electricity connection is a necessary but incomplete measure of energy capability. The chapter distinguishes binary access from service quality and uses current global access data plus the World Bank/ESMAP Multi-Tier Framework to keep reliability, affordability, capacity, safety, and clean cooking inside the definition of useful energy access.

## Primary evidence used

### Tracking SDG 7: The Energy Progress Report 2026

The 2026 report, produced by the IEA, IRENA, UN Statistics Division, World Bank, and WHO, estimates that 655 million people lacked access to electricity in 2024 and approximately two billion people still relied on polluting fuels and technologies for cooking. More than 560 million of those without electricity were in sub-Saharan Africa. The global electricity access rate remained around 92%, while progress slowed and the remaining deficit became increasingly concentrated among rural, low-income, remote, and fragile communities.

Book use: anchors the chapter’s global access claims and supports the argument that the remaining connection gap is geographically and economically harder than the earlier phases of electrification.

Limits: the SDG indicator for electricity access is intentionally broad and does not by itself establish reliability, affordability, or the quantity of electricity available to a household. The chapter therefore uses it as a minimum-access measure rather than evidence of full energy capability.

### World Bank/ESMAP, The Multi-Tier Framework for Energy Access: Insights and Impact from a Decade of Surveys (2026)

The report synthesizes 29 Multi-Tier Framework surveys across 27 countries conducted from 2016 through 2025. The framework replaces a binary connection metric with a six-tier approach that evaluates electricity and cooking access through attributes including capacity, availability, reliability, quality, affordability, legality, health, and safety. The 2026 synthesis reports a substantial divergence between physical connectivity and high-quality service in surveyed countries and identifies grid performance as an important second-generation access challenge.

Book use: supports the chapter’s central conceptual move from “connected/not connected” toward energy access as a spectrum of usable service.

Limits: MTF survey countries are not a statistically complete sample of all low- and middle-income countries. Tier thresholds are a measurement framework, not a claim that every household requires identical appliances or consumption. The chapter does not use MTF to rank human welfare by electricity use.

### World Bank/ESMAP, Global Electrification Platform 3.0

The Global Electrification Platform models least-cost electrification pathways using grid extension, mini-grids, and stand-alone solar rather than assuming one delivery architecture is universally optimal.

Book use: supports the chapter’s argument that centralized grid expansion and distributed systems can each be appropriate depending on settlement density, distance, expected demand, and local costs.

Limits: least-cost electrification models depend on assumptions about demand, technology costs, network costs, financing, and geography. Model-selected technologies do not remove the need for local engineering, institutional judgment, or future system integration.

## Distinctions preserved

- **Connection versus service.** A physical connection does not establish adequate duration, reliability, power quality, capacity, or affordability.
- **Basic access versus full capability.** A small amount of electricity can be transformative without supporting refrigeration, productive machinery, cooling, or other higher-power services.
- **Grid versus distributed access.** Grid extension, mini-grids, and stand-alone systems are delivery architectures with different strengths rather than ideological endpoints.
- **Supply cost versus household affordability.** Low generation cost does not eliminate connection fees, wiring, appliance costs, network charges, or irregular household income.
- **Electricity access versus clean cooking.** A household can have electric lighting while continuing to rely on polluting cooking fuels.
- **Energy use versus welfare.** The chapter does not treat higher household consumption as a universal measure of human value; it treats dependable service as a constraint on what activities are feasible.

## Counterevidence and uncertainty

Binary access metrics remain useful because they are comparable across countries and capture a severe first-order deprivation. The chapter should not imply that they are worthless merely because they are incomplete.

Entry-level distributed systems can deliver important benefits quickly, but they may cap users at lower service tiers if capacity cannot grow. Conversely, extending a large grid can be expensive and slow in remote regions yet provide a stronger path to future high-power demand when it is ultimately justified.

Affordability interventions can improve access but can also damage utility finances if subsidies are poorly targeted or unreimbursed. Prepaid systems can help households manage cash flow while making self-disconnection harder to distinguish from adequate service. The chapter keeps these tradeoffs open rather than prescribing one tariff model.

The 2026 access totals will be revised as underlying national survey and census data improve. Treat the headline counts as the best current global estimates, not exact enumerations of every unserved person.

## Falsification triggers for later revision

Revisit this chapter if:

- the 2026 SDG 7 access estimates are materially revised;
- newer MTF evidence shows physical connections converging rapidly toward high-tier service across surveyed low-income systems;
- distributed energy systems broadly demonstrate inexpensive progression to high-power, high-reliability service without later grid integration, strengthening the case for a more decentralized long-run architecture;
- empirical evidence shows connection affordability no longer materially limiting uptake where infrastructure is present;
- clean cooking access accelerates enough to make the chapter’s two-billion-person scale materially outdated.
