# Chapter 2 research note: cereal appropriability and state formation

## Claim under review

Chapter 2 argues that stored grain became unusually legible and extractable: it could be counted, guarded, divided, transported, and demanded, helping make taxation and hierarchy more practicable. That is plausible as a mechanism, but the strongest empirical version of the claim is now contested and should not be presented as settled.

## Evidence for the appropriability mechanism

Joram Mayshar, Omer Moav, and Luigi Pascali, in *The Origin of the State: Land Productivity or Appropriability?* (Journal of Political Economy, 2022), distinguish agricultural productivity from crop appropriability. Their argument is that cereals are comparatively visible, divisible, storable, and harvestable on a predictable schedule, making them easier for an elite to seize or tax than roots and tubers that can remain underground and be harvested less visibly.

Using several historical and ethnographic datasets, they report that relative suitability for cereals is associated with hierarchical institutions and state formation, whereas land productivity by itself does not produce the same result in their specifications. This is stronger evidence for Chapter 2's argument than a generic appeal to the "legibility" of grain because it attempts to distinguish the extraction mechanism from the simple surplus-productivity story.

Source: Joram Mayshar, Omer Moav, and Luigi Pascali, “The Origin of the State: Land Productivity or Appropriability?”, *Journal of Political Economy* 130(4), 2022. DOI: 10.1086/718372.

## Strongest current counterevidence

A 2026 published comment by Nikolai Cook, Thibaut Duprey, Anthony Heyes, and Martino Pelli re-examines the paper's Ethnographic Atlas identification strategy. They report two important qualifications. First, the appropriability result appears when distinguishing states from tribe-chiefdom societies, but not as a broad monotonic relationship across all levels of hierarchy. Second, the statistical significance of the instrumental-variable result is sensitive to a small number of extreme observations: in their analysis, modest winsorization or trimming removes conventional statistical significance.

That does not establish that cereal appropriability is irrelevant. It does mean the book should not write as though cereal properties are a demonstrated general cause of hierarchy everywhere agriculture appears.

Source: Nikolai Cook, Thibaut Duprey, Anthony Heyes, and Martino Pelli, “The Origin of the State: Land Productivity or Appropriability? A Comment,” *Journal of Political Economy* 134(7), 2026. DOI: 10.1086/740225.

## What would make the chapter's claim false?

The relevant claim would be substantially weakened if societies dependent on easily concealed or continuously harvestable crops developed comparable taxation and hierarchy at similar rates after controlling for other geographic and institutional differences; if cereal suitability stopped predicting state formation in better historical datasets; or if the Mayshar-Moav-Pascali result proved to be entirely driven by specification choice and outliers.

The archaeological record also prevents a deterministic story. Dhra′ provides evidence of sophisticated predomestication storage around 11,300–11,175 calibrated years before present, but the earliest granaries were not palace storehouses. Kuijt and Finlayson place them between residential structures and explicitly discuss the possibility of communal storage. Storage therefore predates the later political institutions that could appropriate it.

Source: Ian Kuijt and Bill Finlayson, “Evidence for food storage and predomestication granaries 11,000 years ago in the Jordan Valley,” *PNAS* 106(27), 2009. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.0812764106.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 2

Keep the chapter's concrete observation: a basket of grain is easier to count, store, guard, ration, and seize than many dispersed food resources. Treat that as a mechanism that can make extraction easier, not as a universal law of state formation.

A future manuscript pass should attribute the empirical appropriability argument to Mayshar, Moav, and Pascali, then immediately include the 2026 Cook et al. qualification. The strongest formulation is roughly: cereal agriculture may have made some early surpluses unusually appropriable, but the breadth and statistical robustness of that causal claim remain disputed. This preserves the chapter's larger point that energy storage changes institutional possibilities while respecting its own warning that physics does not select the politics.
