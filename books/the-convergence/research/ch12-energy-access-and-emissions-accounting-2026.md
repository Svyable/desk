# Chapter 12 — Energy Access and Emissions Accounting (2026)

## Why this note exists

Chapter 12 uses three contemporary facts to keep the book's final argument grounded: hundreds of millions of people still lack electricity, roughly two billion people still cook with polluting fuels and technologies, and fossil carbon dioxide emissions remain near record levels. Those facts are directionally strong, but each metric has a boundary that matters to the thesis.

The purpose of this note is to make those boundaries explicit so the conclusion can argue for energy as a physical substrate of capability without treating a binary connection, a tonne of carbon, or an aggregate global count as a complete measure of welfare.

## Current access figures

The 2026 edition of *Tracking SDG 7: The Energy Progress Report*, produced jointly by the IEA, IRENA, UN DESA Statistics Division, World Bank, and WHO, reports that in 2024:

- about 655 million people lacked access to electricity;
- the global electricity-access rate was about 92%;
- more than 560 million of the people without electricity lived in Sub-Saharan Africa;
- about two billion people used polluting fuels and technologies for cooking;
- about 970 million people in Sub-Saharan Africa lacked access to clean cooking.

Source: World Bank / SDG 7 custodian agencies, 24 June 2026: https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/06/16/accelerating-universal-energy-access

These figures support the manuscript's claim that the development gap remains physically embodied in electricity and cooking services. They do not support the stronger claim that everyone counted as having access receives reliable, affordable, high-quality energy.

## The strongest measurement counterargument

The World Bank's Multi-Tier Framework exists precisely because binary access statistics flatten important differences. Current World Bank/ESMAP terminology measures electricity access across seven attributes: capacity, availability, reliability, quality, affordability, formality, and health and safety. A household can therefore be counted as connected while still having an energy service too weak, intermittent, expensive, informal, or unsafe to support the capabilities the book cares about.

Source: World Bank / ESMAP, *Beyond Connections: Energy Access Redefined*: https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/energy/publication/energy-access-redefined

Terminology note: early MTF material used **duration** where later World Bank work uses **availability**, and older summaries sometimes described the connection-status dimension as **legal** access where current MTF publications use **formality**. The concepts substantially overlap, but manuscript revisions should use the current labels when citing current World Bank/ESMAP material.

Source: World Bank, *Measuring Energy Access in Household Surveys*: https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/lsms/priority-themes/survey-methods/energy

This is not a reason to discard the 655 million figure. It is a reason to treat it as a lower-bound style indicator of deprivation rather than a complete count of inadequate energy service. The manuscript's language is strongest when it moves from "access" to concrete service failures such as refrigeration, lighting, cooling, pumping, communications, and productive equipment.

A second counterpoint follows from the same framework: low electricity consumption is not automatically evidence of deprivation, because efficient appliances can provide more service with less energy. The relevant object is the service delivered under conditions of adequate reliability, quality, and affordability.

## Household air pollution

WHO's December 2025 household-air-pollution fact sheet estimates that around 2.1 billion people cook using open fires or inefficient stoves burning kerosene, biomass, or coal. WHO attributes an estimated 2.9 million premature deaths per year in 2021 to household air pollution.

Source: World Health Organization, 16 December 2025: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/household-air-pollution-and-health

This directly supports Chapter 12's claim that an energy problem can simultaneously be a poverty, infrastructure, and public-health problem. The causal wording should stay disciplined: WHO describes the burden as deaths attributable to exposure to household air pollution; the figure should not be written as if every death were caused by cooking fuel alone without the epidemiological attribution model in between.

The access and WHO numbers also come from different statistical products and should not be forced into false numerical consistency. *Tracking SDG 7* rounds the clean-cooking deficit to about two billion people, while WHO's health fact sheet says about 2.1 billion people use polluting cooking fuels and technologies. Both describe the same order of magnitude but serve different measurement purposes.

## Fossil carbon dioxide emissions

The peer-reviewed *Global Carbon Budget 2025*, published in *Earth System Science Data* on 13 May 2026, estimates 2025 fossil CO2 emissions at 10.4 gigatonnes of carbon, equivalent to 38.1 gigatonnes of CO2, about 1.0% above 2024. The 2025 value remains a projection based on available 2025 data rather than a fully consolidated final inventory.

Source: Friedlingstein et al., *Global Carbon Budget 2025*, *Earth System Science Data* 18 (2026): https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/3211/2026/

That distinction matters in the final chapter. "Projected at about 38.1 billion tonnes" is accurate. "Were 38.1 billion tonnes" would imply a degree of finality the source does not claim.

The same paper provides useful counterevidence to any simple story in which efficiency has already detached global prosperity from fossil carbon at the system level. The authors report that global fossil emissions still increased in 2025, even though the carbon intensity of energy has declined over the preceding decade. Improvements in energy intensity and carbon intensity have not yet been sufficient to offset growth in global energy demand.

That is a stronger and more interesting fit with the book's thesis than either triumphalism or despair: capability can become more efficient while total environmental pressure still rises because the scale of demanded services rises too.

## What Chapter 12 can safely claim

1. **Energy deprivation is still large and materially consequential.** The 2026 SDG 7 custodians estimate 655 million people without electricity access in 2024 and roughly two billion relying on polluting cooking fuels and technologies.
2. **Binary access is an incomplete capability metric.** Reliability, quality, availability, affordability, safety, formality, and usable capacity matter after a connection exists.
3. **Household energy quality has a measurable health burden.** WHO attributes about 2.9 million premature deaths in 2021 to household air pollution.
4. **The fossil era's external cost is not historical only.** The Global Carbon Budget estimates 2025 fossil CO2 emissions at a record-scale 38.1 GtCO2, still rising despite improvements in carbon intensity.
5. **These facts do not make raw energy consumption a welfare score.** They support the narrower claim that adequate, reliable, low-damage energy services remain physical prerequisites for many human capabilities.

## What would make the chapter's synthesis false or misleading?

The synthesis would be misleading if it implied that energy access alone explains differences in welfare, that a grid connection guarantees useful service, or that high national energy use proves broad human capability. It would also fail if it used global deprivation to excuse waste or environmental damage in already affluent systems.

The strongest counterargument is therefore not that energy does not matter. It is that after a minimum service threshold, variation in welfare can be dominated by institutions, distribution, public goods, housing, health systems, social trust, and other non-energy factors. Chapter 12 already acknowledges that boundary. The empirical discipline above makes the acknowledgement operational rather than rhetorical.

## Editorial implication

The current manuscript figures are supportable as written, with one useful interpretive guardrail: the 655 million electricity-access statistic should not be allowed to stand in for the larger population experiencing unreliable, unaffordable, or low-capacity service. If Chapter 12 is revised later, prefer language such as "still lacked even basic access to electricity" or immediately pair the binary statistic with the service-quality caveat.

For current MTF references, also prefer **availability** over the older label **duration**, and **formality** over **legality**, unless quoting or discussing the older framework terminology directly. That small vocabulary change keeps the book aligned with current World Bank usage without changing the underlying argument.

That change would strengthen, rather than weaken, the book's final claim. The thesis is about useful capability, and the Multi-Tier Framework shows why a wire reaching a household is not yet the same thing as useful capability arriving there.
