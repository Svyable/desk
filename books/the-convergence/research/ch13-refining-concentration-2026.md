# Chapter 13 — Refining concentration and supply-chain depth

## Purpose

This note strengthens Chapter 13's claim that material constraints often sit downstream of geology. The relevant scarcity may be not ore in the ground but the capacity to refine, separate, shape, qualify, and manufacture material into the form an industrial system can actually use.

## Current evidence

The International Energy Agency's *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* reports that refining concentration across key energy minerals reached a new high in 2025. The average share held by the leading refined supplier rose to about 70%, from 68% in 2020. The leading producers — Indonesia for nickel and China for most other key energy minerals — accounted for more than three-quarters of total supply growth between 2023 and 2025. In manganese, nickel, and graphite, nearly all recent supply growth came from the leading supplier.

That finding strengthens the chapter's distinction between geological abundance and industrial availability. A mineral can be mined in several jurisdictions while a later stage of the chain remains concentrated enough to create a practical bottleneck.

The IEA's project-level outlook makes the point more concretely. By 2035, announced rare-earth mining projects outside the leading producer could provide nearly 50 kt of capacity, but planned refining and separation capacity outside the leading refining country is under 40 kt, while downstream metals, alloys, and magnet capacity totals only about 18 kt on a rare-earth-content basis. The same upstream/downstream mismatch appears in lithium, graphite, nickel, and cobalt: diversification in mining can outrun diversification in the processing and manufacturing stages that convert mined material into factory inputs.

Book use: supports the Chapter 13 line that the important question is not whether civilization has enough atoms but whether it has adaptable supply chains. It also gives empirical weight to the warning that another mine in a friendly jurisdiction may not resolve a vulnerability located in refining or downstream manufacturing.

## Strongest counterevidence

Concentration is not destiny. The 2026 Outlook identifies rare-earth refining as an exception to the broader trend: new projects in the United States and higher production in Malaysia reduced concentration modestly between 2023 and 2025. That is evidence that targeted investment and policy can diversify a difficult supply chain rather than merely describe its vulnerability.

The Outlook also revised the projected 2035 copper supply gap downward, from about 30% in the previous year's analysis to about 25%, as new projects advanced, especially in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Zambia. This matters because it demonstrates that projected shortages are responsive to investment, project development, technology, and policy. A gap in one year's scenario is not a geological countdown clock.

A second caution is that concentration by itself does not establish fragility. A concentrated supplier can remain reliable for long periods, while a geographically diverse market can still be exposed to common technologies, shipping routes, financing conditions, or policy shocks. The relevant policy question is the consequence and recoverability of disruption, not the Herfindahl index in isolation.

## Claim boundary

The defensible Chapter 13 claim is therefore narrower than 'critical minerals are scarce.' It is that energy-system buildout can become constrained at whichever material-system stage is hardest to expand on the required timetable — mining, refining, separation, component manufacturing, qualification, transport, or permitting. Those stages can move independently, and successful diversification at one stage can expose concentration at the next.

This preserves the chapter's adaptive-system argument: markets, technology, recycling, substitution, and new projects can loosen constraints, but they do so with different lead times and cannot be assumed to arrive instantly.
