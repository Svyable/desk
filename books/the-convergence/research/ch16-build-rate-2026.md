# Chapter 16 — The Build Rate: research and falsification note

## Purpose

This note supports the chapter’s distinction between technological possibility and deployed infrastructure. The chapter argues that transition speed depends not only on equipment cost and performance but also on the rate at which manufacturing, finance, permitting, grid connection, construction, labor, and replacement cycles can turn designs into operating assets.

## Primary evidence used

### International Renewable Energy Agency, Renewable Capacity Statistics 2026

IRENA reports that global renewable power capacity reached 5,149 GW at the end of 2025 after 692 GW of additions during the year, a 15.5% increase. Solar accounted for the majority of the additions, with wind second. IRENA also reports strong geographic concentration: Asia accounted for most new capacity while additions in several other regions remained much smaller.

Book use: supports the chapter’s concrete claim that renewable deployment has reached a historically large annual build rate and the argument that global technology availability does not imply geographically uniform deployment capability.

Limits: installed nameplate capacity is not electricity generation, dependable capacity, or delivered energy. Different statistical institutions can report different annual addition totals because of technology scope, timing, revisions, and treatment of retirements. The chapter therefore attributes the 692 GW figure specifically to IRENA rather than presenting it as a universal accounting identity.

### International Energy Agency, Electricity 2026 — Grids

The IEA reports more than 2,500 GW of renewable generation, storage, and large-load projects stalled in grid connection queues worldwide. It estimates that planning, permitting, and completing grid infrastructure can take roughly 5–15 years, versus approximately 1–5 years for solar and wind projects and 1–3 years for data centres. It also estimates that annual grid investment would need to rise by roughly 50% from the current level of about USD 400 billion by 2030 to meet forecast electricity demand.

Book use: supports the chapter’s central example of mismatched infrastructure clocks and the claim that a faster generation or demand layer can run into a slower network layer.

Limits: queue capacity is not a forecast of completed projects. Many projects withdraw, overlap, change size, or are speculative. The reported build-time ranges are broad international comparisons, not fixed engineering constants. Faster permitting alone does not establish that equipment, labor, finance, or local acceptance are adequate.

### International Energy Agency, World Energy Investment 2026

The IEA estimates total global energy investment at approximately USD 3.4 trillion in 2026, about 5% above 2025, with clean-energy investment around USD 2.2 trillion. The report tracks ongoing capital spending rather than merely announced project values.

Book use: supports the distinction between financial mobilization and physical completion. Large capital flows can coexist with bottlenecks in equipment, skilled labor, permitting, interconnection, or institutional execution.

Limits: investment totals aggregate very different assets and regions. A dollar of spending cannot be converted mechanically into a fixed quantity of capacity, and higher expenditure can reflect higher equipment or financing costs rather than greater physical output.

## Distinctions preserved

- **Stock versus flow.** Installed infrastructure is a stock; annual construction and retirement are flows that change it.
- **Capacity versus generation.** Nameplate GW do not describe annual energy output or reliability contribution.
- **Technology cost versus system cost.** A cheap component can coexist with expensive connection, financing, land, storage, or network requirements.
- **Money versus productive capacity.** Capital is necessary for construction but does not instantly create factories, skilled workers, transformers, permitted corridors, or project-management capability.
- **Technical possibility versus buildability.** A technology can work in principle and still diffuse slowly because complementary institutions and supply chains are weak.
- **Global learning versus local deployment.** Tradable equipment can benefit from global scale while financing, permitting, grids, labor, and political risk remain strongly local.

## Counterevidence and uncertainty

The chapter could understate how quickly modular technologies can alter large stocks when manufacturing capacity compounds rapidly and replacement is accelerated by superior economics. Solar PV and batteries demonstrate that deployment curves can steepen dramatically rather than following a fixed turnover schedule.

It could also overstate institutional rigidity. Queue reform, grid-enhancing technologies, flexible connection agreements, standardized designs, digital permitting, new suppliers, and workforce expansion can raise throughput without waiting for entirely new physical networks.

Conversely, current record deployment does not guarantee continued acceleration. Trade restrictions, financing costs, local opposition, commodity constraints, factory overcapacity followed by consolidation, grid congestion, or policy reversal can slow build rates.

The chapter intentionally does not claim that faster is always better. Permitting and technical review serve legitimate safety, environmental, property, reliability, and democratic functions. The relevant objective is high-quality institutional throughput: decisions that are both timely and competent.

## Falsification triggers for later revision

Revisit this chapter if:

- authoritative revisions materially change IRENA’s 2025 renewable-capacity addition estimate;
- grid connection queues cease to be a meaningful constraint across major electricity markets;
- average grid development times fall enough that network infrastructure routinely scales as fast as the generation and large-load projects connecting to it;
- evidence shows capital availability, rather than physical and institutional throughput, becoming the dominant bottleneck across most major clean-energy sectors;
- modular manufacturing and deployment accelerate enough that the stock-flow distinction no longer explains observed transition lags in major energy systems.
