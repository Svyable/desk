# Chapter 7 — Long-run electrification baseline

## Why this note exists

Chapter 7 currently makes a well-supported contemporary claim: global electricity demand grew about 3% in 2025 while total energy demand grew 1.3%, and the load growth came from several sectors rather than one fashionable technology. The chapter also correctly warns that one year does not establish an age.

The missing test is the long run. If electricity is becoming a genuinely more general carrier, its share of final energy should have risen over decades, not merely in a strong recent year.

## Primary / authoritative evidence checked

### IEA, *Key World Energy Statistics 2021* — final consumption

Source: https://www.iea.org/reports/key-world-energy-statistics-2021/final-consumption

The IEA's historical world final-consumption breakdown reports electricity at **9.5% of world total final consumption in 1973**. The same table reports coal at 13.6%, oil at 48.5%, natural gas at 14.1%, biofuels and waste at 12.6%, and other sources at 1.7%.

Use: establishes a long-run baseline before modern EVs, heat pumps, hyperscale data centres, and mass deployment of wind and solar.

Caution: this is a 2021 statistical vintage. Do not imply that a 1973 reconstructed share and a 2025 estimate come from a single unchanged workbook or that tenths of a percentage point are meaningful across vintages.

### IEA, *Electricity 2026* — demand outlook

Source: https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/demand

The IEA puts electricity at about **21% of total final energy consumption in 2025** and projects **24% in 2030**. The report also records global electricity demand of about 28,200 TWh in 2025 and expects demand growth to average 3.6% per year from 2026 through 2030.

Use: the observed 2025 share is the appropriate modern comparison; the 2030 figure is a forecast and must remain labeled as such.

### IEA, *Global Energy Review 2026* — cross-check on the recent acceleration

Sources:
- https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/electricity-demand
- https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/global-trends

The IEA estimates that electricity demand rose around **3% in 2025**, adding roughly **800 TWh**, versus **1.3%** growth in total energy demand. Buildings contributed nearly 45% of the annual increase, transport more than 10%, and data-centre consumption rose about 17% (around 70 TWh of additional demand).

Use: supports the chapter's existing claim that the recent load growth is broad-based rather than reducible to AI or EVs alone.

## What the long-run comparison changes

On the IEA's long-run accounting, electricity's share of world final consumption has **more than doubled**, from 9.5% in 1973 to about 21% in 2025. That is strong evidence for a structural shift in the carrier used by end-use machines.

But the same comparison is also useful counterevidence against an overheated version of the thesis. The shift took more than half a century, and roughly four-fifths of final energy in 2025 was still not delivered as electricity. Aviation, shipping, feedstocks, high-temperature industrial processes, existing combustion equipment, infrastructure turnover, and local economics all slow or limit direct electrification.

The better claim is therefore not that society is rapidly replacing every molecule with an electron. It is that electricity has steadily gained share while becoming the common carrier for a widening set of valuable services, and that the pace has recently accelerated in several end-use sectors at once.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

A rising electricity share does not by itself prove that electricity is the deepest or inevitable endpoint of energy systems. The share could rise because economies become more service-heavy, because particular countries industrialize around grid power, or because accounting conventions make efficient electrification look especially favorable in final-energy terms. Direct combustion and molecular fuels retain physical advantages in several applications, and electricity's value remains conditional on generation, networks, reliability, price, and capital turnover.

A stronger falsification test for Chapter 7 is prospective: if electricity is truly becoming the preferred general-purpose carrier, its share of final consumption should keep rising across multiple sectors even when individual technologies or policy regimes disappoint. If the share stalls while recent demand growth proves concentrated in a narrow set of loads, the chapter's “age” language should be weakened.

## Suggested manuscript use

Place a short paragraph immediately after the sentence **“One year does not establish an age.”** The paragraph should do two jobs at once:

> Seen over half a century, the shift is both larger and slower. IEA statistics put electricity at 9.5 percent of world final energy consumption in 1973 and about 21 percent in 2025. More than doubling its share matters. Taking fifty-two years to do it is a useful antidote to any story in which the world simply wakes up electric.

Then return to the existing 2025 sector breakdown. This adds a durable baseline without interrupting the chapter's concrete opening or turning the section into a statistical catalogue.

## What would make this false?

- A revised, methodologically comparable IEA historical series materially changes the 1973-to-2025 direction or scale.
- Electricity's final-consumption share stops rising for a sustained period despite continued availability of electric substitutes.
- Recent growth is shown to be dominated by one temporary load category rather than broad adoption across buildings, transport, industry, and computation.
- System costs or reliability requirements make direct electrification systematically inferior to molecular alternatives across a much wider set of end uses than current evidence suggests.
