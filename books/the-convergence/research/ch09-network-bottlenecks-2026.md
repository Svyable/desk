# Chapter 9 — Network bottlenecks in 2026

## Claim under review

Chapter 9 asks whether networks, interconnection, transformers, firm capacity, and permitting are becoming the pacing constraints as generation technologies get cheaper and faster to deploy.

The strongest version of that claim is too broad. A large interconnection queue is not the same thing as a shortage of transmission, and neither is proof that the network is the binding constraint for every project. Many projects are speculative, duplicated, economically weak, or withdrawn for reasons that have little to do with wires. The useful claim is narrower: in the United States, several independently measured frictions now show that connecting and delivering electricity can take materially longer than procuring the generation or load equipment itself, and these frictions increasingly shape where and when projects can operate.

## Interconnection: the queue is huge, but attrition matters

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's *Queued Up: 2026 Edition* compiles transmission-interconnection data from all seven U.S. ISO/RTOs and 50 non-ISO utilities, representing about 98% of installed U.S. generating capacity. At the end of 2025, roughly 8,200 projects were actively seeking transmission interconnection: 1,312 GW of generation plus about 749 GW of storage, or a little over 2,060 GW in total.

That gross number is visually irresistible and analytically dangerous. Berkeley Lab also reports that only 13% of capacity entering queues from 2000 through 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025; 75% had withdrawn and 10% remained active. Queue capacity therefore measures developer interest and congestion in the process, not a forecast of what will be built.

The stronger evidence is time. Among regions with usable duration data, projects reaching commercial operation in 2025 had spent a median of more than five years between interconnection request and operation. Berkeley Lab notes that this duration has been rising. A project can own panels, turbines, batteries, servers, land, and financing while still waiting for studies, upgrades, agreements, or network capacity that determine whether those assets can actually exchange power with the grid.

The 2026 edition also records an important sign that reform has not yet produced a clean before-and-after result. FERC Order 2023 and related process changes are being implemented, but Berkeley Lab says it is still too early to assess their full impact. Chapter 9 should resist narrating today's queue as if no institutional response were underway.

Primary source: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, *Queued Up: 2026 Edition, Characteristics of Power Plants Seeking Transmission Interconnection As of the End of 2025* (2026): https://emp.lbl.gov/queues

## Transmission: congestion is concentrated, not universal

The U.S. Department of Energy released its draft *2026 National Transmission Needs Study* on July 9, 2026. DOE's central finding is not that the entire grid is uniformly saturated. It reports that most measured transmission congestion is concentrated in about 5% of hours, especially during periods of large day-ahead versus real-time price differences, high net load, cold weather, and high intermittent generation.

That concentration is narratively useful because it explains why a network can look adequate most of the year and still impose large reliability or cost penalties at particular times and places. It also prevents a sloppy inference from average utilization to system sufficiency. A road that flows freely at 3 a.m. can still be the constraint at 5 p.m.; transmission has its own version of rush hour, except that weather, plant outages, load shape, and power flows redraw the map.

DOE identifies substantial within-region and interregional needs associated with new generation, new load, congestion relief, reliability, and resilience. But the study remains a draft under public consultation, with comments due September 7, 2026. Its quantitative findings should therefore be cited as a current federal assessment, not as settled final results.

Primary source: U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Electricity, draft *2026 National Transmission Needs Study* (released July 9, 2026): https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-needs-study

## Transformers: a mundane component can set the schedule

The transformer case gives Chapter 9 a concrete object rather than another graph of gigawatts.

DOE's Office of Electricity reports that distribution-transformer lead times rose from roughly three to six months in 2019 to 12 to 30 months in 2023. In a March 5, 2026 DOE convening, officials gave more recent industry context: distribution-transformer demand had risen 41% since 2019; 2024 order lead times were commonly one to two years or longer; and large transformers used for substations and generators could face lead times of three to four years.

The same DOE work complicates a pure "not enough factories" explanation. Utilities collectively use tens of thousands of transformer configurations; DOE says it identified more than 80,000 distribution-transformer varieties nationwide. The agency's working group has therefore focused not only on manufacturing capacity but also on specification harmonization, interchangeability, procurement, and standardization. National Grid and manufacturers participating in the March 2026 convening described active SKU-reduction and factory-expansion efforts.

That is exactly the kind of systems point the book can use. A network bottleneck may be partly steel, copper, electrical steel, factory capacity, labor, and capital. It may also be the accumulated consequence of incompatible specifications and slow procurement rules. Physical scarcity and institutional design can occupy the same transformer.

## Strongest counterargument

Networks are not always the bottleneck, and the identity of the bottleneck can move quickly.

A project can die in the queue because its economics deteriorate, because equipment prices change, because a tax or permitting assumption changes, because a developer submitted multiple alternative sites, or because the customer disappears. A data center can be delayed by chips, cooling equipment, financing, zoning, water, or construction labor rather than electricity. A generator with an executed interconnection agreement can still fail to build. Conversely, new transmission or transformer capacity can be approved and then wait on local siting, procurement, or demand that never arrives.

The proper causal test is therefore project-specific: what item actually determines the earliest feasible operating date? If the answer is an interconnection study, a network upgrade, a substation, a transformer, or an energization date, the network is the pacing constraint in that case. If another prerequisite arrives later, it is not.

This distinction keeps Chapter 9 from turning a real infrastructure problem into a universal slogan.

## Recommended manuscript use

A strong opening scene would begin with a transformer or substation delivery date rather than with "the grid" in the abstract. The object is banal, heavy, standardized only imperfectly, and capable of deciding when a much more glamorous project can switch on.

From there, widen to the queue data, but pair every gross-capacity number with the attrition rate and the five-plus-year median duration for projects that actually reached operation in 2025. Then move from project interconnection to system transmission, using DOE's finding that congestion is concentrated in a small share of hours to show why network adequacy is a timing and topology problem rather than merely a question of annual energy totals.

The chapter's cleanest formulation is not "generation is cheap; wires are scarce." It is that mature energy systems increasingly compete on coordination time: the slowest necessary component, study, permit, upgrade, factory slot, or institution can determine when otherwise available technology becomes useful.

That argument also fits the book's broader voice and intellectual standard: begin with a physical object, make the quantitative claim specific, show the mechanism, and keep the disconfirming evidence beside it rather than after the fact.
