# Chapter 19 — Demand flexibility research note

## Core claim

The chapter argues that demand flexibility is a real power-system resource because shifting or briefly reducing electricity use can change peak capacity needs, congestion, renewable-curtailment exposure, and resilience. It does **not** argue that flexible demand replaces firm generation, transmission, or storage in all conditions.

## Primary evidence

The International Energy Agency's *Electricity 2026* flexibility analysis reports that only around 100 GW of demand response was utilised globally in 2024. It estimates roughly 75 GW in industry and around 30 GW in buildings, with transport still below 5 GW. It also notes that residential air conditioning contributes around 600 GW to global peak demand, while only a small share is currently used as demand response. The IEA explicitly distinguishes utilised demand response from economically viable, technically enabled, and theoretical potential; this distinction is central to the chapter's factual discipline.

The same IEA analysis describes the system value of demand response as reducing peak capacity requirements, deferring some grid investment, lowering renewable-integration costs, and strengthening resilience during stress. It also describes practical barriers: limited smart-meter and controllable-device penetration, regulatory restrictions on aggregation, weak price exposure, behavioural inertia, and the need for customer incentives and acceptance of automation.

The IEA's June 2026 *Scaling Up Demand Flexibility* report provides the broader institutional frame. It argues that electricity demand is rising while electrification and variable renewable generation increase the value of short-term flexibility. Its case studies emphasize that flexibility depends on digital technologies, consumer engagement, market rules, and regulation rather than on controllable devices alone.

## Falsification / counterevidence

The strongest falsifier would be evidence that real-world demand-response participation systematically fails to materialize at meaningful scale once comfort, industrial production, customer override, rebound effects, communications failures, and participation constraints are included. A second falsifier would be evidence that the marginal system cost of enabling, measuring, compensating, and administering flexible demand usually exceeds the avoided capacity, network, or balancing cost.

The chapter therefore avoids treating technical potential as dependable capacity. A load that can theoretically move is not necessarily contractible, measurable, dispatchable, or available during the system's highest-risk hours. Likewise, a load that can shift for minutes is not equivalent to a resource that can sustain a multi-day reliability event.

The chapter also preserves distributional counterevidence. Dynamic prices can reward customers who own flexible devices while exposing customers with rigid essential loads to higher costs. Low-income households may have less discretionary consumption to shift, renters may lack control over building equipment, and medically vulnerable customers may not be able to curtail cooling or heating safely. These constraints mean that flexibility policy requires consumer protection and cannot be evaluated only by aggregate megawatts.

## Boundaries on current facts

- The ~100 GW figure is the IEA's estimate of globally utilised demand response in 2024, not a measured census of every program.
- The ~600 GW air-conditioning figure is an estimated contribution to global peak demand, not available demand-response capacity.
- IEA projections and potential estimates are scenario- and assumption-dependent; the manuscript uses them to establish scale and mechanism, not certainty about future deployment.
- The chapter's examples involving water heaters, refrigeration, EV charging, and industrial scheduling are mechanism illustrations. They should not be read as claims that every device or process in those categories is flexible in every operating context.
