# Chapter 17 desalination: electricity dependence and the demand-side counterfactual

## Why this matters

Chapter 17 uses desalination as a clean example of a recurring thesis in the book: abundant energy can relax one physical constraint while making a different set of constraints more important. The existing manuscript is directionally sound, but its headline desalination statistics come from an older source frame. The IEA's March 22, 2026 analysis offers more current and more concrete evidence for the same argument.

## Current evidence

The IEA reports that reverse osmosis and other membrane technologies now account for more than 80% of installed desalination capacity globally. In the Middle East and North Africa, membrane systems account for more than 60% of capacity, and the region's last major new thermal desalination plant was added in 2018. The technology mix is therefore shifting toward electricity-driven separation rather than the older model in which large thermal desalination plants were tightly coupled to fossil-fuel heat and power.

Scale makes the electricity connection tangible. The average desalination plant is now roughly ten times larger than it was fifteen years ago. The largest plants can produce about 1 million cubic metres of water per day and can draw as much electricity as a city of roughly 200,000 households.

Looking forward, the IEA estimates that growth in desalination plus further electrification of the sector will add about 190 TWh to global electricity demand by 2035, roughly the annual electricity consumption of 60 million households. That is a stronger basis for Chapter 17's point than a generic count of plants: desalination is not merely spreading geographically; it is becoming a material electricity-system load in places where water security can depend on it.

The regional concentration also sharpens the infrastructure argument. The Middle East and North Africa still hold more than 40% of global desalination capacity, and the Gulf Cooperation Council countries alone account for about one-third. In some countries and island systems, municipal water supply depends almost entirely on desalination. Large desalination sites can therefore become critical infrastructure whose interruption is a water-security event, not just an industrial outage.

## Strongest counterargument

More energy supply is not necessarily the cheapest or most resilient way to create more usable water. The same IEA analysis contrasts two Australian responses during the Millennium Drought. Southeast Queensland emphasized demand-side measures and cut per-capita water use by about 40% over a decade. Perth expanded desalination capacity; that relieved water-system pressure, but doubled the energy intensity of its water supply while per-capita use fell only about 10%.

A primary-source check makes the Southeast Queensland case even more concrete. The Queensland Government's 2010 *South East Queensland Water Strategy* reports average residential consumption of 282 litres per person per day in 2004–05 and 143 litres in 2008–09. It separately reports average urban consumption in central Southeast Queensland and the Gold Coast falling from about 450 litres per person per day in 2005 to about 230 from mid-2007 to mid-2009. Those are different series and should not be spliced together, but both show that demand can move dramatically when scarcity becomes salient.

The causal claim needs restraint. These reductions occurred during an extreme drought alongside restrictions, appliance and retrofit programs, public campaigns, pricing and other demand-management measures. The government figures therefore demonstrate the scale of demand reduction achieved under a policy package; they do not isolate how much any single intervention caused. This is important disconfirming evidence for any version of the chapter's thesis that slides from "energy expands the feasible water set" to "more energy-intensive supply is the natural answer to water scarcity." Conservation, leakage reduction, reuse, storage, agricultural efficiency, pricing, and allocation can beat new supply on cost or resilience. Desalination is especially valuable where dependable high-quality water is worth its capital and energy cost; it is not a universal first resort.

There is also substantial technological counterevidence to a static energy-intensity story. Modern seawater reverse osmosis typically requires far less total energy than thermal desalination, and state-of-the-art plants can do better still. Electrification therefore raises electricity demand while simultaneously replacing more energy-intensive thermal processes. A claim about rising electricity dependence should not be mistaken for a claim that desalination is becoming less efficient.

## Implication for the manuscript

A stronger version of the Chapter 17 argument is:

> Desalination shows both sides of energy abundance. Better membranes and plentiful electricity can turn seawater into dependable municipal supply at a scale that was once impractical. But the resulting water system inherits new dependencies on grids, coastal infrastructure, capital, maintenance, and brine management. And sometimes the cheapest cubic metre is the one a city learns not to demand.

The useful distinction is between **technical capability** and **system choice**. Cheap, reliable electricity expands the menu. It does not decide which item should be ordered.
