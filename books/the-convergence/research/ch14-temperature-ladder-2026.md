# Chapter 14 — The Temperature Ladder: research and falsification note

## Purpose

This note supports the chapter’s distinction between low-, medium-, and high-temperature industrial heat, and between heat supplied as energy and fuels/feedstocks that also perform chemical work. The chapter’s argument is intentionally narrower than “industry can be electrified”: different processes have different temperature, chemistry, capital-stock, reliability, and infrastructure constraints.

## Primary evidence used

### U.S. Department of Energy, Industrial Decarbonization Roadmap (2022)

DOE’s roadmap reports that process heating was the largest U.S. manufacturing end use in 2018, consuming 7,576 TBtu of fuel, steam, and electricity, or 51% of onsite manufacturing energy. It also reports 360 million metric tons CO2e associated with process heating, or 31% of manufacturing energy-related greenhouse-gas emissions. The roadmap emphasizes that applicability of electrification technologies depends on temperature range and process, and identifies electrification, low-carbon fuels/feedstocks, carbon capture, and efficiency as distinct industrial decarbonization pillars.

Book use: establishes the scale and heterogeneity of industrial process heat and supports the claim that a large thermal load cannot be treated as a single technology category.

Limits: the figures are U.S.-specific and based on 2018 manufacturing data. They should not be presented as current global shares. The roadmap is a government technology strategy, not an ex-post evaluation proving that its preferred pathways will reach stated goals.

### International Energy Agency, Heat Pump Monitor 2026

The IEA reports that industrial heat-pump deployment remains limited but that commercially available systems could technically supply up to around 20% of global industrial heat demand, concentrated in low- and medium-temperature processes. The report also stresses that industrial installations are less standardized than building heat pumps, face long-lived incumbent assets, require integration with production systems, and may not always be economical without supportive conditions.

Book use: supports the chapter’s claim that low- and medium-temperature industrial heat offers substantial but bounded electrification potential, and that technical potential should not be confused with adoption.

Limits: scenario shares are conditional on assumptions about policy, technology, electricity prices, and investment. “Technically supply” is not equivalent to “cost-effectively replace” or “will be deployed.”

## Key distinctions preserved

- **Heat versus chemistry.** In blast-furnace ironmaking, carbon is not only a heat source; it participates in reduction chemistry. In cement, switching kiln heat away from fossil combustion does not eliminate CO2 released by limestone calcination.
- **Technical feasibility versus economic adoption.** A technology can work physically and still fail to deploy because of capital cost, downtime, electricity prices, grid upgrades, reliability, financing, or equipment replacement cycles.
- **Greenfield versus brownfield.** New plants can be designed around electrical infrastructure from the start. Existing plants inherit boilers, furnaces, substations, piping, controls, and staff practices that raise conversion costs.
- **Energy carrier versus infrastructure.** Electrification transfers demand from fuel-delivery networks to generation, transmission, distribution, transformers, and onsite electrical systems.
- **Demonstration versus diffusion.** A successful pilot shows operation under specified conditions; it does not establish mass adoption, cost convergence, maintainability, or supply-chain readiness.

## Counterevidence and ways the chapter could be wrong

The chapter would be too pessimistic if industrial electrical technologies standardize and fall in cost much faster than current integration practice suggests, or if cheap clean electricity makes early retirement of fossil equipment routinely economical. It would also be too pessimistic if higher-temperature heat pumps expand their practical envelope quickly enough to cover far more demand than current commercial systems.

The chapter would be too optimistic if grid buildout becomes the binding constraint for a large fraction of industrial electrification, if clean-electricity prices remain structurally high for industrial users, if high-temperature electric equipment proves less reliable at scale than demonstrations suggest, or if firms delay investment because policy and product-demand uncertainty dominates operating savings.

The hydrogen discussion should remain bounded. Hydrogen made by electrolysis is useful where chemical properties, storability, or very high-temperature service justify conversion losses; the chapter does not claim hydrogen is broadly preferable to direct electrification.

The cement discussion must preserve process emissions. Electrifying heat does not remove calcination CO2. The steel discussion must distinguish scrap-based electric arc furnaces from primary ironmaking; EAF deployment does not by itself eliminate the need for a low-emissions primary iron route where scrap supply or quality is insufficient.

## Falsification triggers for later revision

Revisit this chapter if:

- authoritative evidence shows commercial industrial heat pumps routinely and economically serving temperatures materially above the range described here;
- a major industrial sector adopts direct electrification at scale without the grid and retrofit constraints emphasized in this chapter;
- evidence shows industrial thermal storage becoming a standardized, low-cost substitute for firm electricity in continuous high-temperature processes;
- primary steel or cement production shifts enough that the chemistry-versus-heat distinction needs a different representative example;
- newer manufacturing-energy data materially changes the process-heat share used from DOE’s 2018 baseline.
