# The Temperature Ladder

A steel mill and a dairy plant can both be described as factories that need heat.

That description is true in the way that saying a canoe and a container ship both need propulsion is true.

The temperature matters.

So does the material being heated, the speed of the process, the pressure, the atmosphere around it, the shape of the equipment, whether the heat must be uniform or concentrated, whether it arrives continuously or in bursts, and what happens if the process stops halfway through a batch. Industrial heat is not one problem. It is a ladder of problems, and the higher the temperature climbs, the more the engineering changes.

This is where a simple story about electrification begins to fray.

For many uses of energy, electricity is extraordinarily good at replacing combustion. An electric motor converts electrical energy into motion efficiently and with fine control. A heat pump can move more heat into a building than the electrical energy it consumes because it is transferring heat rather than creating all of it from scratch. Resistance heating can turn electricity into heat nearly wherever a conductor can be placed. Induction can heat metals directly. Electric arcs can reach temperatures that make ordinary flames seem modest.

The physical possibility is broad.

The economic and industrial possibility is narrower.

That difference matters because the argument for energy abundance can otherwise become too easy. If clean electricity gets cheap enough, one might imagine that every furnace, kiln, boiler, dryer, reactor, and refinery simply plugs in. The fuel pipe disappears. The emissions fall. Industry becomes another branch circuit.

Some of it can.

Some of it cannot, at least not by replacing a burner with a wire and leaving everything else untouched.

The United States Department of Energy’s Industrial Decarbonization Roadmap offers a useful scale for the issue. Using 2018 manufacturing data, it estimated that process heating accounted for roughly half of onsite manufacturing energy use. That number is not a timeless global constant. It is a snapshot of one industrial economy. But it captures the size of the category: making things often means heating things.

Food is dried, cooked, pasteurized, evaporated, and sterilized. Paper is dried. Chemicals are distilled and reacted. Metals are melted, forged, annealed, reduced, and heat-treated. Glass is melted. Cement raw meal is heated until mineral transformations occur. Refineries separate mixtures through heat and pressure. Steam moves through factories because it is an old, flexible way to transport thermal energy from one place to many.

The word heat hides all of this variety.

At the lower rungs of the temperature ladder, the opportunities for electrification are already substantial.

The International Energy Agency’s 2026 Heat Pump Monitor estimates that commercially available heat pumps could technically supply around a fifth of industrial heat demand globally, concentrated in low- and medium-temperature processes. That is a technical potential, not a forecast of adoption. Installed industrial heat-pump capacity remains small relative to the size of industrial heat demand, and integration into existing plants can be difficult. The IEA’s own scenarios diverge sharply depending on policy, electricity prices, investment, and innovation.

That qualification is the interesting part.

A heat pump in a house can sometimes be installed as a piece of equipment. A heat pump in a factory can become part of the process architecture.

A food plant may have a warm wastewater stream leaving one stage while another stage requires moderate-temperature heat. A well-designed heat-pump system can upgrade heat that would otherwise be rejected and send it back into the process. The opportunity is not simply to buy electricity instead of gas. It is to redesign the flow of energy through the plant.

This can produce gains that a fuel-switching spreadsheet misses.

When natural gas enters a boiler, the plant buys chemical energy, burns it, and turns some of that energy into useful steam. When a heat pump recovers low-grade waste heat and raises its temperature, the plant may reduce both fuel consumption and the amount of heat it has to throw away. The more carefully the process is integrated, the less useful it becomes to ask which single fuel replaced which other fuel.

The unit of improvement becomes the system.

That same systems logic helps explain why cheap electricity is powerful without being magical.

Suppose electricity becomes inexpensive enough that an electric boiler can compete with a gas boiler on energy cost. That does not mean the plant will switch the next morning. A boiler can last for decades. Replacing it may require downtime, electrical upgrades, new transformers, permits, engineering studies, space, capital approval, and confidence that the electricity price advantage will persist. A factory manager is not buying an abstract stream of joules. The manager is altering a production asset whose failure can stop revenue.

Industrial equipment is full of this kind of memory.

A plant built around steam develops pipes, controls, maintenance knowledge, spare-parts inventories, operating procedures, and staff expertise around steam. A plant built around gas develops another set of dependencies. The fuel is embedded in equipment and organization at the same time.

This is why turnover matters.

A transition that looks slow from year to year can become rapid when large classes of equipment reach replacement age. The reverse is also true. A technology can be economically attractive on paper and spread slowly because the incumbent equipment still works.

The path of least resistance is often not conversion.

It is waiting.

That becomes harder as the temperature rises.

Low-temperature heat is unusually friendly to heat pumps because heat pumps gain their advantage from moving heat across a temperature difference. As the required temperature increases, the engineering becomes more demanding. Materials face harsher conditions. Refrigerants and compressor designs become more constrained. Efficiency changes. The available source temperature matters more.

The boundary is moving. Industrial heat pumps have been extending into higher temperature ranges, and the IEA’s recent analysis treats temperatures up to around 150 degrees Celsius as commercially addressable in some applications. But a cement kiln is not a 150-degree problem.

Neither is primary steelmaking.

At higher temperatures, direct electrical methods become more important.

But temperature is not a ranking of difficulty.

A process can sit high on the ladder and still be unusually well suited to electricity. An electric arc does not struggle to become hot enough to melt steel scrap. Induction can put energy directly into conductive material. Plasma and other electrothermal methods can deliver very high temperatures without asking an electric boiler to impersonate a flame. In those cases, the temperature itself may be less troublesome than the geometry of the equipment, the quality requirements of the product, the available electrical connection, and the economics of replacing what is already there.

The reverse can happen lower down. A modest-temperature process may be technically easy to electrify and commercially awkward because the plant is built around a steam network, because the retrofit would require a long shutdown, because the local grid cannot supply the new load, or because the electricity tariff destroys the operating-cost advantage. The physical ladder still matters. It narrows some technology choices. It does not tell you, by itself, which project is easiest.

The more useful question is not simply how hot the process gets. It is what the heat is doing, how it reaches the material, what else the fuel is doing, and what must change around the equipment for a new energy carrier to become useful.

Resistance heating is conceptually simple: pass current through a resistive element or material and it gets hot. Induction uses electromagnetic fields to generate heat inside conductive materials. Electric arc furnaces strike an arc between electrodes and material, producing intense heat that can melt steel scrap. Infrared, microwave, radio-frequency, plasma, and other electrothermal methods can be useful where the process benefits from heating a material directly rather than warming a large volume of combustion gas first.

These are not merely cleaner versions of fire.

They can change the geometry of heat.

A flame usually transfers heat from combustion products to a surface and then through a material. Electromagnetic methods can deposit energy differently. Induction can heat a metal workpiece without first heating a furnace chamber to the same degree. Microwave energy can interact with some materials volumetrically. An electric arc can concentrate immense power in a small region.

That control can be valuable even when carbon is not the first concern.

A manufacturer may care about product quality, speed, yield, atmosphere control, maintenance, or the ability to turn a process on and off quickly. Electrification sometimes wins because the process gets better.

This is a recurring pattern in energy history.

New energy carriers spread fastest when they do more than replace old energy unit for unit.

Electric motors did not merely reproduce the steam engine at a smaller scale. They allowed factories to reorganize machines around individual drives. Electric light did not merely reproduce a gas flame without the pipe. It changed safety, control, and where light could be used. The strongest case for industrial electrification may likewise come from processes redesigned around the properties of electricity rather than from electrically imitating a burner.

But the ladder has another complication.

In some industries, fuel is not only a source of heat.

It is a chemical participant.

Steel is the clearest example.

Traditional blast-furnace ironmaking uses coke not merely because coke burns hot. Carbon helps remove oxygen from iron ore. If the carbon disappears, the process needs another reducing agent or an entirely different route. This is why hydrogen-based direct reduction attracts attention: hydrogen can perform part of the chemical work that carbon performs, producing water rather than carbon dioxide at the reduction stage if the hydrogen itself is produced with low emissions.

But hydrogen is not electricity in a bottle.

Producing it by electrolysis consumes electricity. Compressing, storing, transporting, and handling it introduces additional equipment and losses. Direct use of electricity is generally preferable where it can provide the desired service economically because each conversion step has a cost. Hydrogen becomes interesting where its chemical properties or storability solve a problem that direct electrons do not solve well.

That is a narrower and more useful claim than calling hydrogen the fuel of the future.

The same discipline applies to cement.

A cement kiln requires high-temperature heat, but heat is only part of the emissions problem. Calcination releases carbon dioxide from limestone as part of the chemistry that produces clinker. An electrically heated kiln can eliminate combustion emissions if the electricity is low-carbon. It does not eliminate the process emissions from the limestone itself.

This distinction is easy to lose because both sources of carbon dioxide leave the same site.

One is energy emissions.

The other is chemistry.

Cheap clean electricity attacks the first directly. The second requires changes such as lower-clinker cement, alternative chemistries, carbon capture, material efficiency, or combinations of them.

The lesson is not that electrification fails.

It is that the boundary of the energy problem must be drawn correctly.

A process can be energy-intensive without all of its emissions being caused by energy. A process can use a combustible fuel without combustion being the only reason the fuel is there. A plant can technically electrify while remaining uneconomic because grid capacity, equipment cost, or downtime dominates the decision.

Once those distinctions are made, the industrial transition looks less like one giant fuel switch and more like a portfolio of engineering problems.

That is good news.

One universal solution would create one universal failure mode.

A portfolio allows different processes to use different advantages.

Low-temperature heat pumps can recover waste heat. Electric boilers can serve steam loads where electricity is competitive. Thermal storage can separate the moment electricity is bought from the moment heat is needed. Induction can serve appropriate metal processes. Electric arc furnaces can melt scrap. Hydrogen can serve some chemical and high-temperature roles. Nuclear heat, geothermal heat, concentrated solar heat, biomass, or fossil fuels with carbon capture may remain relevant in particular places and industries.

The right mix will not be the same everywhere.

An aluminum smelter near abundant hydropower faces a different energy system from a chemical plant beside a gas field. A food processor with large low-temperature loads is not a cement plant. A new factory in a region with spare grid capacity is not an old factory whose substation was sized for lights and motors rather than electric furnaces.

Geography remains inside the machine.

So does time.

A common mistake in transition analysis is to compare the mature cost of a new technology with the average cost of an existing one and declare a winner.

Factories do not replace themselves at average cost.

They make marginal decisions at specific moments.

If a furnace is already paid for and has fifteen years of expected life, the relevant comparison includes the cost of retiring it early. If the furnace fails and must be replaced anyway, the comparison changes. If a company is building a new plant, the ability to design the entire electrical system from the start changes it again.

Brownfield and greenfield are different worlds.

This matters especially for industries with long-lived capital.

A household water heater is not cheap to replace, but a steel mill, cement kiln, cracker, or refinery unit can embody hundreds of millions or billions of dollars and sit inside a network of dependent equipment. Industrial decarbonization therefore has a calendar. Miss a replacement window and the next one may be years away.

Policy can help or hurt by changing how predictable that calendar feels.

A company considering electrification must form views about future electricity prices, grid reliability, carbon rules, fuel prices, equipment availability, product demand, and the durability of subsidies or standards. If those signals change wildly, the rational response may be to postpone irreversible investment.

Uncertainty has an energy cost.

Not because electrons care about politics, but because capital does.

This is one reason industrial policy often ends up looking less elegant than a carbon-price diagram. Demonstration plants, loan guarantees, contracts, tax credits, procurement rules, infrastructure grants, technical standards, and public research programs all try to solve different parts of the investment problem. Some are useful. Some become expensive ways to protect weak projects. The hard part is distinguishing the two before the money is gone.

Technology demonstrations deserve particular caution.

A demonstration can prove that a process operates.

It does not prove that thousands of plants will adopt it.

Industrial history is full of technologies that worked technically but failed on cost, maintenance, integration, reliability, financing, or supply chain. Scale changes things. A system that performs beautifully with a dedicated engineering team can disappoint when handed to an ordinary plant that has to make product every day.

The opposite also happens. A technology dismissed as niche can become ordinary once manufacturers standardize it, installers learn it, financing improves, and equipment gets cheaper through repetition.

The future cost curve is partly an organizational curve.

Industrial heat pumps show this clearly. Their components are not science fiction. The obstacle is often the engineering required to integrate them into real facilities. Every successful installation can expand the pool of people who know how to size, install, operate, and maintain the next one. Standardization can turn a custom project into a product category.

That is how infrastructure becomes invisible.

First it is a specialist intervention.

Then it becomes a line item.

The grid may be the decisive constraint on how quickly that happens.

Electrifying a large thermal load can multiply a factory’s electricity demand. A site that once received most of its energy through a gas pipeline may suddenly need much more electrical capacity. The plant can buy new equipment and still be unable to run it if the local distribution network, transmission system, substation, or transformer supply cannot support the load.

This connects the temperature ladder back to the network constraint.

Electrification moves energy through a different infrastructure.

A gas pipeline and an electric grid are not interchangeable pipes. They have different capacities, balancing requirements, storage options, capital costs, failure modes, and political histories. Moving industrial heat onto the grid can reduce direct fossil-fuel use while increasing the value of transmission, generation, flexibility, and local distribution upgrades.

The plant gets cleaner.

The system gets more electrical.

Those are not the same statement.

Thermal storage offers one bridge between them.

Heat is often easier to store than electricity. Hot water, steam accumulators, molten salts, refractory bricks, phase-change materials, and other thermal stores can absorb energy when electricity is cheap or abundant and release heat when the process needs it. In some industrial applications, this can allow a factory to behave less like a rigid electrical load and more like a flexible one.

That flexibility can matter enormously in a grid with variable wind and solar output.

If a process can preheat material, charge a thermal store, or shift part of its demand by a few hours without harming production, cheap electricity can become more valuable than a simple annual average price suggests. The factory can consume when the system wants consumption.

But flexibility is not free either.

Storage equipment costs money. Heat leaks. Space is finite. Some processes cannot pause. A glass furnace may operate continuously for reasons far deeper than the hourly power price. Food safety, chemical stability, product quality, labor schedules, and contractual delivery obligations can all limit how much a plant can dance with the grid.

Demand flexibility is a property of a process, not a moral virtue.

The same should be said about efficiency.

A highly efficient gas process can sometimes use less primary energy than a poorly designed electric replacement, especially if the electricity is generated inefficiently from fossil fuels. In a cleaner grid, the emissions comparison changes. With a heat pump, the efficiency comparison can change dramatically again. With direct renewable electricity, primary-energy accounting itself becomes tricky because there is no thermal conversion loss at a power plant to count in the same way.

Metrics that were useful in the fossil system can become misleading during the transition.

What matters is the service delivered, the resources consumed, the emissions produced across relevant boundaries, and the constraints imposed on the rest of the system.

There is another reason to resist simple accounting.

Industrial energy is entangled with trade.

If one country electrifies steel production while another continues to use coal, the first may reduce domestic emissions and raise production costs while importing more steel from the second. Whether global emissions fall depends on what actually changes, not where the accounting line is drawn. Border measures, product standards, green procurement, and low-carbon material markets are attempts to prevent climate policy from becoming a relocation policy.

They also create opportunities for industrial competition.

If low-emissions steel, cement, chemicals, fertilizers, and other materials acquire durable markets, firms that learn to produce them cheaply can gain an advantage. If those markets depend entirely on temporary policy premiums, investment may remain fragile.

The cheapest clean electron in the world is only valuable to industry if a business can build a production system around it.

That sentence contains most of this chapter.

Energy abundance can make industrial decarbonization much easier. It can support direct electrification, heat pumps, hydrogen production, electrochemistry, carbon capture, recycling, desalination, synthetic fuels, and processes that are currently too energy-intensive to compete.

But the abundance has to arrive in the right form.

At the right place.

With the right reliability.

At a price industrial users can trust.

And it has to meet equipment capable of using it.

The temperature ladder makes this visible because each rung asks a different question. At low temperatures, how much waste heat can be recovered? At moderate temperatures, can steam and hot-water systems be electrified economically? At high temperatures, which direct electrical technologies fit the material and process? Where fuel is also a feedstock, what chemical substitute is required? Where emissions come from process chemistry, what remains after the energy source changes?

There is no single answer because there is no single furnace.

This is not a weakness in the energy thesis.

It is the thesis becoming more precise.

Civilization advances not merely by acquiring more energy, but by learning to deliver the right kind of energy to the right transformation with less waste, lower cost, and fewer unwanted consequences.

Fire was powerful because it turned chemical bonds into heat wherever fuel could be carried.

Electricity is powerful because it can become motion, light, computation, heat, or chemical change with extraordinary control.

The industrial transition will be the place where those two forms of power negotiate their boundary most visibly.

The boundary will move.

It will not vanish.
