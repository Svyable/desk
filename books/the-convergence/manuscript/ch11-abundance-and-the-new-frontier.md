# Abundance and the New Frontier

In April 2025, a target the size of a peppercorn sat inside a chamber at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory and briefly did something no power plant on Earth can yet do.

It produced a fusion yield of 8.6 megajoules after 2.08 megajoules of laser energy struck the target. The experiment set a record target gain above four. By June 2026, the National Ignition Facility had achieved ignition eleven times.

That is a remarkable scientific result.

It is also a useful place to begin a chapter about abundance, because almost every word in the previous paragraph can be misunderstood.

The target produced more fusion energy than the laser energy delivered to it. The facility did not produce net electricity. The lasers themselves require far more energy than reaches the target. A commercial plant would also need to fire repeatedly, manufacture and inject targets cheaply, capture the released energy, survive neutron bombardment, maintain its machinery, and sell power often enough to recover its capital cost.

The distance between a successful experiment and an ordinary energy service is the subject of this chapter.

We have reached a peculiar moment in energy history. Some technologies that looked exotic a generation ago are now commodities. Others are crossing from laboratory to field demonstration. Still others have crossed an important scientific threshold while remaining nowhere near commercial routine.

They are often discussed together under one word: abundance.

The word is attractive because it gestures toward a world in which energy stops being the first question asked of every ambitious physical project. Desalinate more water. Produce fertilizer without natural gas. Remove carbon dioxide from the air. Make synthetic fuels. Heat industrial processes. Run more computation. Manufacture materials with fewer compromises imposed by fuel cost. Perhaps, eventually, do things we have not bothered to imagine because they are currently absurdly expensive.

There is a serious idea inside that vision.

There is also a category error waiting to happen.

Cheap energy is not the same thing as a cheap civilization.

A kilowatt-hour is one input into a desalination plant, a steel mill, a data center, a direct-air-capture system, or an electrolyzer. Each machine also needs capital, land, materials, equipment, permits, skilled people, maintenance, supply chains, and some way to move its output to a customer. If electricity became free tomorrow, none of those things would become free with it.

The frontier therefore matters less as a parade of miraculous machines than as a test of how constraints move.

Solar power offers the clearest example of a technology that has already moved much farther than the rhetoric surrounding it sometimes acknowledges.

In 2025, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the global weighted-average cost of electricity from newly commissioned utility-scale solar photovoltaic projects was about $44 per megawatt-hour. More than 90 percent of new utility-scale renewable projects commissioned that year produced electricity below the cost of the cheapest new fossil-fuel alternative in their market.

That result would have sounded implausible when modern photovoltaic manufacturing was young.

The interesting part is not simply that solar cells improved. Researchers examining the history of photovoltaic costs have found that the decline came from a mixture of factors: higher module efficiency, larger manufacturing plants, improvements in silicon use, process innovation, economies of scale, and learning throughout the supply chain.

A solar panel became cheap through thousands of improvements that gradually made the object less special.

That ordinariness is what technological abundance looks like in practice.

Not infinite energy. Not free energy. Repetition.

A photovoltaic module can be manufactured by the millions, shipped in a container, bolted to a rack, wired to standardized electrical equipment, and financed using assumptions grounded in a large operating history. The physics remain impressive, but the commercial product has become boring enough for procurement departments.

Batteries are undergoing a similar transition.

The International Energy Agency reported that 108 gigawatts of new battery-storage capacity were deployed worldwide in 2025, 40 percent more than the year before. Installed capacity had grown to eleven times its 2021 level. Lithium-iron-phosphate chemistry, once a minority of deployments, accounted for about 90 percent.

That growth reflects more than one breakthrough. Work by Jessika Trancik and colleagues has shown that lithium-ion battery cost declines came from changes in materials, manufacturing, scale, design, and performance rather than one magical chemistry crossing a threshold.

The result is a machine whose role keeps widening.

A battery can move solar generation from noon toward dinner. It can respond to frequency changes faster than a thermal plant. It can provide reserve capacity, support a congested network, smooth a large industrial load, and keep a data center alive long enough for other backup systems to start.

But batteries also demonstrate why abundance should not be confused with completeness.

Most grid batteries remain short-duration assets. The IEA notes that many projects still cluster around roughly two hours of storage, although four-hour systems are becoming more common. A battery that can shift a sunny afternoon into an evening peak is not automatically a solution for a wind drought lasting several days, a seasonal mismatch, or a regional transmission failure.

Cheaper storage solves particular timing problems.

Then the next timing problem becomes visible.

This pattern repeats throughout the energy frontier.

Enhanced geothermal power begins with a resource that is physically enormous and commercially awkward: heat beneath our feet.

Conventional geothermal plants work best where hot water or steam already reaches accessible formations. That geography limits them. Enhanced geothermal systems attempt to widen the map by drilling into hot rock, creating or improving subsurface flow paths, circulating fluid through them, and bringing the heat back to the surface.

The resource is old. The toolkit is increasingly borrowed from a younger industrial giant.

Horizontal drilling, directional steering, subsurface imaging, fiber-optic sensing, high-temperature equipment, and hydraulic stimulation were developed at enormous scale in oil and gas. Geothermal developers are now trying to turn that accumulated competence toward heat rather than hydrocarbons.

That transfer is more interesting than the phrase “geothermal breakthrough” suggests.

It is an example of one industry inheriting another industry’s learning curve.

In July 2026, the U.S. Department of Energy highlighted results from a drilling demonstration led by Oxy in which two geothermal boreholes were drilled to nearly four miles below the surface. The project was designed around a prosaic goal: reduce drilling cycle time in hard, hot rock.

That is not a spectacular scientific milestone.

It may be commercially more important than one.

For geothermal, a large part of project cost and uncertainty sits underground before the plant has sold a kilowatt-hour. Faster drilling, longer-lived wells, better reservoir characterization, lower failure rates, and repeatable completion methods can turn a vast theoretical resource into a bankable one.

The strongest version of the geothermal abundance story runs ahead of the evidence, however.

The Department of Energy continues to fund field-scale tests precisely because substantial questions remain. Reservoir performance must persist. Induced seismicity has to be managed. Drilling remains expensive. Geology varies by site. Exploration risk can destroy project economics before construction begins. A technology that works in Nevada does not automatically transfer to Pennsylvania merely because both places contain hot rock.

The resource can be enormous while the usable resource remains constrained by engineering.

That distinction is easy to lose whenever we talk about energy in geological terms.

Sunlight striking Earth is enormous. Heat stored in the crust is enormous. Uranium contains enormous energy. Deuterium in seawater is enormous. None of those statements tells us the price of useful electricity delivered to a socket at 6:30 on a winter evening.

Civilization does not consume theoretical resources.

It consumes engineered flows.

Fusion makes this distinction almost comically vivid.

The achievement at the National Ignition Facility deserves to be understood on its own terms. In December 2022, NIF became the first laboratory to achieve fusion ignition. Repeated experiments since then have shown that the result was not a one-off accident. The April 2025 shot produced 8.6 megajoules of fusion energy from 2.08 megajoules of laser energy delivered to the target. In June 2026, another ignition experiment produced 7.9 megajoules with a target gain of roughly 3.8.

The experiment works.

The power plant does not yet exist.

That sentence should not diminish the science. It should protect it from bad extrapolation.

A commercial fusion plant must solve a different collection of problems from a laboratory ignition shot. It must convert energy into electricity efficiently. It must operate repeatedly rather than occasionally. Components facing intense neutron flux must survive long enough to justify their replacement cost. Fuel cycles must work at industrial scale. Maintenance cannot require rebuilding the machine after every successful pulse. The entire system must fit inside an economic and regulatory structure that can finance it.

Even success creates new constraints.

Suppose a fusion plant eventually produces electricity cheaply and reliably. The first scarcity it relaxes is fuel and generation. The grid still has to carry the electricity. Factories still have to build the plant. Turbines, transformers, magnets, lasers, superconductors, shielding materials, and cooling equipment still have to come from somewhere. Skilled technicians still have to maintain it.

The closer a technology gets to abundance, the more visible its surrounding system becomes.

This is not peculiar to fusion.

Solar has already made its surrounding system visible. In regions where panels can be installed quickly, interconnection queues, transmission, land access, inverter performance, storage, and midday price cannibalization become more important. Batteries make lithium processing, manufacturing capacity, fire codes, duration, and cycling economics visible. Nuclear makes financing and construction competence visible. Geothermal makes drilling and subsurface risk visible.

Every cheap component reveals the expensive component next to it.

That is why the most interesting question about energy abundance is not whether the marginal cost of generation can fall toward zero during some hours. In several electricity markets, it already does.

The better question is what happens when low-cost energy persists long enough, often enough, and predictably enough for people to redesign other systems around it.

History provides a clue.

Aluminum was once an expensive material because separating it from its ore required enormous effort. The Hall-Héroult process made electrolytic production practical in the late nineteenth century, but it did not make electricity irrelevant. It made cheap electricity one of the defining inputs of the industry. Aluminum smelters moved toward places where power was abundant and dependable.

Energy abundance did not eliminate industrial geography.

It changed the geography.

The same logic applies to possible future industries.

Consider hydrogen produced by splitting water with electricity.

The chemistry is straightforward. The economics are not. An electrolyzer converts electricity into a fuel that can later be burned, used in a fuel cell, fed into chemical processes, or combined with captured carbon to make synthetic hydrocarbons.

Every conversion loses energy.

That makes cheap clean electricity unusually important. If electricity is expensive, producing hydrogen and then converting it again can look absurd beside using the electricity directly. If clean electricity is extremely cheap during many hours, the comparison changes. Processes that once seemed wasteful can become useful because they solve a storage, transport, chemical-feedstock, or high-temperature problem electricity alone does not solve gracefully.

But again, lower electricity prices do not abolish the rest of the system.

Electrolyzers cost money. Hydrogen must be compressed, stored, piped, liquefied, or converted. New infrastructure leaks, embrittles materials, consumes land, and creates safety requirements. Synthetic fuels need carbon feedstocks and additional conversion equipment. Aviation customers care about energy density, compatibility, and delivered fuel price, not the price of the original electron.

Abundance expands the set of tolerable inefficiencies.

It does not repeal engineering.

Desalination is another useful test because it is often invoked as though cheap energy would solve water scarcity by itself.

Modern reverse-osmosis plants already convert seawater into fresh water at industrial scale. Electricity is important because high-pressure pumps must force water through membranes. If electricity becomes cheaper, operating cost falls.

Yet the plant still needs membranes, pumps, intake systems, pretreatment, maintenance, coastal access, financing, and a place to discharge concentrated brine. The fresh water must then be transported, sometimes uphill and far inland, through infrastructure that may cost more than the desalination itself.

Cheap power can make more water economical.

It cannot move Los Angeles next to the ocean any more than it can move Phoenix downhill.

The same mistake appears in discussions of carbon removal.

Pulling carbon dioxide directly from ambient air is difficult partly because the molecule is dilute. Fans move large volumes of air. Sorbents bind the carbon. Heat and electricity regenerate the material and compress the resulting gas. Cheap low-carbon energy can lower a meaningful part of the cost.

But the equipment, sorbents, land, pipelines, storage reservoirs, monitoring, and liability regime remain. A cheap megawatt-hour does not decide who owns a carbon dioxide plume underground for the next century.

Institutions follow the energy frontier like a shadow.

This is the strongest counterargument to the abundance thesis developed throughout this book.

Perhaps energy is becoming less important precisely because cheap energy turns attention toward everything else.

Once lighting consumes a trivial share of household income, the constraint on reading at midnight is no longer lamp fuel. Once a smartphone battery lasts all day, the interesting limits on communication become attention, trust, networks, regulation, and human time. Once electricity is cheap enough for a factory, its competitive advantage may depend more on logistics, software, skill, capital, or market access.

If abundance succeeds, energy can disappear from the foreground.

That does not make it unimportant.

It makes it infrastructure.

The roads that work are not the roads we discuss at dinner. Clean water is most noticeable when it stops. Electricity is already treated this way in wealthy places. A switch produces light with so little drama that the entire power system can vanish from consciousness.

Abundance is therefore partly a story about invisibility.

The most successful energy technology may be the one that stops feeling like an energy technology at all.

Solar modules are moving in that direction. Batteries are beginning to. Heat pumps are just appliances to the people who do not care about their coefficient of performance. An electric motor is rarely experienced as a civilizational achievement even though modern life is full of them.

The frontier moves because yesterday’s frontier becomes a component.

This gives us a better way to think about the technologies still arriving.

A laboratory record matters because it proves a previously uncertain physical possibility.

A field demonstration matters because it tests whether the possibility survives contact with geology, weather, equipment, maintenance, and operators.

A first commercial plant matters because somebody has agreed to finance and insure the system.

A fleet matters because repetition begins to expose the difference between one good project and a production technology.

A commodity matters because the buyer no longer needs to care how extraordinary the underlying science once was.

Fusion is still near the beginning of that sequence. Enhanced geothermal is somewhere in the middle, with real commercial plants and active field experiments but large unresolved questions about where and how cheaply it can scale. Batteries and solar have moved much farther toward commodity infrastructure.

Putting them in one category called “clean energy technology” obscures more than it reveals.

They occupy different positions on the road from possibility to ordinariness.

That road has no guarantee at the end.

Technologies can stall. Materials can become expensive. Public opposition can delay projects. A demonstration can fail when scaled. A cost curve can flatten. Trade restrictions can break a supply chain. Cheap hardware can collide with expensive capital. A technically elegant system can lose to an uglier one that is easier to manufacture, finance, repair, and permit.

Progress is lumpy because reality votes at every stage.

This is also why forecasts of abundance should be held lightly.

The future does not owe us the continuation of a learning curve.

Solar and battery costs fell through decades of engineering, scale, policy, manufacturing investment, and global trade. Their history shows what cumulative learning can do. It does not prove that every technology will repeat the same trajectory. Nuclear construction costs famously did not. Geothermal wells will not become semiconductor chips merely because both benefit from learning. Fusion will not become cheap because solar did.

The useful optimism is conditional.

If we can make clean electricity cheaper, build networks fast enough to move it, store enough of it to match demand, and add firm generation where weather and storage are insufficient, then energy ceases to veto an expanding range of projects.

Some projects will still fail for other reasons.

That is the point.

A civilization with more controllable energy gets to discover more precisely what its other constraints are.

Water may turn out to be a pipeline problem. Carbon removal may turn out to be a materials and governance problem. Synthetic fuels may turn out to be a capital-utilization problem. AI may turn out to be limited by chips, data, algorithms, or institutions after electricity catches up. Spaceflight may remain brutally constrained by mass even if terrestrial electricity becomes very cheap.

Abundance does not eliminate scarcity.

It reveals the next scarcity sooner.

That idea connects the frontier technologies in this chapter to the much older technologies behind us.

Fire relaxed the constraint of raw metabolism and exposed the problem of reliable fuel and control. Agriculture relaxed seasonal food scarcity enough to expose storage, property, hierarchy, and disease. Coal relaxed the land constraint on energy and exposed mines, machinery, pollution, and transport. Electricity relaxed the need to put mechanical work beside a prime mover and exposed the network. Cheap computation exposed power and cooling. Nuclear exposes construction capability.

There has never been a final energy technology because there has never been a final constraint.

The plausible promise of the next frontier is therefore not a world without limits.

It is a world in which a larger number of limits become worth confronting.

Cheap solar makes storage worth improving. Better batteries make transmission and duration more valuable. Better grids make electrification easier. Electrification makes clean firm power more valuable. Better drilling may make geothermal available where no conventional reservoir exists. Fusion research may eventually add another source of firm power if it crosses the long distance from ignition to industry.

Each improvement changes the return to the next one.

That is where abundance becomes more than a low electricity price.

It becomes option value.

More energy, delivered with enough control and reliability, allows a society to attempt processes it previously rejected before the first blueprint was drawn. Some of those processes will be wasteful. Some will be frivolous. Some will turn out to matter enormously.

No central planner can know the full list in advance because many uses only become obvious after the constraint relaxes.

The same was true of electricity.

No nineteenth-century engineer planning a central station needed to predict semiconductor fabs, magnetic-resonance scanners, air-conditioned skyscrapers, cloud computing, or refrigerators in every kitchen in order for electrification to be valuable. Once reliable power became ordinary, entrepreneurs and households found uses for it that the original system was not built to serve.

Abundance is valuable partly because it creates room for surprise.

That is a stronger claim than saying more energy is always better, and a weaker one than saying cheap energy solves everything.

More useful energy expands capability. What people do with that capability still depends on knowledge, institutions, distribution, and judgment. External costs still count. Access still matters. Waste remains possible. A society can have abundant electricity and use it badly.

The energy thesis survives only if it remains that modest.

Energy is not progress itself.

It is what makes a larger set of physical futures possible.

The final chapter asks whether that claim holds across the entire arc of the book—from fire to food surplus, coal to grids, efficiency to AI, reactors to fusion—and whether energy really deserves to be called the common denominator underneath them all.
