# Abundance and the New Frontier

In June 2026, at a laboratory site in Utah, engineers began preparing a test whose ambition was almost comically modest compared with the rhetoric that often surrounds new energy technologies. They wanted to circulate water through hot rock for a long time.

The Frontier Observatory for Research in Geothermal Energy—FORGE—had spent years drilling, stimulating, measuring, and learning how fractures behaved several kilometers below the surface. An enhanced geothermal system does not wait for nature to provide a convenient hydrothermal reservoir. Engineers drill into hot rock, create or improve pathways through it, inject fluid, recover heat, and try to make the underground heat exchanger persist. The August 2026 extended-circulation test was designed to answer the question that matters after the impressive drilling photographs and short demonstrations: can the reservoir keep working long enough to resemble an energy resource rather than an experiment?

The U.S. Department of Energy described sustained circulation as a critical step toward commercial electricity generation and noted that only a handful of enhanced-geothermal projects worldwide had reached this stage. That phrasing is useful because it contains both promise and restraint. The heat is real. The drilling techniques are real. The test is real. Commercial abundance is not yet real.

That distinction is the subject of this chapter.

Energy abundance is easy to imagine because the physical quantities available to us are enormous. Sunlight striking Earth dwarfs human energy consumption. Heat stored in the crust is vast. Uranium contains extraordinary energy density. Deuterium is abundant enough to make fusion sound almost mythological when described only in fuel terms. The difficulty has never been proving that the universe contains energy.

The difficulty is building machines and institutions that can turn a useful fraction of it into dependable services at a tolerable total cost.

By that standard, abundance is not a resource estimate. It is a production condition.

A resource becomes abundant when the next useful unit can be delivered cheaply enough, reliably enough, and with sufficiently low external cost that activities previously constrained by energy begin to change. The relevant frontier is therefore not merely more generation. It is the point at which energy ceases to be the expensive part of a decision.

We have seen versions of this before.

Cheap coal changed how much mechanical work Britain could command. The grid changed where power could be produced and consumed. Falling lighting costs changed the amount of darkness people accepted. Efficiency repeatedly lowered the resource cost of a service and then widened the market for it. None of these transitions created literal infinity. They created practical abundance relative to the old constraint.

The current energy transition contains technologies at very different distances from that condition. Solar photovoltaics and lithium-ion batteries are already manufactured at enormous scale. Nuclear fission is a mature source whose construction economics vary sharply by institutional setting. Enhanced geothermal is moving from field demonstration toward possible replication. Fusion has crossed scientific milestones that would have sounded implausible not long ago, but remains far from commercial power.

Putting all of them under the label “clean energy technology” can hide more than it reveals.

The most mature example is solar.

In 2025, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the global weighted-average levelized cost of electricity from newly commissioned utility-scale solar photovoltaic projects was about $44 per megawatt-hour, essentially unchanged from 2024 after years of steep decline. More than 90 percent of utility-scale renewable projects commissioned that year produced electricity below the cost of the cheapest new fossil-fuel alternative in their market. Onshore wind averaged about $33 per megawatt-hour.

These are remarkable numbers, but they are not the price of an electricity system.

Levelized cost measures the plant. It does not by itself pay for transmission, distribution, balancing, reserves, curtailment, storage, or the fact that a megawatt-hour delivered at noon is not identical in system value to a megawatt-hour delivered during a windless evening peak. As solar and wind penetration rises, the old question—how cheaply can this generator produce energy?—increasingly gives way to a harder one: how cheaply can the system provide electricity when and where it is needed?

This is the same migration of constraint that appeared in Chapter 9.

Cheap generation can make networks, flexibility, land, permitting, interconnection, and firm capacity more valuable. A technology can win the busbar-cost contest and still encounter a system that cannot absorb unlimited output at the same value.

That is why batteries matter to the abundance story in a way that their chemistry alone does not capture.

Lithium-ion batteries were not born as grid infrastructure. Their rise was driven through consumer electronics, then electric vehicles, manufacturing scale, materials improvements, energy-density gains, and production learning. Micah Ziegler and Jessika Trancik reconstructed the technology’s long-run improvement and found a real price decline of roughly 97 percent per unit of energy capacity from commercial introduction through the period they studied. Their companion work with Juhyun Song decomposed the decline and found no single magic cause. Research and development, manufacturing scale, materials, cell design, and other improvements accumulated.

The lesson is almost the opposite of a generic “learning curve.”

Technologies do not get cheaper because time passes. People discover ways to make them cheaper, and markets become large enough for those discoveries to matter.

IRENA’s more recent system-cost data show what happened when those improvements reached the grid. Utility-scale battery-storage installed costs fell from about $2,571 per kilowatt-hour in 2010 to $192 per kilowatt-hour in 2024, a 93 percent decline. Annual gross battery-storage deployment rose from almost nothing to 169 gigawatt-hours over the same period.

The result is not that intermittency has been solved. Duration still matters. A four-hour battery is not seasonal storage. A battery that can shift solar generation from afternoon into evening does not guarantee power through a week of adverse weather. Storage losses, degradation, materials, financing, land, fire safety, and network location all matter.

But the economic boundary moved.

Tasks that were once absurdly expensive for batteries became ordinary enough to finance at scale. Frequency regulation, peak shaving, renewable shifting, backup, and grid services entered the feasible set. Solar and storage began to be designed together rather than treated as unrelated industries.

In May 2026, IRENA published an analysis of solar and wind paired with battery storage for round-the-clock supply. In favorable high-resource regions, modeled firm renewable costs had fallen from above $100 per megawatt-hour in 2020 to roughly $54–82 per megawatt-hour by 2025. The exact result depends on location, financing, resource quality, storage assumptions, and the meaning of “firm,” and the study should not be read as proof that every power system can replace every dispatchable plant with one standardized hybrid.

Still, it marks a change in the question. Storage is no longer merely a hypothetical future answer appended to a cheap-renewables argument. In some markets it is becoming part of the thing being built.

Abundance tends to arrive this way: not as one miraculous technology, but as several cost curves intersecting.

A cheap solar module is more useful when inverters are cheap. Both are more useful when batteries are cheap. Batteries are more useful when power electronics and control software are capable. All of them are more useful when transmission and interconnection are available. The system cost can fall even if no component reaches zero.

This is why the dream of “energy too cheap to meter” has always been slightly misleading. Metering is not the enemy. The real threshold is when the energy component of a service becomes small enough that some other constraint takes over.

Desalination is a good example.

Removing salt from seawater is physically possible and commercially routine. Reverse-osmosis plants use pressure to push water through membranes that reject salts. Energy is a significant operating input, but so are membranes, pretreatment, pumps, intake and outfall infrastructure, financing, maintenance, and environmental management. If electricity became free tomorrow, desalinated water would not become free. It would become cheaper until capital, infrastructure, brine disposal, geography, and water distribution dominated the bill.

That is what abundance actually does. It reveals the next scarcity.

The same would happen with synthetic fuels. Cheap electricity can lower the cost of electrolytic hydrogen, but electrolyzers, water, compression, storage, transport, carbon capture for hydrocarbons, conversion losses, and end-use equipment remain. Direct electrification will often be more efficient where it is possible. In aviation, shipping, chemicals, and some industrial processes, however, molecules may retain value precisely because electricity cannot conveniently perform every task at the point of use.

Energy abundance does not erase engineering. It changes which engineering problems are worth solving.

Geothermal offers a different route to the same frontier because its attraction is not merely cheap energy but controllable energy.

Conventional geothermal is geographically constrained by accessible heat, permeability, and fluid. Enhanced geothermal systems attempt to loosen one of those constraints by borrowing techniques from modern drilling and reservoir engineering. If hot rock can be accessed and engineered across a wider geography, geothermal could provide firm power without fuel deliveries and with a relatively small surface footprint.

The word “if” is doing real work.

DOE’s decision in February 2026 to make up to $171.5 million available for next-generation geothermal field tests was not evidence that the technology had become commercially mature. It was evidence that critical questions still required field-scale answers. A separate Pennsylvania demonstration selected in April proposed converting a horizontal shale-gas well to geothermal service in the Appalachian Utica Shale, testing whether oil-and-gas drilling knowledge could be repurposed for heat extraction in a new region. DOE explicitly described the selection as a field test and noted that award negotiations could still fail.

This is the appropriate evidence level: promising engineering under test.

If FORGE and commercial successors demonstrate durable reservoirs, predictable drilling, manageable induced seismicity, competitive well costs, and repeatable construction, the accessible geothermal resource could expand dramatically. If reservoirs degrade, drilling costs remain high, or suitable geology proves narrower than hoped, the frontier will be smaller.

Abundance claims should be written so failure remains possible.

Fusion demands even stricter discipline because its scientific progress is so easy to convert into premature economic language.

On December 5, 2022, the National Ignition Facility at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory produced more fusion energy from a target than the laser energy delivered to that target. That was a genuine scientific milestone: target gain above one. It did not mean the facility generated net electricity. The lasers consumed far more electrical energy than they delivered to the target, and NIF was built for stockpile stewardship and high-energy-density science rather than as a prototype commercial power station.

The experiments have nevertheless continued to improve.

On June 20, 2026, NIF achieved ignition for the eleventh time. Lawrence Livermore reports a measured fusion yield of 7.9 megajoules, plus or minus 0.4 megajoules, from 2.065 megajoules of laser energy delivered to the target—a target gain of roughly 3.8. That result is far beyond the first gain-above-one experiment.

It is still not a power plant.

A commercial inertial-fusion system would need efficient drivers, rapid target manufacture and injection, repeated shots, heat capture, durable chamber materials, tritium management, maintenance, and electricity generation at an economic cost. Magnetic-confinement fusion has its own engineering chain. JET’s final deuterium-tritium campaign demonstrated impressive sustained fusion-energy production, but JET was also an experimental machine, not a commercial generator.

The frontier moved. The category did not.

This distinction matters because the abundance story becomes weaker, not stronger, when laboratory milestones are presented as deployment. A reader does not need fusion to be commercial today to understand its significance. The honest fact is already extraordinary: humans can now create controlled fusion conditions that repeatedly release more energy from the target fuel than the laser deposits into that target. The remaining distance is engineering, systems integration, economics, and time.

There is another reason to resist treating abundance as a forecast of one winning technology.

Energy systems value different attributes.

Solar converts a widely available flow with no fuel cost but varies with time and weather. Batteries move electricity across time but do not create it. Nuclear fission produces dense, controllable low-carbon power but can demand formidable construction and institutional capability. Geothermal can offer firm output where the subsurface and drilling economics cooperate. Wind has different geographic and temporal patterns from solar. Transmission can make all of them more valuable by connecting diverse regions. Flexible demand can sometimes provide the cheapest “storage” by changing when a service occurs.

A high-abundance system is likely to be a portfolio because the cheapest way to satisfy one constraint is not necessarily the cheapest way to satisfy all of them.

This is also why cost declines do not guarantee endless cost declines.

Solar’s global weighted-average levelized cost was essentially flat from 2024 to 2025. IRENA describes renewable costs as stabilizing after more than a decade of steep decreases. Geothermal and several dispatchable renewable technologies became more expensive in its 2025 dataset. Financing costs, trade barriers, commodity prices, land, grid congestion, labor, and supply chains can offset technological improvement.

The history of batteries and solar demonstrates that dramatic improvement is possible. It does not establish a law that every energy technology becomes cheaper forever.

Nor does cheap energy guarantee good outcomes.

A civilization with abundant electricity can desalinate water, recycle materials, synthesize fuels, run heat pumps, electrify transport, operate factories, cool buildings, and perform vast amounts of computation. It can also illuminate empty buildings, mine low-value material, automate surveillance, produce weapons, or consume resources faster because energy is no longer the binding cost.

Energy expands the feasible set. Institutions, prices, law, culture, and politics still decide what enters it.

The environmental side changes rather than disappears. Solar farms use land and materials. Batteries require mining and processing. Transmission crosses landscapes and communities. Nuclear produces long-lived waste and carries low-probability, high-consequence risks that institutions must manage. Geothermal drilling can induce seismicity and requires water and subsurface stewardship. Fusion, if commercialized, would bring its own materials, tritium, activation, and industrial questions.

A clean-energy system can have far lower climate and air-pollution costs than the fossil system without becoming impact-free.

This is important because abundance is sometimes imagined as permission to stop counting.

The better version is the opposite. When energy gets cheaper, society can afford to count costs that once looked unavoidable.

Coal smoke was tolerated because industrial power was valuable and alternatives were limited. Leaded gasoline survived because mobility had become indispensable before its external costs were fully internalized. A richer technological frontier can make cleaner substitutions feasible. Abundance can therefore reduce social cost not only by lowering the price of energy but by widening the menu of ways to produce the same capability.

That may be the most consequential form of abundance: optionality.

A grid with many generation sources, storage, transmission, flexible demand, and firm low-carbon power is not abundant merely because annual terawatt-hours are high. It is abundant because losing one pathway does not collapse the service. A factory with several credible sources of heat or electricity has bargaining power. A country less dependent on imported fuel has strategic room. A household with efficient appliances needs less exposure to price shocks for the same comfort.

Abundance includes resilience.

It also includes access, or it is a peculiar kind of abundance.

Hundreds of millions of people still lack electricity, and billions still lack clean cooking. For them the frontier is not fusion or synthetic aviation fuel. It is a reliable connection, a clean stove, refrigeration, a pump, a fan, a light, or enough power to run a small business. The marginal value of energy remains strongly unequal across the world.

A future in which affluent regions run enormous low-carbon loads while other communities remain energy-poor would demonstrate technical abundance without human convergence.

That would matter to the thesis of this book. If energy is a substrate of capability, then distribution determines who receives the capability.

The strongest counterargument to an abundance chapter is therefore not that the technologies are fake. Solar and batteries are deployed at scale. Fission operates at scale. Enhanced geothermal is being tested in the field. Fusion ignition is experimentally real.

The stronger objection is that cheap components do not necessarily produce a cheap, reliable, equitable system. Networks can lag. Financing can dominate. Local opposition can be rational. Materials can bottleneck. Manufacturing can concentrate geographically. Demand can expand. External costs can migrate. A laboratory milestone can remain a laboratory milestone for decades.

Any claim that the energy problem is about to be “solved” should fail against that evidence.

The narrower claim survives.

The frontier of useful energy is moving because several technologies have improved enough to change what can plausibly be built. Solar and batteries have already crossed from expensive specialties into mass infrastructure. Nuclear remains capable of producing large amounts of firm low-carbon electricity where institutions can build and operate it well. Enhanced geothermal is testing whether drilling can turn a geographically narrow resource into a broader one. Fusion has crossed a scientific threshold while remaining on the far side of the engineering and economic threshold.

These are not equivalent achievements. They should not be placed on one smooth curve.

The planned figure for this chapter therefore needs categories rather than a triumphant line: demonstrated physical result, demonstrated commercial result, current scale, cost metric, main remaining constraint, and source date. Solar belongs in the commercially deployed column. Lithium-ion storage does too. Fission does. Enhanced geothermal belongs between demonstration and early commercialization depending on the project. Inertial and magnetic fusion remain experimental.

If that table changes over the next decade, the chapter should change with it.

The claim would need to narrow if solar and battery cost declines stalled while system costs rose enough to erase their advantage; if enhanced geothermal repeatedly failed to demonstrate durable, economical reservoirs; if new nuclear programs could not reproduce acceptable construction outcomes even under stable standardized institutions; or if fusion’s target gains improved without credible progress on driver efficiency, repetition rate, materials, fuel cycle, and plant economics.

Abundance is not a promise that every frontier technology succeeds.

It is the condition created when enough of them do.

The civilization that follows will not be defined by a single cheap electron. It will be defined by the things that become ordinary once energy is no longer the first reason to say no.
