# Abundance and the New Frontier

Abundance is an easy word to ruin.

Used carelessly, it means infinite energy, free materials, effortless growth, or a future in which every constraint dissolves because a chart went down and to the right.

That is not what is happening.

A more useful definition is narrower: abundance begins when a useful input becomes cheap, capable, and dependable enough that some other constraint becomes the first reason to say no.

That has happened before.

Artificial light became abundant enough that the price of illumination stopped determining whether most people could read after sunset. Mechanical power became abundant enough that factories stopped arranging every machine around a single shaft. Computing became cheap enough that the number of arithmetic operations was no longer the first constraint on many problems.

The resource did not become infinite. Its scarcity moved down the list.

Energy is beginning to show the same pattern in some places and for some services.

The cleanest evidence is not a futuristic reactor or a laboratory headline. It is a solar farm nobody regards as exotic.

In 2025, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the global weighted-average levelized cost of newly commissioned utility-scale solar photovoltaic generation was about $44 per megawatt-hour. Onshore wind was about $33. More than 90 percent of utility-scale renewable projects commissioned that year produced power below the cost of the cheapest new fossil-fuel plant available in their market under IRENA's methodology.

There are enough caveats in that sentence to make a consultant comfortable.

Levelized cost is not a retail electricity bill. It is not the cost of a reliable grid. It does not tell you what electricity is worth at 3 a.m. during a wind lull, what a new transmission line costs, or whether a solar project can interconnect this decade. It compresses financing, construction, operations, fuel, and output assumptions into a plant-level comparison.

Still, the number matters.

For most of industrial history, obtaining useful energy at scale meant paying continually for fuel. A coal plant consumes coal. A gas turbine consumes gas. The fuel bill arrives with the electricity.

A solar plant is different. Once built, it waits for sunrise.

That fact does not make its electricity free. Capital, land, maintenance, inverters, grid equipment, financing, and replacement all remain. But it changes the economic structure of producing another kilowatt-hour when the sun is available.

The machine has no appetite.

Wind has the same peculiar quality. The resource is not scarce in the way mined fuel is scarce. The scarce things are sites, machines, networks, storage, permitting, capital, materials, social consent, and the ability to match production with demand.

In other words, when the fuel gets cheap, the rest of the system becomes visible.

That is exactly what the previous chapters have been moving toward.

The transformer matters because the solar module became easy to buy. Interconnection matters because hundreds of projects can afford to exist. Financing structure matters for nuclear because the uranium is not what dominates the cost of building the plant. The constraint migrates toward whatever still refuses to scale.

Battery storage offers another version of the same story.

In 2025, the International Energy Agency counted 108 gigawatts of new battery storage capacity deployed worldwide, 40 percent more than the year before. Global installed battery storage capacity was eleven times higher than in 2021. Around 80 percent of the new capacity was utility-scale.

That is not a laboratory curve. It is equipment being installed.

The chemistry has also changed as the market has learned what it values. Lithium-iron-phosphate batteries, less energy-dense than chemistries favored for some vehicles, accounted for roughly 90 percent of storage deployments by 2025. Stationary storage does not care much whether a battery is elegant per kilogram. It cares whether the battery is cheap, durable, safe enough, manufacturable, and willing to cycle thousands of times while sitting on concrete.

Technology improves partly by becoming better suited to the job it actually has.

Most grid batteries are still short-duration machines. Many cluster around two hours, with a growing number reaching four hours or more. They are excellent at moving solar generation from the middle of the day into the evening, smoothing short imbalances, providing grid services, and responding quickly when the system needs power.

They do not turn February into August.

A two-hour battery is not seasonal storage. A four-hour battery does not solve a week of low wind. A gigawatt of discharge capability says nothing by itself about how many gigawatt-hours are behind it.

This matters because abundance arguments fail when every improvement is treated as a universal substitute.

Cheap solar is not firm power. A battery is not a transmission line. A transmission line is not a generator. A nuclear reactor is not a peaker. A gas turbine is not a fuel-free resource. A heat pump is not a building retrofit.

Energy systems become powerful by combining imperfect machines.

The surprising future is therefore unlikely to arrive as one technology winning.

It will arrive when several technologies become cheap enough that engineers can afford to use them together.

Oversize the solar array. Add a battery. Move some load to noon. Build a transmission line to a region with different weather. Keep firm generation for the hours when weather and storage do not cooperate. Use software to decide what should run when. Add demand that can wait fifteen minutes, two hours, or a day.

None of those moves abolishes scarcity.

Together they can reduce how often scarcity wins.

This is a different conception of abundance from the old fantasy of a single miraculous fuel.

It is redundancy becoming affordable.

For most of history, redundancy was expensive because every extra unit of capability required more material, more labor, more fuel, or all three. When equipment costs fall sharply, deliberately building more capacity than is needed at every instant can become rational.

A solar system that sometimes produces electricity nobody needs may still be cheaper than designing the entire system around avoiding curtailment. A battery that spends much of the day waiting may be valuable precisely because it is available for the few hours when the grid is tight. A transmission line can look underused on average while being indispensable during a narrow set of conditions.

This offends a certain kind of efficiency instinct.

Unused capacity looks wasteful.

But resilience has always contained some apparent waste. Hospitals keep spare equipment. Water systems maintain reserve capacity. Networks route around failures. The question is not whether every asset works every minute. It is whether the system produces the service people need at an acceptable cost.

Abundance can look inefficient at the component level because it creates options at the system level.

That is one reason energy abundance, if it comes, may feel less dramatic than promised.

There may be no morning when electricity becomes too cheap to meter. The phrase has survived far better than the economics behind it.

Instead, cheap energy will appear as projects that become worth attempting.

Desalination runs more often when power is inexpensive. Data centers shift workloads toward lower-cost hours or regions. Industrial heat can be electrified where the economics make sense. Hydrogen can be produced when electricity would otherwise have little value, though converting electricity into hydrogen and back wastes enough energy to keep the process from becoming a free lunch. Direct air capture, synthetic fuels, fertilizer, metals refining, cooling, pumping, and recycling all change when energy is cheaper at the margin.

The important phrase is at the margin.

A civilization does not need energy to be free for new behavior to emerge. It only needs the cost of energy to stop being the decisive part of the decision.

Then other costs take over.

Water. Land. Carbon. Permits. Equipment. Skilled labor. Local politics. Transmission. Capital. Time.

Abundance is not the disappearance of constraint.

It is the promotion of the next constraint.

Geothermal power makes this visible because the energy source is enormous and the access problem is brutal.

Heat beneath the Earth's surface is not rare. Economically useful reservoirs are.

Traditional geothermal development has therefore clustered in places where geology is unusually cooperative: heat, fluid, and permeability appear together close enough to the surface to exploit.

Enhanced geothermal systems try to loosen that geographic requirement. Drill into hot rock, create or improve pathways for fluid, circulate it, and bring heat back to the surface.

The idea is simple enough to explain in one sentence and difficult enough to occupy research programs for decades.

The interesting recent progress is not that the Earth's crust got hotter. It is that drilling is getting better.

In a Department of Energy demonstration completed in 2026, the GLADE project drilled two geothermal boreholes in hard basement rock to nearly four miles below the surface. The project was designed to reduce total drilling cycle time by transferring tools and practices from oil and gas drilling into geothermal conditions.

That is a recurring pattern in technological history: abundance often arrives through a borrowed competence.

The steam engine improved mining, then coal fed steam. Oil drilling techniques now have a chance to make a different subsurface energy resource more accessible. Manufacturing improvements developed for consumer electronics helped batteries become grid equipment. Semiconductor fabrication turned abstract computation into an industrial input.

Civilizations reuse their tricks.

But geothermal still belongs on the frontier side of this chapter, not the abundance side.

The United States has only around four gigawatts of geothermal electricity capacity today. DOE analyses describe vastly larger technical potential, including scenarios reaching hundreds of gigawatts by mid-century, but potential is not deployment. Field tests are not fleets. A well drilled successfully is not yet a cost curve.

The difference between those statements is the difference between optimism and bookkeeping.

Fusion requires even more bookkeeping.

In December 2022, the National Ignition Facility produced the first laboratory fusion experiment in which energy released from the target exceeded the laser energy delivered to the target. Since then, the laboratory has repeated ignition multiple times.

On April 7, 2025, a NIF experiment delivered 2.08 megajoules of laser energy to a tiny target and produced 8.6 megajoules of fusion energy, a target gain of 4.13. On June 20, 2026, NIF achieved ignition for the eleventh time, producing 7.9 megajoules with a target gain of roughly 3.8.

Those are extraordinary scientific results.

They are also an excellent opportunity to practice not lying with a denominator.

The laser energy reaching the target is not the electricity consumed by the facility. Target gain is not wall-plug gain. NIF was built primarily for stockpile stewardship, not as a commercial power plant. A useful fusion power station would need a complete system that repeatedly creates targets, fires at an economically useful rate, captures the resulting energy, survives neutron damage, converts heat into electricity, powers its own equipment, maintains itself, and does all of that cheaply enough to compete with alternatives that already exist.

The gulf between ignition and commercial electricity remains large.

That does not diminish ignition.

It locates it.

There is a bad habit in technology writing of treating every laboratory milestone either as the dawn of a revolution or as irrelevant until it reaches a supermarket shelf. Both reactions are lazy.

A frontier milestone is evidence that one barrier moved.

It does not tell you how many barriers remain.

The history in this book should make that distinction familiar by now. A steam engine patent was not the Industrial Revolution. Pearl Street was not universal electrification. The first controlled nuclear chain reaction was not a commercial reactor fleet. The first transistor did not contain the internet.

Technologies become civilizational when a chain of improvements survives contact with cost, manufacturing, regulation, infrastructure, maintenance, and demand.

The frontier is where one of those links has just become plausible.

Abundance is where enough of them have become ordinary.

The distance between the two can be measured in decades.

Sometimes it disappears faster.

Solar photovoltaics spent much of the twentieth century as expensive specialty equipment. Then manufacturing scale, process improvements, materials efficiency, supply chains, financing, and cumulative deployment drove costs down until solar became the largest source of new power capacity additions in much of the world.

Lithium-ion batteries followed a similar but not identical path. They improved across cost, energy density, durability, and manufacturing scale because consumer electronics, electric vehicles, and stationary storage each paid for learning that the others could reuse.

The lesson is not that every technology follows a learning curve automatically.

Learning curves are records of what happened, not laws that guarantee what happens next.

Costs can rise. Commodity prices can reverse gains. Supply chains can concentrate. Safety requirements can add expense. The easiest manufacturing improvements can be exhausted. A technology can become technically excellent and still lose economically to something improving faster.

Deployment creates opportunities to learn. It does not force learning to occur.

That is the strongest argument against assuming an age of energy abundance is inevitable.

Low-cost generation can coexist with high electricity bills. Cheap batteries can coexist with interconnection delays. Solar modules can become commodities while transformers remain scarce. A country can possess excellent resources and poor institutions. A household can live near abundant generation and still be unable to afford reliable service.

The distance between cheap technology and cheap capability is filled with systems.

There is also a more fundamental objection.

If energy becomes cheaper, people may simply find more uses for it.

The efficiency paradox returns at a larger scale.

Cheaper electricity can stimulate data centers, air conditioning, desalination, industrial production, synthetic fuels, transportation, and uses nobody has bothered to invent while energy remained expensive. Total consumption may rise even while each individual service becomes dramatically more efficient.

That is not necessarily a failure.

The question is what the additional energy does.

A terawatt-hour used to cool homes during lethal heat is different from one wasted through a poorly insulated building. Electricity used to replace dirty household cooking can improve health even if consumption rises. Energy used to manufacture disposable objects no one values has a different claim on resources.

Raw quantity does not settle the argument.

Capability does.

That is why the most meaningful abundance metric is not joules per person.

It is the range of valuable things people can reliably afford to do, adjusted for what those capabilities cost the rest of the world.

Land matters. Materials matter. Pollution matters. Climate matters. Noise, water, risk, and local disruption matter. An energy system that produces cheap electricity by imposing unbearable costs elsewhere has not escaped scarcity. It has moved the bill.

The best technologies reduce several constraints at once.

A heat pump can reduce delivered energy while increasing heating service. An electric motor can turn a larger fraction of input energy into motion. Solar can produce electricity without a continuing fuel stream. Batteries can shift energy without combustion. Nuclear can produce large amounts of firm power from small quantities of fuel and land, if the surrounding construction system can deliver the plant. Geothermal may eventually add firm power without fuel logistics in places that could not previously access it.

Fusion, if it becomes practical, would add another option.

The important word is option.

Civilization becomes more capable when it has several ways to solve the same problem.

That is what abundance ultimately buys: not infinite consumption, but room to choose.

A factory can choose whether to burn fuel or use electricity. A grid can choose among generators, storage, transmission, and flexible demand. A household can heat with a machine that moves ambient heat rather than creating all of it from combustion. A data center can choose among locations partly on the basis of available power. A country can decide that a scarce fuel should be reserved for the uses where its properties matter most.

Options create resilience because they prevent one shortage from becoming every shortage.

The deepest form of energy abundance may therefore be optionality.

That is less cinematic than limitless power.

It is also more useful.

The frontier technologies in this chapter matter because they could widen the option set. The deployed technologies matter because they already have.

Keeping those categories separate is an act of optimism, not pessimism.

It lets us be impressed by a fusion target without pretending it powered the building. It lets us celebrate a four-mile geothermal well without counting hypothetical future gigawatts as present supply. It lets us recognize astonishing declines in solar and battery costs without pretending the grid around them is solved.

Progress becomes easier to see when milestones are allowed to be exactly what they are.

Some are proofs.

Some are products.

Some are infrastructure.

The distance between them is where most of the work happens.

The history of energy has been a history of pushing more technologies across those boundaries.

Fire became a skill. Coal became an industry. Steam became a machine system. Electricity became a network. Nuclear fission became a fleet in some countries and a construction challenge in others. Solar panels became commodities. Batteries are becoming grid equipment.

The next frontier is not one device.

It is the possibility that enough different forms of useful, controllable energy become cheap and scalable at the same time that energy ceases to dominate the cost of more human ambitions.

If that happens, the world will not run out of constraints.

It will discover them faster.

That may be the clearest sign that abundance has arrived.

The next chapter has to ask whether this book's argument survives that success.

If energy stops being the obvious bottleneck, was it ever the fundamental one?
