# Energy Becomes Invisible

A candle is a poor way to buy light.

That sounds unfair to the candle. For most of human history it was an extraordinary piece of technology: portable fuel, wick, combustion, and illumination packaged into an object small enough to hold in one hand. It could turn darkness into usable time.

It was also expensive, dim, smoky, fragile, and hungry.

The mistake is to compare the candle with an electric bulb as two objects. The useful comparison is what each lets a person do.

Both are machines for buying sight after sunset.

That change in the unit of account matters.

Energy statistics are full of fuels and kilowatt-hours because those things can be measured. People mostly want something else. They want a warm room, a cold drink, a kilometer of travel, a ton of steel, a lit desk, a lifted elevator, a searched database, a line of code returned before they lose patience.

Energy is usually an intermediate input to a service.

The service is what enters life.

This is easy to say and surprisingly easy to forget. A civilization that needs ten units of fuel to produce a useful result is not obviously more capable than one that can produce the same result with one. Raw energy consumption can rise because a society is doing more useful work. It can also rise because machines are inefficient, buildings leak, motors waste heat, distances are badly arranged, or fuel is cheap enough that nobody has bothered to care.

Counting energy without asking what it became is like counting flour without asking whether anyone ate.

The history of artificial light makes the distinction unusually clean because the output can be compared across radically different technologies.

Roger Fouquet and Peter Pearson reconstructed seven centuries of lighting in the United Kingdom by estimating not merely the price of candles, gas, oil, or electricity, but the price of the lighting service those energy systems delivered. Their long series follows a succession of technologies that each changed the relationship between energy input and visible light.

The result is almost comic in scale.

By 2000, they estimate, lighting services in the United Kingdom cost less than one three-thousandth of their 1800 level in real terms. Over the same period, per-capita consumption of lighting services rose roughly 6,500-fold.

Those numbers sit awkwardly beside the ordinary picture of industrial progress.

If you looked only at the fuel, you would see substitutions: tallow and wax giving way to whale oil and town gas, gas losing ground to electricity, incandescent lamps improving, fluorescent tubes spreading, later technologies arriving. If you looked only at household expenditure, you might conclude that lighting eventually became a minor item.

If you look at the service, you see something else.

Light became almost free enough to stop feeling like a scarce resource.

That is a recurring form of progress. The system underneath becomes more elaborate while the user’s experience becomes simpler. More engineering. Less attention.

A person in 1800 had to think about the candle because the candle imposed itself on the household. It had to be purchased, stored, trimmed, lit, watched, replaced, and rationed. The brightness available from it was poor by modern standards. Reading after dark was a decision with a visible cost.

A modern room can be flooded with light by touching a plastic switch.

The electricity meter moves, but not enough for most people to notice. The grid responds. Generators alter output somewhere else. Wires warm slightly. Power electronics regulate current. A semiconductor junction or phosphor emits photons. The room becomes bright.

The user experiences almost none of this as energy.

The user experiences visibility.

William Nordhaus made a related point in his history of lighting, but his target was economic measurement. Conventional price indexes struggle when a new technology does not simply make an old product cheaper but delivers a much better version of the underlying service. A candle and an electric lamp are both sold as lighting technologies, yet they are separated by enormous differences in luminous output, convenience, reliability, cleanliness, and labor required to obtain the light.

Nordhaus reconstructed the labor cost of illumination across technologies and found declines far larger than ordinary price measures suggested. The point was not that official statistics were foolish. The point was that measuring the price of a thing becomes difficult when the thing quietly changes category.

A horse and a train both provide transport. A letter and a video call both provide communication. A fireplace and a heat pump both provide warmth. A candle and an LED both provide light.

Calling them substitutes is true but incomplete.

The service can survive while almost everything about its physical delivery changes.

This is where the grid story from the previous chapter becomes economically important. Electricity did not merely move energy away from the coal pile. It helped separate the service from the user’s knowledge of the conversion chain.

A household connected to a grid does not need to decide which generator should respond when a lamp turns on. A refrigerator does not care whether the next kilowatt-hour comes from falling water, split uranium, natural gas, moving air, or sunlight collected a hundred miles away, so long as the electrical system delivers the required power within technical limits.

The user buys cold.

The network settles the physics.

This separation creates an odd accounting problem for a book about energy and civilization. If the energy system becomes better, its importance may become less visible precisely because people can stop thinking about it.

That is the opposite of how importance usually looks.

A scarce input announces itself. A plentiful and reliable input recedes.

When food is scarce, people talk about food. When water stops running, pipes become interesting. When electricity fails, everyone suddenly discovers substations. Functioning infrastructure has the peculiar habit of erasing itself from attention.

The same thing happens with efficiency.

Suppose two machines deliver identical mechanical work. One requires twice as much fuel. If we measure civilization by gross fuel consumption, the wasteful machine appears more energetic. If we measure useful capability, nothing has improved.

The distinction sounds obvious at the scale of one motor. It becomes slippery at the scale of an economy because energy changes form many times before anyone receives a service.

Coal can enter a power plant carrying chemical energy. Combustion releases heat. A turbine converts some of that heat into rotation. A generator turns the rotation into electricity. Transmission and distribution carry the electricity to a building. A motor converts electricity back into rotation. A pump moves water. The water cools a process. The process produces something people actually want.

At each step, some energy becomes less available for useful work. Thermodynamics is not sentimental about ambition.

The total quantity is conserved in the physicist’s sense. The ability to make the energy do what you want is not.

This is why energy analysts distinguish energy from exergy and from useful work. Exergy is a measure of how much useful work can in principle be extracted as a system moves toward equilibrium with its environment. Useful work is what survives real machines and real losses to perform an economic function: mechanical motion, useful heat, useful cooling, light, and related outputs.

The vocabulary can become intimidating faster than the idea deserves.

Not all joules are equally useful.

A kilowatt-hour of electricity arriving at a motor is a highly controllable input. The same amount of low-temperature waste heat drifting into summer air may contain energy in the formal accounting sense while being nearly useless for the task at hand.

Robert Ayres and Benjamin Warr pushed this distinction into economic history. In their work on U.S. growth, they argued that useful work—the output after conversion losses—tracks economic activity more meaningfully than raw energy inputs alone. Their growth model is a strong claim and should not be treated as a settled replacement for conventional growth economics. Capital, labor, institutions, knowledge, organization, trade, and technology do not disappear because useful work is included in a production function.

But the physical point underneath their model is difficult to escape.

An economy cannot use energy it fails to convert.

A steam engine that turns 5 percent of its fuel’s potential into useful mechanical work and a turbine system that turns far more into controlled output are not economically equivalent simply because the fuel ledger records the same heat content entering both.

Efficiency changes capability before it changes consumption.

That distinction will matter in the next chapter, because lower energy requirements per service do not guarantee lower total energy use. When a service becomes cheaper, people often buy more of it. Light is again the obvious example.

Fouquet and Pearson’s numbers pair an enormous decline in the real price of lighting with an enormous increase in lighting consumption. That is not a contradiction. It is the normal response to a service becoming dramatically more useful and affordable.

For centuries, darkness constrained activity because artificial light was expensive. Then the constraint weakened. People did not respond by preserving the old quantity of light and pocketing every efficiency gain. They illuminated streets, factories, shops, offices, hospitals, schools, stadiums, billboards, kitchens, warehouses, parking lots, porches, runways, tunnels, bedrooms, and eventually screens we carry in our pockets.

The efficiency gain created a larger market for illumination.

Whether that counts as an energy saving depends on what question is being asked.

Per lumen-hour, the answer is emphatically yes. Per household, city, or economy, the answer can be more complicated because the quantity of service changes.

This is one reason arguments about energy often collapse into confusion. Different people are answering different questions with the same word.

Efficiency can mean less energy per unit of service.

Energy intensity can mean less energy per unit of economic output.

Total energy consumption can still rise.

All three can happen at once.

Zsuzsanna Csereklyei, María del Mar Rubio-Varas, and David Stern examined this broader relationship using data from 99 countries from 1971 to 2010 alongside longer historical evidence. Their central stylized fact is not that richer societies stop using energy. Per-capita energy use tends to rise with income. But the relationship is less than proportional: as countries become richer, energy use per unit of economic output tends to fall.

Wealth and energy separate partially, not completely.

The same work also finds a long-run shift toward higher-quality energy carriers. That matters because a unit of electricity can perform many tasks with a degree of control that an equivalent quantity of low-grade heat cannot. An economy can therefore become more capable even when its raw energy accounting changes less dramatically than its services do.

This is not a loophole that allows the physical world to be ignored.

Quite the opposite.

The point is to account for the physical world more carefully.

Primary energy is one layer. Final energy delivered to users is another. Useful work after conversion is another. Energy service is another. Welfare is another still.

None can be substituted casually for the next.

A cold house using little energy may be efficient, or it may simply be cold. A wealthy household using more electricity may be wasteful, or it may be cooling a home in a deadly heat wave. A factory can reduce energy intensity by improving machinery, by outsourcing an energy-intensive process, by changing its product mix, or by shutting down. A country can appear to decouple growth from domestic energy use while importing goods whose energy use occurred elsewhere.

The denominator is doing political work even when the equation looks neutral.

This is the strongest counterargument to a simple story of progress through falling energy intensity. A ratio can improve while burdens are moved rather than removed.

Industrial economies have repeatedly displaced physical costs away from the point of consumption. Electricity moves combustion away from the lamp. Global supply chains move mining, smelting, assembly, and pollution away from the customer. Digital services can make an activity look weightless at the interface while data centers, transmission lines, cooling systems, chip fabs, mines, and power plants remain resolutely physical.

The interface gets cleaner faster than the system does.

This is not an argument against abstraction. Abstraction is one of civilization’s great achievements. It lets people use systems far more complicated than any one person could understand end to end.

A child can turn on a light without studying electrodynamics.

A surgeon can rely on sterile instruments without running a steel mill.

A programmer can rent computation without building a power plant.

The danger begins when abstraction becomes amnesia.

If the service is all we see, we can forget what has to remain true underneath it.

This book is interested in energy precisely because so much modern life is organized to hide energy successfully.

Consider refrigeration.

The service is not electricity. It is a narrow temperature range maintained for hours or days without the user thinking about heat flow. The refrigerator removes heat from an insulated box and dumps it into the room. The compressor cycles. The grid supplies power. The food remains edible longer.

The meaningful gain is not that society learned to consume electricity in kitchens.

The meaningful gain is that spoilage slowed.

That change alters shopping frequency, household labor, food distribution, restaurant logistics, medicine, vaccines, industrial chemistry, and the geography of supply chains. The energy service reaches outward into institutions that do not look like energy institutions at all.

The same is true of motive power.

A motor is useful because something moves when commanded. The customer does not value rotational energy in the abstract. The customer values the elevator arriving, the pump maintaining pressure, the machine tool cutting metal, the conveyor advancing a package, the fan moving air.

Computation pushes the abstraction even further.

A person asking a search engine a question is separated by so many layers from the physical energy involved that the question appears almost immaterial. Fingers move. Radio signals travel. Routers forward packets. Servers execute instructions. Storage devices retrieve data. Cooling systems remove heat. Power supplies convert voltage. Generators somewhere replenish the electricity being consumed.

The final service can be a sentence.

The physical chain can span continents.

This is where the title of the chapter becomes literal. Energy does not become invisible because it vanishes. It becomes invisible because technology moves attention upward in the stack.

The better the lower layers work, the more the user can think in terms of outcomes.

A candle asks to be managed.

A light switch asks to be touched.

A voice assistant may not even require that.

The physical burden has migrated into infrastructure.

This migration has distributional consequences.

When energy services are delivered through networks, appliances, buildings, and capital equipment, access depends on more than the existence of energy somewhere in the economy. A household may live near a power plant and still lack reliable service. An inefficient rental property can impose large heating or cooling bills on tenants who do not control the building envelope. A household that cannot afford an efficient appliance may pay more over time for the same service.

Cheap energy services are partly a technology story and partly an institution story.

Who finances the equipment? Who owns the building? Who maintains the grid? Who bears the upfront cost? Who gets disconnected? Who lives near the pollution? Who can wait for a return on an efficiency investment?

These questions prevent the service framework from becoming a celebration of engineering detached from politics.

They also sharpen what engineering actually accomplishes.

When a heat pump delivers several units of heat movement for each unit of electricity consumed, that is a physical advantage. Whether a tenant receives the benefit depends on rent, installation cost, building design, climate, electricity price, and ownership. The coefficient of performance does not settle the social outcome.

Likewise, a more efficient light source can make illumination cheap, but cheap illumination can be used for a schoolroom or an advertising screen. Technology expands the feasible set. It does not choose the objective.

Capability is not welfare.

This distinction matters for the book’s central thesis.

If energy is fundamental to civilization, we should expect human advance to track more than gross energy consumption. We should expect progress to appear in the amount of useful capability extracted from energy and in the cost of obtaining the services people value.

That prediction fits the history of lighting much better than a simple fuel ledger does.

The astonishing fact is not that modern Britain burns or consumes some multiple of the energy used in 1800. The astonishing fact is that an ordinary person can summon quantities of clean, steady light that would have been ruinously expensive two centuries earlier and can do so with almost no labor devoted to the act of illumination itself.

The same pattern appears across modern life.

Mechanical work became cheaper.

Transport became faster and less laborious.

Heating and cooling became controllable.

Food could be refrigerated.

Water could be pumped and treated.

Information could be stored, copied, transmitted, and computed at costs that repeatedly collapsed.

Each service has its own history and its own external costs. Not all improve monotonically. Some become cheap enough to create new harms. Some remain scarce because networks, land, capital, regulation, or materials become the bottleneck. Some reach saturation. Some generate demand faster than efficiency can reduce their resource requirements.

But the direction of technical progress is often the same: more service from less attention to the energy underneath.

That is why a modern economy can feel less energy-intensive than an older one even while depending on energy more completely.

A blacksmith watched fuel become heat and heat become work.

A factory worker could see the shaft turning overhead.

A household once watched the coal bin empty.

Today the service often arrives through an interface.

The causal chain has grown longer. Dependence has not grown weaker.

In some ways it has grown stronger because more activities now assume that cheap, high-quality energy will be available continuously. A hospital cannot compensate for unreliable electricity by reminding itself that average annual energy supply is adequate. A data center cannot run on statistical abundance. An elevator needs power when the passenger presses the button.

The service perspective therefore points toward reliability as well as efficiency.

The value of an energy system is not simply how much energy it contains or even how efficiently it converts that energy. It matters whether useful output can be delivered where and when it is wanted, at a cost people can bear, without external costs overwhelming the gain.

This is a harder standard than consumption.

It is also closer to what civilization is for.

The candle did something extraordinary. It let humans purchase a little more day.

Two centuries of lighting technology did not merely make candles cheaper. It made the old rationing problem almost disappear for billions of people with reliable electricity access.

Once the service became cheap, demand exploded. Streets glowed. Factories ran after sunset. Homes filled with lamps. Cities became visible from orbit.

Efficiency did not end appetite.

It changed the price of appetite.

That is the paradox waiting in the next chapter.
