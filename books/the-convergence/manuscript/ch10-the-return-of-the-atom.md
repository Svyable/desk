# The Return of the Atom

A nuclear reactor is an odd machine for a civilization in a hurry.

The fuel is almost absurdly concentrated. The plant is not.

A few tonnes of uranium can support years of operation, but the useful machine around that fuel is a cathedral of concrete, steel, pumps, valves, cabling, control systems, cooling systems, inspections, procedures, trained operators, emergency plans, financing agreements, regulators, suppliers, and accumulated engineering judgment. The fission reaction itself is not the part that takes a decade to build.

This is what makes nuclear power unusually revealing.

In the physical sense, it is one of the most concentrated energy technologies humans have ever made practical. In the institutional sense, it may be one of the least forgiving. A country can understand the physics perfectly and still be bad at building reactors.

That distinction matters now because electricity demand is rising, low-carbon firm power is valuable, and the world has begun talking about nuclear energy with a confidence that would have sounded unfashionable not long ago.

The return is real, but it is not uniform.

The International Atomic Energy Agency’s Power Reactor Information System listed a little more than four hundred reactors operating worldwide in 2026 and roughly seventy more under construction. The International Energy Agency reported that ten reactor construction starts occurred in 2025, nine of them in China and one in Russia. Over the preceding decade, 94 percent of reactors that began construction used Chinese or Russian designs.

That is not the picture of a universal nuclear renaissance.

It is the picture of a technology whose momentum is concentrated in places that have retained the ability to build it repeatedly.

The distinction between knowing how and being able to do is easy to miss in discussions of energy. We often talk about technologies as though they sit on a shelf waiting for capital. Solar panel. Battery. Gas turbine. Reactor. Pick one, pay the price, connect it to the grid.

A reactor does not behave that way.

Its price is partly a property of the machine and partly a property of the institution attempting to produce the machine.

Jessica Lovering, Arthur Yip, and Ted Nordhaus assembled historical reactor-construction cost data across several countries and found exactly the pattern that simple arguments about nuclear tend to erase. Costs did not move along one universal curve. Some national programs achieved long runs of declining or comparatively stable costs. Others experienced severe escalation.

The same broad technology family produced different economic histories.

That makes two common statements about nuclear equally unsatisfying.

“Nuclear is cheap.”

“Nuclear is expensive.”

Compared with what, built where, by whom, financed how, under which design, after how many previous units, and with what definition of cost?

Those questions are not evasions. They are the subject.

Consider the recent American experience at Plant Vogtle in Georgia.

Construction on Units 3 and 4 began in 2009. The two AP1000 reactors entered commercial operation in 2023 and 2024. Georgia Power now has two large new reactors expected to operate for decades, producing firm electricity without direct carbon emissions from generation. The physical asset is substantial and real.

So were the delays and cost escalation.

By 2021, Georgia Power’s forecast share of project capital cost had risen to $9.2 billion, above the $7.3 billion the state regulator had previously approved for recovery. The project had also lived through contractor bankruptcy, redesign and rework, supply-chain problems, workforce challenges, and the pandemic.

Calling Vogtle a failure is too simple. Calling it a success without discussing what it cost to reach operation is too simple in the other direction.

The reactors work.

The construction system struggled.

France offers a similarly uncomfortable example. Work on the Flamanville 3 EPR began in 2007. The reactor was initially expected to enter service in 2012 at an estimated construction cost of about €3.3 billion. EDF later put its estimated completion cost at €13.2 billion in 2015 euros, excluding interim interest. The reactor first connected to the grid in December 2024 and reached full nuclear power in December 2025.

Again, the ending complicates the slogan.

Flamanville is not evidence that an EPR cannot work. The reactor exists, fissions uranium, heats water, makes steam, and produces electricity.

It is evidence that getting from a finished design to a finished plant can consume years and billions that a technology comparison made on a whiteboard may not capture.

There is a temptation to blame regulation for everything that went wrong.

Some regulation is slow, inconsistent, duplicative, or poorly designed. Nuclear projects can suffer when requirements change during construction, when design reviews and licensing are poorly sequenced, or when multiple institutions share authority without sharing a schedule. A regulatory process that cannot distinguish meaningful safety work from procedural accumulation imposes real cost.

But “regulation” is too broad a bucket to explain a welded pipe installed incorrectly.

Construction quality matters. Supplier capability matters. Design maturity matters. Sequencing matters. Skilled labor matters. Project management matters. A safety regulator may discover a defect without having caused the defect.

This is one reason nuclear arguments so often become ideological. The technology’s disappointments and strengths are both large enough to support selective storytelling.

Supporters can point to extraordinary energy density, high capacity factors, small land footprints per unit of generation, low operational carbon emissions, long plant lives, and fuel inventories that can be stockpiled far more easily than continuous gas supply.

Critics can point to catastrophic accidents, radioactive waste, large upfront capital requirements, difficult decommissioning, construction overruns, long project schedules, proliferation concerns, and the political consequences of asking communities to accept a risk they may regard as qualitatively different from other industrial risks.

Both lists are true enough to be useless unless the question is narrower.

The useful question for this book is whether nuclear power expands the controllable energy available to civilization at a cost and speed institutions can repeatedly manage.

Sometimes it clearly does.

France built most of its present nuclear fleet within a comparatively compressed period and has for decades generated a large share of its electricity from reactors. South Korea developed a standardized domestic program and then exported the APR1400 design to the United Arab Emirates. At Barakah, four units entered commercial operation in sequence from 2021 through 2024.

The contrast with Vogtle and Flamanville is not proof that one country possesses a secret recipe.

Projects differ in labor markets, financing, regulation, design maturity, supply chains, ownership structure, site conditions, and how much prior experience the builder can draw on. Export projects also sit inside political and commercial arrangements that are hard to reproduce elsewhere.

Still, successful programs weaken the strongest anti-nuclear claim.

If nuclear construction were inherently incapable of predictable delivery, repeated programs could not perform materially better than one-off projects. They sometimes do.

The opposite inference should be resisted too.

A country cannot import institutional competence merely by ordering the same reactor model.

A standardized design helps only if the rest of the system is capable of repeating it. The welders have to be trained. The suppliers have to qualify components. The regulator has to know the design well enough to inspect it consistently. The project manager has to sequence thousands of tasks. The owner has to make decisions before indecision becomes delay. Financing has to survive years in which money goes out and no electricity comes back.

Serial construction is partly a technology of memory.

The first unit teaches lessons. The second can use them. The third should not have to rediscover how the first poured concrete, routed cable, qualified a supplier, or handled a regulator’s question.

If the series stops for twenty years, some of that memory leaves with the workforce.

This helps explain why the recent nuclear experience of advanced economies is so awkward. A country can remain excellent at operating existing plants while losing the industrial muscle required to build new ones.

Operation and construction are different capabilities.

A utility can run a forty-year-old reactor at a high capacity factor without possessing an experienced new-build supply chain. A regulator can be highly competent at overseeing operating plants while facing a first-of-a-kind licensing process. A nation can have nuclear engineers and still lack enough qualified specialty manufacturers or construction supervisors.

The machinery of competence has a half-life too.

That is where finance enters the reactor building.

For a gas plant, fuel costs dominate much of the economic life. For a nuclear plant, a much larger share of cost arrives before the first kilowatt-hour is sold. The project borrows, spends, and accumulates financing charges while concrete is poured and equipment is installed.

Time therefore becomes money with unusual force.

The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency has shown how sensitive nuclear economics are to the cost of capital. In one illustration, financing costs can account for roughly two-thirds of the cost of nuclear electricity at a 9 percent cost of capital, but less than one-third at 3 percent.

The reactor is the same.

The financial system around it is not.

This is why debates that compare overnight construction cost without comparing financing assumptions can produce nonsense with impressive decimal places. A project financed by a sovereign or regulated utility under a stable long-term framework does not face the same capital cost as a merchant generator exposed to construction risk, power-price risk, and political risk for a decade before revenue arrives.

The cost of capital is not a footnote to nuclear construction. It is one of the technologies.

That sounds metaphorical. It is not.

Lower financing cost changes the number of projects that can exist. Predictable regulation changes financing cost. Repeat construction changes perceived construction risk. A stronger supply chain changes schedule risk. A shorter schedule reduces interest during construction. The pieces reinforce one another.

Or they reinforce failure.

A delayed project accumulates financing charges. The higher final cost produces political backlash. The backlash reduces confidence in the next project. A long pause erodes the workforce and supplier base. The next plant begins with less experience and a higher risk premium.

The machine can enter a negative learning curve because the institution around it has stopped learning.

This is the nuclear version of the constraint migration described in the previous chapter.

Uranium is not scarce in the same sense that transmission capacity may be scarce at a particular substation. The energy density of the fuel solves one physical constraint so completely that other constraints become more visible: qualified fabrication, concrete quality, project sequencing, regulatory competence, public consent, financing, cooling water, and the ability to carry knowledge from one build to the next.

The atom is abundant enough to expose the bureaucracy.

That line should not be read as an argument for abolishing the bureaucracy.

Nuclear plants can fail in ways that justify unusual care. The history of Three Mile Island, Chernobyl, and Fukushima makes any serious discussion of nuclear power different from a discussion of a solar inverter. Low-frequency, high-consequence accidents change public tolerance, insurance arrangements, emergency planning, and regulatory philosophy.

A civilization interested in abundant energy cannot simply wish those concerns away.

It also cannot evaluate safety by counting only spectacular accidents.

Fossil power imposes harms through air pollution, mining, extraction, transport, routine combustion, and climate change. Hydropower changes rivers and can fail catastrophically. Batteries and renewable technologies require mining, manufacturing, land, and disposal systems. Every energy system moves risk around.

The relevant comparison is not nuclear versus innocence.

It is nuclear versus the alternatives capable of supplying the same service within the same power system.

That service matters.

A nuclear plant is not merely a pile of annual megawatt-hours. Once operating, it can provide large amounts of electricity from a compact site with relatively little fuel movement and high availability. In a system with large shares of weather-dependent generation, that kind of firm output can be valuable even when its standalone levelized cost is higher than the cheapest solar or wind project.

But “firm” does not mean “flexible in every way.”

Large reactors are big individual units. An unexpected outage can remove more than a gigawatt at once. Plants require maintenance and refueling. Cooling systems can be constrained by heat or water conditions. Transmission must be strong enough to carry the output away from the site. A fleet with many large reactors creates its own reliability planning requirements.

Once again, there is no free energy technology. There are systems with different failure modes.

Small modular reactors are meant to change some of nuclear’s economics by changing the unit of construction.

The promise is attractive: smaller modules, more factory fabrication, less capital at risk per project, simpler replication, and the possibility of adding capacity in increments rather than betting on a single multi-gigawatt site.

The promise is not yet the same thing as a global operating record.

The IEA expects the first wave of commercial SMR projects to emerge around 2030. Canada began construction in 2026 on a 300-megawatt unit at Darlington, one of the most important tests of whether standardized smaller reactors can convert the factory-learning argument into lower delivered cost.

For now, an SMR is best treated as a hypothesis with concrete attached.

Its economics will depend on whether factory repetition compensates for losing some of the scale advantages of very large reactors. A smaller pressure vessel is not automatically a cheaper megawatt. The hoped-for learning curve arrives only if enough nearly identical units are ordered for factories and crews to learn.

Volume is not a side effect of standardization. It is the condition that makes standardization valuable.

This is why the current geography of nuclear construction deserves more attention than the rhetoric of revival.

The IEA’s 2026 review found roughly 78 gigawatts of nuclear capacity under construction worldwide, one of the highest totals in three decades. Half was in China. Advanced economies together represented a much smaller share.

The atom has returned more strongly in places that kept building atoms.

That is an institutional fact hiding inside an energy statistic.

It suggests a narrower conclusion than either nuclear enthusiasm or nuclear rejection usually allows.

Fission is a mature physical technology with an unsettled production technology.

Humans know how to split heavy nuclei, control the reaction, remove the heat, make steam, turn a turbine, and operate reactors for decades. The frontier is increasingly the ability to reproduce the entire socio-technical system around those steps at predictable cost and schedule.

That system includes safety.

It includes finance.

It includes public legitimacy.

It includes whether the fifth project can remember the fourth.

A country that can do those things may find nuclear power an unusually durable source of firm low-carbon electricity. A country that cannot may discover that the densest practical fuel on Earth still produces scarce electricity because the bottleneck moved outside the core.

The lesson is larger than nuclear.

Civilization does not advance merely by discovering powerful physical processes. It advances when those processes become repeatable, financeable, governable, maintainable, and ordinary enough to disappear into the background of daily life.

Fire had to become a hearth. Coal had to become an industrial system. Electricity had to become a grid.

Fission has to become a construction capability.

The next chapter turns from a technology whose physics are mature and whose institutions remain uneven to a wider field of technologies moving at very different speeds: batteries and solar already scaling by the terawatt-hour and gigawatt, geothermal trying to borrow techniques from oil and gas, and fusion still fighting for the right to be called an energy source at all.

The interesting frontier is no longer a single machine.

It is the rate at which different machines can become ordinary.
