# The Return of the Atom

In July 2023, a new nuclear reactor began commercial operation in Georgia.

That would have been an ordinary sentence in another era. In the United States of the early 1970s, utilities were ordering reactors by the dozen. By the time Vogtle Unit 3 entered service, however, the event had acquired the weight of a historical return. The Georgia Public Service Commission called it the first newly constructed U.S. reactor to become operational in more than thirty years. Unit 4 followed in 2024.

The plant near Waynesboro is therefore an awkward place to begin a chapter about nuclear power, which is exactly why it is useful. Vogtle is evidence for nearly every argument people make about fission at once.

The reactors are large sources of electricity that do not emit carbon dioxide while operating. They can run at high output for long periods. Their fuel contains energy at a density that makes coal and gas look bulky. Once built, reactors can operate for decades. In a power system increasingly shaped by weather-dependent generation and rapidly growing electrical loads, those qualities have become newly valuable.

Vogtle also took far longer and cost far more than originally expected.

Georgia Power’s share of the project was originally certified at $4.418 billion. By the final prudency proceedings, the company reported roughly $10.2 billion in construction and capital costs for its share before certain adjustments, while the Georgia Public Service Commission approved $7.562 billion as recoverable construction and capital cost under a settlement. Financing added billions more. The commission’s own record is unusually useful because it separates money spent, money judged recoverable from customers, and financing rather than collapsing them into one headline “cost.”

The two new units ultimately work. The argument is over what it took to make them work.

That is the modern nuclear problem in miniature.

The physics of fission has not become mysterious. Commercial reactors have been producing electricity for more than half a century. The International Atomic Energy Agency’s Power Reactor Information System records operating history, generation, outages, construction dates, and technical characteristics across the global fleet. The hard question is not whether uranium can boil water and turn a turbine. It is whether institutions can repeatedly turn a reactor design into a finished power station at a cost and schedule that a modern electricity system can tolerate.

Nuclear power is unusually good at exposing the difference between a technology and a production system.

A reactor is often discussed as if it were a machine that can be priced like a turbine or a battery cell. In reality, a large nuclear project is closer to a temporary industrial civilization. It requires thousands of workers, specialized concrete and steel, forgings, pumps, valves, cables, instrumentation, quality assurance, regulators, inspectors, financing, supply chains, trained operators, and years of coordinated construction. The final plant contains a reactor, but the project that creates the plant is an institution made physical.

That distinction helps explain why nuclear cost arguments are so unsatisfying when reduced to a single global number.

Jessica Lovering, Arthur Yip, and Ted Nordhaus assembled reactor-level historical construction-cost data across seven countries and found sharply different trajectories. Some national programs experienced escalating costs. Others achieved periods of stability or decline. The same broad physical technology family did not produce one universal economic curve.

The variation is not noise around a single nuclear truth. It is the subject.

France provides the classic counterpoint to the troubled one-off project. Its nuclear program is often compressed into a heroic story about the 1973 oil shock and Prime Minister Pierre Messmer’s 1974 decision to accelerate reactor construction. The real history was slower and more institutional.

France had been building nuclear expertise for decades. It had created research institutions, trained engineers, developed fuel-cycle capabilities, and argued internally over reactor technologies. In 1969 it chose to abandon its domestic natural-uranium graphite-gas design in favor of pressurized-water reactors. An initial program was approved before the oil shock. The Messmer plan accelerated something that already had an industrial base beneath it.

The result was extraordinary by modern construction standards. During the most intensive decade, France connected roughly four to six reactors of 900 or 1,300 megawatts to the grid each year. The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency’s 2024 review of nuclear new-build financing and project management emphasizes that this achievement depended on more than a political announcement. Standardization, a capable utility, a mature industrial fabric, long-term state commitment, financing arrangements, and a community of engineers and managers had accumulated over decades.

France did not discover a shortcut around nuclear complexity. It built an organization capable of repeating it.

Repetition matters because a first-of-a-kind project and a serial program are different economic objects.

When a design changes during construction, workers relearn tasks. Suppliers fabricate components they may never have made before. Quality problems can trigger rework in systems where documentation and tolerances are strict. A delayed project keeps capital tied up while producing no electricity, and the financing clock continues running. Skilled crews disperse when the pipeline disappears, taking tacit knowledge with them. Years later, the next project may be described as another copy even though much of the production system has to be rebuilt.

This is one reason construction duration matters almost as much as overnight cost.

Overnight cost is an estimate of what a plant would cost if financing during construction could be ignored—as if it appeared overnight. Real reactors do not. A project that takes ten years exposes its investors to ten years of interest, inflation, schedule risk, management cost, and changing market conditions before the asset earns revenue. Two plants with similar physical construction costs can have very different delivered electricity costs if one takes twice as long to finish.

The distinction is central to Vogtle. Georgia Power’s regulatory filings separately tracked construction and capital costs, allowance for funds used during construction, and financing collected from customers. Those categories are not interchangeable, and neither are the burdens they create.

The same distinction complicates comparisons with solar, wind, batteries, and gas turbines. A solar farm can often be built in modules and begin producing value relatively quickly. A large reactor concentrates a great deal of capital into one project whose revenue arrives late. That makes nuclear unusually sensitive to the cost of capital and the credibility of the schedule.

Cheap financing can make a capital-intensive reactor look much more competitive. Expensive financing can make the same physical plant look ruinous.

This is not a special pleading for nuclear. It is a property of infrastructure with high upfront cost and long construction periods.

The reward for surviving that construction problem is an asset with unusual characteristics.

Nuclear fuel is extraordinarily energy dense. Refueling intervals are long. Existing reactors can operate at high capacity factors, meaning they produce near their rated output for much of the year apart from refueling and maintenance. Their land footprint per unit of generation can be small. Operating emissions of carbon dioxide are low, while lifecycle emissions remain low relative to fossil generation even after construction and fuel-cycle activities are counted.

Those characteristics matter more in an electricity system than a simple comparison of annual megawatt-hours suggests.

Chapter 9 argued that the constraint is migrating into the network. A grid with abundant variable generation still has to match supply and demand through every hour, survive outages, move electricity across congested lines, and carry enough dependable capacity through difficult weather. Storage, transmission, flexible demand, overbuilding, firm generation, and interregional trade can all contribute. Nuclear is one possible firm low-carbon resource inside that portfolio.

Calling it “firm” does not mean infallible.

Reactors have planned outages. They can trip unexpectedly. Large units create large contingencies: when a gigawatt-scale reactor disconnects, the system must replace a gigawatt-scale loss quickly. Heat waves, drought, cooling-water constraints, maintenance problems, or common-mode technical issues can affect output. A fleet built around identical designs can gain from standardization while also sharing vulnerabilities.

Reliability is a system property, not a personality trait of one technology.

The strongest case for nuclear is therefore not that it replaces every other source. It is that a high-electricity system may value a diverse set of resources with different failure modes, fuel requirements, land needs, and production profiles.

The strongest case against a large nuclear expansion is equally concrete: if each new plant requires a decade or more of construction, repeatedly overruns its budget, and needs extraordinary financing or political intervention to survive, then its physical advantages may arrive too slowly and expensively to matter at the required scale.

Both propositions can be true in different places.

This is why the phrase “nuclear is expensive” is incomplete. So is “nuclear is cheap.”

A more useful question is: which nuclear, built where, by whom, under what financing structure, using what design, after how many prior units, with what supply chain, and measured at which boundary?

Lovering and her coauthors found that national construction-cost experience diverged enough to make this unavoidable. Nuclear economics is partly reactor physics, but much of the variance appears after the physics is settled.

South Korea, France, the United States, Germany, Canada, Japan, and India did not build identical institutions around reactors. They differed in standardization, project sequencing, industrial policy, regulation, labor, financing, utility structure, and the continuity of construction programs. Even within one country, performance changed across time.

That makes nuclear an unusually sharp test of the larger thesis of this book.

Energy abundance is never just a resource waiting to be unlocked. Coal needed mines, railways, engines, finance, and labor. Electricity needed generators, standards, grids, utilities, and regulation. Nuclear fission adds another layer: the energy inside the fuel is so concentrated that the surrounding institutional system becomes more visible, not less.

The atom is abundant in one sense and demanding in another.

A small quantity of fuel can support enormous output. But the machinery and governance required to control the reaction are elaborate because the consequences of losing control can be severe.

Safety is not an external concern added after the economics. It shapes the economics.

Nuclear plants contain multiple engineered barriers and redundant safety systems. Components important to safety are manufactured, installed, documented, tested, and inspected under requirements stricter than those governing ordinary industrial construction. These requirements impose cost and time because society has decided that a low-probability, high-consequence technology should not be built casually.

Some of that burden is unavoidable. Some may reflect regulatory processes that can be improved. Distinguishing the two is harder than slogans about “red tape” imply.

The historical safety record also resists simplification. Severe nuclear accidents are rare, but they are not imaginary. Three Mile Island, Chernobyl, and Fukushima changed regulation, public opinion, plant design, and the economics of nuclear power far beyond the damaged sites themselves. Chernobyl involved a reactor design and operating system with characteristics very different from most current Western reactors. Fukushima followed an earthquake and tsunami that overwhelmed plant protections. The accidents differ technically, but together they demonstrate why nuclear institutions are judged not only by average performance but by tail risk.

Fossil generation has tail risks too, along with chronic harms that are easier to normalize because they are dispersed: air pollution, mining deaths, explosions, methane leakage, and climate change. A fair comparison cannot count nuclear accidents while treating fossil damage as background.

Nor can a fair nuclear argument ignore waste.

Used nuclear fuel is compact in volume compared with the enormous material flows of fossil combustion, but its radioactivity requires isolation and stewardship over long periods. Geological disposal is technically developed and is moving toward implementation in some countries, yet political consent and institutional durability remain difficult. The quantity is manageable; the obligation is real.

Mining, enrichment, plant construction, decommissioning, and waste management also carry material and environmental costs. Low-carbon does not mean impact-free.

The distribution of those costs matters. A reactor can provide electricity to millions while concentrating construction disruption, emergency planning, water use, tax dependence, or waste responsibilities in particular communities. Nuclear facilities can also become durable sources of skilled employment and local revenue. As with transmission lines, mines, dams, and data centers, national benefit does not erase local consequence.

Then there is proliferation.

Civil nuclear power and nuclear weapons are not the same technology, but parts of the fuel cycle overlap with capabilities that can matter for weapons programs. Enrichment and reprocessing deserve particular scrutiny. The international safeguards system exists because the energy density that makes fission useful also comes from nuclear processes with strategic significance.

None of these complications make nuclear uniquely disqualified. They make it unusually institutional.

That institutional burden is part of why recent interest in small modular reactors has been so intense. The promise is not merely a smaller reactor. It is a different production model: more factory fabrication, smaller increments of capital, repeatable designs, shorter site schedules, and the possibility of learning through series rather than rebuilding a bespoke megaproject each time.

The promise remains largely a promise.

A smaller unit sacrifices some economies of physical scale. Factory production only becomes cheap if enough units are ordered to keep factories and suppliers busy. Licensing a standardized design does not guarantee standardized construction. First projects still carry first-of-a-kind risk. The economic case depends on whether manufacturing learning and schedule reduction can outweigh the disadvantages of smaller size.

This is exactly the kind of claim the next decade can test.

If small reactors begin entering service repeatedly at predictable cost and schedule, the nuclear production system will have changed. If they remain a succession of expensive demonstrations and canceled order books, changing the reactor’s size will not have solved the institutional problem.

The same discipline applies to advanced reactors promising higher temperatures, different coolants, passive safety, better fuel utilization, or new industrial uses. Their physical ideas may be compelling. Until projects operate, their commercial performance remains a forecast.

The distinction between demonstrated physics and demonstrated production is the line this book has tried to hold throughout.

Fission has already crossed the physics threshold. It has crossed the commercial threshold too; hundreds of reactors have operated around the world. What remains unsettled is whether new nuclear construction can recover the repeatability required for another large expansion, especially in countries that allowed their construction ecosystems to atrophy.

The IAEA’s reactor database makes the split visible. Some countries are adding reactors and maintaining active construction programs. Others operate large fleets while building little. A global reactor count therefore hides two different questions: how valuable are existing nuclear assets, and how capable are institutions of producing new ones?

Existing plants often benefit from the fact that their construction risk is already behind them. Keeping a safe reactor operating can be economically different from deciding to build a new one. A system can rationally value its existing nuclear fleet while remaining cautious about new megaprojects.

This distinction also explains the renewed interest in extending reactor lifetimes. If a plant can safely operate for additional decades after refurbishment, the system gains firm low-carbon generation without repeating the full civil-construction project. Lifetime extension is not free, and aging management matters, but the capital problem is different.

So is uprating an existing reactor, restarting a closed one, or adding a new unit at a site with transmission, cooling, workforce, and licensing infrastructure already present.

The return of the atom may therefore look less like the first nuclear age than advocates or critics expect.

It could include a mixture of long-lived existing plants, life extensions, uprates, selected large reactors in countries with capable build programs, smaller reactors if their production model proves itself, and advanced designs where they earn a commercial role. It may coexist with enormous solar, wind, storage, and transmission buildout rather than replacing them.

The important variable is not ideological purity. It is whether a technology can deliver a valuable energy service within the constraints of the system around it.

For nuclear, the service is unusually attractive: dense, controllable, low-carbon electricity that can operate for decades.

The constraint is equally clear: society must build and govern the machine well enough that the promise survives contact with concrete, steel, finance, regulation, and time.

Vogtle demonstrates both sides. Unit 3 and Unit 4 are real reactors delivering real electricity. The construction record is also real. Pretending either half away makes the lesson smaller.

France demonstrates something different. A country that invests for decades in a standardized industrial system can build at a pace that looks almost alien from the perspective of a one-off modern megaproject. That record does not prove it can be copied automatically. It shows that the reactor cannot be separated from the institution that reproduces it.

This is what would force the chapter’s thesis to narrow. If new nuclear projects across very different institutional settings began converging on similar cost and schedule simply because reactor technology improved, then the importance assigned here to production systems would be overstated. If, in the other direction, even mature standardized programs consistently failed to build economically while firm low-carbon alternatives supplied the same system value faster and more cheaply, nuclear’s role in an abundant electricity future would shrink.

The evidence today supports neither universal conclusion.

The atom did not leave. The ability to build around it did, in some places.

Its return will be measured less by announcements than by repetition.
