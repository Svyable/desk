# The Return of the Atom

Before asking what happens when energy becomes abundant, it is worth looking at a technology that has been promising abundance for longer than most of its advocates have been alive.

In 2024, three large nuclear reactors reached the grid in three very different places.

Barakah Unit 4 began commercial operation in the United Arab Emirates, completing a four-reactor program built in sequence. Vogtle Unit 4 entered commercial operation in Georgia, the second of the first new U.S. reactors completed in more than three decades. Flamanville 3 finally connected to the French grid in December after one of the most notorious construction delays in modern nuclear history.

The fuel in all three plants obeys the same physics.

The projects did not.

That difference is the interesting part.

Nuclear power occupies an odd place in the history of energy. Its scientific basis is mature. Commercial reactors have been splitting atoms for electricity since the middle of the twentieth century. The energy density of uranium remains almost comically high beside chemical fuels. A reactor can produce large amounts of low-carbon electricity from a compact site, run through nights and winters, and operate for decades.

None of those facts tells you how long it will take to build the next plant.

The atom is not new. The production system around it is the problem.

That distinction matters because nuclear is often discussed as though it were a referendum. Cheap or expensive. Safe or dangerous. Necessary or obsolete. Renaissance or failure.

Those binaries obscure the actual record.

At the end of 2025, the International Energy Agency counted about 420 gigawatts of nuclear capacity operating worldwide. Construction was substantial: by early 2026, roughly 78 gigawatts of reactors were under construction in fifteen countries, one of the highest levels in three decades.

But the map was lopsided. Half of the capacity under construction was in China. Ten reactor projects began construction in 2025; nine were in China and one in Russia. Across the preceding decade, 94 percent of reactors that started construction used Chinese or Russian designs.

That is not what a uniform global renaissance looks like.

It looks more like a technology whose ability to reproduce itself has become concentrated.

This is a different kind of bottleneck from the transformer in the previous chapter. A transformer is a component. Nuclear construction requires a whole temporary civilization: designers, welders, regulators, financiers, concrete suppliers, quality-assurance systems, specialized manufacturers, inspectors, operators, project managers, and political institutions willing to keep making decisions across many years.

The reactor vessel may be the spectacular object. The repeatable institution is the scarce one.

Barakah makes the point cleanly.

The United Arab Emirates did not begin by inventing a novel reactor. It selected the Korean APR1400 design and built four units at one site. Construction started on Unit 1 in 2012, Unit 2 in 2013, Unit 3 in 2014, and Unit 4 in 2015. The units then entered commercial operation in 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2024.

That sequence is almost suspiciously neat by nuclear standards.

It did not happen because concrete suddenly became easy or nuclear-grade welding ceased to be demanding. It happened inside a program designed around repetition. The same site, a common design, overlapping crews, an experienced Korean supplier network, a regulator learning alongside the project, and a national government treating the plant as a strategic program rather than four unrelated construction jobs all mattered.

The fourth unit did not have to rediscover the first unit.

This is what people usually mean when they invoke learning curves in nuclear power, but the phrase can become too smooth. A reactor is not a solar module. It is not stamped out by the million on an automated line. Site conditions differ. Regulation evolves. components arrive from large supplier networks. Civil works remain stubbornly physical. A single defect can stop progress while thousands of people wait.

Still, repetition can move a large project away from improvisation.

It changes the questions workers need to answer. The second team has seen the drawing before. The regulator has reviewed the same system. The supplier knows where documentation failed last time. The sequence of lifts is no longer theoretical. The project schedule contains fewer firsts.

That is industrial knowledge, and it is easy to lose.

Vogtle shows what the loss can cost.

Construction of Units 3 and 4 began in 2009. They were Westinghouse AP1000 reactors, the first new nuclear units built in the United States in more than thirty years. Unit 3 entered commercial operation in July 2023. Unit 4 followed on April 29, 2024.

By then, the project had survived years of delay, the bankruptcy of Westinghouse, contractor turnover, design and construction problems, and repeated cost increases.

Georgia's Public Service Commission eventually approved an agreement limiting the amount of Georgia Power's construction and capital costs recoverable from ratepayers to $7.562 billion. The utility expected its own construction and capital cost for the two units to reach roughly $10.75 billion. That figure did not represent the entire cost of the plant, because other co-owners paid their shares and financing sits awkwardly beside construction accounting. It is nevertheless enough to kill the fantasy that cost is a single number handed down by reactor physics.

The number depends on what is counted, who pays it, when the money is spent, and how long the money waits before the plant earns revenue.

Nuclear is unusually sensitive to that waiting.

A solar farm can begin producing while other projects are still being developed. A gas plant has fuel costs but often a shorter construction schedule. A large nuclear reactor absorbs capital for years before commercial operation. Interest compounds during that period, which means delay is not merely inconvenient. Delay changes the economics of the machine.

The International Energy Agency describes this directly. New nuclear projects are difficult to finance because they combine scale, capital intensity, technical complexity, long construction lead times, and the possibility of large overruns. Commercial lenders want predictable future cash flows. Governments therefore tend to appear somewhere in the structure, even in projects that are formally private: as owner, guarantor, regulator, rate-setter, buyer, or bearer of construction risk.

The electricity market is only part of the reactor.

Finance is another subsystem.

This is why two arguments that sound contradictory can both be true. Nuclear plants can have low fuel costs and long operating lives once completed. New nuclear construction can also be financially punishing when schedules slip and the cost of capital is high.

The operating plant and the building project are different economic objects.

Flamanville 3 makes that distinction impossible to ignore.

Construction began in 2007. The reactor was intended to demonstrate France's next generation of European Pressurized Reactor technology. Instead, the project accumulated delays associated with engineering, manufacturing, welding, quality control, and regulatory requirements. EDF's estimate of completion cost eventually rose to €13.2 billion in 2015 euros, excluding interim interest.

The reactor first connected to the French grid on December 21, 2024.

Even then, it was not finished learning how to be a power plant.

Startup testing continued through 2025. Flamanville reached full nuclear power only on December 14 of that year, nearly a year after first grid connection. The distinction between “connected” and “fully operational” is not pedantry. Nuclear startup is itself a staged industrial process: fuel loading, first criticality, low-power testing, grid synchronization, power ascension, inspections, trips, corrections, more testing.

A giant machine has to prove itself while becoming itself.

Flamanville is often used as evidence that nuclear power is inherently too slow and expensive to build. That is understandable. A project that starts in 2007 and reaches full power in 2025 is not a public-relations problem. It is an industrial fact.

But one failed production system does not prove that every production system must fail in the same way.

The stronger argument is harder on both sides.

Nuclear advocates cannot explain away every delay as regulation, politics, or incompetence. The technology itself makes demanding institutions necessary. Large reactors contain thousands of safety-significant components. Quality assurance is not ornamental. Heavy forgings, specialized concrete, seismic requirements, redundant systems, containment structures, and complex commissioning all create real opportunities for error. A project with a severe downside must be built differently from a warehouse.

The complexity is not imposed on the technology from outside.

Some of it belongs to the technology.

Critics, however, cannot treat every expensive Western project as the inevitable expression of fission. If outcomes vary greatly across countries and programs, then institutional capability is part of the causal story. A civilization can become better or worse at building difficult things.

That is uncomfortable because it removes the comfort of a universal answer.

The same reactor class can be shaped by financing, procurement, labor markets, regulatory sequencing, supply-chain continuity, site repetition, standardization, and the accumulated experience of the organizations involved. “Nuclear cost” is therefore less like the price of copper and more like the cost of performing a complicated operation.

Skill matters.

So does memory.

The historical cost literature reflects the same problem. Researchers have assembled large international datasets of reactor construction costs and found enormous variation across countries and eras. They then disagree, reasonably, about what should count. Overnight construction cost tries to isolate the plant itself from financing. All-in cost captures more of what the owner actually bears. Delivered electricity cost adds assumptions about utilization, operating expense, fuel, lifetime, and capital structure.

Comparisons become nonsense if those measures are mixed casually.

The financing question is especially important because nuclear plants are so capital intensive. At a low cost of capital, decades of high-output operation can amortize an enormous initial investment. At a high cost of capital, the same reactor design can become uneconomic before the first atom is split.

This creates a peculiar feedback loop.

A country that is good at building nuclear may be offered cheaper capital because investors expect projects to finish. Cheaper capital makes the next project easier to finance. Repeated projects keep suppliers alive and workers experienced. Experienced institutions reduce execution risk further.

A country that stops building can experience the reverse. Suppliers leave. Skills retire. Regulatory knowledge becomes episodic. The next plant is described as “standardized,” but the organizations building it may be standardizing themselves from scratch.

Industrial capability compounds in both directions.

This makes the sequence of projects almost as important as the specification of any single project.

A country that orders one reactor, waits a decade, changes designs, changes contractors, rewrites requirements, and then asks an unfamiliar supply chain to begin again is not really running a nuclear program. It is buying a succession of prototypes. Each project inherits the formal lessons of the last one but may lose the people who learned them in their hands.

Documents preserve instructions. They do not preserve fluency.

A crew that has already solved a difficult installation problem can approach the next unit with a memory of tolerances, workarounds, sequencing, and inspection points that no procedure can fully capture. A regulator that has reviewed a design before knows where ambiguity tends to hide. A supplier with continuing orders has a reason to retain tooling, qualified processes, and people. A project manager who has seen commissioning fail in one particular way carries a different kind of knowledge into the next schedule.

That knowledge is perishable because the organizations carrying it have ordinary economics. A specialized factory cannot live indefinitely on the hope of an order. Skilled workers will take other jobs. Engineering teams disperse. Vendors exit narrow markets. The physical design can remain unchanged while the human system capable of building it decays.

This is why the usual first-of-a-kind versus nth-of-a-kind distinction can be misleading if it is treated as a property of the reactor alone. The nth copy of a design may still behave like a first if it is the first one for a new owner, a new site, a new regulator, a new constructor, or a supply chain reassembled after a long pause.

Repetition has to occur somewhere real.

This also changes how policy should be judged. A government can announce support for nuclear without creating an order book. It can fund design work without sustaining a construction workforce. It can approve one demonstration and still leave every participant uncertain about whether there will be a second. Those actions may be worthwhile, but they are different from building a production system.

The production system needs cadence.

Cadence does not mean rushing safety work or guaranteeing projects regardless of performance. It means creating enough continuity that success on one unit can reduce uncertainty on the next. That may require standardized designs, a visible pipeline, repeat sites, stable regulation, long-lived supplier relationships, or some combination of them. Which institutional arrangement works best will differ by country. The underlying logic does not.

Learning needs another attempt before the learners disappear.

There is an uncomfortable implication here for both public and private finance. A one-off reactor can be evaluated as a stand-alone asset. A nuclear program cannot. Some of the value of the first project may exist in capabilities that lower the risk of later projects: trained crews, qualified vendors, tested regulatory pathways, construction routines, and a more realistic schedule. Those benefits are hard to capture on the balance sheet of Unit 1.

The reverse is true as well. A project that overruns badly can destroy the political and financial conditions required for Unit 2, which means the learning that finally arrives near the end of construction may never be used again.

The first plant can therefore be both a power station and an institutional option.

Whether that option is exercised determines whether expensive learning becomes an asset or a sunk cost.

That makes nuclear a particularly clear example of a broader theme in this book: useful energy is never only a fuel problem.

Coal required mines, railways, boilers, and firms capable of coordinating them. Electricity required generators, wires, standards, markets, and operators. A nuclear plant requires all of that plus an institutional stack capable of managing a low-probability, high-consequence technology over a project life measured in decades.

The energy source is physically dense.

The institution around it is socially dense.

This also explains why small modular reactors attract so much attention.

The promise is not merely a smaller reactor. It is a different production model: more factory fabrication, smaller increments of capital, repeatable modules, shorter construction periods, and a chance to move some nuclear work from unique megaprojects toward manufacturing.

If that model works, it attacks several of nuclear's weakest economic features at once.

But the conditional matters.

A factory does not create a learning curve merely because someone draws one in a presentation. Factories need orders. Orders require licensing, customers, sites, financing, supply chains, and confidence that the design will not change halfway through production. The first units may be expensive precisely because they are buying the factory, the workforce, and the regulatory learning that later units are supposed to exploit.

Canada began construction of a 300-megawatt small modular reactor at Darlington in 2026, an important milestone. It is not yet proof that the economics have been solved.

The appropriate posture is curiosity with a stopwatch.

If small reactors are supposed to improve nuclear's production function, then the test is empirical. Do successive units take less time? Do labor hours fall? Does factory throughput rise? Do financing costs decline as execution becomes predictable? Does licensing become reusable without becoming complacent? Are designs actually repeated, or does each customer demand a bespoke machine?

The atom has heard promises before.

What nuclear can contribute to an electrified civilization depends partly on how those questions are answered.

Its physical advantages are difficult to dismiss. Fission is dispatchable in a way weather-dependent generation is not. Existing nuclear fleets already provide large volumes of low-carbon electricity. Fuel is compact and can be stored on site. Plants can operate for many decades. In systems with large amounts of wind and solar, firm low-carbon generation can reduce how much balancing must be performed by storage, transmission, demand response, or fossil backup.

Those benefits do not prove that every new reactor is worth building.

A grid with cheap solar, wind, batteries, flexible demand, strong interconnection, hydro, geothermal, or gas with carbon management may have cheaper ways to obtain reliability. A country with little nuclear experience may face a very different construction frontier from one with an active program. Existing plants may be economically valuable even where new plants are not. Lifetime extension is not the same decision as new build.

The relevant comparison is system-specific.

That makes “nuclear versus renewables” one of the least useful debates in energy.

A high-electricity system is not choosing a mascot. It is assembling a portfolio of machines with different costs, operating characteristics, build times, geographic requirements, and failure modes.

The question is what each machine is good for, and whether the institutions around it can deliver what the spreadsheet assumes.

For nuclear, that last clause is doing a lot of work.

The current construction map says as much. Expansion is real, but concentrated. China is building repeatedly. Russia continues to export designs. Several emerging economies are adding reactors. The United States and much of Western Europe, despite operating large existing fleets, have struggled to reproduce new large-reactor construction at comparable speed.

That divergence is not a footnote to the technology.

It is the technology as civilization encounters it.

A reactor design sitting in a database is not electrical capacity. Neither is an approved license, a financing announcement, a concrete pour, or a vessel on a ship.

Capacity appears when thousands of separate competencies arrive at the same place in the right order and stay coherent for years.

The atom is dense. The schedule is fragile.

That is why nuclear belongs here, after the chapter on networks and before the chapter on abundance. It is a hard test of the book's central idea. Civilization does not advance merely by discovering denser sources of energy. It advances when it learns to make difficult energy systems repeatable, financeable, governable, and useful.

Physics can open a frontier.

Institutions decide whether we can build there twice.

If nuclear power expands in the coming decades, the decisive breakthrough may not be a new reaction at all. It may be the recovery of something less glamorous: the ability to finish the same difficult job again, a little faster, with fewer surprises.

That is how an exotic technology becomes infrastructure.

And infrastructure, once it works, has a strange habit of disappearing into the background.

Only then does the next question become unavoidable.

What do we do when more forms of useful energy become cheap enough, clean enough, and dependable enough that energy is no longer the first constraint?
