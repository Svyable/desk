# The Efficiency Paradox

In 1865, William Stanley Jevons was worried about running out of coal.

Britain had built an industrial economy around the stuff. Coal pumped mines, drove factories, moved ships and trains, heated homes, smelted iron, and supported a commercial system whose scale would have been difficult to imagine a century earlier. Jevons looked at that dependence and asked whether better technology could stretch the resource far enough to preserve British prosperity.

The reassuring answer was to make engines more efficient: get more work from each ton, waste less coal, and make the remaining reserves last longer.

Jevons thought that confused efficiency with conservation.

A more economical engine did need less coal for a given amount of work. But cheaper power could make an engine profitable where it had previously been too expensive. New factories could open. Existing ones could expand. Coal saved in one process could support another. Industries linked to cheap power could grow along with demand for transport, iron, machinery, and more coal.

The machine could use less fuel per task while the economy used more fuel in total.

That possibility became known, much later, as the Jevons paradox. The name makes it sound like a trick. It is really a question about what happens after engineers make a service cheaper.

Suppose a machine requires ten units of energy to produce one unit of service. An improvement cuts the requirement to eight. If demand stays fixed, energy use falls by twenty percent.

The difficulty begins when the cheaper service changes demand.

A driver whose car goes farther on a gallon may drive more. A homeowner whose insulation lowers heating costs may keep more rooms warm. A factory with cheaper motor power may operate longer or lower prices and sell more output. A data center that can perform an inference with half the electricity may simply perform many more inferences.

The engineering calculation is still correct. It is just incomplete unless demand truly remains fixed.

Economists call the portion of expected savings that disappears through additional consumption the rebound effect. If a twenty-percent efficiency improvement would have saved twenty units of energy under fixed demand but actual consumption falls by only fifteen, the missing five units are rebound. If consumption does not fall at all, rebound is one hundred percent. If consumption rises above its starting point, the strong form of the Jevons paradox has appeared: backfire.

That vocabulary helps because it separates a technical fact from an economic response. Efficiency improves the relationship between input and service. Rebound describes what people and economies do with that improvement afterward.

The most intuitive case is direct rebound. A service becomes cheaper to use, so people use more of that same service. Better fuel economy lowers the fuel cost of a mile. A more efficient air conditioner lowers the electricity cost of an hour of cooling. A better lamp lowers the cost of illumination.

There is also indirect rebound. Money saved on one energy service is spent somewhere else. A household that spends less on heating may spend the difference on meals, electronics, travel, housing, or something else with its own energy and material requirements. The original energy saving still exists in the furnace; some of the economic saving has simply reappeared as demand elsewhere.

Economy-wide effects can travel much farther. Efficiency can lower production costs, prices, and the threshold at which a technology becomes worth adopting. Profits attract investment. Workers and capital move. Supply chains reorganize. Entire industries can change scale over years rather than months.

These are related effects, but they are not three instruments measuring the same object at increasing magnification. A study of extra miles driven after fuel economy improves estimates a different response from a household model that follows re-spending, and both differ again from a macroeconomic model in which prices, investment, production, and trade can adjust. Calling all three results “rebound” is useful shorthand only if the level of analysis travels with the number.

That was the level Jevons cared about. He was not writing about one thrifty household leaving the lights on longer. He was looking at a national industrial system in which more efficient steam power widened the field in which steam power could be profitably used.

More than a century later, Harry Saunders gave the argument a formal economic treatment. In 1992 he examined what had become known as the Khazzoom-Brookes postulate: the claim that economy-wide responses to efficiency improvements could be large enough to offset some, or under particular conditions all, of the engineering savings.

Saunders did not prove that backfire must occur. His result was narrower and more useful: standard neoclassical growth theory could produce it under plausible assumptions. An efficiency improvement need not merely reduce the energy requirement of a fixed economy. It can change the economy itself.

That distinction tends to disappear when the debate is compressed into a yes-or-no question: does efficiency save energy?

At the level of a device, usually yes. At the level of a household, often yes, but by less than the laboratory estimate. At the level of an economy over years or decades, the answer depends on prices, income, substitution, investment, technology, saturation, and growth.

The evidence is correspondingly untidy.

A major review by Steve Sorrell, John Dimitropoulos, and Matt Sommerville found that direct rebound for common household energy services in OECD countries should generally be below thirty percent. That is large enough to matter, but nowhere near a universal law of backfire. If a vehicle becomes more fuel efficient and drivers respond by travelling somewhat farther, most of the expected fuel saving can still survive.

Broader household estimates can be larger because they ask a broader question. A 2025 meta-regression by Marvin Schütt, Anke Jacksohn, Tobias Möllney, and Katrin Rehdanz assembled more than a thousand published rebound estimates. In its main specifications, the modeled mean total microeconomic rebound for the authors’ specified reference-study configuration fell roughly in the forty-to-fifty-percent range. That is not a new universal rebound rate. It is an estimate for a particular combination of study characteristics, and the paper finds that methodology, data, scenario construction, and estimation choices help explain why published results differ so much.

Later work by Mona Chitnis, Roger Fouquet, and Steve Sorrell found much larger combined rebound effects for some British household services and much smaller effects for others. One category, entertainment and computing, produced a negative estimate, meaning the pattern of indirect spending reinforced rather than weakened the initial savings.

That variation is awkward for anyone hoping to turn Jevons into a slogan. Heating is not lighting. Driving is not refrigeration. A household already keeping every room at a comfortable temperature may react differently to cheaper heat than one that has been tolerating cold rooms because energy is expensive. A commuter with a fixed schedule faces different constraints from a household gaining access to a car for the first time.

Income matters. Saturation matters. The initial level of service matters.

Cooling makes the point especially clearly. An efficient air conditioner can reduce the electricity needed to cool a given room to a given temperature for a given number of hours. In an affluent household that already cools the entire home through summer, the response may be modest. The thermostat falls a degree, the machine runs a little more often, and most of the technical saving remains.

Put the same improvement into a hot region where many households could not previously afford much cooling and the result can look very different. Lower operating costs can turn air conditioning from an occasional luxury into a normal service. More rooms are cooled. More hours are cooled. More households buy units. Buildings, workplaces, and shops begin to assume mechanical cooling.

The efficiency improvement has not failed. People received more cooling.

Lighting, the case from the previous chapter, followed the same broad pattern over a much longer span. Modern lamps require a tiny fraction of the energy older technologies needed to produce a given amount of illumination. Civilization did not preserve candle-era light levels and pocket the entire saving. It lit streets, factories, airports, offices, warehouses, homes, signs, parking lots, sports fields, tunnels, shop windows, server rooms, and the undersides of kitchen cabinets.

That last one may not rank among humanity's great achievements, but it is exactly the point. Once a service becomes cheap, it seeps into places that were never worth serving before.

The phrase “energy savings” can therefore hide two different ambitions. One is to reduce the energy required to provide a service. Efficiency is very good at this. The other is to reduce total energy consumption or emissions across an economy. Efficiency can help, sometimes enormously, but it cannot guarantee the outcome by itself.

A society can take part of an efficiency gain as conservation and part as more service. It can use the resulting wealth to expand other forms of consumption. The split is not chosen by the machine designer. It emerges from prices, preferences, constraints, institutions, and all the other things that determine demand.

That matters when efficiency is used as climate policy.

Suppose a government requires better insulation, more efficient motors, tighter appliance standards, and higher vehicle fuel economy. Engineers can estimate large savings by applying those improvements to the existing stock of machines. Those estimates are useful, but they are conditional. If lower operating costs stimulate extra use, some savings disappear. If efficiency raises productivity and output, some reappear elsewhere. If cheaper services accelerate adoption, total demand can rise even while energy use per service falls.

This is not an argument against efficiency standards. A car that travels twice as far on a liter remains a better machine. But if the social goal is to reduce petroleum consumption, the final result also depends on how many cars exist, how far they travel, where people live, what alternatives are available, and what fuel costs.

Efficiency policy and emissions policy overlap. They are not identical.

The same distinction clarifies a recurring confusion about economic growth. Energy intensity—the amount of energy consumed per unit of economic output—has fallen in many countries over long periods. Rich economies often produce far more GDP per unit of primary energy than they once did.

That is genuine progress. It does not determine what happens to total energy use.

If output grows faster than energy intensity falls, aggregate consumption can still rise. A country can become steadily more efficient and steadily more energy-hungry at the same time because both sides of the ratio are moving.

Computing makes this almost comically obvious.

The energy required for an elementary computation has fallen by extraordinary amounts. Had humanity preserved the computing demand of the early electronic era, electricity use for computation would now be tiny. Instead we put computers into phones, cars, cameras, warehouses, factories, watches, televisions, hospitals, farms, airplanes, thermostats, toys, payment systems, and nearly every office desk on Earth.

Efficiency did not make computation disappear from the energy system. It made computation cheap enough to colonize other systems.

Artificial intelligence sharpens the same question. Better chips, more efficient models, quantization, specialized hardware, and algorithmic improvements can all reduce the energy needed for a unit of AI work. But cheaper inference can also make AI economical in more products, for more users, running more often. The future electricity demand of AI depends on both sides of that equation: energy per task and the number of tasks society chooses to perform.

Recent economy-wide empirical work suggests that the response can sometimes be very large. Stephan Bruns, Alessio Moneta, and David Stern used structural vector autoregressions to estimate the effect of energy-efficiency shocks in the United States. Their results put rebound around one hundred percent after four years, implying that the initial energy savings were eventually offset in aggregate.

That finding deserves attention. It does not settle the question.

Economy-wide rebound is unusually difficult to measure. Efficiency is not a single observable event that arrives while everything else politely stands still. New machines appear alongside changing prices, trade, regulation, demographics, industrial structure, energy supply, capital investment, and technological change. Researchers have to infer an efficiency shock and separate its effects from all the other forces moving through the system.

Different models include different mechanisms, and different time horizons produce different responses. A 2021 review led by Paul Brockway and Steve Sorrell concluded that economy-wide rebound may erode more than half of the energy savings expected from efficiency improvements. The same review emphasized the diversity of methods and assumptions in the literature.

“More than half” is a serious challenge for policy models that assume engineering savings translate almost one-for-one into lower national energy demand. It is still not evidence that efficiency always increases energy use.

The ladder now matters. Below-thirty-percent direct rebound for mature household services, broader microeconomic estimates that can be materially larger, and economy-wide estimates near or above one hundred percent do not contradict one another merely because the numbers differ. They can describe different boundaries around the response. The wider the boundary, the more behavior, re-spending, production, investment, and price adjustment the estimate is allowed to capture—and the more the answer depends on assumptions about how those channels work.

Direct rebound for many household services is modest. Some indirect effects can reinforce savings. Some services saturate. Regulations, taxes, congestion, land constraints, time budgets, and simple lack of interest all limit demand.

There are only so many hours in a day to drive a car. There is only so much light a room needs before additional brightness becomes irritating. A refrigerator that uses half the electricity does not normally inspire a family to leave the door open in celebration.

Jevons was writing about a different economic moment: coal and steam were spreading through an industrial economy with enormous unsatisfied demand for mechanical work. Cheaper power could open entirely new fields of use. Britain was not a mature household deciding whether to run the dishwasher once more. It was discovering how many forms of production could be reorganized around fossil energy.

That makes Jevons insightful without making him universal.

Rebound tends to be larger when efficiency lowers the cost of something people want much more of, when a service has many latent uses, when complementary technologies are ready, and when growth is not constrained elsewhere. It tends to be smaller when the service is already saturated, when other costs dominate, or when time, space, regulation, and prices limit additional use.

The more interesting question, then, is not whether rebound exists. It is what rebound tells us about progress.

If a low-income household installs an efficient refrigerator and uses the savings to cool the home during dangerous heat, electricity consumption may fall less than an engineer expected. The household also receives more useful service. If a factory adopts efficient motors, expands output, and lowers the price of its product, energy use may rebound while productive capability rises. If a city installs efficient lighting and uses the savings to illuminate dangerous intersections that were previously dark, the rebound carries an obvious benefit.

Not all rebound is waste.

Sometimes it is the route by which efficiency becomes abundance.

That does not make all additional consumption valuable. Cheap energy can support trivial uses as easily as important ones. Efficiency can encourage larger vehicles, bigger houses, more disposable goods, longer commutes, brighter advertising, and other consumption whose private benefit may be small compared with its external costs. Demand reveals willingness to pay, not moral worth.

For climate policy, that distinction is decisive. The atmosphere does not care whether an extra ton of carbon dioxide came from a useful journey or a pointless one. If the objective is lower emissions, emissions have to be constrained or displaced directly through cleaner supply, carbon prices, fuel taxes, standards, or other institutions. Efficiency helps, but it should not be mistaken for the whole system.

This also answers a larger challenge facing an energy-centered account of civilization. If advanced societies can become more efficient, perhaps energy should eventually fade in importance. Perhaps we learn to do so much with so little that physical throughput becomes almost irrelevant.

History points in a subtler direction. Efficiency reduces the energy required for a particular capability, which is one of the ways civilization advances. The resulting cheapness often expands the number of people who can afford the service, the number of places it can be used, or the scale at which it is deployed.

A more efficient engine does not abolish demand for motion. A more efficient lamp does not abolish demand for light. A more efficient computer does not abolish demand for computation. Often the service simply becomes ordinary.

The deepest effect of efficiency may therefore be productivity: more capability from the same physical input.

What happens next is not an engineering constant. Society can cash part of the gain as lower energy use, part as lower prices, part as broader access, part as economic growth, and part as uses nobody had bothered to imagine when the machine was expensive.

Jevons saw the pattern before the modern vocabulary existed. Making coal more useful increased the economic territory coal could occupy.

The technologies changed. The question did not.

When technology gives us more capability per unit of energy, what do we do with the surplus?

Most of the time, civilization does some saving and some spending.
