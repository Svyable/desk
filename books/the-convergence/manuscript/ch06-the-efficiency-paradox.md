# The Efficiency Paradox

In 1865, William Stanley Jevons looked at the steam engine and saw a problem hiding inside an improvement.

Britain had spent decades learning to get more work from coal. Engines improved. Furnaces improved. Waste heat was captured. Manufacturers learned to use fuel with greater economy. The obvious conclusion was comforting: if each task required less coal, the nation might conserve its coal.

Jevons thought the opposite could happen.

In *The Coal Question*, he argued that making coal more economical could make coal-powered work cheaper, enlarge the number of profitable uses for it, and thereby increase aggregate consumption. He was writing about a particular country, fuel, and industrial moment, and much of his broader depletion argument belongs to the nineteenth century. But the mechanism he described survived him. In his chapter on the economy of fuel, Jevons noted that coal saved in one use could be employed in others and that the profits from economy encouraged wider application. The original 1866 text is available in the public-domain [second edition of *The Coal Question*](https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html).

The unsettling part is not that efficiency fails. Efficiency works.

A more efficient engine really does need less fuel to produce a given amount of mechanical work. A better lamp really does need less electricity to produce a given amount of light. A more aerodynamic vehicle really does require less energy to overcome drag at a given speed. These are physical improvements, not accounting tricks.

The paradox begins one step later, when people respond to the improvement.

If the cost of obtaining a service falls, demand for that service may rise. A family may heat more rooms. A driver may travel farther. A factory may run a machine longer or install more machines. A company may redesign a product around a capability that used to be too expensive. Savings can also be spent elsewhere, where they purchase goods and services that require energy of their own.

The engineering calculation asks how much energy would be saved if behavior stayed fixed. The economic question asks whether behavior stays fixed.

Arguments about efficiency often become confused because one side answers the first question while the other answers the second.

Imagine a lamp that becomes twice as efficient. If a household wants exactly the same amount of light for exactly the same number of hours, electricity use for lighting falls by half. That is the technical saving. But the household may respond to cheaper light by using brighter lamps, illuminating more rooms, leaving outdoor lights on longer, or buying lighting for places that were previously left dark. Some of the technical saving is then taken back by additional service demand.

That portion is usually called direct rebound.

The word can sound like a defect in the technology, but nothing has malfunctioned. The lamp is still more efficient. The household simply values light enough to buy more when light becomes cheaper.

The distinction matters because rebound is often described with moral language that the underlying economics does not contain. A person who heats a previously cold bedroom after installing insulation has “rebounded” in the accounting sense, but may also have become more comfortable or healthier. A low-income household may use an efficient heating system to reach a safe indoor temperature it could not previously afford. The expected energy saving is smaller than an engineer assuming constant comfort would predict, yet the efficiency improvement has delivered a real welfare gain.

Cooling makes the point even harder to ignore.

The International Energy Agency estimated in 2023 that roughly 3.5 billion people lived in hot climates but only about 15 percent owned air conditioning. The same analysis estimated that cooling access was already averting around 190,000 heat-related deaths per year during 2019–2021. Its report, [*Sustainable, Affordable Cooling Can Save Tens of Thousands of Lives Each Year*](https://www.iea.org/reports/sustainable-affordable-cooling-can-save-tens-of-thousands-of-lives-each-year), also warns that rapidly growing cooling demand can strain grids and increase emissions where electricity remains carbon-intensive.

An efficient air conditioner sold into a household that previously had no cooling may increase electricity demand from zero to something substantial. In a narrow accounting exercise, the energy saving relative to an imagined fixed amount of cooling does not exist because the service itself is new. In human terms, the machine may reduce heat stress, protect sleep, make indoor work possible, or lower mortality risk.

This is not a loophole in the rebound concept. It is a reminder to name the objective before judging the outcome.

For a wealthy household already heating and cooling a large home comfortably, rebound may mostly mean extra consumption. For a household rationing heat or unable to escape dangerous temperatures, the same response can mean relief from deprivation. The percentage alone does not tell us whether the additional service was frivolous, valuable, or necessary.

Affordability keeps the distinction current. The IEA’s [2026 *Household Energy Affordability* report](https://www.iea.org/reports/household-energy-affordability) finds that lower-income households are often stuck in less efficient homes and face higher exposure to energy-cost shocks. In advanced economies alone, more than 120 million households spend over ten percent of income on residential heating, cooling, and appliances. Efficiency in such a household can be partly “spent” on comfort because the household had been consuming less comfort than it wanted or needed.

Direct rebound is only the first layer.

Suppose the household spends less on electricity because its new lamp uses less power. The money saved does not disappear. It may be spent on food, entertainment, clothing, travel, or a larger home. Producing those things also requires energy. This is indirect rebound: efficiency in one service changes spending elsewhere.

Then there are producer and economy-wide effects. Lower energy-service costs can change prices, production, investment, wages, technology choices, and the structure of industries. A more efficient motor can make an energy-intensive production process more competitive. Cheaper transport can widen labor markets and supply chains. Better computing efficiency can make software economical in places where computation was once too expensive. Over time, whole categories of activity can emerge around a service whose effective price has collapsed.

At that scale, rebound stops looking like a consumer deciding to drive an extra mile. It becomes structural change.

Harry Saunders formalized this possibility in his 1992 paper [“The Khazzoom-Brookes Postulate and Neoclassical Growth”](https://doi.org/10.5547/ISSN0195-6574-EJ-Vol13-No4-7). His result was not that efficiency must increase energy use. It was that standard growth theory could generate that outcome under plausible conditions. Efficiency can reduce the effective cost of energy services and also contribute to economic growth, creating additional channels through which energy demand rises.

That is a theoretical possibility, not an empirical law.

The distinction is essential because “Jevons paradox” is often inflated into a claim that every efficiency improvement inevitably backfires. The evidence does not support that.

Empirical estimates vary widely by technology, sector, income, time horizon, and method. A major review by Steve Sorrell and John Dimitropoulos found that direct rebound for household energy services in OECD countries was generally expected to be below 30 percent, while emphasizing substantial methodological uncertainty. Their [review of the rebound literature](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolecon.2007.08.013) explains why estimates can be biased when researchers confuse changes in energy prices with changes in energy efficiency or fail to measure the service itself. A related 2009 empirical review concluded that direct rebound for OECD household energy services should generally be less than 30 percent.

A rebound of 20 percent does not mean efficiency increased energy consumption. It means one-fifth of the engineering saving was offset by additional demand and four-fifths remained.

Backfire is the stronger case: rebound greater than 100 percent, where total energy use rises above what it would have been without the efficiency improvement.

That can happen in models and appears in some empirical settings, but it should not be treated as the default.

The range of results is itself evidence. Biying Yu, Junyi Zhang, and Akimasa Fujiwara studied household appliances and vehicles in Beijing and found no evident rebound for several end uses while finding significant rebound for others. Their [2013 empirical study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2013.02.024) reported very different upper-bound estimates across air conditioning, washing machines, microwave ovens, and cars. The response depended on what the device did and how households used it.

That is what we should expect if rebound is a demand response rather than a law of thermodynamics.

People do not value every energy service in the same way. There is only so much refrigeration a household wants. Once food is cold enough, making refrigeration cheaper may not induce much additional use. Lighting historically behaved differently because artificial light was once extremely scarce and had many unrealized uses. Travel can be different again because cheaper mobility changes where people can live, work, shop, and socialize. Heating and cooling demand depend on climate, building quality, income, and whether occupants were already comfortable before the efficiency improvement.

The service matters. So does time.

In the short run, a driver with a more efficient car can travel somewhat farther, but roads, jobs, homes, and cities remain where they were. In the long run, cheaper travel can influence vehicle choice, residential location, commuting distance, logistics networks, and land use. What begins as a small behavioral response can become an infrastructural response.

This is why the strongest versions of the rebound argument live at the economy-wide scale—and why they are hardest to estimate cleanly.

Anne Berner, Stephan Bruns, Alessio Moneta, and David Stern attempted to identify economy-wide efficiency shocks in France, Germany, Italy, the United Kingdom, and the United States. Their [2022 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eneco.2022.105939) estimated rebound effects between 78 and 101 percent after two years. That is a striking result: in their model, much of the potential energy saving from efficiency was offset by broader economic responses.

It is also not the last word.

Economy-wide rebound cannot be observed by placing a meter on one appliance. Researchers have to infer a counterfactual: how much energy would the economy have used if efficiency had not improved? The answer depends on identification assumptions, model structure, time horizon, prices, technological change, and what counts as an efficiency shock. Different methods can produce different estimates.

David Stern’s [2020 review of economy-wide rebound](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2020.111870) captures the state of the argument well. Theory allows a wide range of outcomes. Historical evidence suggests rebound can be large. Simulation and econometric studies remain mixed. Some general-equilibrium studies find rebound around 100 percent, but the literature does not justify declaring universal backfire.

The right conclusion is narrower than either camp usually wants.

Efficiency reliably reduces the energy required for a specified service. It does not reliably tell us what total energy consumption will do after people, firms, prices, and technologies respond.

That uncertainty becomes easier to understand if we return to Chapter 5’s distinction between energy and energy services. Efficiency lowers the energy cost of a service. Lower cost expands the feasible set.

A household can take the gain as lower energy use, more service, more spending elsewhere, or some combination. A firm can take it as lower cost, higher output, lower prices, new products, or investment. An economy can take it as conservation, growth, structural change, or all three at once.

Efficiency is therefore not a conservation policy by definition. It is a productivity improvement whose effect on total energy depends on demand.

The history of lighting makes this visible over a long enough horizon. The cost of useful light fell by orders of magnitude while the amount of light people consumed rose enormously. It would be strange to call that history a failure of lighting efficiency. The entire point of improving lighting technology was to make illumination abundant enough that people no longer had to ration darkness by the candle.

The same pattern appears in computation with even greater force.

Early electronic computers were expensive machines operated by institutions. Improvements in transistors, integrated circuits, architecture, manufacturing, storage, networking, and software reduced the energy and cost required for a unit of computation. If demand for computation had remained fixed at its early level, the world could have banked enormous energy savings.

Instead, cheap computation escaped the computer room.

It entered offices, cars, phones, factories, hospitals, homes, cameras, logistics systems, power grids, financial markets, entertainment, scientific instruments, and eventually machine-learning systems that consume staggering amounts of arithmetic precisely because arithmetic became cheap enough to use at that scale.

The efficiency gain was real. So was the expansion.

This is the deeper reason Jevons remains relevant. He noticed that the value of an efficiency improvement cannot be understood by freezing the surrounding economy in place.

But there is another mistake waiting on the other side.

If rebound is large, one might conclude that efficiency is pointless for environmental policy. That does not follow either.

First, rebound below 100 percent still leaves net energy savings relative to the no-efficiency counterfactual. Second, even when total energy use rises, efficiency can lower the energy intensity of the service and expand welfare. Third, environmental damage depends on more than total energy. A kilowatt-hour from one source can have very different emissions and health consequences from a kilowatt-hour from another. If the policy goal is lower carbon emissions, pricing or constraining carbon directly can address an externality that efficiency alone only influences indirectly.

The distinction between energy and emissions matters more as electricity decarbonizes.

Suppose an electric motor becomes more efficient and its use expands. If the additional electricity comes from a low-carbon system, the climate consequence may be much smaller than the same rebound in a coal-heavy system. Conversely, an efficiency program can reduce electricity consumption while producing disappointing emissions reductions if the saved electricity was already low-carbon.

Conservation, efficiency, decarbonization, and access overlap, but they are not synonyms.

The same is true of welfare.

Allan Mazur examined industrialized nations and found that, at already-high levels of consumption, increases in per-capita energy or electricity use did not correspond to further improvement in the quality-of-life indicators he studied. His [2011 analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2011.02.024) is a useful restraint on any claim that more energy consumption must always mean a better life.

The finding does not imply that energy is irrelevant.

A country with reliable heating, refrigeration, sanitation, transport, hospitals, communications, and electricity has already used energy to remove many constraints that matter enormously to health and material welfare. Once those constraints are relaxed, adding another unit of consumption may do little for life expectancy. The marginal value changes.

This is why the welfare curve is not a straight line.

At low levels of access, energy services can be transformative. Electricity can replace dangerous lighting fuels, power pumps, preserve food and medicine, support communications, and make cooling possible in dangerous heat. Clean cooking can reduce exposure to household smoke and hours spent gathering fuel. Refrigeration and mechanical power can remove constraints that affluent societies barely remember as constraints.

At high levels of consumption, the next increment may power a larger vehicle, a second freezer, a longer flight, or a room kept colder than necessary. These may be valued choices, but they are not equivalent to first access.

Averages blur this distinction.

An economy can become more energy efficient while poor households remain energy deprived. It can reduce energy intensity while total consumption rises. It can increase total consumption while emissions fall. It can lower total consumption while welfare rises. None of these combinations is contradictory once input, service, distribution, and external cost are separated.

Joel Millward-Hopkins and colleagues pushed this separation to an extreme in their [2020 decent-living scenario](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2020.102168). They modeled whether modern technologies and a sufficiency-oriented system could provide material conditions for decent living at far lower global final-energy use than today’s affluent consumption patterns. The result is a scenario, not a forecast. It assumes efficient technologies and major changes in provisioning and consumption. But as a counterexample it matters: high human capability does not logically require every society to converge on the highest current levels of energy throughput.

That finding forces this book’s thesis to become more precise.

If civilization’s advance were measured by raw energy consumption, efficiency would be a conceptual nuisance. Every machine that did more with less would make progress look smaller. Every rich society that stabilized energy use while improving services would look stagnant. Every wasteful system would score well simply for burning more.

That cannot be right.

The more defensible quantity is capability: how much useful service can be delivered, with what reliability and control, to how many people, at what total cost.

Efficiency matters because it stretches the capability obtainable from a given energy flow. Rebound matters because people often spend some of that stretch on more capability.

Consider a road that becomes easier to travel. If the only goal were to minimize movement, improving the road would be self-defeating because more people might use it. But roads are usually improved because movement has value. Congestion, pollution, land use, and other costs still matter; they may justify prices or constraints. Yet the increase in travel is not evidence that the road failed to become more useful.

Energy efficiency is similar. It makes a physical service cheaper in energy terms. Society then decides—through billions of choices, prices, institutions, and technologies—how much of the gain to convert into conservation and how much to convert into activity.

That is why Jevons’ insight is more interesting than the slogan attached to his name.

He was not merely warning that thrift can backfire. He was describing a civilization that kept turning savings into scale.

The steam engine became economical enough to spread. Mechanical power became cheap enough to reorganize production. Electricity became cheap and controllable enough to disappear into walls. Light became cheap enough to flood the night. Computation became cheap enough to become an environment rather than a machine.

Each transition reduced the resource required for some unit of service and enlarged the set of services worth demanding.

The tension is permanent because efficiency serves two legitimate purposes. It can reduce the burden of doing what we already do, and it can make previously extravagant things ordinary.

Policy has to decide which outcome it wants.

If the goal is lower household bills, efficiency can work even with rebound because consumers receive more service or spend savings elsewhere. If the goal is lower energy demand, rebound has to be estimated rather than ignored. If the goal is lower emissions, efficiency can help, but the carbon content of supply and the treatment of emissions themselves matter. If the goal is human development, the distribution of useful services matters more than an aggregate percentage.

No single rebound number can answer all four questions.

The chapter readiness test therefore ends with a result that would force the thesis to narrow. If repeated evidence showed that societies could expand material capability—more transport, industrial output, thermal comfort, computation, and resilience—without either increasing useful energy or improving the conversion and control of existing energy, then energy would be a weaker substrate than this book proposes. The rebound literature does not show that. It shows something subtler: efficiency can increase capability faster than it reduces throughput, and demand determines how much of the technical saving survives.

The paradox dissolves once the objective is named.

If the objective is to use fewer joules, efficiency is an instrument whose final effect must be measured. If the objective is to do more with each joule, efficiency has already succeeded.

Civilization has spent centuries doing exactly that—and then discovering new things worth doing.
