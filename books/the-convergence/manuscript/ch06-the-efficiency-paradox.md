# The Efficiency Paradox

In 1865, William Stanley Jevons was worried about running out of coal.

Britain had built an industrial economy around the stuff. Coal pumped mines, drove factories, moved ships and trains, heated homes, smelted iron, and supported a commercial system whose scale would have been difficult to imagine a century earlier. Jevons looked at that dependence and asked an uncomfortable question: could better technology stretch the resource far enough to preserve British prosperity?

The reassuring answer was obvious. Make engines more efficient. Get more work from each ton. Waste less coal, and the remaining reserves should last longer.

Jevons did not find that answer reassuring.

He thought it confused efficiency with conservation.

A more economical engine did indeed need less coal for a given amount of work. But that was only the first move. Cheaper power could make an engine profitable in places where it had previously been too expensive. New factories could open. Existing factories could expand. Coal saved in one process could support another. Industries linked to cheap power could grow along with the demand for transport, iron, machinery, and more coal.

The machine could use less fuel per task while the economy used more fuel in total.

That possibility became known, much later, as the Jevons paradox. The name makes it sound like a clever puzzle. It is really an argument about what happens when engineering improvement meets human appetite.

The engineering arithmetic is simple.

Suppose a machine requires ten units of energy to produce one unit of service. An improvement cuts the requirement to eight. If demand for the service stays fixed, energy use falls by twenty percent.

Nothing paradoxical has happened.

The difficulty begins when the cheaper service changes demand.

A driver whose car goes farther on a gallon may drive more. A homeowner whose insulation lowers heating costs may keep more rooms warm or raise the thermostat. A factory with cheaper motor power may operate longer, buy another machine, or lower its prices and sell more output. A data center that can perform an inference with half the electricity may simply perform vastly more inferences.

Efficiency changes behavior because it changes what behavior costs.

This does not mean every efficiency gain produces a frenzy of new consumption. It means the original engineering calculation is incomplete unless demand truly remains fixed.

Economists call the portion of expected savings that disappears through additional consumption the rebound effect.

If a twenty-percent efficiency improvement would have saved twenty units of energy under fixed demand, but actual consumption falls by only fifteen, the missing five units are rebound. If consumption falls by ten, rebound has erased half the expected savings. If consumption does not fall at all, rebound is one hundred percent. If consumption rises above its starting point, the strong form of the Jevons paradox has appeared: backfire.

Those categories are useful because they separate a fact from a slogan.

Efficiency always improves the technical relationship between input and service. Rebound describes what people and economies do with that improvement afterward.

There are several ways the savings can leak away.

The most intuitive is direct rebound. A service becomes cheaper to use, so people use more of that same service. Better fuel economy lowers the fuel cost of a mile. A more efficient air conditioner lowers the electricity cost of an hour of cooling. A better lamp lowers the cost of illumination.

The second route is indirect. Money saved on one energy service is spent somewhere else. A household that spends less on heating may spend the difference on restaurant meals, electronics, travel, housing, or almost anything else. Those purchases have their own energy and material requirements. The original energy saving still exists in the furnace; some of the economic saving has simply reappeared as demand elsewhere.

Then there are economy-wide effects.

Efficiency can lower production costs. Lower costs can reduce prices. Lower prices can increase sales. Higher profits can attract investment. New technologies can become economical. Entire industries can change scale. Workers and capital can move. Supply chains reorganize. The effects can take years and travel far from the machine where the efficiency improvement first occurred.

This was the level Jevons cared about.

He was not making a claim about one thrifty household leaving the lights on longer. He was looking at a national industrial system in which more efficient steam power widened the field in which steam power could be profitably used.

More than a century later, Harry Saunders gave the argument a formal economic treatment. In 1992 he examined what had become known as the Khazzoom-Brookes postulate: the claim that economy-wide responses to efficiency improvements could be large enough to offset some or, under particular conditions, all of the engineering savings.

Saunders did not prove that backfire must occur.

That qualification matters.

His point was that standard economic growth theory could produce it under plausible assumptions. Efficiency does not merely reduce the quantity of energy required for a fixed economy. It can change the economy itself.

The distinction is easy to lose because public debate tends to demand one verdict.

Does efficiency save energy, yes or no?

At the level of a device, usually yes. At the level of a household, often yes, but by less than the laboratory estimate. At the level of an economy over years or decades, the answer becomes an empirical question about prices, income, substitution, investment, technology, and growth.

The evidence reflects that complexity.

A major review of direct rebound in household energy services by Steve Sorrell, John Dimitropoulos, and Matt Sommerville concluded that for common household services in wealthy countries, direct rebound was generally less than thirty percent. That is not trivial. But it is also nowhere near a universal law of backfire.

If a car becomes twenty percent more fuel efficient, a direct rebound below thirty percent means most of the expected fuel saving survives even after drivers respond by driving somewhat more.

That is one important piece of the story.

It is not the whole story.

Later work using a long historical record of British household services found substantially larger combined effects for several categories. Mona Chitnis, Roger Fouquet, and Steve Sorrell estimated rebound in carbon emissions from efficiency improvements across heating, lighting, cooking, refrigeration and washing, entertainment and computing, and private vehicle travel. Their estimates varied sharply by service. Some were large. One category even showed a negative combined rebound, meaning indirect spending patterns reinforced rather than weakened the savings.

That result is awkward for anyone hoping to turn rebound into a universal rule.

The size depends on what becomes efficient and what people do next.

Heating is not lighting. Driving is not refrigeration. A household already keeping its rooms at a comfortable temperature may respond differently to cheaper heat than a household that has been tolerating cold rooms because energy is expensive. A wealthy commuter whose travel schedule is fixed may react differently to fuel economy than a household gaining access to a car for the first time.

Income matters. Saturation matters. The initial level of service matters.

This is why rebound is often largest precisely where an efficiency improvement unlocks a previously constrained use.

Consider cooling.

An efficient air conditioner can reduce the electricity needed to cool a given room to a given temperature for a given number of hours. In an affluent household that already cools the entire home all summer, the response may be modest. The thermostat may fall a degree. The system may run slightly more often. Most of the technical saving can remain.

Now put the same efficiency improvement into a hot region where many households could not previously afford much cooling.

The lower operating cost can turn air conditioning from an occasional luxury into a normal household service. More rooms are cooled. More hours are cooled. More households buy units. Buildings begin to be designed around mechanical cooling. Workplaces expect it. Shops install it. The efficiency improvement is doing something socially valuable, but the aggregate electricity saving may be far smaller than an engineer would predict by holding usage constant.

Calling that outcome a failure of efficiency misses what actually happened.

People received more cooling.

The same logic appears in lighting, the case from the previous chapter. Modern lamps require a tiny fraction of the energy that older technologies needed to produce a given amount of illumination. Civilization did not respond by keeping candle-era light levels and enjoying an enormous reduction in electricity demand.

It illuminated streets, factories, airports, offices, warehouses, homes, signs, parking lots, sports fields, monuments, tunnels, shop windows, server rooms, and the undersides of kitchen cabinets.

The efficiency gain was real. So was the increase in demand.

This is why the phrase “energy savings” can hide two different ambitions.

One ambition is to reduce the energy required to provide a service. Efficiency is excellent at this.

The other is to reduce total energy consumption or emissions across an economy. Efficiency can help, sometimes enormously, but it cannot guarantee the outcome by itself.

A society may choose to take an efficiency improvement as conservation. It may take it as more service. It may split the gain between the two. It may use the resulting wealth to expand other forms of consumption.

Efficiency expands the feasible set.

It does not choose what happens inside it.

That is the heart of the paradox.

The distinction becomes especially important when efficiency is used as climate policy.

Suppose a government requires better building insulation, more efficient motors, tighter appliance standards, and higher vehicle fuel economy. If the objective is to reduce fossil-fuel emissions, engineers can calculate large savings by multiplying efficiency improvements across the existing stock of machines.

Those calculations are useful. They are also conditional.

If lower operating costs stimulate extra use, some savings vanish. If efficiency raises productivity and output, more can vanish elsewhere. If cheaper services accelerate adoption, total demand can rise even while energy use per service falls.

This is not an argument against efficiency standards.

It is an argument against asking efficiency to do a job that belongs partly to prices, carbon constraints, clean supply, infrastructure, and policy.

A car that travels twice as far on a liter remains a better machine. If the social goal is to reduce petroleum consumption, however, the final result also depends on how many cars exist, how far they travel, where people live, what alternatives are available, and what fuel costs.

The same distinction clarifies a recurring confusion in discussions of economic growth.

Energy intensity—the amount of energy consumed per unit of economic output—has fallen in many countries over long periods. Rich economies often produce far more GDP per unit of primary energy than they once did.

This is genuine progress in efficiency.

But a falling ratio does not determine the numerator.

If output grows faster than energy intensity falls, total energy use can still rise. A country can become steadily more efficient and steadily more energy-hungry at the same time.

There is no contradiction because the denominator is moving too.

The history of computing makes this almost comically obvious.

The energy required for an elementary computation has fallen by extraordinary amounts. If humanity had preserved the computing demand of the early electronic era, the electricity required for computation would now be negligible.

Instead we put computers into phones, cars, cameras, warehouses, factories, watches, televisions, hospitals, farms, airplanes, thermostats, toys, payment systems, and nearly every office desk on Earth.

Efficiency did not make computation disappear from the energy system.

It made computation cheap enough to colonize other systems.

This is one reason the rebound debate has returned with artificial intelligence. Better chips, more efficient models, improved quantization, specialized hardware, and algorithmic advances can all reduce the energy required for a unit of AI work. It is tempting to draw a straight line from those improvements to lower electricity demand.

But cheaper inference can also make AI economical in more products, for more users, running more often.

The future energy demand of AI will therefore depend on both sides of the equation: energy per task and the number of tasks society decides to perform.

The same was true of steam.

The same was true of light.

What changes from technology to technology is the size of the response.

Recent economy-wide empirical work suggests that response may sometimes be very large. Stephan Bruns, Alessio Moneta, and David Stern used structural vector autoregressions to estimate the effect of energy-efficiency shocks in the United States. Their results put rebound around one hundred percent after several years, implying that the initial energy savings were eventually offset in aggregate.

Other empirical work across large European economies and the United States has also produced high economy-wide estimates.

Those findings deserve attention because they challenge the comfortable assumption that efficiency improvements automatically translate one-for-one into lower national energy demand.

They do not settle the question.

Economy-wide rebound is unusually difficult to measure. Efficiency is not a single observable event that strikes an economy while everything else politely stands still. New machines arrive alongside changing prices, trade, regulation, demographics, industrial structure, energy supply, capital investment, and technological change. Researchers must infer an efficiency shock and separate its effects from all the other forces moving through the system.

Different models include different mechanisms. Different time horizons produce different responses. Estimates can therefore vary widely.

A 2021 review led by Paul Brockway and Steve Sorrell surveyed the growing literature and concluded that economy-wide rebound may erode more than half of the energy savings expected from efficiency improvements. The authors also emphasized how diverse the evidence and methods remain.

“More than half” is a serious policy problem.

It is not the same claim as “efficiency always increases energy consumption.”

That stronger claim does not survive the evidence.

Direct rebound for many household services is modest. Some indirect effects can reinforce savings. Some services saturate. Some efficiency improvements reduce costs that are too small to stimulate much additional use. Regulations, taxes, congestion, land constraints, time budgets, and simple lack of interest can all limit demand.

There are only so many hours in a day to drive a car.

There is only so much light a room needs before additional brightness becomes irritating.

A refrigerator that uses half the electricity does not normally inspire a family to leave the door open in celebration.

Human demand is elastic, but not infinitely so.

This is where the original Jevons argument needs historical context.

Jevons was writing about coal at a moment when steam power was spreading into an industrial economy with enormous unsatisfied demand for mechanical work. Cheaper power could open entirely new fields of use. The Britain he observed was not a mature household deciding whether to run the dishwasher one more time. It was an economy discovering how many forms of production could be reorganized around fossil energy.

The room for expansion was immense.

That makes Jevons insightful without making him universal.

The paradox is strongest when efficiency lowers the cost of something people want much more of, when the service has many latent uses, when complementary technologies are ready, and when growth is not constrained elsewhere.

It is weaker when the service is already saturated, when other costs dominate, when time or space limits use, or when policy captures the efficiency gain as reduced resource consumption.

This leads to a more interesting question than whether rebound is “real.”

Of course it is real. The serious question is what rebound tells us about progress.

One answer is that efficiency should not be judged only by whether aggregate energy consumption falls afterward.

If a low-income household installs an efficient refrigerator and uses the savings to cool the home during dangerous heat, total household electricity use may not fall as much as expected. Yet the household is receiving more useful service at the same or lower cost.

If a factory adopts efficient motors, expands output, lowers the price of its product, and employs more workers, energy use may rebound while economic capability rises.

If a city installs efficient lighting and responds by illuminating dangerous intersections that were previously dark, the rebound carries a benefit.

Not all rebound is waste.

Sometimes rebound is the mechanism by which efficiency turns into abundance.

That observation also prevents the opposite mistake: assuming that more consumption is automatically good because it reveals latent demand.

Cheap energy can support trivial uses as easily as valuable ones. Efficiency can encourage larger vehicles, bigger houses, more disposable goods, longer commutes, brighter advertising, or other consumption whose benefits may be small compared with its external costs.

Demand reveals willingness to pay, not moral worth.

The climate does not care whether an extra ton of carbon dioxide came from a useful journey or a pointless one.

If the objective is lower emissions, the most reliable strategy is therefore to attack emissions directly while still improving efficiency.

Clean electricity changes the emissions consequence of rebound. Carbon prices change the cost signal. Fuel taxes reduce the amount of an efficiency gain that becomes cheaper energy service. Building codes can lock in lower demand. Urban design can change transport needs. Supply standards can ensure that additional electricity demand is met with lower-carbon generation.

Efficiency remains part of the system.

It simply stops being mistaken for the system.

This is also the answer to a larger challenge facing this book.

If civilization can become more efficient, perhaps the importance of energy should fade. Perhaps advanced societies will learn to do so much with so little that energy throughput becomes almost irrelevant.

The historical record points in a subtler direction.

Efficiency reduces the energy required for a particular capability. That is one of the ways civilization advances. But the resulting cheapness often expands the number of capabilities people can afford to use, the number of people who can access them, or the scale at which they are deployed.

A more efficient engine does not abolish the demand for motion. A more efficient lamp does not abolish the demand for light. A more efficient computer does not abolish the demand for computation.

Often it does the opposite.

It makes the service ordinary.

The deepest effect of efficiency may therefore be neither conservation nor backfire. It is productivity.

Efficiency converts scarcity into option value.

The same quantity of energy can produce more motion, more heat moved, more light, more computation, more material transformed, or more of whatever service the machine was built to provide. Society then decides whether to cash that gain as lower energy use, higher consumption, lower prices, broader access, economic growth, or some mixture of all five.

That decision is distributed across billions of people, firms, machines, prices, rules, and institutions. No engineer controls it from the efficiency label on an appliance.

Jevons understood this before the modern language existed.

He saw that making coal more useful increased the economic territory coal could occupy.

What he could not know was how many later technologies would repeat the pattern in different proportions. Cars, air conditioners, refrigerators, electric motors, lighting, aviation, and computers each changed the effective price of a service and then encountered a different demand response.

There is no single rebound number hiding behind them.

There is only a recurring question.

When technology gives us more capability per unit of energy, what do we do with the surplus?

Sometimes we save it.

Sometimes we spend it.

Most of the time, civilization does both.
