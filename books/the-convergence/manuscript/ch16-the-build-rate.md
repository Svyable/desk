# The Build Rate

A civilization does not run on technologies that exist.

It runs on technologies that have been built.

The difference is easy to lose in an age when invention travels at the speed of a press release. A solar cell reaches a new efficiency record. A battery chemistry survives another thousand cycles. A reactor design receives approval. A geothermal well reaches hotter rock. A factory announces a new line. A government sets a target.

All of these events can matter.

None is infrastructure.

Infrastructure begins when steel is ordered, workers are trained, permits are signed, substations are expanded, equipment arrives, financing closes, projects connect, and machines survive long enough to become ordinary. The physical economy moves at the speed of deployment, not discovery.

That speed has become one of the central facts of the energy transition.

In 2025, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the world added 692 gigawatts of renewable generating capacity. Solar dominated the increase. Wind added another large block. The total was a record.

The number is impressive because it describes hardware that was actually installed, not a scenario.

It is also incomplete.

A gigawatt of solar capacity is not a gigawatt of continuous output. A newly built wind farm is useful only if it can connect. A battery factory does not strengthen a grid until cells become systems, systems become projects, and projects become available when the network needs them. A reactor under construction is not yet a reactor on the busbar.

Capacity is a stock.

Construction is a flow.

The future depends on both.

This distinction sounds almost too elementary to deserve a chapter. In practice, it explains why energy transitions can look simultaneously fast and slow.

They are fast when measured by the growth rate of a new technology.

They are slow when measured against the size and durability of the installed system it must change.

A technology can double from a small base and remain small. It can become the majority of new additions while older equipment continues operating for decades. It can dominate investment without dominating energy supply. It can fall dramatically in unit cost while the institutions around it build only incrementally.

The arithmetic of transition is therefore not just cost decline.

It is cost decline multiplied by build rate, constrained by the size of the existing stock.

Consider a city with a million furnaces, boilers, vehicles, transformers, motors, and buildings. If a better technology appears tomorrow, almost none of the old stock disappears tomorrow. Owners replace equipment when it breaks, when renovation makes replacement sensible, when regulation changes, when fuel costs shift, or when the new option is valuable enough to justify retiring useful machinery early.

The replacement schedule matters as much as the invention.

A household appliance may turn over in years. A car fleet turns over more slowly. Buildings last for generations. Transmission corridors can serve for most of a human lifetime. Power stations can operate for decades. Industrial plants are collections of assets with different ages, maintenance cycles, financing structures, and shutdown costs.

This is why the phrase energy transition can mislead.

It sounds like changing shirts.

It is closer to rebuilding a city while people continue living in it.

The old system has to keep working while the new one grows through it.

That creates two very different ways to accelerate change.

One is to make the new technology better.

The other is to increase the system's ability to build.

Modern energy debates lavish attention on the first.

The second is increasingly the harder problem.

Solar photovoltaics make the point vividly. Module manufacturing expanded, costs fell, supply chains thickened, installers accumulated experience, and projects could be divided into repeatable units. The result is a technology that can now be deployed at a scale that would have looked implausible a generation ago. IRENA's 2026 capacity statistics record nearly seven hundred gigawatts of renewable additions in a single year, with solar accounting for most of them.

That is not merely a story about better semiconductor physics.

It is a story about factories, glass, aluminum, inverters, shipping, land agreements, construction crews, finance, standards, procurement, and repetition.

Learning happened in the laboratory.

It also happened on loading docks.

A technology becomes historically important when a society learns not just how to make one, but how to make the next ten thousand with less friction.

This is where deployment creates knowledge that design cannot fully anticipate.

The first project discovers awkward interfaces. The tenth standardizes them. The hundredth creates specialized suppliers. Workers learn which steps actually consume time. Lenders learn which risks are routine and which are fatal. Regulators learn which requirements protect the public and which merely reproduce paperwork. Manufacturers learn where defects arise. Developers learn which contracts survive delays.

The product improves because the production system improves.

But the reverse can happen too.

A sector can possess excellent technology and poor build capability.

Nuclear power offers examples. The physics of light-water reactors is not new, yet construction outcomes vary enormously by country and era. A program that repeats a design with experienced suppliers and stable project teams is a different industrial object from a one-off megaproject assembled after a long hiatus. The reactor may look similar on a diagram. The institutions building it are not.

The same principle applies to grids.

The International Energy Agency's *Electricity 2026* describes more than 2,500 gigawatts of renewable generation, storage, and large-load projects waiting in grid connection queues worldwide. It estimates that planning, permitting, and completing new grid infrastructure can take roughly five to fifteen years, while many renewable projects and data centers can be built in a fraction of that time.

The faster layer is colliding with the slower layer.

This is what happens when build rates diverge.

Imagine a region where solar projects can be completed in two years but major transmission takes ten. For a while, the region can connect projects into spare network capacity. Then the slack disappears. At that point, another decline in module prices may matter less than a year removed from a transmission schedule.

The bottleneck has moved from generation technology to institutional throughput.

That word, throughput, belongs here.

A permitting office has throughput. An engineering firm has throughput. A transformer factory has throughput. A port has throughput. A skilled trade has throughput. A utility's interconnection team has throughput. A legislature can even be thought of as having throughput when laws, budgets, and approvals accumulate faster than the institution can resolve them.

None of these constraints is permanent.

All of them can become binding.

The temptation is to respond by declaring one of them the real constraint.

It is usually more useful to ask which one is binding now.

If a transformer manufacturer adds a second line, the constraint may move to electrical steel. If steel supply expands, the delay may move to testing equipment. If equipment arrives, the utility may lack crews. If crews are hired, rights-of-way may be unresolved. If routes are permitted, a local substation may need expansion.

A build system is a chain of complements.

Its speed is often governed by the slowest necessary step.

This is why money alone cannot instantly accelerate infrastructure.

Capital matters enormously. The IEA's *World Energy Investment 2026* estimates total global energy investment at about $3.4 trillion in 2026, with roughly $2.2 trillion going to clean energy. Those figures describe an extraordinary mobilization of capital.

But spending is not the same as completion.

If a country doubles funding for transmission while the supply of qualified engineers, high-voltage transformers, specialized cable, and permitted corridors barely changes, the immediate effect can be higher prices rather than twice as many lines.

The physical economy cannot always absorb money at the speed money can be authorized.

This is not an argument against investment.

It is an argument for thinking about productive capacity alongside finance.

The distinction is familiar in war, where governments quickly discover that a budget is not ammunition. It is familiar in housing, where cheap credit cannot create zoned land, construction labor, and materials by decree. It is familiar in health care, where appropriations cannot train an experienced nurse overnight.

Energy infrastructure obeys the same rule.

A dollar is a claim on resources.

It is not the resource itself.

This becomes especially important when many sectors try to expand at once.

Electrification asks for more transformers, more switchgear, more cable, more substations, more motors, more power electronics, more generation, and more construction labor. Data centers ask for some of the same equipment and workers. Factories producing batteries, chips, heat pumps, electrolyzers, and other equipment add their own electricity demand while also competing for capital and industrial inputs.

A boom can create the shortages required to slow the boom.

That is not evidence that the transition has failed.

It is what rapid scaling looks like from inside the supply chain.

Prices rise in the constrained component. Delivery times lengthen. New suppliers enter. Buyers redesign around substitutes. Factories expand. Standards change. The constraint may eventually ease.

The danger lies in mistaking a temporary bottleneck for a permanent physical limit, or in making the opposite error and assuming every bottleneck will dissolve on schedule.

Both mistakes confuse possibility with rate.

Copper exists in the Earth. That does not tell us how quickly new mines, refineries, wire plants, and recycling systems can expand.

Transmission technology exists. That does not tell us how quickly a corridor can be agreed, permitted, financed, and constructed.

Skilled work can be taught. That does not mean an experienced workforce can be summoned instantly.

Factories can be built. That does not mean they arrive before the demand they are supposed to serve.

The rate is a fact of its own.

This is also why geographic comparisons are so revealing.

IRENA's 2025 installation data show extraordinary global growth alongside extraordinary concentration. Asia accounted for most new renewable capacity. Some regions added many times more than others, even after accounting for differences in population and existing system size.

The sun does not shine only in Asia.

The difference is not resource physics alone.

Countries vary in manufacturing depth, financing cost, grid access, land rules, political stability, procurement systems, permitting, utility competence, labor availability, demand growth, and the ability to coordinate large projects.

A technology can be global while build capability remains local.

That gap matters for forecasts.

A model may assign the same capital cost to two projects in two countries. The actual investor faces different interest rates, contract risks, import rules, currencies, connection delays, tax systems, and probabilities of finishing on time.

The engineering drawing travels easily.

The institutional environment does not.

This is one reason global cost curves should not be mistaken for universal deployment curves.

A cheap module is a tradable object.

A functioning power system is a local achievement.

The build-rate perspective also changes how we think about technology competition.

Suppose Technology A produces electricity more cheaply once completed, but takes eight years to permit and build. Technology B costs somewhat more per unit of output but can be installed in eighteen months. Which is cheaper?

The answer depends on what the system needs and when.

If demand is flat and capital is patient, Technology A may dominate. If a large factory needs power in two years, Technology B may be worth more despite a higher levelized cost. If B can be built modularly while A requires one large commitment, option value matters. If A lasts twice as long, the comparison changes again.

Time is part of cost.

Not metaphorically.

A delayed asset produces no service while capital remains committed and demand goes unmet or is served by something else. Construction risk affects financing. Long schedules expose projects to inflation, political change, design revisions, litigation, and supply-chain shocks.

Fast construction does not automatically mean low total cost.

It means speed has economic value.

That value is rising in parts of the electricity system because demand itself is beginning to move faster.

Data centers can appear on planning maps on schedules that utilities once associated with much smaller loads. New manufacturing projects can request hundreds of megawatts. Electric vehicles and heat pumps diffuse through millions of decentralized purchases rather than a single utility plan. A grid built for years of slow demand growth can suddenly face concentrated requests that outrun its traditional planning cycle.

The IEA's electricity outlook is notable not only because it expects demand to grow.

It is notable because different pieces of the system now operate on different clocks.

That is the build-rate problem in its purest form.

A data center may be designed in months.

A gas turbine may be ordered against a crowded manufacturing book.

A solar farm may move from finance to construction quickly but wait years for interconnection.

A transmission project may require a decade.

A nuclear project may take longer still in some jurisdictions.

A building retrofit proceeds one property at a time.

A vehicle fleet turns over through millions of household decisions.

There is no single transition speed.

There are overlapping clocks.

Policy becomes more effective when it notices them.

A subsidy aimed at equipment cost is useful when equipment cost is binding. It can be nearly irrelevant when the binding problem is connection time. A faster permit is valuable when permitting is slow. It does little when a key transformer has a three-year delivery date. Workforce training matters when workers are scarce. It does not solve an insolvent buyer.

Good policy is not the maximum application of every accelerator.

It is diagnosis.

That is difficult because the bottleneck changes as policy succeeds.

Early solar policy could focus on demand creation and manufacturing scale. Later systems with large solar fleets may care more about storage, network capacity, market design, and midday curtailment. Early electric-vehicle policy may focus on vehicle cost. Later policy may care more about charging infrastructure, distribution upgrades, apartment access, and peak management.

Maturity changes the problem.

The institution that keeps using the first solution after the first constraint has disappeared begins solving yesterday.

This is one reason targets are both useful and dangerous.

A target can coordinate investment. Manufacturers see expected demand. Utilities can plan networks. Training programs can anticipate labor needs. Governments can align standards and procurement.

But a target can also hide the build system beneath a single number.

Fifty gigawatts by 2030 sounds precise.

It says nothing about the factory, the queue, the line, the substation, the crew, the financing, the land, the maintenance, or the backup needed to make those gigawatts useful.

The target is the top line of a production plan.

The transition is the production plan.

This is where optimism and realism can finally stop pretending to be enemies.

There is genuine evidence for optimism in the speed at which some technologies now scale. Hundreds of gigawatts of renewable capacity can be added globally in a year. Battery factories can expand rapidly. Modular technologies can be manufactured in parallel rather than constructed entirely on site. Supply chains respond to demand. Learning lowers costs. Repetition can make the extraordinary routine.

There is equally genuine evidence for caution.

Grid investment has lagged the assets seeking connection. Large infrastructure often requires years of planning. Regional deployment remains uneven. Capital can outrun skilled labor and equipment. Fast-growing demand can consume newly created capacity before the system feels abundant.

Neither observation cancels the other.

They describe the same transition at different layers.

The useful question is not whether the energy transition is fast or slow.

It is: fast relative to what?

Fast relative to the previous year's installations?

Fast relative to demand growth?

Fast relative to the retirement of old assets?

Fast relative to climate goals?

Fast relative to grid expansion?

Fast relative to the life of buildings, vehicles, and factories?

A percentage without a denominator can flatter almost any story.

The physical system supplies the denominator.

This brings the argument back to civilization.

Humanity's energetic power did not rise simply because people discovered fire, steam, electricity, fission, photovoltaics, or electrochemistry. It rose because societies developed the ability to reproduce useful arrangements at scale.

The steam engine mattered when mines, factories, foundries, machinists, coal networks, and finance made engines common.

Electricity mattered when generators, wires, standards, meters, motors, appliances, and utilities became a system.

Modern solar matters because a global industrial machine can turn purified silicon into modules by the hundreds of millions and place them in projects across continents.

The deepest technology may be the capacity to build technologies.

That capacity is easy to overlook because it has no single inventor.

It lives in firms, training systems, ports, codes, factories, banks, laboratories, regulators, construction crews, software, standards, and habits. It is distributed knowledge made physical.

When it is healthy, projects become boring.

Schedules become predictable. Components arrive. Designs repeat. Costs stop surprising everyone. Workers know the sequence. Regulators know the questions worth asking. Lenders can price risk without guessing wildly. Maintenance has a supply chain. Replacement parts exist twenty years later.

Boring is a high form of industrial achievement.

The heroic prototype proves that something can work.

The boring installation proves that society knows how to have another one.

That is the test facing every promise of energy abundance.

Can it be repeated?

Can it be financed?

Can it connect?

Can it be maintained?

Can suppliers expand without moving the bottleneck somewhere worse?

Can the system train people as quickly as it orders machines?

Can institutions make decisions at the pace the technology allows without abandoning the reasons those decisions require scrutiny?

And can the build rate remain high for long enough to change the stock, not merely the headlines?

The answers will differ by technology and place.

That is precisely why build rate deserves to be treated as a first-class variable rather than an afterthought.

The future arrives one completed asset at a time.

A forecast can draw the destination in an afternoon.

Civilization still has to build the road.
