# The Cost of Reliability

A power system can be cheap on average and still fail at the moment that matters.

This sounds obvious until electricity is discussed almost entirely through annual totals.

A solar farm produces a certain number of megawatt-hours in a year. A wind fleet produces another number. A nuclear plant produces another. Analysts divide costs by output and compare technologies. The arithmetic is useful. It is not the system.

The system has to answer a harder question.

What happens at 7:14 on a freezing January evening when demand is high, the sun is gone, wind output is weak across a large region, a transmission line is unavailable, and several generators are already committed?

Electricity has always had this problem because supply and demand must remain balanced continuously. What changes in a system with more variable generation is not the existence of reliability, but the shape of the problem.

Cheap energy is not the same thing as dependable power.

A grid needs both.

This distinction is one of the most important in the entire energy transition because it separates two quantities that are easy to blend together: energy and capacity.

Energy is how much electricity a resource produces over time.

Capacity is how much power it can deliver at a moment.

A third concept sits beside them: flexibility, the ability to change production or consumption when the system needs it.

The cheapest future grid will depend on how these three fit together.

Solar power demonstrates why.

Solar modules can produce very low-cost electricity during sunny hours in favorable locations. As more solar is added, those hours can become flooded with electricity. Prices fall. Sometimes they become negative. The marginal value of another solar megawatt at noon can decline even while solar remains one of the cheapest generation technologies ever built.

Nothing has gone wrong with the module.

The system has changed around it.

The valuable question becomes what to do with electricity when it is abundant and how to serve demand when it is not.

That is a storage question.

It is also a transmission question, a demand-flexibility question, a generation-diversity question, and sometimes a fuel-security question.

Batteries have moved quickly because they solve several of those problems at once.

The International Energy Agency’s 2024 battery report described power-sector battery storage as the fastest-growing commercially available energy technology in 2023. Costs had fallen dramatically from the levels of the previous decade. The technology can respond in seconds, shift energy across hours, relieve some congestion, support frequency control, and provide capacity during peaks.

Those are real capabilities.

They are not proof that batteries solve every reliability problem.

Duration matters.

A battery rated for four hours can deliver its full power for roughly four hours before it must recharge. That can be enormously valuable for moving afternoon solar output into the evening peak. It is less useful if a region faces several days of low wind and weak solar at the same time.

The distinction between short-duration and long-duration storage is not a semantic one.

It is the difference between moving energy across the clock and moving energy across weather.

The IEA’s more recent Electricity 2026 analysis shows how rapidly utility-scale battery deployment is growing and how useful batteries are for short-term system flexibility. But the same analysis also shows that average durations in many markets remain measured in hours, not days.

This is not a defect.

It is a clue about the job batteries are currently best at.

A power system does not require every resource to solve every problem. It requires the portfolio to solve the full set.

Short-duration batteries can reduce the need for peaking plants, smooth renewable ramps, respond to sudden disturbances, and absorb surplus energy that would otherwise be curtailed. Pumped hydro can provide larger stores in suitable geography. Reservoir hydropower can shift generation across time where water systems allow it. Thermal storage can move heating and cooling demand. Electric vehicles can change when they charge. Industrial loads can sometimes move production or charge thermal stores. Transmission can move power between places whose weather and demand are different.

And firm generators can remain available for periods when storage and variable renewables are insufficient.

The hard argument is not whether storage matters.

It plainly does.

The hard argument is how much of each kind of flexibility a reliable low-carbon grid needs, and what it costs to keep rarely used resources available.

That last part is easy to underestimate.

A generator that runs only a few days a year may produce expensive electricity per unit of output because its capital and maintenance costs are spread over very few megawatt-hours. Yet the system may value it precisely because those are the days when everything else is tight.

Insurance looks wasteful until the accident.

Electricity reliability has the same political problem as insurance. Voters notice a plant that sits idle. They notice a transmission line that seems underused. They notice a capacity payment to a generator that rarely runs. They do not easily observe the blackout that did not happen.

The absence of failure is difficult to photograph.

This creates a recurring pressure to optimize systems around average conditions.

Average conditions are where most of the energy is produced.

Extreme conditions are where reliability is tested.

That does not mean grids should be designed for every imaginable catastrophe. Redundancy has costs. Overbuilding can waste capital. A system that prepares for a once-in-ten-thousand-year event may impose far more cost on consumers than the avoided damage justifies.

Reliability is an optimization problem under uncertainty.

The uncertainty comes from several places at once.

Demand forecasts can be wrong. Weather can be unusual. Generators fail. Fuel deliveries are interrupted. Rivers run low. Heat waves reduce thermal-plant efficiency while raising air-conditioning demand. Cold weather can freeze equipment and strain gas networks at the same time electricity demand rises. Transmission lines trip. Wildfires force lines out of service. Drought affects hydropower. A cyberattack can disable systems whose physical hardware is intact.

No resource is perfectly firm.

That sentence matters because energy debates often divide technologies into reliable and unreliable categories as if reliability were an intrinsic moral property.

A coal plant can fail.

A gas plant can be unable to obtain gas.

A nuclear plant can trip.

A hydro system can run short of water.

A wind farm can have little wind.

A solar plant can have night.

A battery can be empty.

The useful question is probabilistic: how much dependable contribution does a resource make to the system during the hours of greatest risk?

That contribution changes as the system changes.

The first solar farm added to a summer-peaking grid may reduce peak demand on conventional generators substantially because the peak occurs in daylight. The fiftieth solar farm may contribute less to the evening peak because solar has already pushed the peak later. The capacity value of a technology is not a permanent label attached at the factory.

It is relational.

PJM, the regional grid operator serving all or part of thirteen states and the District of Columbia, makes that relationship unusually visible because it publishes an accreditation called effective load carrying capability, or ELCC. The point of the calculation is not to ask how much electricity a resource can produce in a year. It is to estimate how much adding that resource changes the system’s ability to meet demand during periods when shortage risk is highest.

The answer moves.

For PJM’s 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction, the final class ratings published in February 2026 assigned fixed-tilt solar an ELCC of 7 percent and tracking solar 10 percent. Four-hour storage was rated at 59 percent, six-hour storage at 68 percent, eight-hour storage at 71 percent, and ten-hour storage at 78 percent. Nuclear was rated at 96 percent, coal at 85 percent, gas combined cycle at 78 percent, and single-fuel gas combustion turbines at 67 percent.

Those numbers can look like a league table if they are detached from the model that produced them. They are not.

In an earlier PJM auction, fixed-tilt solar had been rated at 9 percent and tracking solar at 14 percent. The hardware did not become intrinsically less dependable in the interval. The modeled system changed: load shape, fleet composition, outage assumptions, storage, weather, and the timing of risk all affect how much another unit helps. PJM has explicitly noted that a greater share of winter reliability risk can shift class ratings.

This is why accreditation should be read as a property of a resource inside a particular system, not as a permanent engineering badge. A four-hour battery can be extraordinarily valuable when the dominant scarcity event lasts three hours and much less decisive when the hard event stretches across a long winter night or several low-renewable days. A solar plant can contribute substantially to a summer afternoon peak and very little to a winter evening peak. A thermal generator with fuel constraints can look firm in a nameplate table and disappoint during a correlated fuel-system failure.

The model boundary matters too. ELCC estimates depend on assumptions about future demand, forced outages, weather, storage dispatch, imports, and the resource mix itself. PJM publishes assumed resource mixes, hourly load scenarios, and critical performance hours alongside its results because the numbers only make sense in that context.

The useful lesson is not that one set of ELCC values has settled the argument.

It is that reliability value is something a system produces.

Storage has the same property.

The first few batteries can capture high-value price spreads and cover short peaks. As more batteries are added, the easiest arbitrage opportunities shrink and the residual reliability problem may stretch across longer periods. The value of another four-hour battery depends on how many other four-hour batteries already exist and what the net-load shape has become.

This is why technology cost curves are not enough.

A battery can become cheaper every year while the marginal system need shifts toward longer duration.

A solar module can become cheaper every year while the value of noon generation falls.

A gas turbine can be expensive to operate yet remain valuable as backup.

The system decides the value at the margin.

Markets try to translate that value into prices.

Energy markets pay for electricity produced. Capacity markets, where they exist, pay resources for being available to meet future peak demand. Ancillary-service markets pay for functions such as frequency regulation and reserves. Some systems rely more heavily on scarcity pricing. Others use regulated planning and procurement.

Each design is an attempt to solve the same awkward fact: reliability is a service that is only partly captured by the number of kilowatt-hours sold.

Poor market design can distort investment.

If prices are capped too tightly during scarcity, generators or storage may not earn enough in rare high-value hours to justify being built. If capacity payments are too generous or badly structured, consumers can pay for resources that contribute little when needed. If rules privilege familiar technologies, newer forms of flexibility may be excluded. If interconnection takes years, technically useful resources can remain stuck in queues.

A reliable grid can be economically inefficient.

An efficient-looking grid can be fragile.

The task is to avoid both.

Demand deserves more attention here because reliability discussions are often supply obsessed.

Demand is not fixed.

Some of it is nearly inflexible. A hospital cannot switch off critical equipment because the wholesale price spikes. A household may be unwilling to sit in dangerous heat. A semiconductor fabrication line may not tolerate interruptions.

Other demand can move.

A water heater can preheat. An electric vehicle can charge later. A cold-storage warehouse can alter compressor timing within thermal limits. A building can precool. Some industrial processes can shift batches. Data centers may move certain computing workloads across time or geography.

Every megawatt of demand that can move away from a tight hour is functionally similar, for that hour, to adding supply.

That makes flexible demand a resource.

But it is not free supply.

People have preferences. Businesses have production schedules. Equipment wears differently. Automation costs money. Customers may not trust utilities to control appliances. Price signals can burden people who have less ability to change when they consume.

A wealthy household with a battery, smart thermostat, electric vehicle, and flexible schedule can respond to time-varying prices more easily than a renter in an inefficient apartment who comes home from a night shift and needs cooling immediately.

Reliability policy can become distribution policy without announcing itself.

This is one reason the phrase demand response can sound more frictionless than the thing itself.

There is a human being on the other side of the meter.

Transmission changes the reliability problem in another way.

Weather is correlated, but not perfectly.

A larger grid can connect regions with different wind patterns, solar conditions, hydrology, and demand peaks. It can share reserves and reduce the amount of backup each area needs to hold alone. Geographic diversity is a form of insurance.

But transmission also creates shared dependencies.

A large interconnected grid can move power across vast distances, but failures on major corridors can affect large regions. Building lines takes years. Local opposition can be intense. A region that relies heavily on imports during scarcity must trust that neighboring regions will still have surplus power and that political rules will permit exports when everyone is stressed.

Interconnection reduces some risks and creates others.

That is the pattern again.

Constraints move.

A system with abundant solar may discover an evening capacity constraint. Add batteries and it may discover a multi-day weather constraint. Add long-distance transmission and it may discover a permitting constraint. Add flexible demand and it may discover a participation or equity constraint. Add firm low-carbon generation and it may discover a capital-cost or construction constraint.

The purpose of planning is not to find the technology that ends constraints.

It is to assemble a system whose constraints are manageable.

This becomes especially important as electricity takes on more of civilization’s work.

Electrify transportation, heating, industry, and computation, and outages become more consequential. A gasoline car with a full tank does not care about a two-hour grid outage. An electric vehicle may not care either if it was charged the night before. A home with gas heat may ride through a short electrical interruption differently from a fully electric home whose heat pump and circulation equipment are offline.

Electrification can improve resilience in some cases and concentrate dependence in others.

A distributed battery can keep a house or critical facility operating during an outage. A microgrid can isolate from a failing network. Electric vehicles may eventually provide backup power more commonly. Rooftop solar paired with storage can create local resilience.

But none of those is automatic.

Solar panels without the right inverter and controls often shut down during a grid outage. A battery reserved entirely for market arbitrage may be empty when a storm arrives. A microgrid requires design, protection systems, maintenance, and fuel or energy planning of its own.

Resilience is designed.

It is not a by-product of owning fashionable hardware.

The same is true at national scale.

A low-carbon grid with wind, solar, batteries, nuclear, hydro, transmission, flexible demand, and perhaps low-emissions fuels can be extremely reliable.

So can a fossil-heavy grid.

Either can also fail if planning assumptions are wrong, infrastructure is poorly maintained, fuel systems are exposed, or extreme events exceed design expectations.

The energy transition therefore cannot be judged only by levelized cost or annual emissions.

It must survive bad weeks.

This is where the phrase energy abundance needs its sharpest qualification.

Abundance measured over a year can coexist with scarcity measured over an hour.

A region can curtail terawatt-hours of renewable electricity in spring and face shortage risk during a winter evening. The contradiction disappears once time is included.

Energy is not merely a quantity.

It has a timestamp.

Storage changes timestamps.

Transmission changes locations.

Flexible demand changes schedules.

Firm generation changes confidence about what will be available when forecasts are wrong.

A reliable system buys all four in some combination.

The combination will evolve.

Battery costs may keep falling. Long-duration storage may become commercially routine. Advanced geothermal may provide firm power in more places. Nuclear plants may become easier or harder to build. Hydrogen or other fuels may provide seasonal backup. Vehicle fleets may become a vast distributed storage resource. Better forecasting may reduce reserve needs. More transmission may smooth weather. Demand automation may become nearly invisible.

Any of those developments could change the least-cost portfolio substantially.

That is why confident claims about the final grid mix are usually less useful than claims about the functions the grid must perform.

It must produce enough energy.

It must deliver enough power at the peak.

It must respond quickly to disturbances.

It must survive equipment failures.

It must bridge predictable daily mismatches and rarer longer shortages.

It must recover after failure.

And it must do all of that at a cost people and industries will continue to pay.

Those functions are stable even when the technologies are not.

The remarkable fall in battery costs is evidence for optimism because it expands the set of problems that can be solved cheaply. The rapid deployment described by the IEA is evidence that storage is moving from demonstration to infrastructure.

But optimism is strongest when it is specific.

Batteries are already very good at several things.

They do not need to be good at everything.

A mature energy system will not be built by insisting that one technology become universal. It will be built by letting technologies specialize, compete, complement one another, and reveal which reliability problems remain after each new layer is added.

The grid does not care whether a megawatt is ideologically elegant.

It cares whether the megawatt is there.

At the right second.

For long enough.
