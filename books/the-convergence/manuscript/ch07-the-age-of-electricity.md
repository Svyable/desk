# The Age of Electricity

A gasoline car, a gas furnace, a blast furnace, a diesel generator, and a server rack do not look like members of the same family.

They draw on different fuels, operate at different temperatures, and do different kinds of work. One moves a person. One heats a room. One reduces iron ore. One produces backup power. One performs computation.

Yet an increasing number of their replacements share the same input.

A battery-electric car turns electricity into motion. A heat pump turns it into useful heat by moving thermal energy rather than creating it directly. An electric arc furnace uses it to melt steel. A battery stores it. An electrolyzer uses it to split water. An induction stove, a warehouse robot, a data center, a commuter train, and a factory motor can all be connected to the same carrier.

The striking thing is not simply that electricity use is growing.

It is that electricity can be generated in one place, moved through a network, transformed almost instantly, and converted into light, motion, heat, sound, chemistry, or computation at the point where the service is needed. Coal cannot do that without first becoming something else. Oil cannot do that. Natural gas cannot do that.

Electricity is not a primary energy source in the ordinary sense. It is a carrier.

That sounds like a technical distinction until an economy begins reorganizing around it.

The nineteenth-century grid separated the location of generation from the location of work. The twenty-first-century electrification wave is extending that separation into sectors that remained stubbornly tied to direct combustion. Vehicles no longer need to carry liquid fuel if they can carry stored electricity. Buildings no longer need to burn gas for every unit of heat if a heat pump can move several units of ambient heat with one unit of electric input. Some industrial processes can replace flames with resistance, induction, arcs, plasma, or electrochemistry.

The common interface matters.

A device that uses electricity does not need to know whether the electrons entering it were ultimately supplied by a gas turbine, a nuclear reactor, a hydroelectric dam, a wind farm, a solar array, or a battery that charged two hours earlier. The physical grid still has to balance supply and demand, and the source matters enormously for cost, emissions, and reliability. But from the perspective of the end-use machine, the carrier is standardized.

That makes electricity unusually compatible with technological substitution.

Change the generator, and the refrigerator can stay the same.

Change the car from gasoline to electric, and the same power system that serves the refrigerator can now serve part of transportation.

Add solar, nuclear, wind, geothermal, storage, or a new transmission line, and the benefits can flow into many sectors without replacing every end-use device again.

The grid becomes something like an energy interface between a changing collection of sources and a changing collection of machines.

That is one reason the phrase “Age of Electricity” is more than marketing.

In 2025, according to the International Energy Agency, global electricity demand grew by about 3 percent. Total global energy demand grew by 1.3 percent. Electricity was therefore expanding more than twice as fast as energy use overall.

One year does not establish an epoch.

But the pattern is not confined to one year.

Electricity demand has been growing faster than total energy demand over a longer period, and the list of reasons is broad rather than singular. Air conditioning is spreading. Appliance ownership is rising. Electric vehicles are replacing part of petroleum demand. Heat pumps are displacing some direct combustion in buildings. Industrial processes are electrifying where economics and engineering allow it. Data centers are adding a new category of concentrated load. In wealthy countries where electricity demand had been nearly flat for years, growth has begun to return.

The important part is the diversity.

If electricity growth depended on one fashionable technology, the story would be fragile. A slowdown in that technology could end it.

Instead the same carrier is being pulled by cooling, transport, buildings, industry, digital infrastructure, and rising living standards in countries where electricity consumption per person remains far below the levels common in richer economies.

This is what a general-purpose energy carrier looks like when more of an economy begins to converge on it.

The word convergence is useful because none of these technologies has to dominate by itself.

An electric car and a heat pump do not solve the same problem. Their common feature is that both shift demand away from a dedicated fuel supply chain and toward a shared electrical system.

That shift can improve efficiency before the electricity itself becomes cleaner.

An internal-combustion engine discards a large share of the chemical energy in gasoline as waste heat. An electric motor converts a much larger share of its electrical input into motion. A resistance heater converts electricity into heat efficiently at the point of use, while a heat pump goes further by moving heat from outside to inside and can deliver several units of heat for each unit of electricity consumed under suitable conditions.

This is one of the reasons comparisons based only on primary-energy totals can become misleading during electrification.

When a coal plant burns fuel to produce electricity, statistical systems count the thermal energy entering the plant, including the heat that never becomes electricity. When a wind turbine or solar panel supplies electricity, there is no analogous boiler loss to count in the same physical way. Different statistical agencies handle this accounting problem with different conventions.

A country can therefore reduce measured primary energy while delivering the same or more useful energy services simply by replacing combustion with technologies that avoid some conversion losses.

That is not statistical magic.

The lost heat was real.

Electrification can remove some of it.

This makes the transition easy to misunderstand in both directions.

People who equate progress with higher primary-energy consumption can miss a genuine increase in useful capability. People who equate lower primary energy with lower environmental impact can miss the materials, land, generation, and infrastructure required to produce and deliver the electricity.

The service is what matters. So is the whole system that provides it.

Consider transportation.

Electric vehicles still accounted for only a minority of the global vehicle fleet in 2025, but new sales had become large enough to alter fuel demand at the margin. The IEA estimated that electric vehicles displaced roughly 1.7 million barrels of oil per day that year. In China alone, the displacement was around one million barrels per day.

That is no longer a laboratory effect.

It is a fuel substitution visible in global energy balances.

Yet the electricity used by the global EV fleet was still only around one percent of final electricity demand.

The asymmetry is instructive.

Replacing a significant amount of petroleum transport does not require an equivalent amount of electrical energy because the electric drivetrain is far more efficient. The vehicle still requires steel, batteries, roads, tires, charging equipment, and a power system. None of those disappear. But the energy chain changes.

The same basic logic applies to heat pumps.

A furnace creates heat by burning fuel. A heat pump mostly moves heat that already exists in the environment. The colder the weather and the worse the equipment, the harder that becomes, but the physical advantage can still be large.

The sales path has not been smooth. Global heat-pump sales actually fell slightly in 2025, according to the IEA, even as Europe returned to growth and heat pumps outsold gas boilers in Germany for the first time. The United States declined that year after a stronger earlier period.

This is useful counterevidence.

A technology can make energetic sense without marching upward in a clean exponential curve. Equipment costs matter. Electricity and gas prices matter. Building stock matters. Installer capacity matters. Regulation matters. Climate matters. A household replacing a broken furnace does not experience “electrification” as a grand civilizational trend. It experiences a contractor quote.

Transitions occur through millions of such mundane decisions.

That is one reason the broad electricity numbers are more informative than any single product category.

In 2025, the buildings sector accounted for nearly 45 percent of the increase in global electricity demand. Transport contributed more than 10 percent, more than double its average share of electricity-demand growth over the previous decade. Data-center electricity consumption rose much faster in percentage terms, but its roughly 70 terawatt-hour increase was still small beside the roughly 800 terawatt-hours of total global electricity-demand growth.

This prevents another fashionable mistake.

Artificial intelligence is becoming an important electrical load. It is not the entire Age of Electricity.

Most new power demand still comes from the ordinary spread of appliances, cooling, buildings, industry, mobility, and rising incomes.

That fact matters because it makes electrification harder to dismiss as a temporary technology cycle.

The supply side is changing at the same time.

In 2025, renewables supplied the overwhelming majority of new power-generation capacity added worldwide. The International Renewable Energy Agency recorded 692 gigawatts of new renewable capacity, taking the global total to 5,149 gigawatts. Solar accounted for roughly three-quarters of the renewable additions, with wind supplying most of the rest.

Installed capacity is not electricity generation.

A gigawatt of solar does not produce as many annual kilowatt-hours as a gigawatt of a plant that can run near full output for most of the year. Capacity factors differ. Wind and solar output varies with weather and time of day. Hydropower varies with water. Nuclear plants can run at high capacity factors but require long construction schedules and substantial capital. Gas turbines can respond quickly but consume fuel and emit carbon unless their emissions are otherwise controlled.

The distinction matters enough to repeat because capacity statistics are irresistible to anyone looking for a dramatic chart.

A record year of solar installations does not mean solar produced a record-sized share of every hour of electricity. It means the machinery capable of producing future electricity expanded rapidly.

Generation tells the next part of the story.

By 2025, renewables taken together were producing roughly as much electricity globally as coal. Nuclear generation was also rising. In the European Union, solar and wind together generated a larger share of electricity than fossil fuels for the first time.

But coal had not vanished.

Natural gas generation had not vanished.

Electricity can be a low-carbon carrier. It is not intrinsically one.

A battery-electric vehicle charged from a coal-heavy grid has a different emissions profile from the same vehicle charged from a low-carbon grid. A heat pump can reduce emissions dramatically in one power system and much less in another. An electric furnace can eliminate on-site combustion while shifting the emissions to the generator supplying it.

Electrification therefore creates an opportunity for decarbonization, not a guarantee.

This is the strongest counterargument to treating electricity itself as progress.

If the power sector remains dirty, electrification can simply move combustion upstream.

There are still reasons the move can matter. Electric motors and heat pumps can be more efficient than direct combustion. Pollution may be easier to control at large centralized plants than across millions of small engines and burners. Most importantly, once an end use is electric, cleaning the grid can clean many uses at once.

But the emissions benefit depends on the grid actually getting cleaner.

That is happening unevenly.

The same year that solar supplied more than a quarter of the increase in total global energy demand, natural gas supplied around 17 percent. Oil demand still rose. Coal demand still rose. Low-emissions sources supplied most of the growth, but fossil fuels continued to expand too.

The energy transition is not a neat substitution in which one stack disappears as another rises.

For now, it is partly an addition.

The world is building enormous amounts of low-carbon generation while still consuming enormous amounts of fossil fuel.

This is the efficiency paradox at a larger scale.

Better energy technology expands what is possible. It does not automatically decide what retires.

Cost matters here.

Solar and wind are not merely environmental technologies anymore. In many markets they are among the cheapest sources of new electricity generation.

IRENA’s 2026 cost analysis put the global weighted-average levelized cost of newly commissioned utility-scale solar photovoltaic generation in 2025 at about $44 per megawatt-hour and onshore wind at about $33. More than 90 percent of the utility-scale renewable projects commissioned that year produced electricity below the cost of the cheapest new fossil-fuel plant available in their respective markets, by IRENA’s methodology.

That is a remarkable economic change.

It is also easy to overstate.

Levelized cost of electricity is a plant-level measure. It does not price every system requirement created by adding that plant.

A solar farm can produce very cheap electricity at noon and no electricity after sunset. A wind farm can produce at inconvenient times. As variable generation becomes a larger fraction of a system, the value of another unit depends increasingly on when and where it arrives. Storage, transmission, backup capacity, flexible demand, and market design matter more.

Cheap generation is not the same thing as cheap reliable electricity around the clock.

That distinction will become central in the chapters ahead.

Still, falling generation cost changes the boundary of what can be electrified.

A process that would never have made sense at expensive power prices may become viable when electricity is cheap for many hours. Batteries can shift some of those hours. Flexible industrial loads can operate when power is abundant. Electrolyzers can turn electricity into hydrogen. Thermal storage can move heat through time. Data centers can, at least in principle, locate part of their load near abundant power.

This is where electrification begins to blur the old division between energy sectors.

The oil system was optimized for portable liquid fuel. The gas system was optimized for molecules delivered through pipelines. The electricity system was historically optimized for simultaneous generation and consumption because large-scale storage was difficult and expensive.

Now those systems are beginning to overlap.

Batteries turn electricity into stored chemical potential and back again. Electric vehicles are mobile storage devices, even if most are not yet used as grid resources. Electrolyzers turn electricity into a molecule. Heat pumps displace delivered gas. Electric furnaces displace combustion. Solar on a roof turns a consumer into a producer for part of the day.

The old categories remain useful, but the borders are becoming porous.

This creates a powerful strategic advantage for electricity.

A society that expands its electrical system is not betting on one end use.

It is building a platform that can serve many.

The analogy to the grid as an API is imperfect, but useful. A software interface allows different programs to exchange standardized instructions without each application needing to understand the internal machinery of the other. Electricity does something similar in physical form. Generators put usable electrical energy onto a common network. End-use machines draw from it according to standardized voltages, frequencies, and market arrangements.

The analogy fails wherever physics refuses abstraction.

Power cannot be routed with the frictionless freedom of data. Transmission lines have thermal limits. Frequency must be maintained. Voltage must be controlled. Supply and demand must balance continuously. Geography matters. Weather matters. Rotating machines, inverters, transformers, protection systems, and operators all contribute to stability.

The interface works only because an enormous hidden system keeps it working.

That hidden system is precisely what becomes more important as more of civilization plugs into it.

Electrification creates dependency as well as optionality.

A gasoline car can keep moving through a local blackout if fuel is available. A gas furnace with electronic controls may still fail, but the fuel network itself is distinct from the electric grid. An industrial facility with several independent energy inputs can sometimes continue partial operation when one fails.

An economy that electrifies transport, heating, industry, and computation concentrates more critical activity on the electrical network.

The reward is a carrier that can be produced from many sources and used by many machines.

The price is that the network becomes more consequential.

Reliability stops being a technical concern of utilities and becomes a condition of ordinary economic life.

This is already visible during outages.

A blackout once meant darkness, stopped motors, and spoiled food. In a more electrified economy it can also mean stranded vehicles, disabled heat pumps, disconnected communications, interrupted computation, silent payment terminals, idle automated warehouses, and factories whose control systems cannot function.

The grid becomes more valuable as more systems depend on it.

It also becomes a larger point of failure.

That does not argue against electrification any more than dependence on roads argues against transport.

It argues for taking the infrastructure seriously.

There is another limit to the electricity story: not every useful energy service is easy to electrify.

A passenger car is easier than a long-haul aircraft. Low-temperature building heat is easier than some forms of extreme industrial heat. A city bus with predictable routes is easier than an ocean-going container ship. Some chemical processes need molecules as feedstocks, not merely energy. Certain applications care about energy density, weight, storage duration, or refueling speed in ways that current batteries do not satisfy cheaply.

Electricity can reach into many sectors without conquering all of them.

Hydrogen, synthetic fuels, biofuels, fossil fuels with carbon management, direct heat, and other carriers may retain roles where their properties fit the problem better.

The claim is not that every joule will become an electron.

It is that an unusually large share of future useful work can plausibly be organized around electricity, and the evidence increasingly shows that this reorganization is underway.

The IEA expects electricity’s share of total final energy consumption to rise from about 21 percent in 2025 to 24 percent by 2030.

Three percentage points can sound small.

Across the world energy system, it is not.

It represents a migration of enormous amounts of useful work into a carrier whose production mix can change independently of the machines consuming it.

The forecast may be wrong.

Economic growth can weaken. Electricity prices can rise. Grid construction can lag. Permitting can slow projects. Heat-pump sales can stall. Electric-vehicle adoption can soften. Data-center plans can be cancelled. Industrial electrification can prove more expensive than anticipated. Fuel prices can alter the economics in the other direction.

Forecasts should be treated as forecasts.

The stronger evidence is the observed direction of travel.

Electricity demand is already growing faster than total energy demand. Electric vehicles are already displacing measurable oil consumption. Renewables already dominate new generation-capacity additions. Solar and wind already compete economically with new fossil generation in many markets. Buildings, transport, industry, and computing are already drawing more heavily on the same carrier.

No single one of those facts proves an age.

Together they describe a system changing its center of gravity.

The deepest reason is not environmental policy, though policy matters.

It is not solar cost, though solar cost matters.

It is not artificial intelligence, electric cars, or heat pumps, though all three matter.

The deeper reason is that electricity is extraordinarily controllable.

It can be switched in milliseconds. It can be converted into many forms of work. It can be transmitted across distance. It can be metered precisely. It can power machines from the microscopic to the enormous. It can be produced by technologies whose underlying physical principles have almost nothing in common.

That makes electricity a natural destination for a civilization that increasingly values precision and flexibility as much as raw energy quantity.

The transition also clarifies the thesis of this book.

Civilization does not advance simply by consuming more fuel.

It advances when energy becomes easier to direct toward the thing that is wanted.

Fire made heat controllable. Storage made biological surplus durable. Coal made ancient solar energy spendable at industrial rates. Steam turned part of that stock into mechanical work. The grid made power movable. Efficiency made services cheaper.

Electrification brings those threads together.

It creates a common carrier through which many different sources can serve many different forms of useful work.

That does not make the carrier clean by definition. It does not remove the need for mines, factories, generation, wires, storage, capital, institutions, or land. It does not abolish scarcity.

It changes where scarcity shows up.

If electricity becomes the preferred input for a growing share of transport, heat, industry, and computation, then the decisive question is no longer only how cheaply humanity can generate another kilowatt-hour.

It is whether that kilowatt-hour can reach the machine that wants it, at the moment it is needed.

The age of electricity therefore carries its next constraint inside its success.
