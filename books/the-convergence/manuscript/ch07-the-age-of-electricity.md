# The Age of Electricity

A car, a furnace, a server rack, and a steel mill do not look like members of the same technological family.

One moves people. One heats a building. One performs computation. One turns ore and scrap into metal. For most of industrial history, their energy systems were correspondingly different. The car carried liquid fuel. The furnace burned gas, oil, coal, or wood. The computer consumed electricity because electronics had no practical alternative. Heavy industry used whatever combination of fuels, heat, steam, and mechanical power its process demanded.

Now the boundaries are becoming less rigid.

A battery can feed an electric motor. A heat pump can replace combustion for some forms of heating. An electric arc can deliver industrial heat. Electrolyzers can turn electricity into hydrogen. A data center can absorb hundreds of megawatts through the same broad power system that serves homes and railways. The machines remain different, but more of them can accept the same carrier.

That carrier is electricity.

The change is visible in the global statistics. The International Energy Agency’s *Global Energy Review 2026* estimates that total world energy demand grew 1.3 percent in 2025. Electricity demand grew about 3 percent, adding roughly 800 terawatt-hours in a single year. Electricity therefore grew around 2.3 times faster than total energy demand. The IEA calls this the arrival of an “Age of Electricity,” but the phrase is more useful as a proposition to test than as a slogan. The observed 2025 data are available in the [Global Energy Review 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/global-trends).

One year can mislead. Weather alone can move electricity demand sharply because cooling and heating loads respond to temperature. In 2024, unusually intense heat helped push global electricity-demand growth to 4.4 percent. In 2025, cooler conditions in several large markets slowed that growth. Industrial cycles matter too. So do fuel prices, hydrology, policy, and economic structure.

The longer comparison is harder to dismiss. The IEA reports that electricity demand grew at an average annual rate of 2.8 percent from 2014 to 2024, already faster than overall energy demand. Its current five-year forecast expects the gap to widen: electricity consumption is projected to rise from about 28,200 TWh in 2025 to 33,600 TWh in 2030, an average annual growth rate of 3.6 percent. Those 2026–2030 values are forecasts, not observations, and should be treated as such. But they are not based on one fashionable end use. The expected growth comes from buildings, industry, electric vehicles, cooling, heat pumps, and data centers. The [IEA’s Electricity 2026 demand outlook](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/demand) projects electricity to grow at least two and a half times as fast as overall energy demand through 2030.

If that forecast is even approximately right, the important transition is not that the world has discovered a new source of energy. It is that more activities are converging on a common way of receiving energy.

Electricity is not primary energy in the ordinary sense. It has to be generated from something else: falling water, moving air, sunlight, uranium, coal, gas, oil, geothermal heat, biomass, or stored chemical energy. What makes it unusual is the range of sources that can produce it and the range of machines that can use it.

This many-to-many quality was already visible in the grid described in Chapter 4. What is different now is scale. The nineteenth-century grid displaced shafts and local prime movers inside factories and cities. The twenty-first-century transition reaches into sectors that were built around direct combustion.

Road transport is an obvious example because the substitution happens inside a familiar object. An internal-combustion car stores chemical energy in liquid fuel and converts part of it into motion through repeated combustion, heat, pressure, and mechanical transmission. A battery-electric car stores electrochemical energy, releases it as electricity, and sends that electricity through power electronics to a motor.

The electric drivetrain does not merely swap one fuel tank for another. It changes the conversion chain.

In 2025, according to the IEA’s [Global Energy Review electric-vehicle data](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-electric-vehicles), electric-car sales rose by more than 20 percent to 21 million vehicles, about one quarter of all cars sold worldwide. China crossed a more striking threshold: electric cars represented more than half of annual car sales there for the first time.

Those sales figures are not the same thing as fleet share, and fleet share is not the same thing as displaced oil. Cars remain on roads for years. Charging requires generation and networks. Battery production requires mining and manufacturing. The emissions result depends on the electricity used to charge the vehicle and on lifecycle effects.

But the energy conversion itself changes quickly once the vehicle is electric. The IEA’s 2025 EV outlook estimated that global electric vehicles consumed about 180 TWh in 2024, only around 0.7 percent of final electricity consumption. Under its stated-policies scenario, EV electricity demand could rise to 780 TWh by 2030. Yet total road-transport energy demand in that scenario rises only about 5 percent while vehicle-kilometres traveled rise almost 20 percent. The reason is not restraint. Electric drivetrains require less energy for the same movement than combustion drivetrains.

Electrification can therefore increase electricity demand while reducing the primary or final energy needed for a service.

This is one reason the transition can look strange in aggregate statistics. A gasoline car replaced by an electric car shifts demand from petroleum into the power system, but the number of joules entering the vehicle can fall substantially. A gas furnace replaced by a heat pump can do something even stranger: the machine uses electricity not principally to create heat but to move heat from one place to another. Under suitable conditions, one unit of electrical energy can deliver several units of heat to a building.

If the purpose is a warm room, counting only energy purchased at the meter misses what the device is doing.

The heat-pump market also demonstrates why technological transitions refuse to move in smooth lines. Global heat-pump sales fell about 2 percent in 2025, according to the IEA. Sales rose 11 percent in Europe and heat pumps outsold gas boilers in Germany for the first time, while U.S. sales fell around 13 percent amid refrigerant changes, supply constraints, and weaker housing construction. The [IEA’s 2026 heat-pump review](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-heat-pumps) is a useful warning against reading one global growth rate as a universal transition speed.

Electrification is not a single adoption curve. It is thousands of substitutions, each governed by equipment turnover, capital cost, local prices, regulation, climate, infrastructure, and the physical demands of the task.

Some tasks are easy to electrify. Electronics are electric by definition. Motors are mature. Resistance heating is simple. Heat pumps are well suited to many buildings. Railways can draw power continuously from wires. Other tasks are harder. Long-distance aviation values fuels with high energy density. Some industrial processes require extreme temperatures, chemical feedstocks, or equipment designed around combustion. Ships, mines, farms, and remote facilities have constraints that a wall socket does not solve.

The Age of Electricity should therefore not be understood as the disappearance of molecules.

Oil, gas, coal, biomass, hydrogen, and other fuels will remain useful or difficult to replace in many applications. Even a deeply electrified system may use molecules as feedstocks, storage media, backup fuels, or inputs to processes that electricity cannot economically perform directly. The claim is narrower: electricity is becoming the preferred carrier for a growing range of useful work because it is unusually controllable, convertible, and compatible with technologies whose costs are falling.

The supply side is changing at the same time.

In 2025, solar photovoltaic generation increased by roughly 600 TWh, reaching nearly 2,700 TWh globally and more than doubling its 2022 output. The IEA describes that annual increase as the largest ever observed for any electricity source outside rebound years following major economic shocks. Solar’s share of global electricity generation moved above 8 percent. The same year, combined renewable and nuclear generation grew by more than the entire increase in global electricity supply, while fossil generation declined slightly as lower coal generation outweighed a modest increase in gas. The observed supply figures are reported in the [Global Energy Review 2026 electricity analysis](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/electricity-supply).

Installed capacity tells an even more dramatic story, but capacity must not be confused with generation. IRENA reports that global renewable power capacity reached 5,149 GW at the end of 2025 after 692 GW of additions, a 15.5 percent increase. Renewables represented 85.6 percent of net power-capacity expansion that year. Those numbers describe maximum generating capacity connected to systems, not the electricity actually produced. A gigawatt of solar, hydro, wind, geothermal, or fossil capacity can generate very different annual energy depending on resource availability, outages, dispatch, and capacity factor. The definitions and country tables are in [IRENA’s Renewable Capacity Statistics 2026](https://www.irena.org/Publications/2026/Mar/Renewable-capacity-statistics-2026).

The distinction matters because the economics of new electricity supply are changing faster than the composition of the installed system.

IRENA’s project database finds that more than 90 percent of utility-scale renewable projects commissioned in 2025 produced electricity at a levelized cost below the cheapest new fossil-fuel plant available in their market. Its global weighted-average cost for newly commissioned utility-scale solar PV was about $44 per megawatt-hour in 2025, unchanged from 2024, while onshore wind fell to about $33 per megawatt-hour. These are levelized generation costs under IRENA’s methodology, not retail electricity prices and not complete system costs. Transmission, storage, balancing, financing conditions, curtailment, backup, and local network investment do not vanish because a generator is cheap. The underlying methods and 2025 project data are published in [IRENA’s Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2025](https://www.irena.org/Publications/2026/Jul/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2025).

Still, a world in which new generators can be built without a continuous fuel bill is different from the one that created the industrial energy system.

A coal plant is a machine attached to a fuel supply. So is a gas turbine. Its economics remain coupled to extraction, processing, transport, and commodity prices for as long as it operates. A solar module or wind turbine requires material and energy to manufacture, install, maintain, and eventually replace, but its operating energy arrives as a flow rather than a purchased fuel. The capital cost is paid to construct a device that intercepts that flow.

This does not make renewable electricity free. It changes where the costs sit.

That shift helps explain why solar cost decline matters beyond the solar industry itself. Goksin Kavlak, James McNerney, and Jessika Trancik examined photovoltaic module costs from 1980 to 2012 and decomposed the decline into mechanisms rather than treating learning as magic. Their [2018 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2018.08.015) found important roles for research and development, improvements in module efficiency, economies of scale, and reductions in material use, with different mechanisms dominating at different times.

A learning curve is a historical pattern. The mechanism is what makes it durable.

This matters because electricity becomes more attractive as a universal carrier when both sides of the system improve together. End-use machines become better at turning electricity into services. Generators become better at producing electricity from diverse primary sources. Power electronics become better at controlling flows between them. Batteries and other forms of storage can move some electricity through time. Transmission can move it through space. Software can coordinate loads that were once passive.

The result is not one technology but a stack.

A battery-electric car is useful because batteries, motors, power electronics, charging equipment, generation, and networks all work together. A heat pump is useful because compressors, refrigerants, controls, buildings, and the power system work together. A solar farm is valuable because modules, inverters, land, finance, transmission, forecasting, and balancing work together.

When one layer improves faster than the others, the bottleneck moves.

This is the part of the electricity story that triumphalist cost curves tend to omit. A cheap solar module cannot connect itself to a congested grid. A wind project with excellent economics can wait years for interconnection. A household may want an electric vehicle but lack convenient charging. A factory may be able to replace combustion equipment with an electric process but discover that its local substation cannot supply the additional load. A data center may possess land, servers, and customers yet wait for hundreds of megawatts of firm electrical capacity.

The carrier becomes more valuable, and therefore the infrastructure that carries it becomes more valuable too.

That is the bridge to the next two chapters. Artificial intelligence will make the power requirement of computation unusually visible. Grid queues, transmission lines, transformers, storage, and permitting will reveal that cheap generation is not the same thing as deliverable electricity.

Before reaching those constraints, though, the environmental claim around electrification needs to be narrowed.

Electricity is not clean by nature.

In 2025 coal remained a central part of global power generation. The IEA’s current forecast expects coal to remain the largest single fuel source for electricity through 2030 even while renewables become the largest source category in aggregate. Natural-gas generation is also forecast to grow. In the United States, the IEA expects strong electricity-demand growth through 2030 to be met partly by more gas-fired generation even as solar expands and coal declines. In India, it expects coal generation to continue rising while coal’s share falls because electricity demand grows faster still. The [Electricity 2026 supply outlook](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/supply) makes the uncomfortable point clear: electrification and decarbonization can move in the same direction without moving at the same speed.

A gasoline car charged from a low-carbon grid can reduce emissions substantially. The same car charged from a coal-heavy grid still gains drivetrain efficiency, but the emissions result is less favorable. A heat pump operating on increasingly clean electricity can displace direct fossil combustion. A heat pump on a carbon-intensive grid may still reduce emissions in many circumstances because of its high efficiency, but the answer depends on climate, equipment performance, and the generating mix. Industrial electrification can eliminate combustion at the factory gate while shifting emissions upstream to power plants.

The wire hides the source. Climate accounting cannot.

Nor can it ignore time. Annual generation shares are averages. The emissions associated with an additional electrical load depend partly on which generators respond when the load appears. A system with abundant midday solar can have a different marginal emissions profile at noon than after sunset. Storage, flexible demand, transmission, and dispatch therefore influence the climate value of electrification as surely as the appliance itself.

Lifecycle effects matter too. Solar modules, turbines, batteries, motors, transmission lines, transformers, and nuclear plants require mines, factories, concrete, steel, copper, aluminum, chemicals, land, and transport. Low-carbon does not mean impact-free. The relevant comparison is among complete systems delivering comparable services, not between an idealized clean technology and a fossil system whose external costs are conveniently left off the page.

Even with those qualifications, the direction of the global supply mix is changing. The IEA estimates that deployment of solar, wind, nuclear, electric cars, and heat pumps since 2019 avoided roughly 3 billion tonnes of annual carbon dioxide emissions in 2025 compared with a world without that deployment. This is a modeled counterfactual, not a directly observed quantity, and it depends on assumptions about what technologies would otherwise have supplied the same services. But it gives scale to the interaction between cleaner generation and electrified end uses.

The strongest counterargument to an “Age of Electricity” is therefore not that electrification is imaginary. It is that the phrase can compress too many different transitions into one.

Global electricity demand is rising faster than total energy demand, but most additional consumption still comes from emerging and developing economies, where rising electricity use can reflect basic development as much as fuel switching. In 2025, the IEA estimates that those economies accounted for about 80 percent of global electricity-demand growth. More appliances, cooling, industrial production, and commercial activity all raise electricity demand whether or not they replace another fuel.

Advanced economies complicate the story differently. Their electricity demand was stagnant for much of the previous fifteen years as efficiency gains and industrial restructuring offset new uses. Growth has now returned, but the reasons vary by country. Data centers matter enormously in the United States. Heat pumps and electric vehicles matter differently across Europe. Weather can dominate a single year. A resurgence in electricity demand is evidence of change, not proof that every sector is electrifying at the same rate.

The global average also conceals people for whom the Age of Electricity has not arrived at all.

Hundreds of millions still lack electricity access, and many more have connections that are unreliable or too costly to support the services taken for granted in wealthier systems. For them, electrification is not the replacement of a gasoline car with an EV or a gas boiler with a heat pump. It is first access to lighting, refrigeration, communications, pumps, fans, productive machinery, and cooling.

The welfare value of the next kilowatt-hour is not evenly distributed.

This is why electricity’s growing share should not be treated as a civilization score. A wealthy economy can waste electricity. A poor one can use a small amount to remove a severe constraint. A grid can deliver abundant power and still be dirty, fragile, or unequal. Electrification creates a platform for capability; institutions and infrastructure determine what gets built on it.

The more interesting claim is physical.

Electricity is unusually good at joining domains that were once separate. It can turn a solar photon captured in one region into motor torque somewhere else. It can turn nuclear heat into computation. It can turn falling water into refrigeration. It can move energy from a wind farm through a transmission line into a battery and later into a train. The source and the service do not have to resemble each other.

That separation creates optionality.

A household with a gas furnace is tied to a fuel-distribution system and to equipment that expects gas. A household with electric heating can, in principle, receive its energy from whatever mix of generators feeds the grid over the life of the equipment. An electric vehicle purchased today can become lower-carbon without changing its motor if the generation mix improves. An industrial electric furnace can benefit from a new nuclear plant, a new transmission line, a solar farm, or a wind project without being redesigned around the primary source.

Electrification moves some technological change upstream.

This is a powerful property in a period when the supply mix is changing quickly. It allows end-use equipment to remain useful while generation evolves behind the network. The grid becomes a compatibility layer between primary energy and final service.

But optionality is not the same thing as abundance.

The IEA forecasts that electricity’s share of total final consumption will rise from about 21 percent in 2025 to 24 percent in 2030. Even if that forecast proves accurate, three quarters of final energy consumption will still be outside direct electricity. The world is not approaching an all-electric state in five years. It is entering a period in which electricity takes a larger share of a still-growing and still-diverse energy system.

That is enough to change investment, politics, and industrial strategy.

A system that expects more transport, heating, industry, and computation to depend on electricity has to build differently. Generation matters more. So do wires. Peak demand matters more. Flexible loads become more valuable. Storage becomes more valuable. Reliability failures reach into more sectors at once. Electricity prices become embedded in a larger fraction of economic activity.

The carrier’s success increases the consequences of its failure.

There is a useful symmetry here with the history of fire. Early humans gained capability by learning to maintain a controlled energy conversion outside the body. Industrial civilization multiplied that capability with fossil fuels and machines. The grid then separated energy production from the point of use. Electrification now extends that abstraction into domains that once carried their own fuels.

The machine at the edge increasingly asks for electricity and cares less about where the electricity began.

For the first time, the world has a plausible general-purpose energy carrier that can connect a large share of new end-use demand to a supply system whose primary sources can change underneath it. That does not guarantee lower emissions, cheap power, reliable service, or equitable access. It does mean that improvements in generation can propagate across more kinds of human activity than before.

The convergence is not complete. It may never be.

But the direction is measurable: electricity demand is growing faster than total energy demand; electric vehicles are taking a larger share of car sales; heat pumps are displacing some direct combustion; solar is adding generation at unprecedented speed; renewables dominate new generating capacity; and the next wave of computation is arriving as an electrical load large enough for utilities to notice.

The evidence that would force this chapter to narrow is straightforward. If electricity’s share of final energy stopped rising for a sustained period, if major end uses proved resistant to electrification on cost or performance grounds, or if the present demand acceleration turned out to be mostly temporary weather and data-center effects rather than a broad change across buildings, transport, and industry, then the “Age of Electricity” would describe a cycle more than a transition.

The current record does not establish inevitability. It establishes momentum.

And momentum at this scale moves the constraint.

The question is no longer only whether we can generate electricity cheaply. It is whether the rest of the system can deliver enough of it, where and when the new machines ask.
