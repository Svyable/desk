# The Age of Electricity

A gasoline car, a gas furnace, a blast furnace, a diesel generator, and a server rack do not look like members of the same family. They use different fuels, operate at different temperatures, and do different work.

An increasing number of their replacements, however, share the same input.

A battery-electric car turns electricity into motion. A heat pump uses it to move heat. An electric arc furnace uses it to melt steel. An electrolyzer uses it to split water. A warehouse robot, commuter train, induction stove, factory motor, and GPU cluster can all be connected to the same carrier.

That common input matters more than the individual machines.

Coal can be burned for heat or converted into electricity, but a lump of coal cannot directly run a refrigerator, a tram, and a server. Oil is wonderfully portable, but it remains tied to a chain of refining, transport, combustion, and mechanical conversion. Natural gas is versatile, but it still arrives as a molecule with a narrower range of direct uses.

Electricity is different. It can be generated in one place, moved through a network, transformed almost instantly, metered precisely, and converted at the point of use into light, motion, heat, sound, chemistry, or computation. It is not a primary energy source in the ordinary sense. It is a carrier.

That sounds like an accounting distinction until an economy begins reorganizing around it.

The nineteenth-century grid separated the location of generation from the location of work. Electrification in the twenty-first century is extending that separation into sectors that remained tied to direct combustion. A vehicle no longer needs to carry liquid fuel if it can carry stored electricity. A building no longer needs to burn gas for every unit of heat if a heat pump can move ambient heat instead. Some industrial processes can replace flames with resistance, induction, arcs, or electrochemistry.

The end-use machine does not need to know whether its electricity came from a gas turbine, nuclear reactor, hydroelectric dam, wind farm, solar array, or battery charged two hours earlier. The source still matters enormously for cost, emissions, and reliability. But from the machine's point of view, the interface is standardized.

Change the generator and the refrigerator can stay the same.

That is a profound advantage. It means improvements upstream can propagate across many kinds of downstream equipment without replacing all of them again. A cleaner generator can serve homes, trains, factories, data centers, and heat pumps through the same network. A new transmission line or storage project can improve the usefulness of many generators at once.

The grid becomes an interface between a changing collection of sources and a changing collection of machines.

The data show electricity pulling away from energy demand overall. The International Energy Agency estimates that global electricity demand grew about 3 percent in 2025 while total global energy demand grew 1.3 percent. Electricity demand was expanding more than twice as fast as energy demand overall.

One year does not establish an age.

Seen over half a century, the shift is both larger and slower. IEA statistics put electricity at 9.5 percent of world final energy consumption in 1973 and about 21 percent in 2025. More than doubling its share matters. Taking fifty-two years to do it is a useful antidote to any story in which the world simply wakes up electric.

The recent growth is broad, but that does not make every new kilowatt-hour an act of electrification.

Buildings accounted for nearly 45 percent of the increase in global electricity demand in 2025. Transport contributed more than 10 percent, more than double its average share over the previous decade. Data-center electricity use rose about 17 percent, a spectacular growth rate, but its roughly 70 terawatt-hours of additional consumption were still small beside the roughly 800 terawatt-hours of total global electricity-demand growth.

Some of those loads represent direct substitution. An electric vehicle can replace gasoline. A heat pump can replace gas or oil heat. Other loads are additive: more air conditioning, more appliances, more industrial output, more computation. Rising incomes can increase electricity consumption without displacing a fossil-fuel machine at all.

That distinction keeps the argument honest. Faster electricity demand is consistent with electrification, but the aggregate number cannot tell us how much combustion disappeared because of it.

What the composition does show is something narrower. Electricity is useful enough to be pulled simultaneously by buildings, transport, industry, cooling, appliances, and computing. It can become more central to an economy even when the reasons for using more of it are not all the same.

Electrification can also make primary-energy statistics look strange. An internal-combustion engine throws away a large share of the chemical energy in gasoline as heat. An electric motor converts a much larger share of its electrical input into motion. A resistance heater converts electricity into heat at the point of use; a heat pump goes further by moving heat from outside to inside and, under suitable conditions, can deliver several units of useful heat for each unit of electricity consumed.

A coal-fired power plant, meanwhile, burns fuel to produce electricity and loses much of the fuel's thermal energy before the electricity reaches the customer. Wind and solar do not have an analogous boiler loss. Statistical agencies handle these differences with conventions that can make primary-energy comparisons across technologies misleading if they are read too literally.

A country can reduce measured primary energy while delivering the same or more useful service because some conversion losses have disappeared. That is not an accounting trick. The lost heat was real.

But lower primary energy does not automatically mean lower environmental impact. Electric systems still require mines, factories, transmission lines, generators, storage, land, capital, and maintenance. Electrification changes the energy chain. It does not make the chain weightless.

Transportation makes the asymmetry vivid. The IEA's 2026 Global EV Outlook estimates that the global electric-vehicle fleet displaced roughly 1.7 million barrels of oil per day in 2025 while consuming about 250 terawatt-hours of electricity, around one percent of global final electricity demand. A significant amount of petroleum transport can be replaced by a much smaller amount of electrical energy because electric drivetrains waste less energy as heat.

The vehicle still needs a battery, tires, roads, charging equipment, and a power system. None of those vanish. But the useful motion can require less energy input.

Heat pumps make the same point with a different physical trick: they mostly move heat rather than create it. Yet their sales path is a reminder that favorable physics does not produce frictionless adoption. Global heat-pump sales fell about 2 percent in 2025, according to the IEA, even as Europe returned to double-digit growth. Households do not buy civilizational trends. They buy equipment from contractors while comparing installation costs, utility bills, financing, building constraints, and whatever happens to be available when the old furnace fails.

That is useful counterevidence to any story in which the superior technology simply wins.

The supply side is changing at the same time. The International Renewable Energy Agency recorded 692 gigawatts of new renewable power capacity in 2025, bringing the global total to 5,149 gigawatts. Renewables accounted for 85.6 percent of all net power-capacity additions that year.

Capacity is not generation, and the distinction matters. A gigawatt of solar does not produce the same number of annual kilowatt-hours as a gigawatt of a plant that can operate near full output most of the year. Solar disappears after sunset. Wind follows weather. Hydropower follows water. Nuclear plants can run at high capacity factors but usually require long construction schedules and large up-front investments. Gas turbines can respond quickly but consume fuel and emit carbon unless those emissions are otherwise controlled.

Capacity statistics tell us how much machinery exists to produce electricity. They do not tell us what happened in every hour.

Cost statistics require the same caution. IRENA's July 2026 analysis put the global weighted-average levelized cost of newly commissioned utility-scale solar photovoltaic generation in 2025 at about $44 per megawatt-hour and onshore wind at about $33. More than 90 percent of utility-scale renewable projects commissioned that year produced power below the cost of the cheapest new fossil-fuel plant available in their market under IRENA's methodology.

That is an extraordinary industrial achievement. It is not the same thing as saying reliable electricity is now cheap everywhere.

Levelized cost is primarily a plant-level measure. It does not by itself price the full system around the plant. A solar farm may produce very cheap electricity at noon and none at midnight. As variable generation becomes a larger share of supply, the value of another unit depends increasingly on when and where it arrives. Storage, transmission, flexible demand, backup capacity, interconnection, and market design begin to matter more.

Cheap generation is not identical to cheap power around the clock.

Nor is electricity intrinsically clean. A battery-electric car charged on a coal-heavy grid has a different emissions profile from the same car on a low-carbon grid. An electric furnace can eliminate combustion on site while moving emissions to the generator. A heat pump can cut emissions dramatically in one system and much less in another.

Electrification therefore creates an opportunity for decarbonization, not a guarantee.

This is the strongest case against treating electricity itself as progress. If the power sector remains dirty, electrification can simply move combustion upstream. If network investment lags, new electrical loads can strain reliability or force expensive upgrades. If electricity prices rise faster than competing fuels, customers may resist technologies that look efficient on an engineer's diagram.

Its advantages are real, but conditional.

Once an end use is electric, cleaning the grid can clean many uses at once. Efficient motors and heat pumps can reduce energy requirements before the grid is fully decarbonized. Centralized power plants can sometimes control pollutants more effectively than millions of small engines and burners. But the result depends on generation mix, timing, infrastructure, and the pace at which dirtier assets actually retire.

That last point matters because energy transitions often look like addition before they look like substitution. In 2025, solar supplied more than a quarter of the increase in total global energy demand, according to the IEA. Natural gas supplied around 17 percent. Oil demand still rose. Coal demand still rose. The world built enormous amounts of low-carbon generation while continuing to consume enormous amounts of fossil fuel.

The efficiency paradox has a system-level cousin: better technology can expand capability faster than it displaces what came before.

Electricity nevertheless has a structural advantage because it blurs boundaries between sectors. Batteries turn electricity into stored chemical potential and back again. Electric vehicles move part of transportation demand onto the power system. Electrolyzers turn electricity into a molecule. Heat pumps displace delivered gas. Electric furnaces replace combustion in some industrial processes. Rooftop solar turns a consumer into a producer for part of the day.

The old categories remain useful, but their borders are becoming porous.

A software engineer might be tempted to call the grid an energy API. The analogy is imperfect but useful. Generators supply a standardized carrier to a common network; end-use machines draw from it without needing to understand the machinery behind every kilowatt-hour.

Then physics ruins the neat metaphor, as physics tends to do.

Power cannot be routed with the freedom of data. Transmission lines have thermal limits. Voltage must be controlled. Supply and demand have to remain balanced. Geography matters. Weather matters. Transformers, protection systems, inverters, rotating machines, markets, and operators all contribute to stability.

The interface works only because an enormous hidden system keeps it working.

That hidden system becomes more consequential as more of civilization plugs into it.

A blackout in an electrified economy is no longer only darkness, stopped motors, and spoiled food. It can mean disabled heat pumps, interrupted computation, silent payment terminals, idle automated warehouses, stalled charging, broken communications, and factories whose control systems cannot function. The same network that creates optionality on the supply side can concentrate dependency on the delivery side.

This is not an argument against electrification any more than dependence on roads is an argument against transport. It is an argument for taking the infrastructure seriously.

There are also limits to how far electricity can go. A passenger car is easier to electrify than a long-haul aircraft. Low-temperature building heat is easier than some forms of extreme industrial heat. Some chemical processes need molecules as feedstocks, not simply energy. Ocean shipping and aviation care about weight, storage duration, and refueling speed in ways that current batteries do not solve cheaply.

Hydrogen, synthetic fuels, biofuels, direct heat, and fossil fuels with carbon management may retain roles where their properties fit the problem better. No plausible transition turns every joule into an electron.

The narrower claim is stronger: an unusually large share of useful work can be organized around electricity, and the observed system is already moving in that direction.

The IEA forecasts electricity's share of total final energy consumption rising from about 21 percent in 2025 to 24 percent in 2030. Forecasts deserve humility. Economic growth can weaken. Grid construction can lag. EV and heat-pump adoption can soften. Data-center projects can be cancelled. Industrial electrification can prove uneconomic in processes that looked promising on paper.

The observed facts matter more than the forecast: electricity's share of final consumption has already risen substantially; EVs are already displacing measurable oil consumption; renewables already dominate new global power-capacity additions; and buildings, transport, industry, and computing are already drawing more heavily on the same carrier.

No single one of those facts proves an age. Together they describe a system changing its center of gravity.

The reason is not simply climate policy, cheap solar, artificial intelligence, or electric cars. It is that electricity is unusually controllable. It can be switched in milliseconds, transmitted across distance, metered precisely, converted into many forms of work, and produced by technologies whose underlying physics have almost nothing in common.

That makes it a natural destination for a civilization that increasingly values precision and flexibility as much as raw energy quantity.

The progression of this book has been moving toward that point. Fire made heat controllable. Storage made biological surplus durable. Coal made ancient solar energy spendable at industrial rates. Steam turned part of that stock into mechanical work. The grid made power movable. Efficiency made services cheaper.

Electrification brings those threads together in a common carrier.

It does not abolish scarcity; it moves the scarce thing.

If transport, heating, industry, and computation increasingly converge on electricity, the decisive question is no longer only how cheaply another kilowatt-hour can be generated. It is whether that kilowatt-hour can reach the machine that wants it, at the place and moment it is needed.

The age of electricity carries its next constraint inside its success.
