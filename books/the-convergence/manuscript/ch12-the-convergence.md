# The Convergence

In 1922, Alfred Lotka tried to describe natural selection in the language of energy.

He was not writing a history of civilization. His subject was biological evolution, and his argument was more tentative than the later slogans built from it. Organisms, he suggested, do not merely compete for matter. They compete within flows of available energy, and selection can favor systems that increase the rate at which useful energy passes through them. The paper was short, mathematical in places, and cautious about the reach of its own conclusion.

Two decades later, Leslie White made the move Lotka had not. In a 1943 paper called “Energy and the Evolution of Culture,” White argued that cultural development could be understood through the amount of energy harnessed per person and the efficiency of the means used to put it to work. His formulation was sweeping. It also belonged to an anthropology more comfortable than the field is today with ranking cultures along a single evolutionary scale.

In 1964, Nikolai Kardashev pushed the idea outward until it became astronomical. A civilization might be classified by the power available to it: first at the scale of a planet, then a star, then a galaxy. Kardashev’s paper was about communication with extraterrestrial civilizations, not human welfare. Yet the scale endured because it expressed an intuition that is difficult to shake. A civilization able to command more power can, in principle, do things a weaker one cannot.

After eleven chapters, the question is whether that intuition survives contact with history.

The strongest version does not.

More energy is not the same thing as more progress. A furnace can waste fuel. A traffic jam can burn gasoline without moving anyone. An inefficient building can consume more energy than a comfortable one. A wealthy country can add energy use without adding years of healthy life. A state can command immense power and use it for destruction. A civilization can increase throughput while degrading the atmosphere on which its future depends.

If the thesis of this book were that civilization advances whenever a meter spins faster, the evidence would have killed it several chapters ago.

Something narrower survives.

The history we have followed is a history of expanding capability through useful, controllable energy: obtaining it from more sources, converting it into more valuable forms, moving it farther, storing it longer, delivering it more reliably, directing it with greater precision, and reducing the labor or environmental cost of a given service. Raw consumption is one consequence of that process, sometimes an important one. It is not the process itself.

The distinction begins before history.

At Wonderwerk Cave, the evidence was not a number for “human energy use” one million years ago. It was burned bone and plant ash in archaeological sediment. Fire mattered because it extended the organism. Heat could be produced outside the body. Darkness could be pushed back. Landscapes and materials could be altered in ways muscle alone could not accomplish. The relevant change was not merely that more joules were present. The change was that energy became controllable.

Agriculture complicated the story immediately. The first cereal farmers were not obviously more productive per hour of labor than foragers. Some early agricultural populations suffered worse health. Yet cultivation changed the spatial and temporal organization of energy. Grain stored solar energy in a form that could be accumulated, counted, defended, transported, taxed, inherited, and concentrated. Farming supported larger populations and new institutions while distributing its gains unevenly. The system became more capable even when the individual farmer did not become more comfortable.

Coal changed the scale and the clock. Biomass tied energy supply to current photosynthesis and land. Coal opened a stock accumulated across geological time. Steam turned that stock into mechanical work, but not all at once. The famous inventions preceded their largest economic effects by decades. Engines had to diffuse. Mines, railways, factories, finance, engineering knowledge, and supply chains had to reorganize around them. The energy source mattered because institutions learned how to make it useful.

Then electricity changed the geometry.

Pearl Street was not important because a bulb glowed. The achievement was a system: generators, conductors, meters, loads, customers, capital, maintenance, and standards coordinated well enough that energy could arrive as a service. Factory power migrated from shafts to wires. Niagara demonstrated that a power source and its customer no longer had to occupy the same place. Electricity became a carrier able to connect many sources with many uses.

The grid made power abstract enough to disappear into the wall.

That disappearance produced one of the central corrections to the energy thesis. What people value is usually not energy itself. It is the service produced with it.

The history of light makes the correction almost embarrassingly clear. In Britain, the real cost of useful illumination collapsed across the industrial era while the quantity of light consumed exploded. A modern room can be far brighter than a candlelit room while requiring less energy for each lumen-hour. Measuring only fuel misses the improvement. Measuring only efficiency misses the expansion in use. The service tells the fuller story.

Useful work makes the same point at the scale of an economy. A joule of low-temperature waste heat and a joule of electricity are physically equal units of energy but economically different forms of capability. Conversion, timing, location, control, and quality matter. An economy can produce more value from each unit of primary energy because it has become better at turning energy into useful work.

Efficiency therefore does not weaken the book’s thesis. It changes what the thesis has to be about.

A civilization that performs twice as much useful work with the same primary energy has expanded its command of energy even though raw consumption has not risen. A heat pump that delivers several units of heat per unit of electricity has not escaped physics. It has learned to exploit physics more intelligently. A processor that performs more computation per joule increases capability without requiring proportional growth in power.

But efficiency creates its own complication. Jevons saw it in coal. Cheaper services invite more use.

The rebound evidence is not a universal law. For many household services, direct rebound is partial. In other settings and at economy-wide scale it can be large. The durable lesson is simpler: technical savings do not determine total demand. When light becomes cheap, people illuminate more. When motion becomes cheap, people travel more. When computation becomes cheap, computation spreads into objects and activities that previously contained none.

Sometimes that additional demand is wasteful. Sometimes it is the welfare gain.

This matters because the world of 2026 still contains both abundance and deprivation. The latest Tracking SDG 7 data report that 655 million people lacked electricity access in 2024 and roughly two billion still relied on polluting fuels and technologies for cooking. Sub-Saharan Africa contained most of the remaining electricity deficit. A prescription to minimize energy use means something very different in a household with multiple vehicles, climate-controlled empty rooms, and constant connectivity than it does in a household without reliable light, refrigeration, clean cooking, pumping, or cooling.

The welfare value of energy is nonlinear because constraints are nonlinear.

The first reliable kilowatt-hours can remove constraints that dominate daily life. Later increments may deliver convenience, luxury, or waste. This is why high-income countries can experience diminishing welfare returns to additional energy use without proving that energy was unimportant to the capabilities already built into their hospitals, sanitation systems, transport, buildings, food chains, and communications.

Energy is a substrate. Substrates are necessary and insufficient.

That sentence is less grand than saying energy explains civilization, but it fits the evidence better.

Knowledge decides what can be built. Institutions decide what is financed, permitted, maintained, and shared. Law determines ownership and obligation. Culture shapes demand. Trust allows strangers to coordinate. Markets and states allocate resources imperfectly in different ways. Geography sets constraints that engineering can relax but not abolish. None of these can be reduced to joules.

Yet none can become material action without energy either.

A law exists as an idea until institutions enforce it through people, buildings, communications, transport, and machines. Knowledge can describe a vaccine, but manufacturing and refrigeration turn it into doses. A software model can exist as mathematics, but chips and electricity turn the mathematics into computation. Finance can direct capital, but concrete, steel, copper, trucks, furnaces, motors, and workers turn capital into infrastructure.

Energy does not tell civilization what to do. It sets part of the boundary around what civilization can do at scale.

The modern electricity transition makes that boundary unusually visible because so many formerly separate systems are converging on the same carrier. Vehicles, heating, industrial processes, data centers, appliances, and communications increasingly depend on electricity. At the same time, electricity can be generated from coal, gas, falling water, nuclear fission, wind, sunlight, geothermal heat, and stored chemical energy.

This many-to-many architecture is the convergence in the book’s title.

It is not a prediction that every energy use becomes electric. Aviation, shipping, high-temperature industry, chemical feedstocks, and other applications may retain fuels or develop new ones. Nor does electrification guarantee decarbonization. Electricity produced from fossil fuels still carries emissions, and every low-carbon technology has material, land, network, financing, and institutional requirements.

The convergence is deeper than electrification alone. It is the convergence of energy and information.

For most of history, increasing command of energy meant commanding fire, muscle, falling water, wind, fuel, pressure, rotation, or current. The machines became progressively better at controlling where energy went and when. Electronics pushed control into milliseconds and then microseconds. Computation made the control conditional: sense the world, process information, change the flow.

A modern energy system is therefore not only a collection of power sources. It is a network of decisions.

A battery charges when prices, forecasts, or grid conditions justify it. An inverter changes its output in fractions of a second. A heat pump responds to a thermostat. A factory coordinates motors through software. A data center schedules workloads against power, cooling, hardware, and demand. The useful value of energy increasingly depends on information about how to deploy it.

Artificial intelligence tightens the loop. Intelligence now has a conspicuous power bill because frontier computation requires large amounts of electricity, cooling, chips, buildings, and network capacity. At the same time, computation can improve the operation of physical systems. Energy powers information; information can improve the use of energy.

Neither side eliminates the other.

This is why the current bottleneck has migrated into networks. Solar modules can be manufactured quickly. Batteries can be ordered. Data-center buildings can rise faster than transmission corridors. A proposed generator can wait years for interconnection. A large new load can exist on a developer’s plan long before a utility can energize it. Once one component becomes cheap and scalable, a slower complement becomes valuable.

Civilization repeatedly advances this way: solve one constraint, discover the next.

Fire created new demands for fuel. Agriculture created demands for storage, property rules, and political organization. Coal created mines, railways, smoke, and dependence on geological stocks. Electricity created grids and the requirement of continuous balance. Cheap renewable generation creates transmission and flexibility problems. Powerful computation creates electricity and cooling problems. Abundance does not end constraint. It relocates it.

That is why the frontier technologies in the previous chapter had to be separated by evidence level. Solar and batteries are commercial industries. Fission is commercial but institutionally difficult to build in some places. Enhanced geothermal is testing whether modern drilling can create durable reservoirs in more geologies. Fusion has produced repeated scientific ignition at the National Ignition Facility, including a June 2026 shot that yielded about 7.9 megajoules from roughly 2.1 megajoules of laser energy delivered to the target. That is an extraordinary laboratory result. It is not a power plant.

The discipline of the distinction matters because civilization is full of energy that exists physically but not economically.

Sunlight was abundant before photovoltaics. Uranium was energy-dense before reactors. Hot rock existed before directional drilling. Deuterium existed before lasers. A resource becomes part of civilization only through a chain of knowledge, machinery, institutions, finance, networks, maintenance, and acceptable external cost.

The frontier is not the resource. The frontier is our ability to convert the resource into dependable capability.

External cost belongs inside that sentence.

The fossil era demonstrates why. Coal, oil, and gas delivered extraordinary mobility, heat, industrial production, fertilizer, materials, and electricity. They also accumulated carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. The Global Carbon Budget 2025 estimates fossil CO2 emissions reached about 38.1 billion tonnes in 2025, a record, even as the carbon intensity of energy declined over the previous decade. Efficiency and cleaner supply improved, but not fast enough to offset growth in energy demand.

This is the hardest counterexample to any naïve celebration of energetic expansion. A civilization can increase capability today while transferring cost into the atmosphere and the future.

A serious energy metric therefore cannot stop at power. It has to include what is spent to obtain useful capability: primary energy, capital, materials, land, pollution, risk, labor, time, and damage shifted onto other people or generations. Progress means expanding capability while reducing those costs where possible, not maximizing throughput regardless of consequence.

This also clarifies what abundance should mean.

Abundance is not infinite electricity and it is not a promise that every price falls forever. It is the condition in which a useful energy service becomes cheap and dependable enough that another constraint becomes the reason to say no. When illumination became abundant, the limiting question stopped being whether a family could afford a few more minutes of candlelight. When mechanical power became abundant, the question shifted toward machines, markets, materials, and organization. When computation became abundant, attention, data, chips, network capacity, and electricity emerged as constraints.

If low-carbon electricity becomes much cheaper and more plentiful, the effects will be similarly uneven. Some processes will barely change because energy is already a small part of their cost. Others may become newly practical: desalination, synthetic fuels, carbon removal, more intensive recycling, controlled environments, industrial heat, or forms of computation whose scale is difficult to justify today. The exact list is uncertain because cheap inputs create uses that are hard to forecast before the price falls.

The historical pattern is more reliable than the catalog of future applications.

People turn cheaper capability into new activity.

That is not automatically good. It is not automatically bad. It is the mechanism through which technical possibility becomes civilization.

So where does that leave Lotka, White, and Kardashev?

Lotka’s energetic lens remains useful if treated as a lens rather than a law. Living and social systems exist inside energy flows, and access to usable energy changes what they can sustain. White was right to see energy capture and conversion as powerful variables in cultural history, but too much disappears when culture is compressed into a single energetic score. Kardashev’s scale captures something real about physical possibility while saying almost nothing about justice, wisdom, welfare, or survival.

A civilization with more power can do more.

It can also do more harm.

The missing variable is not a second substance that competes with energy. It is organization: the knowledge and institutions that determine what energy becomes.

Perhaps the most defensible measure of advance is therefore not energy consumption but the widening of useful capability per unit of total cost. More light from less fuel. More mobility with less pollution. More computation per joule. More reliable electricity with lower emissions. More people gaining access to basic services while affluent waste declines. More options without proportionally more damage.

That measure is harder to put on a single axis. Reality has earned the inconvenience.

The thesis would fail in a meaningful way if material capability repeatedly expanded without either more useful energy or better conversion and control of existing energy. We would then need to admit that energy was only one input among many, not a common physical substrate. The history in this book does not show that. Even the strongest counterexamples—falling energy intensity, welfare saturation, efficient technologies, digital services—depend on getting more useful work or service from energy, not on escaping energy altogether.

But the opposite falsification matters too. If societies with enormous energy command reliably failed to convert it into broad capability because institutions, knowledge, conflict, or distribution dominated outcomes, then energy could not carry the explanatory weight by itself.

History shows that as well.

The conclusion is a conjunction rather than a slogan. Energy bounds material possibility. Knowledge and institutions shape conversion. Distribution determines who receives the capability. External costs determine whether the gain is durable. Efficiency changes how much capability fits inside a given flow. Networks determine whether the flow arrives where and when it is needed.

Civilization advances when more of those pieces work together.

That is the convergence.

Not a final fuel. Not a machine that ends scarcity. Not a civilization score based on how fast we can burn through the world.

A convergence of sources into carriers, carriers into networks, networks into machines, machines into services, and services into human capability. A convergence of energy and information, because increasingly precise control allows the same physical flow to do more. A convergence of abundance and restraint, because the point of mastering energy is not to waste more of it.

The story began with traces of fire in a cave.

The fire itself is gone. What remains is evidence that someone learned to make an external flow of energy part of human life.

Everything since has been an elaboration of that act.
