# The Convergence

A light switch is a ridiculous object.

You move a piece of plastic a few millimeters and, somewhere beyond the wall, a chain of machines responds. Generators accelerate or batteries discharge. Inverters adjust. Transformers step voltages up and down. Protection equipment watches for faults. Wires cross roads, rivers, neighborhoods, counties, sometimes entire countries. Fuel may be burning. Water may be falling. Wind may be turning blades. Sunlight may be knocking electrons loose from silicon.

None of that appears in the room.

The room simply becomes brighter.

That disappearance is one of civilization’s great tricks.

The history in this book began with another kind of disappearance. Once fire could be tended reliably, a difficult chemical reaction became part of ordinary human life. Once grain could be stored, a season of sunlight could become food months later. Once coal could be mined and steam engines made dependable, forests and muscle no longer set the same ceiling on mechanical work. Once electricity could be generated centrally and routed through wires, machines no longer had to live beside the shaft or stream that powered them.

Again and again, a physical constraint moved out of the foreground.

That is the convergence.

It is tempting to turn that pattern into a law: civilization advances by consuming more energy.

The historical record will not support anything so simple.

Early farmers did not necessarily live better than foragers. The first steam engines did not instantly transform productivity. Electricity changed factories through control and layout as much as through raw energy input. Efficient machines sometimes reduced consumption and sometimes stimulated more of it. Rich countries can use very different quantities of energy while achieving similar outcomes in life expectancy and other measures of welfare. Cheap generation does not build a transmission line. A working reactor design does not guarantee a competent construction program. A fusion target producing more energy than the laser deposits into it does not amount to a power station.

The book has spent eleven chapters making its own thesis harder to state.

That was necessary.

If energy were merely correlated with development, it would be an interesting statistic. If raw consumption were a scoreboard for civilization, the argument would be wrong. If efficiency, knowledge, institutions, distribution, pollution, and human welfare could all be ignored, the argument would be useless.

The stronger claim survives those objections because it is narrower.

Energy is the physical substrate of capability.

A society cannot heat steel, pump water, move freight, refrigerate food, illuminate streets, sterilize instruments, desalinate seawater, train a large computer model, or lift a rocket without transforming energy. Knowledge tells us how. Institutions determine whether the system can be built and maintained. Capital pays for it. Law decides who may do what. Culture determines what people want. But at the point where an intention becomes a physical change in the world, energy enters the accounting.

That does not make energy the purpose of civilization.

It makes energy the price of admission to material action.

Alfred Lotka saw part of this a century ago when he tried to describe evolution in energetic terms. Leslie White later made energy central to a theory of cultural development. Nikolai Kardashev used power consumption as a speculative scale for technologically advanced civilizations. Eric Chaisson has explored energy-rate density as one way of comparing organized complexity across very different systems.

Each of those ideas is useful. None should be mistaken for a complete theory of human flourishing.

A prison can consume enormous amounts of energy. So can a war. So can a traffic jam.

The ability to do more does not tell us whether more should be done.

This is the distinction that matters most at the end of the book: capability is not welfare.

A kilowatt-hour delivered to a neonatal intensive-care unit and a kilowatt-hour wasted through an open warehouse door are physically comparable and morally nothing alike. The same fuel can heat a school or power a missile. An electric grid can support democratic abundance or authoritarian surveillance. Energy enlarges the feasible set. Politics, ethics, incentives, and judgment choose from it.

That separation may sound obvious, but it resolves much of the confusion around energy and progress.

One camp looks at the history of industrialization and sees a nearly continuous rise in energy use alongside population, income, mobility, life expectancy, and technological capability. Another points out that beyond certain thresholds, richer societies often obtain little additional welfare from simply consuming more energy. Both observations can be true.

The first is about capability.

The second is about conversion.

A society that has too little useful energy cannot operate modern hospitals, water systems, cold chains, transit networks, communications infrastructure, or industry at scale. A society that already has enough energy for those services may gain more by improving efficiency, institutions, urban design, public health, education, or distribution than by adding another unit of primary energy.

The important quantity is not fuel burned.

It is what the system can reliably accomplish.

This is why useful work and energy services kept reappearing in earlier chapters. Roger Fouquet and Peter Pearson’s history of lighting shows that people did not want candles, gas, or electricity for their own sake. They wanted light. William Nordhaus made the same measurement problem vivid: the economic value of lighting changed far more than conventional price indexes captured because the service itself became radically cheaper and better. Robert Ayres and Benjamin Warr argued that useful work can tell us more about economic production than undifferentiated primary energy alone.

Civilization becomes more capable when it can obtain more desired service from fewer costly inputs.

Sometimes that means more energy.

Sometimes it means using energy better.

The distinction becomes especially important because the environmental bill for the fossil era has arrived at planetary scale.

The Global Carbon Budget 2025 estimates that fossil carbon dioxide emissions reached another record in 2025, around 38 billion tonnes of CO2. The carbon intensity of energy has been declining in much of the world, but not fast enough to offset growth in total demand. The problem is not that energy ceased to matter. It is that the dominant way of supplying it created an accumulating external cost that the early industrial system did not price into the steam engine, the furnace, the automobile, or the power station.

Any serious theory of progress has to count the smoke outside the factory gate.

That is one reason electricity matters so much in the present transition. Electricity is not clean by definition, but it can separate the machine providing the service from the process producing the energy. A motor does not care whether its electrons came from coal, uranium, falling water, wind, sunlight, or stored electricity. That does not erase lifecycle impacts, mining, land use, waste, or grid constraints. It does make substitution possible in a way that a horse, a steam shaft, or a gasoline engine never allowed.

The common carrier creates room to improve the upstream system without rebuilding every downstream use at once.

That is a powerful form of option value.

It is also unevenly distributed.

In 2024, according to the 2026 edition of Tracking SDG7, 655 million people still lacked access to electricity. Roughly two billion people lacked access to clean cooking. The global electrification rate had reached 92 percent, but the remaining deficit was concentrated heavily in poorer and rural populations, especially in sub-Saharan Africa.

Those numbers expose the danger of discussing abundance as though it were already a universal condition.

For a household cooking over an open fire, the energy problem has not become invisible.

It is smoke in the lungs.

The World Health Organization estimates that household air pollution caused about 2.9 million deaths in 2021. The fuels involved are familiar: wood, charcoal, dung, crop waste, coal, kerosene. They are energy sources, but they are low-quality ones in the sense used throughout this book—difficult to control, locally polluting, labor-intensive, and often inefficient at delivering the desired service.

A clean electric or gas stove may use energy without producing a dramatic increase in measured happiness or national output.

It can still remove hours of fuel gathering, reduce smoke exposure, improve temperature control, and make a kitchen less dangerous.

Energy quality is human experience wearing engineering clothes.

This is also why averages are treacherous.

A country can have abundant electricity and households that cannot afford it. It can have high per-capita energy use and unreliable distribution. It can export fuel while villages nearby lack modern energy services. It can increase renewable generation while transmission bottlenecks keep new projects waiting. It can build a sophisticated grid and allocate its costs badly enough to provoke political backlash.

The system matters all the way to the socket.

This book began with fire because control was the first theme hiding inside energy quantity.

Control is still the theme.

Coal compressed ancient biological time into industrial power, but it was useful because mines, engines, boilers, railways, and firms learned to control the flow. Electricity became transformative because a mechanical load could be switched independently instead of waiting on a common shaft. Modern grids coordinate generation and demand across enormous distances in fractions of a second. Batteries turn time itself into a controllable variable. Data centers care not merely about annual terawatt-hours but whether hundreds of megawatts can be delivered to one site continuously. Nuclear programs succeed or fail partly on whether institutions can control construction complexity. Fusion will matter commercially only when an extraordinary experiment can become an ordinary machine.

The story is less about possessing energy than about disciplining it.

A barrel of oil on an island with no refinery is potential. A gigawatt of solar in a desert with no transmission is potential. Heat four miles underground is potential. Uranium in rock is potential. Sunlight crossing a roof is potential.

Civilization is the machinery that turns potential into service.

That machinery is technical and institutional at the same time.

This is where an energy-first account of history is most likely to overreach. It is easy to treat institutions as decoration added after the physics. The chapters on agriculture, grids, interconnection, and nuclear construction suggest the opposite. Property rules, standards, finance, regulation, labor organization, trade, public legitimacy, and political competence can determine whether a resource becomes useful at all.

A coal seam did not industrialize Britain by itself.

Sunshine does not build a solar industry.

A reactor vessel does not complete a reactor.

Institutions are not rivals to energy in the explanation. They are part of the conversion system.

The same is true of knowledge.

The Sun delivered photovoltaic energy to Earth long before anyone knew how to make a semiconductor junction. Heat existed underground before directional drilling. Atomic nuclei could fission before neutron physics. The physical resource was present. Capability arrived only when knowledge made the resource legible and engineering made it repeatable.

That observation changes how we should think about scarcity.

Some scarcities are geological. Some are technological. Some are institutional. Some are temporary artifacts of ignorance.

They can trade places.

For most of human history, energy itself sat close to the front of the queue. Muscle, wood, wind, water, and crops imposed hard local limits. Fossil fuels relaxed some of those limits and created others: mining accidents, urban smoke, strategic dependence, and eventually climate change. Electrification relaxed the need to colocate work with a prime mover, then made wires and transformers more important. Cheap solar makes midday electricity abundant in some places and thereby raises the value of storage, transmission, flexible demand, and firm generation. Better batteries solve one duration problem and expose the next.

Progress often looks like the migration of the bottleneck.

That is a less comforting idea than technological triumphalism because it offers no final escape.

There will always be a next constraint.

But it is more optimistic than it first appears.

A constraint that becomes visible can be worked on.

The history of energy is full of problems that once looked like features of the world and later became engineering tasks. Darkness after sunset. The distance a person could travel in a day. The amount of mechanical work one worker could command. The preservation of food through summer heat. The ability to communicate across an ocean. The temperature of a building in winter. The computational effort required to simulate a protein or translate a language.

None was abolished in an absolute sense. Their cost fell enough that human attention moved elsewhere.

This is what abundance is for.

Not consumption for its own sake.

Room to care about something else.

That framing also clarifies the efficiency paradox. If an air conditioner becomes twice as efficient and a household uses the savings to cool another room, the engineering gain is real even if total electricity use does not fall by half. A unit of energy now buys more comfort. If millions of households can newly afford cooling, aggregate demand may rise while exposure to dangerous heat falls.

Whether that is progress depends partly on the source of the electricity, the alternatives available, and the external costs.

The rebound is not the moral.

The service is.

The same discipline should apply to future energy technologies. A cheaper solar panel is valuable because of the services it can support, not because a chart points downward. A fusion ignition shot is valuable because it expands what is known to be physically possible, not because it proves a cheap commercial plant is imminent. A new transmission line matters because it allows existing generators and loads to connect more productively, not because steel towers are intrinsically desirable.

Means become meaningful through ends.

This is where the strongest counterargument to the book deserves its full force.

Perhaps energy is too fundamental to be explanatory.

Everything physical requires energy in the same way every novel requires matter. That does not mean a literary critic should explain *King Lear* by counting atoms. If every civilization needs energy, perhaps saying “energy matters” tells us almost nothing about why one society develops faster than another, why some institutions prosper, or why people choose one technology over another.

That objection is correct if the energy thesis is used as a substitute for history.

It should not be.

Energy does not explain why Britain industrialized before China, why one nuclear program learned while another stalled, why one city built transmission and another did not, or why a society uses its wealth for public health rather than private fortification. Those questions require economics, politics, geography, culture, institutions, and contingency.

Energy supplies a different kind of explanation.

It tells us which ambitions are physically cheap, which are physically expensive, and which are physically impossible at a given level of technical control.

It defines part of the opportunity set in which history operates.

That is enough.

We do not need a monocausal theory of civilization to notice that the opportunity set has widened enormously.

A person in a wealthy electrified society can summon mechanical power far beyond human muscle without thinking about it. Water arrives under pressure. Food remains cold. Elevators lift tonnes. Trains move hundreds of people. Air conditioners move heat out of buildings. Networks perform billions of computations while the user moves one finger across glass.

The extraordinary fact is not simply that modern civilization uses a great deal of energy.

It is that ordinary people can command so much useful work indirectly.

Much of that command is hidden behind interfaces.

Tap a screen. Turn a knob. Press a pedal. Open a tap.

The command is tiny.

The physical response can be enormous.

That gap between human effort and controlled physical consequence may be a better measure of energetic progress than tonnes of coal or barrels of oil.

It contains efficiency, infrastructure, knowledge, and institutions in one relationship: how much reliable capability can a person or organization call upon, at what cost, with what external damage, and under whose control?

The answer has improved spectacularly for billions of people.

It remains terrible for many others.

And the improvement has often been purchased with environmental costs that now have to be unwound without surrendering the useful capability built on top of them.

That is the task ahead.

The clean-energy transition is sometimes described as a substitution problem: replace coal with solar, gasoline with batteries, gas boilers with heat pumps, fossil hydrogen with electrolytic hydrogen, perhaps some combustion with nuclear or geothermal heat.

The deeper task is harder.

We are trying to preserve and expand useful capability while changing the energetic metabolism underneath it.

The metric cannot simply be lower energy consumption, because hundreds of millions of people need more access and richer services. It cannot simply be higher energy consumption, because waste and pollution do not become virtues at scale. It cannot be zero environmental impact, because every physical system uses land, materials, infrastructure, and labor.

The useful direction is more capability per unit of damage.

More light per tonne of material.

More mobility per unit of pollution.

More computation per watt.

More comfort per kilowatt-hour.

More reliable electricity per acre, dollar, transformer, hour of construction, and tonne of carbon.

The exact denominator changes with the problem.

The habit of mind should not.

This is where efficiency and abundance stop being opposites.

Efficiency makes each unit of energy more capable. Abundance makes more units available when they are worth using. Clean supply lowers the external cost of using them. Networks deliver them. Storage moves them through time. Institutions decide who gets access and who pays. Knowledge discovers new conversions. Markets, states, households, and firms decide which services are valuable enough to pursue.

Civilization advances when those pieces improve together.

Not neatly. Not everywhere. Not without reversals.

There is no reason to expect the next century to resemble the last one. Electricity may become a larger share of final energy while total primary-energy accounting grows less intuitive. Computation may become an increasingly important energy service while hardware continues to become more efficient. Some wealthy countries may stabilize or reduce energy use while poorer ones increase it sharply. Nuclear may revive in places that can build it well and remain expensive in places that cannot. Fusion may become a commercial industry or remain a scientific triumph with limited power-system relevance. Grid constraints may dominate one decade and disappear in the next after a burst of construction and reform.

The thesis does not depend on predicting which technology wins.

It depends on something more durable.

Every physical future has an energy budget.

The more efficiently, cleanly, reliably, and broadly humanity can meet that budget, the larger the set of futures available to us.

That is why a civilization with abundant energy is not automatically a good civilization.

But a good civilization with too little usable energy is forced to spend more of its attention on limits it might otherwise transcend.

There is a difference between wanting more and being able to do more.

Energy belongs to the second category.

The distinction is easy to miss because the best energy systems disappear into ordinary life. No one holds a ceremony when a refrigerator cycles on. A hospital does not applaud every time backup power remains unnecessary. A factory operator does not marvel at each motor. A commuter does not thank the substation.

Working infrastructure is boring by design.

That boredom is earned.

Behind it sit centuries of accumulated discovery: fire controlled, crops stored, seams mined, engines improved, electrons routed, reactors moderated, semiconductors fabricated, grids synchronized, algorithms compressed.

Each generation inherits an energetic world that would look supernatural to most of the people who came before it.

Then it complains about the utility bill.

That is probably healthy.

The purpose of progress is not to remain impressed by yesterday’s miracle. It is to make the miracle reliable enough that attention can move to the next unsolved problem.

The light switch is ridiculous because it hides almost everything.

It hides geology, physics, engineering, finance, labor, regulation, weather, software, maintenance, and political compromise behind a gesture so small that a child can make it.

Move the plastic.

The room changes.

For most of human history, changing the physical world required a much closer match between the effort of the person and the scale of the result.

Civilization has been widening that gap ever since we learned to keep a fire alive.

What matters now is not how large we can make the gap.

It is what we choose to do with it.
