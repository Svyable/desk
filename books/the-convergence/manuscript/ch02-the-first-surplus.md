# The First Surplus

More than eleven thousand years ago, near the Dead Sea in what is now Jordan, people built a structure for food that was more carefully engineered than the word *storehouse* suggests.

At Dhra’, archaeologists found the remains of purpose-built granaries dating to roughly 11,300–11,175 calibrated years before present. Their floors were suspended above the ground. Air could move beneath the stored food. Rodents had a harder time reaching it. The structures sat among buildings where plant foods were processed, and they appeared before the local transition to fully domesticated cereals was complete. The sequence matters: people were investing in storage technology before agriculture had become the settled package we usually imagine. ([Kuijt & Finlayson, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0812764106))

A granary is not a spectacular machine. Nothing spins. Nothing glows. It does not multiply force. Its achievement is quieter. Food that might have been eaten, spoiled, scattered, or stolen after harvest can remain useful after the season that produced it has passed.

The building moves energy through time.

That is the more useful place to begin the agricultural story than the familiar image of a person dropping a seed into soil and discovering farming. Cultivation was not one invention. Domestication was not one event. Hunting, gathering, tending, planting, harvesting, herding, and storage overlapped for generations in different mixtures. What eventually changed the human energy system was not simply that people learned to grow food. They learned to reorganize landscapes so that a larger share of incoming sunlight passed through plants, animals, buildings, and institutions that made its output predictable enough to build around.

A field of grain is a solar collector with a calendar attached.

That description would have meant nothing to the people who first began tending wild cereals in Southwest Asia, but the physics was already there. Sunlight arrived over months. Plants converted a fraction of it into stems and seeds. Human labor altered which plants survived, where they grew, when they were gathered, and eventually which traits were reproduced. The harvest could be dried and kept after the season that produced it had ended.

Fire had allowed human beings to command an energetic process outside the body. Agriculture did something different. It reorganized a landscape so that diffuse solar energy could be concentrated into forms humans could predict, harvest, store, divide, and return to later.

It is tempting to tell this as the next obvious rung of progress: hunter-gatherers discovered that farming produced more food for less work, adopted the superior technology, accumulated a surplus, and built civilization with the spare time. That story is clean enough to survive in textbooks because it seems to explain its own outcome. It is also precisely the story some of the best quantitative work on early agriculture has made difficult to defend.

Samuel Bowles tried to estimate the caloric return per hour of labor for cereals cultivated by early farmers and compare those returns with foraging. His calculation had to include more than the visible work in a field. Cultivation delays the reward for labor. Grain must be planted, protected, harvested, processed, transported, and stored. Reliance on a small number of crops creates risks different from a wider portfolio of wild foods. The reconstruction is necessarily uncertain, but Bowles concluded that the evidence was inconsistent with the claim that the first cereal farmers simply enjoyed higher labor productivity than early Holocene foragers. ([Bowles, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1010733108))

The first agricultural revolution may therefore have been a peculiar kind of advance: one that could make the human system larger before it made the average human life easier.

This distinction changes what we should look for in the archaeological record. If farming did not necessarily buy an individual more calories for each hour worked, its advantage could lie elsewhere—in the number of people a landscape could support, in the timing of food supply, in the ability to occupy territory more densely, in the possibility of feeding dependents and specialists, and above all in what could be done with a harvest after it had been gathered.

A gazelle is nutritious but inconvenient to inventory. A stand of ripe grain is different. Once harvested and dried, cereal can wait. It can be put in a container, moved into a building, measured out through winter, saved for seed, exchanged, stolen, inherited, promised, guarded, or demanded. A store of grain makes tomorrow’s food partly visible today.

Dhra’ makes that abstraction physical. A suspended floor is an answer to a problem that only matters if food is expected to remain in one place long enough for damp, insects, or rodents to destroy it. Building such a structure requires labor now for calories later. The architecture itself is a bet on continuity.

That is a profound change in the politics of energy. The sunlight embodied in food no longer has to be consumed near the moment and place at which humans encounter it. Some of it can cross time.

Storage is easy to mistake for a secondary detail because it adds no calories by itself. Yet the ability to move a resource through time is a technology in its own right. A reservoir does it with water. A battery does it with electricity. A granary does it with chemical energy assembled by plants. None creates the underlying energy. Each changes when it becomes available and therefore what can be organized around it.

The comparison should not be pushed too literally. Grain spoils, attracts pests, requires protection, and is simultaneously seed, food, commodity, and biological material in ways an electrical battery is not. But the common feature matters: control over timing changes the usefulness of a flow.

The people who built the Dhra’ granaries were not waiting for a modern economy to arrive. The evidence suggests something more interesting. Storage itself may have helped make more intensive cultivation possible. Once a community invests in keeping cereals, a successful season can support the next one. Seed can be reserved deliberately. The consequences of a failed harvest become sharper because more future activity has been organized around the expectation that stores will exist.

A technology that reduces one kind of uncertainty can create another kind of dependence.

For a mobile forager, many resources are encountered as a changing landscape of opportunities. Mobility can be a form of resilience: when one place disappoints, movement may open another. A farming household begins to bind more labor to particular ground and to a production cycle whose payoff lies months ahead. Seed in the soil is work that cannot be taken elsewhere. A crop that fails after months of investment turns lost calories into lost time.

Agriculture concentrates risk along with food.

It also changes reproduction. Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel found a striking demographic signal in archaeological cemetery sequences associated with the transition from foraging to farming: an abrupt rise in the proportion of juvenile skeletons, interpreted as evidence of increased fertility. In his synthesis he estimated an increase in total fertility on the order of two births per woman during the Neolithic Demographic Transition. The claim is about population structure, not a judgment that farming made life healthier. ([Bocquet-Appel, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1208880))

That distinction becomes even more important when we look at what happened after some of the first farming booms. Stephen Shennan and colleagues used summed radiocarbon dates as a proxy for population change across mid-Holocene Europe. They found that the arrival of agriculture was often followed by rapid population growth and then, in many regions, substantial decline. The causes remain debated and likely varied, but the pattern punctures any smooth story in which farming simply raised carrying capacity and population climbed permanently upward. ([Shennan et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms3486))

A system can expand and still be fragile.

The biological record is equally resistant to triumphalism. Bioarchaeological studies of transitions to agriculture frequently find costs: reductions in average stature in many populations, more dental disease, nutritional stress, and infectious disease associated with denser settlement and changing diets. A global review by Amanda Mummert and colleagues found that the long-observed tendency toward reduced stature and poorer health during many agricultural transitions remained visible across later studies, though the magnitude and local pattern varied. ([Mummert et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ehb.2011.03.004))

This is not evidence that foraging was an idyll. Forager populations experienced injury, hunger, violence, disease, and environmental shocks of their own. Nor does every farming transition produce the same health outcome. The point is narrower: population growth, energy capture, and individual welfare can move in different directions.

That may be the central paradox of the first surplus. A livelihood can spread because it is good at producing more people, defending land, storing claims on the future, or supporting larger groups even when it is not obviously superior for each person performing the labor.

The word *surplus* encourages us to imagine abundance after needs have been satisfied. In practice, surplus is more political. It means production above immediate consumption that somebody has the capacity to preserve, allocate, or seize.

Once a harvest can wait, other things can wait for the harvest.

A household can reserve seed rather than eat everything immediately. A community can maintain people whose daily work is not acquiring calories: craftspeople, builders, guards, ritual specialists, traders, administrators. The familiar history of specialization begins here, but it should not be romanticized as the discovery of leisure. A surplus can free some people from subsistence work because other people produce enough to support them. Who is freed, who produces, and who decides are political questions from the beginning.

Stored food is therefore not only a buffer against scarcity. It is a claim that can be controlled.

Imagine two identical harvests. In one village the grain is dispersed among households and consumed locally. In another it is gathered into central stores, recorded, guarded, and redistributed by authorities. The biological energy harvested from the landscape may be similar. The social capability is not. Concentration creates the possibility of coordinated construction, warfare, relief, ceremony, trade, and administration. It also creates the possibility of extraction.

Surplus has two faces because control does.

One face is obvious at Dhra’: storage can keep food edible. It can help a household or community cross a season in which fresh calories are scarce. It can preserve seed. It can reduce the penalty for a short-term mismatch between harvest and hunger.

The other face appears when stored food becomes legible to somebody beyond the household. What can be counted can be promised. What can be promised can become debt. What can be measured can be taxed. A granary is not inherently a state institution, but the physical properties that make grain useful for storage also make it unusually convenient for larger systems of obligation.

This is one reason cereal agriculture has mattered so much to political history. The claim should be handled carefully: societies have built states around many ecological bases, and grain does not mechanically produce bureaucracy. But annual crops that ripen predictably, can be dried, divided, transported, and stored are easier to make visible to institutions than many diffuse or perishable foods.

Energy becomes administrable.

Archaeology cannot give us a complete ledger for the first farming villages, but it can reveal long changes in the distribution of material wealth. Timothy Kohler and colleagues used house size as a consistent, if imperfect, proxy to estimate household wealth inequality across archaeological societies. Their results show inequality generally rising with plant and animal domestication and with sociopolitical scale. The pattern was not universal. Post-Neolithic Eurasia diverged substantially from North America and Mesoamerica, and the authors argued that access to large domesticable mammals helped make agricultural extensification and wealth concentration more powerful in parts of the Old World. ([Kohler et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24646))

That regional difference is important. There is no single agricultural package with a single political destiny. Wheat, rice, millet, maize, potatoes, cattle, llamas, irrigation, rainfall, soil, inheritance rules, warfare, and geography produce different systems. A book about energy becomes useless if it flattens those differences into an equation in which more calories automatically produce a state.

What energy can do is clarify the material possibility beneath the variation. A society cannot support a dense non-farming population unless somebody or something produces the food. It cannot carry a reserve across a bad season unless resources can be stored or reliably obtained elsewhere. It cannot concentrate a large surplus unless institutions can claim, move, protect, or account for it. Energy is the substrate. Institutions determine its path.

The arrival of domesticated animals widened the energy system again. A cow or ox is not simply meat stored on legs. Depending on species and setting, animals can convert plant matter humans cannot digest directly into milk, meat, hides, manure, and muscle. Traction can multiply the area a household can cultivate or the mass it can move. Pack animals expand the radius over which goods can travel. Manure returns nutrients to soil. Herds can reproduce and become wealth that walks.

But animals also consume land and feed. Their usefulness depends on ecological conditions. They can concentrate wealth because large animals are expensive assets and because traction can increase the scale of production that one household can control. Kohler and colleagues’ Old World/New World divergence is valuable precisely because it shows that the same general transition—agriculture—can produce different inequality dynamics depending on the energy converters available within it. ([Kohler et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24646))

Agriculture therefore extends the lesson of fire without merely repeating it. The crucial transition is not simply an increase in quantity. Wild ecosystems already captured immense solar energy. Human beings did not invent photosynthesis any more than they invented combustion. They learned, slowly and unevenly, to alter the biological machinery around them so that a larger share of particular landscapes yielded forms of energy useful to human purposes.

The price of that control was work and constraint. Fields have to be cleared or prepared. Crops have to be protected. Soil fertility can be exhausted. Water can become a source of conflict. Sedentary populations accumulate waste and pathogens. Storage systems require buildings, maintenance, guards, containers, and attention. A society that leans heavily on a few crops can become acutely exposed when disease, drought, flood, frost, or conflict strikes those crops at once.

The same concentration that makes a surplus governable makes it legible to raiders and rulers.

The word *surplus* can hide these costs. It sounds like what remains after everyone has enough. Historically, a surplus is often better understood as production above immediate consumption that somebody has the capacity to preserve and command. A granary can represent security against famine. It can also represent taxes already collected.

That ambiguity would follow civilization for the rest of its history. Greater productive capacity enlarges what a society can attempt, but the distribution of that capacity decides whose possibilities expand. A canal can stabilize harvests and demand coerced labor. An ox can multiply the force applied to a plow and become concentrated capital. A city can support scholarship, craft, sanitation, armies, palaces, markets, temples, and slums at the same time.

Agriculture also introduced a new relationship with land. Foragers can defend territories, and farming did not invent property, conflict, or hierarchy. But a planted field contains labor whose payoff is tied to a place and a future harvest. Seed in the ground is an investment that cannot simply follow its owner across the landscape. Storage deepens the attachment. Irrigation deepens it further. Terraces, cleared fields, orchards, wells, and walls accumulate in particular locations.

Energy capture becomes infrastructure.

This is one of the hidden consequences of the agricultural calendar. Work performed now can change the productivity of a place years later. A ditch dug by one generation can alter water flows for the next. A terrace can hold soil after its builder is gone. Fruit trees can embody patience in living wood. Domesticated seed carries selection across seasons. The landscape begins to remember human labor.

That memory changes inheritance. A person can receive more than tools or knowledge from the previous generation. They can inherit a field already cleared, a granary already built, a herd already grown, a canal already dug, and a social claim that says these things belong to them rather than to someone standing nearby.

Yesterday’s captured energy begins to shape who has options tomorrow.

This helps explain why the agricultural transition was so consequential even if its first practitioners did not receive an immediate productivity dividend. Farming could turn a landscape into a durable apparatus for supporting population. The apparatus might be inefficient in labor hours and still formidable in aggregate. More people could occupy the same region; stored food could sustain them through time; domesticated animals could add traction, transport, manure, milk, and wealth; institutions could form around the resulting concentrations.

Density itself then becomes a source of new capability and new risk.

More people living closer together can support thicker exchange networks, specialized skills, collective construction, and larger pools of knowledge. They can also support epidemics, conflict, and political systems capable of extracting from a larger tax base. Agriculture does not simply add energy to an unchanged society. It changes the scale at which coordination becomes possible and therefore the scale at which both cooperation and coercion can operate.

This is why demographic growth cannot be treated as an uncomplicated scorecard. Bocquet-Appel’s fertility signal tells us something profound about the expansion of farming populations. Shennan’s later boom-and-bust pattern reminds us that expansion can overshoot local systems or collide with social and environmental pressures. The same civilization can become more capable in aggregate while becoming more unequal, more disease-prone, or more vulnerable to synchronized failure. ([Bocquet-Appel, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1208880); [Shennan et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms3486))

The process was never a clean replacement of foraging by farming. Cultivation, gathering, hunting, and herding mixed for long periods and in different proportions. Practices spread, stalled, retreated, and were reinvented. Some communities cultivated plants without relying on them fully. Some stored wild cereals before domestication. Some herded while continuing to hunt. The word *revolution* compresses generations of experimentation into a moment that nobody experienced as a chapter break.

Dhra’ is useful precisely because it interrupts that compression. Its granaries belong to a predomestication context. The building arrives before the category we later call agriculture is complete. A community had already decided that enough plant food would be gathered or cultivated, and that enough of it would matter later, to justify architecture dedicated to protecting it. ([Kuijt & Finlayson, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0812764106))

The sequence tells us something about how energy transitions actually happen. Infrastructure and behavior can precede the mature technology they will later seem designed for. People build storage because storage is useful; storage then changes the economics of cultivation; cultivation increases the value of storage. Cause runs in both directions.

The same pattern will recur with coal mines and steam pumps, power plants and grids, cars and oil infrastructure, data centers and electrical generation. A new source of useful energy and the systems that make it useful often grow together until separating cause from consequence becomes artificial.

What eventually emerged from the Neolithic transitions was a new kind of human energy system. Its generating surface was land. Its converters were plants, animals, and human bodies. Its storage media were grain, roots, oils, preserved foods, seed, and living herds. Its networks consisted of paths, households, storehouses, corrals, waterways, and eventually roads and markets. Its control layer was social: custom, ownership, accounting, obligation, law, and force.

The system could accumulate.

That last property matters because accumulation changes history’s tempo. A fire has to be fed continuously. A harvest can be carried forward. Seed becomes the input to another season. Animals reproduce. Ditches, terraces, walls, wells, and cleared fields embody previous labor and can affect production long after the people who built them are gone.

There is an important asymmetry here. Accumulated capacity is never distributed automatically. One family may inherit fertile land and animals; another may inherit obligations. One village may hold grain in household stores; another may deliver part of the harvest to a central authority. The physical ability to preserve energy through time makes inheritance more consequential because more useful capacity can survive its current owner.

The first surplus therefore contains the beginnings of compounding in both the generous and dangerous senses of the word. Seed produces more seed. A herd can grow. Irrigation can raise future yields. Knowledge can improve cultivation. But wealth can compound too. Claims can compound. Debts can compound. Political power can be fed from a stream of stored production that arrives predictably enough to sustain people whose occupation is preserving the stream.

A surplus can feed a library or an army. The calories do not choose.

This is the same moral neutrality that made fire such a poor synonym for progress. Energy expands the set of feasible actions. Human institutions determine which actions become routine and who receives their benefits.

Yet it would be equally misleading to react against crude technological determinism by pretending the physical substrate does not matter. No institution can redistribute grain that does not exist. No army can remain in the field without food. No city can sustain a large population if its hinterland, trade network, or storage system cannot deliver calories. Administration may decide where energy goes, but it cannot repeal the need for energy to arrive.

That is the balance this book will keep returning to. Energy is not destiny. It is capacity under constraint.

Agriculture dramatically enlarged that capacity by transforming time. Instead of living only from biological flows encountered in the present, communities could shape future flows and preserve part of past ones. They could plant for a season not yet arrived and build a granary for a hunger not yet felt.

That is why the suspended floor at Dhra’ matters. It is architecture built against decay. Air circulation, rodent protection, walls, and elevation are all ways of telling a harvest: not yet.

The command is modest compared with what later civilizations would attempt. But it introduces a pattern that will define increasingly sophisticated energy systems. Useful energy becomes more valuable when it can be separated from the moment of production and made available when demand appears.

The granary is an early answer to intermittency.

Its success did not end scarcity. It did not end hard labor. It did not guarantee health or equality. It did something more specific and more consequential: it made a portion of nature’s annual production schedulable by human beings.

From that scheduling came resilience, population, specialization, property, taxation, hierarchy, trade, and the possibility of institutions whose plans extended farther into the future because their food supply could do the same.

The first surplus was therefore not merely extra food. It was stored possibility—and stored power.

Fire had taught humans to maintain an energetic process. Agriculture taught them to reorganize ecosystems around one, bank part of its output, and build social systems around the bank.

For thousands of years, however, even the largest agricultural civilizations remained tied to a basic annual budget. Food, fodder, timber, charcoal, fibers, and most usable heat ultimately depended on what living landscapes could produce from current sunlight. Land could be improved, irrigated, conquered, traded across, or exploited more intensely, but the photosynthetic clock still set a broad ceiling.

The next transition would loosen that ceiling in a way the builders of Dhra’ could not have imagined. Instead of waiting for plants to collect another season of sunlight, some societies would learn to dig into landscapes containing the compressed remains of sunlight captured by ancient ecosystems millions of years earlier.

The granary would eventually give way to the coal seam.
