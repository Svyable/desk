# The First Surplus

A granary is a peculiar machine. It does not make food. It does not hunt, harvest, grind, cook, or carry anything. It simply prevents something useful from disappearing before you need it.

About eleven thousand years ago, at Dhra′ near the Dead Sea in what is now Jordan, people built circular storage structures with raised floors. The floors rested on beams supported by notched stones, leaving air beneath the stored crop and making it harder for damp, insects, and rodents to ruin the food. The architecture is modest by later standards. There are no palace walls, no tax records, no monumental inscriptions. But the design reveals an idea that would become one of civilization’s quiet foundations: today’s harvest could be made to survive into another season.

Ian Kuijt and Bill Finlayson, reporting the excavations in 2009, dated these purpose-built granaries to roughly 11,300 to 11,175 calibrated years before present. The surprise was not merely their age. They appear before fully domesticated cereals. People were already investing labor in large-scale storage while relying on cultivated and gathered plants that still retained wild forms.

That sequence matters because it complicates the usual story of agriculture. We often imagine a clean technological ladder: humans discover farming, farming raises productivity, productivity creates surplus, surplus creates cities, cities create civilization. The archaeological record is less tidy. Storage, cultivation, sedentism, domestication, population growth, hierarchy, and political organization do not arrive as a package on the same morning.

Some appear before others. Some reinforce one another. Some make life better for a community while making it harder for particular people inside it.

The first agricultural revolution was not obviously a revolution in hourly earnings.

Samuel Bowles tried to test one of the oldest assumptions about the transition to farming: perhaps early people adopted cereal cultivation because it simply produced more calories for each hour of work than foraging. His 2011 analysis compared estimates of caloric returns from wild resources with those from hand-tool cultivation, then tried to account for the less visible costs of farming: processing, storage, delayed returns, and the risk of depending on a narrower set of foods.

The result was inconvenient. Given large uncertainties, Bowles found no convincing evidence that the first cereal farmers were more productive per hour than early Holocene foragers. Farming could require clearing, planting, tending, harvesting, threshing, grinding, storing, and protecting a crop before the calories reached a mouth. A hunter or gatherer often faced uncertainty too, but agriculture concentrated effort into a longer chain whose failure could arrive late.

If farming did not obviously offer an immediate labor-productivity windfall, why did it spread?

One answer is that productivity per hour is not the only kind of advantage a livelihood can have.

A field changes the relationship between people and land. A crop can be encouraged to grow densely in a chosen place. Grain can be dried. It can be moved. It can be counted. It can be divided into rations. It can wait. Those properties are easy to overlook because they are not nutritional properties. A kernel does not contain more energy because someone writes a number beside a basket of it. But countability and storage change what a group can do with the energy already captured by plants.

This is where agriculture begins to resemble an energy system rather than merely a way of obtaining food.

Plants are solar converters. Through photosynthesis they turn a diffuse daily flow of sunlight into chemical energy stored in tissue. Wild ecosystems do this without asking permission. Agriculture changes which plants occupy the landscape, where they grow, how densely they grow, and how much human labor is invested in helping their stored energy end up in human hands.

A harvest compresses months of sunlight into something that can be piled on a floor.

Storage then moves that captured energy through time.

At Dhra′, the technical problem is visible in the building itself. If grain lies directly on damp ground, it can rot. If rodents reach it, the surplus becomes rodent metabolism. If insects reach it, the calories walk away in smaller bodies. A raised floor and protected enclosure do not increase the original harvest. They increase the share of the harvest that remains available later.

That is an efficiency gain, but not the kind represented by a faster tool. It is a reduction in loss.

The same principle appears throughout later energy history. A coal seam is useless if it cannot be kept dry enough to mine. Electricity generated at noon is less valuable to a system that needs it after sunset unless some combination of storage, flexible demand, or dispatchable supply bridges the gap. Fuel that leaks, spoils, evaporates, or cannot be transported is physically present but operationally absent.

The first granaries made food less perishable as a social resource.

They also changed risk. A mobile foraging group could respond to local scarcity partly by moving toward other resources. A more sedentary community with fields and buildings had more invested in a particular place. That created new vulnerabilities, but storage offered a different response: remain in place and carry part of a good season forward.

The granary is therefore both an energy device and a bet about the future. People expend labor now so that future hunger has fewer claims on future behavior.

That bargain can support a larger population.

Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel examined cemetery data across archaeological sequences and identified a pronounced demographic shift associated with the transition to farming. The key signal was an increase in the proportion of juvenile skeletons, which he interpreted as evidence of higher fertility during what has become known as the Neolithic Demographic Transition. The exact demographic mechanisms remain debated and varied by place, but the larger pattern is difficult to miss: farming populations expanded.

This is another reason the word productivity can mislead. A livelihood can fail to produce more calories per labor hour and still support more people per unit of land, especially when cultivation, storage, and sedentary settlement reinforce one another. More total people can survive in a territory even if each person’s working life is not easier.

From the viewpoint of a modern reader, that distinction feels almost unfair. We are accustomed to treating progress as an improvement in the individual’s condition. Population growth is not automatically welfare growth. A settlement with twice as many people is not twice as good. If the extra people are shorter, sicker, working harder, or living under more coercive institutions, the arithmetic of headcount has not answered the human question.

The early agricultural record gives plenty of reason for caution. Farming often narrowed diets. Dense settlements brought people, waste, and pathogens into closer contact. Repetitive labor left marks on bodies. Stored food attracted pests and people willing to steal it. Dependence on a few crops could turn a bad season into a disaster shared by an entire community.

Agriculture made some risks easier to manage and others easier to scale.

This book is interested in energy because energy constrains capability, not because a rising energy total is automatically a rising moral score. Chapter 1 began with fire for the same reason. A larger blaze is not necessarily a better fire. Here the analogous warning is that a larger harvest, a larger granary, or a larger population does not tell us by itself whether individuals lived better lives.

The more defensible claim is that agriculture changed the scale and form of collective capability.

A stored crop can support people who are not gathering food at that exact moment. That does not mean the first farmers immediately invented a permanent class of full-time artisans, priests, soldiers, accountants, and rulers. Specialization develops unevenly, and many early communities remained heavily involved in subsistence work. But the possibility changes when food can be concentrated and released according to a schedule rather than consumed near the time and place it is obtained.

Someone can spend a day shaping stone, repairing a roof, guarding an entrance, making pottery, digging an irrigation channel, or building a wall because stored calories can arrive without that person spending the same day acquiring them directly.

That is what surplus does at its most basic. It separates the timing of energy capture from the timing of energy use.

Imagine two communities with the same annual food energy. One consumes almost everything as it appears. The other can preserve a meaningful share for months. Their annual caloric totals may be identical, yet their options are not. The second community can survive a short interruption, provision a work party, support travel during a lean period, accumulate seed for planting, or concentrate labor on a task whose payoff comes later.

Agriculture also made energy more legible to institutions. A herd can be counted. A field can be measured. A basket of grain can be weighed or estimated. A storehouse can be guarded. A portion can be demanded.

The same properties that make a surplus useful make it extractable.

A cache of roots scattered across a landscape is difficult for a distant authority to assess. Grain gathered into a settlement is easier. It matures on a seasonal schedule, can be divided, stored, transported, and inspected. A granary does not invent taxation, but it creates the kind of resource around which taxation becomes practical.

Those claims are not necessarily predatory. Shared stores can buffer famine. Collective labor can build walls, roads, canals, temples, drainage systems, and defensive works no household could easily produce alone. A political center can coordinate projects across hundreds or thousands of people. Surplus can support people whose knowledge is valuable but whose work does not yield food directly.

It can also support elites, raiders, and armies.

The archaeological record of inequality makes that ambiguity difficult to avoid. Timothy Kohler and a large group of collaborators compared house-size distributions from archaeological sites across several regions as a consistent proxy for household wealth. Their 2017 study found that material inequality generally rose with domesticated plants and animals and with increasing sociopolitical scale. The later divergence was especially striking: post-Neolithic Eurasian societies tended to develop greater household wealth disparities than those in North America and Mesoamerica.

The authors argued that access to large domesticated mammals was one important reason. Oxen and other large animals could extend cultivation, transportation, and the control of land. Horses eventually transformed warfare and political reach. Where wealth could be expanded through animals, land, and military power, it could also become more concentrated.

The finding should not be turned into a universal law. The dataset is an archaeological sample, house area is an imperfect proxy for wealth, and the study itself emphasizes regional differences. Farming did not impose one inevitable political structure everywhere.

But it does expose a weakness in the simplest surplus story. More storable energy does not merely create more occupations. It creates disputes over ownership, access, and enforcement.

That makes the granary an institutional object as much as a technical one. Someone has to decide which grain is seed, which is rationed, which is owed, which is shared, and which can be kept. The food has persisted long enough to become governable.

Fire required continuity of knowledge. Agriculture adds continuity of stock.

Long before fossil fuels, humans were learning to manipulate the timing, density, and ownership of biological energy. Fields concentrated plant growth. Domestication gradually altered the organisms themselves. Storage moved food through seasons. Draft animals later converted plant energy into mechanical work outside the human body. Irrigation redirected water to increase the reliability of plant production. Roads and boats widened the radius across which surplus could be moved.

The gains came with dependence. A mobile forager could draw on many species across a landscape; a farming community might become extraordinarily knowledgeable about a smaller number of crops, soils, seasons, pests, storage conditions, and water systems. When a society specializes around a narrow agricultural base, failure can spread through the same channels that made the system productive.

A failed crop is more dangerous when nearly everyone is waiting on the same crop.

Later energy systems will repeat that bargain. Coal creates capabilities that become hard to replace once factories, railways, and cities assume its availability. Electricity allows an astonishing range of activities, then makes outages more consequential because so many activities have been designed around continuous power. Capability grows by building dependencies on systems that must keep working.

There is a temptation to call the shift from foraging to farming humanity’s first great step toward abundance. The word is too generous if it erases the people who worked harder, ate worse, suffered new diseases, or lost autonomy inside more hierarchical systems.

Yet calling the transition a mistake is also too easy. Farming populations expanded across much of the planet. Fields, storage, and domesticated animals supported settlements at scales difficult to sustain through mobile foraging alone. Over long periods they made possible durable infrastructure, deeper specialization, written administration, large trade networks, and states capable of coordinating resources far beyond a household.

Those later achievements were not hidden inside the first seed like destiny. They became possible because a chain of techniques and institutions gradually made biological energy more regular, concentrated, transferable, and persistent.

Dhra′ catches one part of that chain before the later story has hardened into inevitability.

The granaries there were not attached to palaces. Kuijt and Finlayson note that the earliest storage structures stood between residential buildings rather than inside them, which raises the possibility that storage was initially more communal. Later in the Neolithic, storage increasingly moved into houses and dedicated rooms. The architecture may record a shift in ownership as well as a shift in food technology.

That possibility is more interesting than a generic tale of progress. A community learns to preserve more food, and almost immediately the question appears: whose food?

The raised floor at Dhra′ was built to keep grain away from moisture and pests. It may also have helped create a new kind of social object—food that remained in one place long enough for a community, household, or authority to define a claim over it.

The energy argument has to stop there rather than pretending the physics selects the politics. A granary can become resilience or tribute, seed stock or military provisioning. Energy widens the menu; institutions decide what gets ordered.

That distinction becomes even more important when draft animals enter the system. A domesticated ox is not merely food on legs. Fed on plants, it converts chemical energy into traction. It can pull a plow, move a cart, and expand the amount of land a household can work. Large animals also reproduce, can be accumulated as wealth, and can be controlled by people who control pasture and land.

Kohler’s cross-regional inequality result suggests how profoundly that combination can matter. A source of mechanical power can also become an asset.

The later history of energy will keep fusing those two meanings of power. Coal mines are physical resources and economic property. Oil fields are geological facts and geopolitical prizes. Electrical grids are machines and regulated institutions. Data centers are loads on a network and concentrations of capital. The ability to command energy is entangled with rules about who gets to command it.

The first farmers did not necessarily discover an easier way to live. They helped assemble a system that could support more people, hold more energy across seasons, concentrate more resources in one place, and coordinate labor over longer periods. That system made new forms of resilience possible. It also made new forms of extraction possible.

The surplus was therefore not simply extra food. It was time made material.

A basket of grain carried labor from harvest into winter. Seed carried one growing season into the next. A herd carried plant energy until it was needed as meat, milk, hide, or muscle. A storehouse carried the good fortune of one month into the uncertainty of another.

Once energy could be held this way, human plans could lengthen. A wall could take years. A canal could require thousands of coordinated workdays before returning anything. A craft could demand apprenticeship. Political projects could outlast the harvest that financed their first stones.

At Dhra′, the remains are architectural rather than literary: mud walls, reused grinding stones, beam supports, the traces of a raised floor. The people who built them left no explanation of what they thought they were doing. They may not have imagined themselves crossing a historical threshold. They were solving a practical problem: how to keep food from being eaten by everything except the people who had gathered or cultivated it.

The solution was small enough to fit inside a circular building roughly three meters across.

Inside that space, sunlight captured months earlier could wait.
