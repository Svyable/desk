# The First Surplus

A field of grain is a solar collector with a calendar attached.

That description would have meant nothing to the people who first began tending wild cereals in Southwest Asia, but the physics was already there. Sunlight arrived over months. Plants converted a fraction of it into stems and seeds. Human labor altered which plants survived, where they grew, when they were gathered, and eventually which traits were reproduced. The harvest could then be dried and kept after the season that produced it had ended.

Fire had allowed human beings to command an energetic process outside the body. Agriculture did something different. It reorganized a landscape so that energy arriving diffusely from the sun could be concentrated into forms that humans could predict, harvest, store, divide, and return to later.

It is tempting to tell this as the next obvious rung of progress: hunter-gatherers discovered that farming produced more food for less work, adopted the superior technology, accumulated a surplus, and built civilization with the spare time. The appeal of that story is precisely why it needs to be handled carefully. Some of the best quantitative work on early agriculture begins by attacking it.

Samuel Bowles tried to estimate the caloric return per hour of labor for the cereals cultivated by early farmers and compare it with returns from foraging. His calculation had to include more than the visible work in a field. Cultivation delays the reward for labor. Harvests have to be processed and stored. Reliance on a small number of crops creates risks different from gathering a wider portfolio of wild foods. The evidence is uncertain, as any reconstruction across thousands of years must be, but Bowles found it inconsistent with the simple claim that the first cereal farmers enjoyed higher labor productivity than early Holocene foragers.

The first agricultural revolution may therefore have been a peculiar kind of advance: one that could make the human system larger before it made the average human life easier.

This distinction changes what we should look for in the archaeological record. If farming did not necessarily buy an individual more calories for each hour worked, its advantage could lie elsewhere—in the number of people a landscape could support, in the timing of food supply, in the ability to occupy territory more densely, and above all in what could be done with a harvest after it had been gathered.

A gazelle is nutritious but inconvenient to inventory. A stand of ripe grain is different. Once harvested and dried, cereal can wait. It can be put in a container, moved into a building, measured out through winter, saved for seed, exchanged, stolen, inherited, promised, guarded, or demanded. A store of grain makes tomorrow's food partly visible today.

That is a profound change in the politics of energy. The sunlight embodied in food no longer has to be consumed near the moment and place at which humans encounter it. Some of it can cross time.

Storage is easy to mistake for a secondary detail because a granary contains no dramatic machine. Yet the ability to move a resource through time is itself a technology. A reservoir does it with water. A battery does it with electricity. A grain store does it with chemical energy assembled by plants. None creates the underlying energy. Each changes when it becomes available and therefore what can be organized around it.

The comparison should not be pushed too literally. Grain spoils, attracts pests, requires protection, and is also seed, food, commodity, and biological material in ways an electrical battery is not. But the common feature matters: control over timing changes the usefulness of a flow.

For a mobile forager, many resources are encountered as a moving landscape of opportunities. A farming household begins to bind itself more tightly to particular ground and to a production cycle whose payoff may lie months ahead. That creates vulnerability. A failed harvest is not simply a bad afternoon of hunting. Labor already sunk into a crop cannot be recovered by wishing the season had gone differently. Agriculture concentrates risk along with food.

It also changes reproduction. Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel found a striking demographic signal in archaeological cemetery sequences associated with the transition from foraging to farming: an abrupt rise in the proportion of juvenile skeletons, interpreted as evidence of increased fertility. In his 2011 synthesis, he estimated an increase in total fertility on the order of two births per woman during the Neolithic Demographic Transition. Later work using independent radiocarbon evidence has supported a broad pattern of accelerated population growth after agriculture arrived in Europe, while also finding subsequent episodes of demographic decline.

This is not evidence that every farmer was healthier or happier. A population can grow while individuals endure heavier workloads, narrower diets, infectious disease, inequality, or periods of acute scarcity. Population growth is a system-level outcome. Welfare is an individual and distributional question. Agriculture forces us to stop treating the two as synonyms.

That may be the central paradox of the first surplus. A livelihood can spread because it is good at producing more people, defending land, storing claims on the future, or supporting larger groups even when it is not obviously superior for each person performing the labor.

Once a harvest can wait, other things can wait for the harvest.

A household can reserve seed rather than eat everything immediately. A community can maintain people whose daily work is not acquiring calories: craftspeople, builders, guards, ritual specialists, administrators. The familiar history of specialization begins here, but it should not be romanticized as the discovery of leisure. A surplus can free some people from subsistence work because other people produce enough to support them. Who is freed, who produces, and who decides are political questions from the beginning.

Stored food is therefore not only a buffer against scarcity. It is a claim that can be controlled.

The difference becomes clearer if we imagine two identical harvests. In one village the grain is dispersed among households and consumed locally. In another it is gathered into central stores, recorded, guarded, and redistributed by authorities. The biological energy harvested from the landscape may be similar. The social capability is not. Concentration creates the possibility of coordinated construction, warfare, relief, ceremony, trade, and administration. It also creates the possibility of extraction.

Surplus has two faces because control does.

Archaeology cannot give us a ledger for the first farming villages, but it can reveal long changes in the distribution of material wealth. Timothy Kohler and colleagues used house size as a consistent, if imperfect, proxy to estimate household wealth inequality across archaeological societies. Their results show inequality generally rising with plant and animal domestication and with sociopolitical scale. The pattern was not universal. Post-Neolithic Eurasia diverged substantially from North America and Mesoamerica, and the authors argued that access to large domesticable mammals helped make agricultural extensification and wealth concentration more powerful in parts of the Old World.

That regional difference is important. There is no single agricultural package with a single political destiny. Wheat, maize, rice, cattle, irrigation, rainfall, soil, inheritance rules, warfare, and geography produce different systems. A book about energy becomes useless if it flattens those differences into an equation in which more calories automatically produce a state.

What energy can do is clarify the material possibility beneath the variation. A society cannot support a dense non-farming population unless somebody or something produces the food. It cannot carry a reserve across a bad season unless resources can be stored or reliably obtained elsewhere. It cannot concentrate a large surplus unless institutions can claim, move, protect, or account for it. Energy is the substrate. Institutions determine its path.

This is where agriculture extends the lesson of fire. The crucial transition is not simply an increase in quantity. Wild ecosystems already captured immense solar energy. Human beings did not invent photosynthesis any more than they invented combustion. They learned, slowly and unevenly, to alter the biological machinery around them so that a larger share of particular landscapes yielded forms of energy useful to human purposes.

The price of that control was work and constraint. Fields have to be cleared or prepared. Crops have to be protected. Soil fertility can be exhausted. Water can become a source of conflict. Sedentary populations accumulate waste and pathogens. A storage system can fail spectacularly when the crop on which it depends fails with it. The same concentration that makes a surplus governable makes it legible to raiders and rulers.

The word surplus itself can hide these costs. It sounds like what remains after everyone has enough. Historically, a surplus is often better understood as production above immediate consumption that somebody has the capacity to preserve and command. A granary can represent security against famine. It can also represent taxes already collected.

That ambiguity would follow civilization for the rest of its history. Greater productive capacity enlarges what a society can attempt, but the distribution of that capacity decides whose possibilities expand. A canal can stabilize harvests and demand corvée labor. An ox can multiply the force applied to a plow and become concentrated capital. A city can support scholarship, craft, sanitation, armies, palaces, and slums at the same time.

Agriculture also introduced a new relationship with land. Foragers can defend territories, and farming did not invent property, conflict, or hierarchy. But a planted field contains labor whose payoff is tied to a place and a future harvest. Seed in the ground is an investment that cannot simply follow its owner across the landscape. Storage deepens the attachment. Irrigation deepens it further. Improvements accumulate in particular locations.

Energy capture becomes infrastructure.

That shift helps explain why the agricultural transition was so consequential even if its first practitioners did not receive an immediate productivity dividend. Farming could turn a landscape into a durable apparatus for supporting population. The apparatus might be inefficient in labor hours and still formidable in aggregate. More people could occupy the same region; stored food could sustain them through time; domesticated animals could later add traction, transport, manure, milk, and wealth; institutions could form around the resulting concentrations.

The process was not a clean replacement of foraging by farming. Cultivation, gathering, hunting, and herding mixed for long periods and in different proportions. Practices spread, stalled, retreated, and were reinvented. The word revolution compresses generations of experimentation into a moment that nobody experienced as a chapter break.

What eventually emerged, however, was a new kind of human energy system. Its generating surface was land. Its converters were plants and animals. Its storage medium was biological. Its network consisted of paths, households, storehouses, herds, waterways, and eventually roads and markets. Its control layer was social: custom, ownership, accounting, obligation, law, and force.

The system could accumulate.

That last property matters because accumulation changes history's tempo. A fire has to be fed continuously. A harvest can be carried forward. Seed becomes the input to another season. Animals reproduce. Ditches, terraces, walls, wells, and cleared fields embody previous labor and can affect production long after the people who built them are gone. Human beings begin to inherit not only knowledge and tools but altered productive landscapes.

For the first time on a large scale, yesterday's captured energy could shape who had options tomorrow.

This is a less comforting account of the agricultural transition than the old story of abundance creating civilization, but it is also more powerful. Farming did not have to make everyone richer immediately to transform the human condition. It made energy more schedulable, populations denser, resources more accumulable, and claims on those resources more enforceable. Those properties supported villages, then towns, cities, states, and the enormous variety of institutions that followed. They also supported hierarchy, organized violence, and extraction on scales a small mobile band could not sustain.

The first surplus was therefore not merely extra food. It was stored possibility—and stored power.

Fire had taught humans to maintain an energetic process. Agriculture taught them to reorganize ecosystems around one, bank part of its output, and build social systems around the bank. The next great transition would break a much older ceiling. Instead of depending primarily on the sunlight arriving each year, some societies would learn to dig into landscapes containing solar energy accumulated over geological time.

The granary would eventually give way to the coal seam.
