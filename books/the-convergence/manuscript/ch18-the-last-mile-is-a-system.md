# The Last Mile Is a System

A wire reaches a house.

The statistic changes.

The life inside the house may change less.

This is the problem with treating energy access as a switch.

Connected.

Not connected.

The distinction matters. A household with no electricity at all faces limits that a connected household does not. But the binary hides almost everything that determines what electricity can actually do.

How many hours is it available?

Can the household afford enough of it to run more than a light and a phone charger?

Does voltage stay within a range that appliances can tolerate?

Are outages scheduled or arbitrary?

Can a business rely on the supply without buying a generator?

Can a clinic refrigerate medicine overnight?

Can a school run fans during the hottest part of the day?

Can a family cook without smoke filling the room?

A connection is the beginning of energy access.

It is not the end.

That distinction becomes more important as the global access gap narrows numerically while remaining enormous in human terms. The 2026 edition of *Tracking SDG 7*, produced by the International Energy Agency, the International Renewable Energy Agency, the United Nations Statistics Division, the World Bank, and the World Health Organization, estimates that 655 million people still lacked electricity access in 2024. About two billion people still relied on polluting fuels and technologies for cooking.

Those numbers are lower than they once were.

They are also concentrated.

More than 560 million of the people without electricity were in sub-Saharan Africa. The report found that the global access rate had stalled around 92 percent in 2024 and that the remaining gap was increasingly rural, poor, remote, and difficult to finance.

This is what happens near the end of many infrastructure expansions.

The easy connections are made first.

Dense cities are cheaper to serve than scattered settlements. Customers with reliable incomes are easier to finance than households whose income varies by season. A feeder serving many paying users can justify investment more easily than kilometers of line reaching a small community. Existing roads simplify construction. Stable institutions lower risk. A wealthy customer can pay a connection charge that is impossible for a poor one even if the wire passes directly overhead.

The last mile is rarely only a mile.

It is a bundle of costs.

This is why energy abundance at national scale can coexist with energy poverty at household scale.

A country can have power stations and people without power.

It can have surplus generation at noon and neighborhoods that cannot afford a legal connection.

It can have a transmission network and villages too distant from distribution lines.

It can have a grid connection on paper and outages long enough that businesses treat diesel generators as essential equipment.

Aggregate supply does not automatically become usable service.

Something has to carry abundance through the system.

This is where measurement changes the argument.

The World Bank's Energy Sector Management Assistance Program developed the Multi-Tier Framework because a binary connection metric could not describe the lived quality of energy access. The framework treats electricity access as a spectrum and evaluates attributes such as capacity, duration, reliability, quality, affordability, legality, health, and safety.

The point is not that every household needs the same amount of electricity.

The point is that a connection has dimensions.

A few watts of evening lighting is a real improvement over darkness.

It is not enough to run a refrigerator.

A refrigerator is not enough to run welding equipment.

Four hours of electricity can support some uses and make others impossible. Electricity that arrives twenty hours a day but unpredictably fails during business hours has a different value from electricity that is predictably unavailable for the same four hours each night. A cheap tariff is not affordable if the connection fee requires months of income.

Energy access is a ladder of capability.

That phrase should sound familiar by now.

The entire history of energy is a ladder of capability.

Fire did not matter because hominins crossed a threshold called fire access. It mattered because heat, light, protection, and cooking became more available. Electricity did not transform factories because a meter appeared on the wall. It transformed them because motors could be placed where work required them and supplied reliably enough to reorganize production.

The household version is no different.

The service is the point.

This becomes clearer when a small business enters the story.

Imagine a tailor in a town where the grid has recently arrived. A light allows work after sunset. A phone enables payments and customer contact. An electric sewing machine increases output. A fan makes a hot room tolerable.

Now imagine that voltage fluctuates enough to damage equipment, outages arrive without warning, and power disappears three afternoons each week.

The business is connected.

It is not secure.

The owner can respond by buying a battery, a small generator, or a solar system. Each adds redundancy. Each also adds capital cost that a business in a highly reliable system does not have to bear.

Unreliable electricity can therefore function like a tax on productive activity.

Not because every outage destroys a firm.

Because the firm must design around the possibility.

Inventory changes. Working hours change. Equipment choices change. Backup capital sits idle until needed. Customers learn not to trust delivery times. Some investments never happen because the expected service is too uncertain.

Reliability changes behavior before failure occurs.

This is why energy quality is an economic variable, not merely an engineering preference.

Affordability has the same structure.

A power system may be technically capable of serving a household that cannot afford to use it. The barrier may be the connection fee, internal wiring, appliance cost, tariff, deposit, or irregular income. A low-income household may preserve access by rationing consumption so aggressively that the connection never supports the services policymakers imagined when the line was built.

That household should not be described as unchanged.

A single light may save hours of darkness. Phone charging can matter enormously. Even modest electricity can improve convenience, safety, communication, and information access.

But it should not be described as fully served either.

This is the value of tiers.

They keep improvement visible without pretending completion.

The distinction also helps resolve an argument that otherwise becomes ideological.

Should developing regions build large centralized grids or leap directly to distributed solar and mini-grids?

Both answers can be right in different places.

A dense industrial corridor with rapidly rising demand may benefit from large generation, transmission, and a strong synchronized grid. A remote settlement far from the existing network may receive useful electricity much sooner and more cheaply from a mini-grid or stand-alone solar system.

The technology should follow the service problem.

Not the other way around.

The 2026 SDG 7 report emphasizes distributed renewable energy because many of the remaining unelectrified populations live in remote, lower-income, and fragile settings where conventional grid extension can be slow or uneconomic. Off-grid and mini-grid systems can provide first access without waiting for a national network to reach every settlement.

That is a powerful advantage.

It is not proof that decentralized systems should remain permanently isolated.

A small solar home system may support lighting, charging, radio, and a fan. A mini-grid may support refrigeration, shops, milling, water pumping, and some productive loads. As demand grows, customers may want more power, longer service, industrial equipment, or stronger redundancy.

The architecture can evolve.

A mini-grid can expand. A national grid can arrive later. Distributed systems can remain as backup or become integrated. The transition from no service to basic service does not have to predetermine the final network.

Infrastructure can have stages.

This is a recurring lesson in development because planners are often forced to choose between waiting for the ideal system and deploying an imperfect one now.

The correct answer depends on how reversible the imperfect system is.

A small solar system that immediately replaces kerosene lighting and can later coexist with a stronger grid may have enormous option value. A poorly designed network that locks customers into expensive, unreliable service may be harder to justify merely because it arrived quickly.

Speed matters.

So does the path after speed.

Clean cooking makes the stakes even clearer because electricity access alone does not guarantee a clean household energy system.

Two billion people still rely on polluting fuels and technologies for cooking according to the 2026 SDG 7 report. The burden is strongly unequal: the report describes a large rural gap and an especially severe deficit in sub-Saharan Africa.

Cooking is not a minor side category.

It is one of the oldest energy services in the human story.

A household can have electric light and still burn wood, charcoal, kerosene, or other polluting fuels for meals. The reason may be appliance cost, electricity reliability, tariff design, cooking practices, fuel availability, the size of pots, or the amount of power the local system can deliver.

Again, the wire is not the service.

Clean cooking exposes the limits of technological triumphalism because the engineering options already exist. Electricity can cook. Liquefied petroleum gas can cook. Biogas, bioethanol, and other systems can cook. Improved stoves can reduce some harms where cleaner options remain unaffordable.

The problem is adoption inside real household economics.

A stove used once for a demonstration does not displace smoke.

A fuel supply has to remain available.

The household has to prefer the method enough to keep using it.

The recurring cost has to fit the budget.

The system has to work on the day the meal must be made.

This is infrastructure at its most intimate.

The last mile ends in habits.

That does not mean culture is a convenient excuse for failure. Too often, what is described as preference is adaptation to constraints. A family may continue using biomass because electricity is too unreliable for dinner. It may keep a charcoal stove because an electric cooker cannot handle the largest pot on the available connection. A business may prefer a diesel generator because grid voltage damages motors.

Behavior often contains information about the system.

Listen before blaming the user.

This is where the Multi-Tier Framework becomes more than a measurement device.

It is a philosophy of infrastructure.

Ask what the user can actually do.

Measure the attributes that limit the service.

Improve the binding attribute.

A household with adequate capacity but terrible reliability needs a different intervention from one with reliable supply but unaffordable connection costs. A remote village without any network needs a different intervention from an urban neighborhood sitting beside a feeder but unable to obtain a legal connection.

Binary metrics flatten those distinctions.

Good institutions need them.

The same logic applies in wealthy countries, although the thresholds differ.

A household may be connected to a highly reliable grid and still experience energy insecurity because heating or cooling bills consume too much income. An old building can make a modest tariff expensive by wasting energy. A renter may be unable to improve insulation or install efficient equipment. A shutoff notice is technically different from a blackout and practically similar to the household receiving neither light nor heat.

Infrastructure is only useful when people can command the service.

This is why affordability cannot be solved by supply alone.

More abundant energy can lower costs, and often does. But final bills include networks, taxes, financing, maintenance, fixed charges, market design, housing quality, and the cost of equipment that converts electricity into a service.

A cheap kilowatt-hour does not buy an efficient refrigerator for a household that cannot finance one.

A cheap heat pump is not cheap if a building requires an expensive electrical upgrade first.

A low generation cost may coexist with a high retail bill if networks are costly or poorly managed.

The price of the commodity and the price of capability are different.

That distinction keeps the energy thesis honest.

If civilization advances by expanding useful capability, then counting only fuel or electricity supply is not enough. The system must carry that capability to the user.

The last mile is where abstract abundance meets distribution.

Distribution is not just a moral question.

It is a systems question.

A network with few paying customers can become financially fragile. A utility that cannot recover enough revenue may defer maintenance, which worsens reliability, which encourages wealthy customers to defect to private backup, which weakens the utility further. Poor service and poor finances can reinforce each other.

The opposite cycle is possible too.

Reliable service attracts productive demand. Productive demand improves revenue. Stronger revenue supports maintenance and expansion. Better service makes electric appliances more useful. More useful appliances increase demand and political support for the network.

Infrastructure can compound.

This is why the first kilowatt-hour and the thousandth are not socially identical.

The first may replace darkness.

The next may power refrigeration.

Later electricity may run a machine tool, a pump, an air conditioner, a server, or an induction furnace.

Some services saturate. Others create new layers of economic activity.

The energy ladder is not a ranking of human worth.

It is a description of what different levels and qualities of service make feasible.

This also helps explain why energy access can grow while inequality in energy capability remains large.

The 2026 SDG 7 report notes a striking difference in renewable generating capacity per person between low-income and high-income countries. A global boom can therefore be real while its physical benefits remain unevenly distributed.

There is no contradiction.

Global totals are not local inventories.

The same solar-module cost decline that transforms one country can arrive slowly in another because financing is expensive, currency is unstable, imports are difficult, utilities are weak, or consumers cannot afford upfront costs.

Technology crosses borders more easily than institutions and balance sheets.

This is why finance appears so often in energy-access reports.

The remaining gap is not composed only of people whom engineers forgot to connect. Many live in places where projects face high capital costs and low expected revenue. International public finance can help, but it is limited. Private capital can help, but it demands returns and manageable risk. Subsidies can make access affordable, but badly designed subsidies can bankrupt utilities or disproportionately benefit customers who consume the most.

There is no universal financing trick.

There are tradeoffs.

A connection subsidy can lower the upfront barrier. A lifeline tariff can make basic use more affordable. Prepaid meters can help households manage irregular cash flow while also creating the risk that self-disconnection becomes invisible in outage statistics. Results-based finance can reward actual connections but may still miss service quality. Mini-grid subsidies can accelerate remote access while raising questions about tariffs and future integration with national networks.

The important thing is to measure the outcome that matters.

Not money spent.

Not poles erected.

Not meters installed.

Useful, reliable, affordable service.

That sounds obvious.

It is surprisingly difficult to govern by.

Infrastructure agencies are often organized around construction because construction is visible. A new substation can be photographed. A thousand new connections can be counted. Reliability improvements, voltage quality, appliance affordability, and hours of avoided fuel gathering are harder to summarize in a ribbon-cutting ceremony.

The metric shapes the institution.

If the target is connections, the system optimizes connections.

If the target is capability, the questions become richer and more demanding.

Can clinics operate critical equipment?

Can schools use digital tools when classes are in session?

Can farms pump without destroying the economics of irrigation?

Can businesses run motors without voltage damage?

Can households cook cleanly without devoting an impossible share of income to fuel?

Can the system recover after storms?

Can customers increase demand as their incomes rise without waiting years for upgrades?

This is energy abundance in practical form.

Not infinite supply.

Room to live and build without energy being the first constraint.

The remaining global access gap therefore belongs at the end of a book about civilization's growing command of energy because it prevents the history from turning into self-congratulation.

Humanity can build reactors, photovoltaic factories, continental grids, data centers, and machines that fuse nuclei for fractions of a second.

At the same time, hundreds of millions of people still lack basic electricity and billions still cook with polluting fuels.

Technical frontier and basic deprivation coexist on the same planet.

One does not make the other unreal.

The frontier tells us what is possible.

The access gap tells us what has not yet been distributed.

That gap is not proof that energy technology has failed.

It is proof that technology is only one layer of an energy system.

The rest is finance, institutions, networks, maintenance, service quality, household economics, geography, and politics.

In other words, the same things that have appeared throughout this book whenever a physical possibility had to become ordinary life.

The lesson is not that abundance is an illusion.

It is that abundance has to arrive somewhere.

A solar module in a warehouse is not light.

A generator without a line is not access.

A line without reliable voltage is not industrial power.

A connection that cannot be afforded is not full capability.

A stove no one can fuel is not clean cooking.

Every useful energy service is a chain.

Civilization advances when the chain becomes strong enough that the user can forget it is there.

The wealthy world often experiences energy this way. Lights work. Food stays cold. Pumps run. Elevators move. Servers answer. A broken link is news precisely because functioning is normal.

That normality is a form of abundance.

It should not be confused with extravagance.

Reliable basic service can be modest in energy terms and transformative in human terms.

This is perhaps the most important correction to any theory that measures civilization by total consumption.

The first task is not to maximize the meter.

It is to make useful energy dependable enough that people can build lives on top of it.

Then they can decide what more is worth doing.

The last mile is where the grand energetic story becomes personal.

Not a turbine.

Not a reactor.

Not a national target.

A light that comes on.

A refrigerator that stays cold.

A machine that finishes the job.

A meal cooked without smoke.

A bill that can be paid.

A system trusted enough that nobody has to think about it.
