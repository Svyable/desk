# The Constraint Is the Network

A power plant can exist and still be useless to the customer who needs its electricity.

The missing thing may be a wire.

This sounds too simple to matter. Electricity is supposed to be the universal carrier, the great dissolver of distance. A generator turns motion, sunlight, heat, or nuclear fission into electrical energy; the grid carries it away; a socket at the other end does not ask how the electrons were made.

But the word grid hides a physical machine.

It has voltage limits, thermal limits, stability limits, protection schemes, substations, transformers, rights of way, dispatch rules, maintenance schedules, and neighbors who may prefer that the new line run somewhere else. Electricity is wonderfully fungible only after the network has been built to move it.

That proviso is becoming the point.

For much of the twentieth century, the central problem of electricity was generation. Utilities built coal plants, hydroelectric dams, gas turbines, and nuclear stations large enough to serve growing demand, then extended networks around them. Generation was expensive, centralized, and slow enough that the rest of the system could often be planned in step.

The modern system is less orderly.

Solar farms can be developed faster than high-voltage corridors. Batteries can be ordered before a transmission upgrade clears a study queue. Wind resources are often strongest far from the cities that consume the power. Large data centers can appear on utility forecasts faster than substations can be enlarged. A region may have abundant proposed generation and abundant proposed demand, yet still be unable to connect one to the other on the dates either side wants.

The bottleneck has migrated.

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s 2026 survey of U.S. interconnection queues makes the migration visible. At the end of 2025, roughly 8,200 proposed projects representing more than 2,060 gigawatts of generation and storage were actively seeking transmission connection. The number is enormous—larger than the installed U.S. generating fleet—but it is also one of the most easily abused numbers in the energy debate.

A queue is not a power plant.

It is a request to be studied.

Most requests never become operating projects. Berkeley Lab found that only about 13 percent of the capacity entering U.S. interconnection queues from 2000 through 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025. Roughly three quarters had withdrawn. Projects that did succeed were taking longer: those reaching operation in 2025 had spent a median of just over five years between the interconnection request and commercial operation.

Those facts can support two opposite stories.

The first is that America has more generation waiting in line than it knows what to do with, and that grid connection has become a severe constraint.

The second is that the queue is full of speculative projects, duplicates, optionality, and developers testing several sites at once. If most projects were never going to be built, the queue exaggerates the amount of real generation supposedly trapped behind the network.

Both stories contain truth.

The mistake is choosing one because it is rhetorically convenient.

A project enters the queue because the developer cannot know its connection cost in advance. The network operator studies what would happen if the project connects: which lines overload, whether voltage stays within limits, whether protection equipment must change, and what upgrades are required to preserve reliability. The result may be an affordable connection. It may also be a bill so large, or a timetable so long, that the project no longer works economically.

Developers therefore submit requests under uncertainty. Some hedge by entering several queues. Some projects lose their financing, customer, land, tax credit, equipment slot, or power-purchase agreement while they wait. Others discover that the transmission upgrades assigned to them cost more than the plant itself.

Withdrawal is not proof that the project was fake.

Nor is entry proof that it was real.

The queue is better read as a market trying to discover where the network still has room.

That discovery process has become expensive.

Interconnection rules were designed for a system in which a comparatively small number of large generators arrived one at a time. In many regions, developers were studied sequentially. When an earlier project withdrew, the system operator sometimes had to restudy the projects behind it because the assumed network configuration had changed. A line of independent applications became a chain of dependent calculations.

The obvious administrative response has been to study projects in groups and demand stronger evidence that developers are serious.

FERC’s Order No. 2023 moved U.S. transmission providers toward cluster studies, firmer site-control requirements, financial deposits, study deadlines, and penalties intended to reduce speculative entries and repeated restudies. These changes matter because some of the constraint is procedural rather than physical. A better study process can connect more real projects without building a single new transmission tower.

The distinction matters.

A clogged queue does not prove that the country has run out of wires.

Sometimes it proves that the rules for deciding who gets to use the wires were built for another era.

The strongest evidence for this came from New York in 2026. Under revised interconnection rules, NYISO began accounting more accurately for the fact that new generation can serve load within the same zone rather than requiring every project to be deliverable across the wider system under more conservative assumptions. An initial analysis had identified twenty-four system deliverability upgrades. After the revised treatment, only three were expected to remain necessary, with FERC Commissioner David Rosner citing more than a billion dollars in avoided upgrade costs.

No new conductor had been strung.

The model of the network had improved.

This is a useful warning against a crude version of the chapter’s thesis. The grid can be a constraint without every grid constraint requiring a new line. Better topology, better studies, dynamic line ratings, advanced conductors, storage, demand flexibility, local generation, and more realistic assumptions can all increase the useful capacity of infrastructure that already exists.

Engineers have been finding extra room inside networks for as long as networks have existed.

Reconductoring is one example. A transmission corridor whose towers and rights of way are already in place may be able to carry substantially more power if conventional conductors are replaced with advanced ones that tolerate higher temperatures or sag less. Grid-enhancing technologies can measure real-time line conditions rather than relying on conservative static limits. Batteries can reduce peaks at constrained nodes. Industrial loads and data centers that can shift some work away from stressed hours may create capacity without changing annual energy consumption at all.

These are not minor tricks.

They reveal what the network constraint actually is.

The grid is not a fixed number of terawatt-hours. It is a time-and-place machine.

A line can be unconstrained at 3 a.m. and congested at 6 p.m. A region can have excess wind while a neighboring region burns gas because the interface between them is full. A battery can be valuable not because the country lacks energy over the year, but because power is abundant in the wrong hours. A factory may care less about the national electricity supply than about whether one substation can deliver another fifty megawatts before its equipment arrives.

This is why annual generation statistics can make an electricity system look healthier than it feels from inside it.

Energy balances add up quantities. Networks must satisfy flows.

The difference becomes more important as the generation mix changes.

A coal plant brings its fuel to the generator. A gas plant is usually built near a pipeline. A wind farm must be built where the wind is good. A solar farm benefits from land and sunlight. Hydroelectric power is attached to geography by definition. Nuclear has more siting flexibility, but still needs transmission strong enough to move a large block of output away from the plant.

As generation becomes more geographically constrained by resource quality, land, permitting, water, existing infrastructure, or public acceptance, the network has to do more of the spatial work.

Cheap generation in the wrong place is not cheap power at the socket.

The same problem appears on the demand side.

Chapter 8 described data centers whose proposed loads can reach hundreds of megawatts. Manufacturing plants, electric-vehicle charging depots, hydrogen facilities, electrified heating, and other large loads create similar questions. A utility may have plenty of annual energy available and still be unable to promise firm service at a particular site without a new transformer, feeder, substation, or transmission reinforcement.

The customer sees a delay.

The grid operator sees a reliability problem.

Both are describing the same physics.

This is why the U.S. Department of Energy’s draft 2026 National Transmission Needs Study is notable even before its conclusions are final. Released for public comment on July 9, 2026, it describes a system moving from decades of relatively modest load growth into one facing large new demands from data centers, manufacturing, industry, and electrification. The report identifies pressing transmission needs for reliability, generator interconnection, load interconnection, and congestion relief.

The word draft matters.

A consultation document is not an observed buildout, and a modeled need is not a transmission line under construction. It should not be cited as proof that a particular corridor must exist or that a forecast demand will materialize exactly as modeled.

But the study reveals what planners now think they have to solve.

Not simply generation.

Movement.

Congestion itself is slippery because it is not evenly distributed. DOE’s draft analysis finds that much of the economic pain is concentrated in a small share of hours. That concentration is encouraging in one sense: if the problem lives mainly in certain conditions, storage, flexible demand, regional coordination, or targeted upgrades may solve more than annual averages suggest.

It is discouraging in another.

The hours that matter are often the hours when the system is already under stress—cold snaps, high net load, major price divergence, or periods when weather-dependent output and demand line up badly. Infrastructure earns much of its value in moments when ordinary statistics say it is rarely used.

A transmission line that looks underutilized most afternoons may be the reason a region keeps the lights on during one brutal week.

This makes grids politically difficult to value.

The costs are visible: towers, substations, rights of way, construction crews, financing, and bills.

The avoided failure is invisible because, if the system works, nothing happens.

Reliability is a product whose best performance looks like an absence of events.

Long-distance transmission adds another complication. The best economic project may cross several utility territories, states, tribal lands, regional markets, or regulatory jurisdictions. The people who host a line may not be the people who consume most of its electricity. One region may pay while another receives much of the reliability benefit. A project that reduces system costs overall can still create local losers.

No engineering diagram can settle that allocation problem.

The grid is therefore a network of institutions as much as conductors.

Planning rules decide which future is modeled. Cost-allocation rules decide who pays. Siting law decides who can say no. Market rules determine how congestion is priced. Reliability standards define acceptable risk. Utilities, independent system operators, state regulators, federal agencies, local governments, landowners, developers, and customers each control some part of the path between a proposed line and an energized one.

A society can possess the technical ability to build transmission and still lack the institutional ability to agree on where, when, and for whom to build it.

This is one reason energy abundance cannot be understood at the generator alone.

Imagine two worlds with identical solar panels, wind turbines, batteries, reactors, and gas plants. In the first, transmission can be planned across regions, interconnection studies finish on schedule, equipment suppliers expand capacity, communities are compensated fairly, and prices give flexible users reasons to move demand away from scarce hours. In the second, every project is studied in isolation, cost responsibility is disputed for years, local opposition is ignored until litigation begins, transformer shortages appear late, and customers face no signal when the network is strained.

The technologies are the same.

The usable energy system is not.

This is where the book’s argument about control becomes more important than its argument about quantity.

Civilization does not benefit from energy merely because the energy exists somewhere.

It benefits when energy can be delivered where useful work is waiting, at the time it is needed, at a quality the machine can use, and with enough reliability that people are willing to organize important parts of life around it.

The network supplies that confidence.

It also creates dependence.

A village burning local biomass is constrained in obvious ways, but its fuel supply and point of use are close together. An electrified city can command vastly more useful energy with far greater precision, yet it depends on a sprawling chain of generators, substations, control rooms, software, transformers, transmission corridors, distribution feeders, fuel systems, and neighboring regions.

Greater capability comes with a larger coordination problem.

That does not make electrification a mistake. It makes resilience part of abundance.

A power system that is cheap on average but fragile under stress is not truly abundant. Neither is one whose generation can be built quickly but whose connections take half a decade. Neither is one with terawatts of proposed projects that cannot survive the cost of reaching a customer.

The network sets the practical frontier between available technology and usable capability.

Yet even here, the frontier moves.

Berkeley Lab’s 2026 queue data contained signs of improvement alongside the backlog. Total active queue volume fell about 10 percent during 2025 as withdrawals exceeded new requests, and several regions processed record amounts of interconnection agreements. That could mean reform is starting to work. It could also mean developers are abandoning marginal projects faster than the system is connecting strong ones.

The next several years will distinguish the two.

If cluster studies, stricter deposits, better planning, and more realistic network models materially shorten timelines while completed capacity rises, then today’s queue crisis may look temporary in retrospect. If completion rates remain low and lead times stay long even after procedural reform, the evidence for a deeper physical and institutional constraint becomes stronger.

That is what would make this chapter’s claim false, or at least smaller.

If existing lines can be expanded cheaply enough, storage and flexible demand absorb most local congestion, interconnection reform clears queues rapidly, and transmission construction keeps pace with new generation and load, then the network will stop being the binding constraint in many regions. The bottleneck will move again—to generation, capital, fuel, materials, demand, or something we have not yet named.

Bottlenecks are not eternal truths.

They are where a system fails to keep up with its fastest-moving part.

For the moment, generation technology is moving quickly. Solar modules can be manufactured by the millions. Batteries come off factory lines. Gas turbines, reactors, wind farms, and geothermal projects all have their own constraints, but the menu of ways to make electricity is broad.

The wire remains stubbornly specific.

It has to go somewhere.

That specificity is what makes networks easy to ignore in stories about technological progress. A new generator has a nameplate capacity. A new battery has a chemistry. A reactor has a design. They look like inventions.

A transmission line looks like infrastructure.

But infrastructure is where invention becomes civilization.

The next question is whether one of the oldest ways of making large quantities of electricity—splitting heavy atoms—can return as part of a system now demanding more firm power, more reliability, and more ways around the network’s limits.
