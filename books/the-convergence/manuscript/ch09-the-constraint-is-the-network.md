# The Constraint Is the Network

A power plant can be finished and still be useless to the customer waiting for its electricity.

The missing thing may be a wire. Or a transformer. Or a study showing that the wire and transformer already there can survive one more large connection without overheating, destabilizing the system, or shifting the problem to somebody downstream.

Chapter 8 ended with a model that can be copied in seconds and a substation that cannot. The same mismatch reaches far beyond artificial intelligence. Solar modules, batteries, gas turbines, wind turbines, factories, charging depots, and data centers can all arrive on schedules that the network was not designed to match. Electricity may be a universal carrier, but it is only universal after the machinery for carrying it has been built.

The word grid makes this machinery sound cleaner than it is. The actual object is an accumulation of conductors, towers, transformers, substations, protection equipment, dispatch rules, software, easements, maintenance schedules, markets, and arguments over who pays. A national annual total can say that plenty of electricity exists while a particular industrial site is told that firm service will take years.

This is not a contradiction. Energy balances add quantities. Networks have to satisfy flows in particular places at particular times.

That distinction is becoming harder to ignore.

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s 2026 edition of *Queued Up* found about 8,200 proposed U.S. generation and storage projects actively seeking transmission interconnection at the end of 2025. Together they represented 1,312 gigawatts of generation and about 749 gigawatts of storage. More than two terawatts waiting in line is an irresistible statistic. It is also a dangerous one.

A queue is not a fleet of nearly finished power plants being kept behind a velvet rope.

It is a set of requests to be studied.

Most requests do not become operating projects. Berkeley Lab found that only about 13 percent of the capacity entering queues from 2000 through 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025, while roughly three quarters had withdrawn. Projects that did succeed were taking longer: those reaching commercial operation in 2025 had spent a median of a little more than five years between the initial interconnection request and operation.

The same 2026 update also contains evidence against the most dramatic reading of the backlog. Active queue volume fell about 10 percent in 2025, and the laboratory noted signs that the historical backlog was easing as withdrawals remained high and more projects obtained interconnection agreements.

So what does two terawatts in the queue actually tell us?

Less than the headline suggests and more than skeptics sometimes allow.

Developers submit projects before they know the full cost of connecting them. The system operator studies what the proposed connection would do to the network: which lines would overload, how voltage would behave, whether protection systems would need to change, and what upgrades would be required to preserve reliability. A project that looked economical on a map may receive an upgrade bill that destroys the economics. Another may lose financing, land, a customer, a tax credit, an equipment slot, or a power-purchase agreement while the studies continue. Developers also have reason to preserve options by submitting alternatives in more than one place.

Withdrawal therefore does not prove that a project was fake. Entry does not prove that it was real.

The queue is better understood as a market discovering where the network still has room, and often discovering it slowly.

That slowness has a history. Interconnection procedures were built for a system in which comparatively few large generators arrived one at a time. Studying requests sequentially worked tolerably when the line was short. It works badly when hundreds of projects interact. If an early project withdraws, the electrical assumptions for projects behind it can change, forcing another round of studies. A queue that looks administratively linear becomes electrically entangled.

This is why reform can sometimes create capacity without pouring concrete.

FERC’s Order No. 2023 pushed transmission providers toward cluster studies, stronger site-control and readiness requirements, larger deposits, deadlines, and penalties intended to reduce speculative entries and repeated restudies. The premise is not that paperwork caused every delay. It is that a physical network can be constrained by a bad decision process as well as by a missing conductor.

New York supplied an unusually vivid example in 2026.

NYISO’s older interconnection approach did not fully account for electricity demand within a zone when testing whether new generation could be delivered across the wider system. Its initial cluster analysis identified twenty-four system deliverability upgrades. After NYISO revised the method to model generation and load within zones more realistically, only three of those upgrades were expected to remain necessary. In a July 20 concurrence, FERC Commissioner David Rosner said the change would avoid more than a billion dollars in upgrade costs.

Nothing about the copper had changed.

The model of how the copper would be used had.

This is the strongest counterargument to the chapter’s title. The network can be the constraint without every apparent network constraint requiring a new line. Better studies, better operating assumptions, reconductoring, dynamic line ratings, storage, topology changes, flexible demand, and local generation can all increase the useful capacity of infrastructure that already exists.

That matters because transmission is expensive in ways that a module factory is not. A new conductor may need an existing corridor, a new right of way, agreement among several utilities, permits from multiple levels of government, cost allocation across beneficiaries who disagree about how much they benefit, and acceptance from communities that may see towers more clearly than they see lower congestion costs.

The result is that the cheapest generation is not always the cheapest delivered electricity.

A solar project in a high-resource area can produce low-cost energy and still impose expensive network needs. A wind project can sit where the wind is excellent and the transmission is weak. A nuclear plant can generate around the clock and still require a strong network to move a large block of power away from a single site. A data center can locate near cheap generation and discover that the local substation cannot provide the requested capacity on schedule.

The grid is a time-and-place machine.

A transmission interface may be unconstrained most of the year and critical during a few ugly hours. A battery can be valuable not because the country lacks energy over twelve months, but because electricity is abundant at noon and scarce after sunset. A factory may care less about national terawatt-hours than whether one feeder can deliver another fifty megawatts before its equipment arrives.

The U.S. Department of Energy’s draft 2026 *National Transmission Needs Study* makes this temporal shape unusually explicit. Released July 9 for public comment, the study says that most measured transmission congestion is concentrated in about 5 percent of hours, especially periods with high net load, cold weather, large day-ahead to real-time price differences, and high intermittent generation.

That finding cuts in two directions.

If the problem is concentrated, flexible demand, batteries, regional coordination, better controls, or a targeted upgrade may solve more than annual statistics imply. But the hours that matter are often exactly the hours when the system is already under stress. Infrastructure can look underused on an average day and still earn most of its value during one severe week.

Reliability has an odd commercial problem: when it works, the product is an absence.

Nothing trips. Nothing freezes. No one discovers the emergency transformer lead time during the emergency.

The same DOE study identifies pressing transmission needs associated with reliability, generator interconnection, new load interconnection, and congestion relief. It also points to the potential value of additional interregional transfer capability. But the word draft matters. As of late August 2026, the public-comment period is still open, through September 7. This is a current planning assessment, not proof that every modeled need will become a line or that every forecast load will arrive.

The distinction between a need, an approval, and a completed asset is easy to lose because each belongs to a different clock.

A region can approve a large portfolio of transmission and still spend years engineering, permitting, financing, procuring, and building it. A generator can sign an interconnection agreement and still fail to reach commercial operation. A transformer can be ordered long before anyone sees it on a substation pad. The electricity system is full of projects that are real in one institutional sense and nonexistent in a physical one.

Transformers make this abstraction embarrassingly concrete.

They are among the least glamorous machines in the economy and among the hardest to route around when missing. Transformers change voltage so electricity can move efficiently across long distances and then step back down toward useful levels. Large units are engineered pieces of capital equipment, not warehouse commodities. Distribution transformers are more standardized, but shortages can still spread delays across housing, commercial construction, utility upgrades, and industrial connections.

DOE supply-chain work documented distribution-transformer lead times expanding from roughly three to six months in 2019 to roughly twelve to thirty months in 2023. That evidence is not a 2026 spot quote and should not be treated as one. It does, however, show how quickly an ordinary component can become the pacing item when demand, materials, manufacturing capacity, and utility procurement all tighten at once.

There is a useful lesson in the transformer because it corrects the way technology stories are usually told.

The spectacular component receives the attention: the turbine blade, the battery cell, the chip, the reactor core. The system often waits for something duller. Switchgear. A breaker. A right of way. A protection study. A transformer large enough to fit the load and small enough to arrive before the business case expires.

Civilization advances through systems, and systems are paced by their least substitutable constraint.

Those constraints are not always physical.

A long-distance transmission project can cross utility territories, states, tribal lands, regional markets, farms, suburbs, and ecosystems. The people hosting the line may not be the people receiving most of the electricity. One state may see towers while another sees lower prices. A project can reduce total system costs and still create legitimate local losers.

No load-flow model can decide how those losses should be allocated.

This is why the grid is also a network of institutions. Planning rules decide which futures count. Cost-allocation rules decide who pays. Siting law decides who can delay or reject a route. Reliability standards define acceptable risk. Market design determines how congestion appears in prices. Utilities, grid operators, state regulators, federal agencies, local governments, landowners, developers, and customers each hold part of the path between a proposal and an energized asset.

Calling every delay “permitting” is therefore as sloppy as calling every queue entry a power plant. Some delays are bureaucratic. Some reflect obsolete procedures. Some are the ordinary engineering consequence of changing one piece of an interconnected machine. Some are politics over who benefits and who bears the cost. Some are communities exercising the power to resist infrastructure whose national value does not erase its local footprint.

Speed matters, but so does the thing being sped through.

This is also why a simple build-more-transmission answer is incomplete. Existing corridors can sometimes carry more with advanced conductors. Dynamic ratings can reveal additional capacity when weather conditions permit. Batteries can reduce peaks at constrained nodes. Flexible industrial loads can shift away from scarce hours. Data centers may be able to schedule some training jobs when power is plentiful. Better siting can place new demand where the system has headroom rather than where tax policy or real estate alone looks attractive.

Each option changes the cost of the network constraint.

None abolishes the need for a network.

Imagine two regions with identical power plants and identical annual electricity production. In one, interconnection studies are predictable, transmission is planned across a wide area, operators can see the system well enough to use existing assets efficiently, equipment supply is dependable, and flexible customers face incentives to consume when capacity is available. In the other, projects are studied one by one under unrealistic assumptions, upgrades are assigned unpredictably, neighboring regions plan in isolation, and large customers are indifferent to when and where they consume.

The generation fleet is the same. The amount of useful electricity is not.

This moves the book’s argument another step away from raw energy totals.

Fire mattered because humans could control it. Agriculture mattered partly because energy could be stored and governed. Coal mattered because geological time became available on demand. Electricity mattered because power could be routed with extraordinary precision. The network is what makes that precision social rather than local.

It is also what makes modern abundance dependent on coordination at enormous scale.

A village burning local biomass is tightly constrained, but its fuel source and point of use are close together. An electrified city can command far more useful energy with far greater control, while relying on generators, transmission corridors, substations, control rooms, fuel systems, markets, software, maintenance crews, and neighboring regions it never sees.

Greater capability creates a larger coordination problem.

That is not an argument against electrification. It is an argument for including resilience, delivery, and institutional competence in any serious definition of abundance.

A kilowatt-hour produced somewhere is not yet a capability. It becomes one when it can reach the machine that needs it, at the voltage it can use, at the moment it is needed, often enough that people are willing to organize important parts of life around the expectation that it will be there.

The grid supplies that expectation.

For now, this makes the network an increasingly visible constraint in many fast-growing parts of the electricity system. If interconnection reforms work, if reconductoring and storage release large amounts of existing capacity, if transmission construction accelerates, and if load growth disappoints current forecasts, the bottleneck may move again. It could return to generation, fuel, capital, materials, or demand.

Constraints migrate.

The important question is not whether the grid is the final bottleneck. There is no final bottleneck.

The question is what becomes scarce after the previous scarcity is solved.

In the next chapter, one old answer returns to the center of the debate. When a power system wants large amounts of firm, low-carbon electricity, nuclear fission offers a technically mature source whose hardest constraints often lie not in physics, but in cost, construction, finance, and institutions.
