# The Constraint Is the Network

A transformer is not an impressive machine to look at.

It does not glow. It has no moving blades. It does not announce a new era. Most people encounter one as a gray box behind a fence or a cylinder on a pole and spend no time wondering what it does.

That indifference is a sign of success.

A transformer changes voltage so electricity can move through one part of the grid and become useful in another. When enough of them exist in the right places, along with wires, substations, breakers, relays, control systems, and operators, electricity feels less like an industrial process than a property of the wall.

Then one is missing.

The project can have land. It can have financing. The solar modules can be in a warehouse. The data-center servers can be on order. The utility can agree that the load will eventually be served.

Eventually is doing a lot of work.

The U.S. Department of Energy says lead times for distribution transformers rose from roughly three to six months in 2019 to twelve to thirty months in 2023. Some of that was pandemic disruption. Some was material scarcity and labor. But DOE also found something less dramatic and, in its own way, more revealing: American utilities use more than 80,000 varieties of distribution transformer.

Scarcity can be a factory problem. It can also be a specification problem.

A power system is full of these moments where the limiting object is not the glamorous technology but the unglamorous prerequisite.

The previous chapter ended with an AI model waiting on a transformer. That is a useful place to begin because the same logic applies far beyond computation.

A new generator is valuable only if it can connect. A new load is useful only if it can be served. A battery matters only if power can reach it and leave it. A cheap wind farm behind a constrained transmission line may be less useful than a more expensive generator near demand. A heat pump cannot benefit from abundant generation if the local distribution system cannot carry the peak load.

Electricity is not merely generated. It is delivered.

That distinction is becoming more important as the machines on both ends of the network improve faster than the network between them.

Solar modules can be manufactured in months. Batteries arrive by the container. Data-center campuses can be planned at scales that would have looked like industrial curiosities a decade ago. New gas turbines, nuclear plants, transmission lines, substations, and distribution upgrades move on different clocks.

The clocks have to meet.

Berkeley Lab's 2026 interconnection data give one view of the mismatch. At the end of 2025, about 8,200 projects were actively seeking transmission interconnection in the United States. Together they represented 1,312 gigawatts of proposed generation and about 749 gigawatts of storage.

The number is enormous enough to be dangerous.

It is tempting to read two terawatts in a queue as two terawatts of future power waiting behind paperwork. That would be wrong.

Most queued projects do not get built. Berkeley Lab found that only about 13 percent of capacity entering interconnection queues from 2000 through 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025. Roughly 75 percent had withdrawn, while another 10 percent remained active.

A queue is partly a pipeline and partly a graveyard.

Projects enter for many reasons. Developers test several sites. They discover upgrade costs they cannot justify. Financing changes. Tax policy changes. Equipment costs move. Local permits fail. Power-purchase agreements disappear. Some applications are speculative. Some represent alternatives that cannot all be built.

Gross queue capacity therefore does not measure future generation.

It measures demand for a scarce interface.

That interface has become slow. For projects reaching commercial operation in 2025 in regions with usable duration data, Berkeley Lab found a median period of more than five years from interconnection request to operation.

Five years is not all utility study time. Development, permitting, construction, procurement, financing, and other steps overlap with the interconnection process. Still, the number captures something the headline queue capacity does not.

Time is part of infrastructure.

A power system can contain ample energy in annual totals and still fail to place enough capacity at the right node by the required year. It can have cheap generators and expensive congestion. It can have a national surplus and a local shortage. It can have steel, copper, transformers, rights of way, permits, workers, and capital, yet have them in the wrong sequence.

Networks turn abundance into a coordination problem.

This is why electricity differs from many other commodities. A barrel of oil can sit in a tank until someone wants it. A truck can choose another road. Data packets can often be rerouted dynamically through many paths.

Power obeys Kirchhoff before it obeys a contract.

Electricity follows the physical network. Flows redistribute according to impedance. Transmission lines have thermal and stability limits. Voltage must stay within bounds. Generators and loads affect one another through a synchronized system whose failures can propagate faster than committees can convene.

You cannot fix a congested line by asking electrons to be reasonable.

The result is a peculiar kind of scarcity. The system may not be short of fuel or even generating equipment. It can be short of transfer capability at a particular hour.

DOE's draft 2026 National Transmission Needs Study makes that timing visible. Its congestion analysis finds that the majority of measured transmission congestion is concentrated in roughly 5 percent of hours, particularly during periods of high net load, cold weather, large day-ahead versus real-time price differences, and high variable generation.

That is not evidence that the grid is fine 95 percent of the time.

It is evidence that the expensive problem is concentrated.

Many infrastructures are built for their hardest hours. A bridge that is adequate except when commuters use it is not especially adequate. A storm sewer that works except in storms has misunderstood its occupation.

The power grid is similar, with a complication: the critical hours move.

A hot summer evening can strain air-conditioning load. A winter cold snap can raise heating demand while affecting generators and fuel supply. A midday solar surplus can be followed by a steep evening ramp. A large industrial customer can arrive in a region faster than the transmission plan that would have anticipated it.

The annual average conceals the hour when the network earns its keep.

This also explains why plant-level cost curves can mislead when they escape into public argument.

A solar farm may have a low levelized cost. A battery may be falling rapidly in price. A gas plant may provide dispatchable capacity. A nuclear plant may run at a high capacity factor. None of those facts says what another unit is worth at a particular node in a particular hour.

Value depends on the system around the machine.

When the network is roomy, generators compete mainly on the cost and qualities of generation. When the network becomes congested, location becomes part of the product. A megawatt near a constrained load is not economically identical to a megawatt hundreds of miles away behind a saturated corridor.

Distance re-enters the price.

There is a parallel on the demand side.

The data center that can move to a region with available generation and transmission may be easier to serve than the one committed to a specific metropolitan market. An industrial plant that can alter production hours may impose less system cost than an equally large load that must run continuously. An EV fleet that charges overnight may interact with the grid differently from one that piles onto the evening peak.

Demand is not simply a quantity either. It has geography and time.

This is where flexible load begins to look like infrastructure.

A battery can move electricity through time. Demand response can move consumption through time. Transmission moves electricity through space. Grid-enhancing technologies can sometimes increase how much an existing line safely carries. Reconductoring can replace a conductor without acquiring an entirely new corridor. Better interconnection studies can identify upgrades more accurately. Storage can absorb production where transmission would otherwise be constrained.

None of these is a universal substitute for new wires.

That matters because "build more transmission" can become another slogan.

Some places clearly need more transfer capacity. DOE's 2026 draft study identifies substantial regional and interregional needs, and several system operators have approved unusually large transmission portfolios. But a constraint should be diagnosed before it is prescribed.

If congestion occurs in a few recurring hours, storage or flexible demand may sometimes be cheaper than a large expansion built primarily for those hours. If a generator queue is clogged by speculative or duplicate applications, process reform can matter. If a connection study assumes an unnecessarily expensive network upgrade, better modeling can change the answer. If the delay is a transformer factory slot, a transmission line alone will not help.

The right question is not, "What infrastructure do we like?"

It is, "What determines the earliest feasible operating date?"

That question is less romantic and more useful.

It also makes counterevidence easier to see.

The network is not the binding constraint everywhere. A project can wait years for a connection and still fail later because its financing collapses. A transmission line can be approved while the generators it was meant to serve withdraw. A region can have available network capacity and no economic project ready to use it. A data center can obtain power and then be delayed by chips, cooling equipment, local construction, or customer demand.

Bottlenecks migrate.

The book has encountered this before. Better steam engines increased the value of coal. Electrification increased the value of generation and networks. Efficiency lowered the cost of services and sometimes expanded demand. Cheap renewable generation increases the value of transmission, storage, flexibility, and market coordination because more low-cost energy is only useful if the system can move it to a place and time that needs it.

A solved constraint exposes the next one.

That does not mean every new bottleneck is proof of progress. Bad institutions can manufacture scarcity. Permitting can be slow without being careful. Planning rules can preserve assumptions long after the technology changes. Cost-allocation fights can make rational projects wait because nobody agrees who should pay. Local opposition can block lines whose benefits are broad while their physical footprint is narrow.

Some delay protects legitimate interests. Some delay is simply delay.

The difficult part is telling the two apart.

Transmission is a useful case because its benefits and burdens are distributed unevenly. A line may lower prices and improve reliability across several states while crossing farms, forests, towns, and tribal lands that experience the construction directly. The people asked to host infrastructure are not irrational for noticing that everyone else's system benefit arrives in their field.

A civilization that wants more network capacity has to solve political geography as well as electrical engineering.

This is one reason lines take longer than software.

Rights of way are physical. Environmental review is physical. Land ownership is physical. Community consent, state authority, federal authority, utility territories, cost allocation, and regional planning all meet on a map.

The electrons do not care which regulator approved the line. The line very much does.

The transformer story contains the same lesson at a smaller scale.

DOE's count of more than 80,000 distribution-transformer varieties is not merely trivia about utility procurement. It suggests that some supply-chain scarcity is embedded in accumulated institutional choices. Utilities optimized equipment for their own standards, histories, voltages, mounting practices, accessories, and maintenance routines. Individually reasonable decisions can produce a collectively difficult market to manufacture for.

Standardization can create capacity without adding a factory.

But even that has a counterargument. Specifications exist for reasons. Climate differs. Network configurations differ. Utility practices differ. A universal component that ignores those differences may be cheaper to order and worse to operate.

The useful target is not sameness. It is unnecessary variety.

That distinction belongs to a larger theme in infrastructure: slack and redundancy often look inefficient until the day they are needed.

A grid built only for average conditions would be cheap and terrible. A transformer inventory sized to perfect forecasts would fail whenever the forecast did. A transmission network with no spare capacity would turn every new factory into an emergency.

Resilience is partly the art of paying for capacity before the spreadsheet can prove exactly when it will earn a return.

Too much slack is waste. Too little becomes fragility. Infrastructure lives in that uncomfortable interval.

The same is true of planning under uncertainty.

Electric utilities once had relatively slow-moving demand growth. That made long planning cycles tolerable. Now some of the fastest-growing loads arrive in blocks of hundreds of megawatts and can change plans quickly. At the same time, generation is changing location as wind, solar, storage, gas, nuclear, and industrial demand compete for network access.

The old grid was not designed for today's map because today's map did not exist.

No planner can know the next map perfectly either.

That uncertainty argues against both paralysis and overconfidence. Build too little and useful projects wait. Build too much in the wrong places and customers pay for stranded capacity. The goal is not a flawless forecast. It is a system that can adapt faster when the forecast is wrong.

That is a different definition of infrastructure quality.

Speed matters. So does optionality.

A network with several feasible paths can absorb surprises better than one dependent on a single corridor. Standard equipment can be replaced more easily than bespoke equipment. Interregional links can allow regions to help one another during different peaks. Storage and flexible load can create time for slower physical expansions to arrive.

The most valuable network may be the one that leaves the next planner several good choices.

That creates a planning problem that is easy to miss when every investment must be justified by a project already standing in line.

Reactive planning sounds prudent because it waits for evidence. A factory requests service, a generator requests interconnection, congestion appears, and only then does the system build the upgrade. The trouble is that large network assets often arrive on a slower clock than the projects asking for them. By the time demand becomes undeniable, the infrastructure needed to serve it may already be late.

The opposite approach has its own danger. Building ahead of demand can produce stranded assets, political backlash, and bills for customers who never receive the promised benefit. Forecasts are fallible. Developers change plans. Technologies move. A corridor built for one future can be poorly placed for the future that actually arrives.

Anticipatory capacity therefore cannot mean guessing confidently and pouring concrete.

It means treating some network investments as options rather than as answers to a single forecast. A larger substation site can leave room for another transformer. A corridor can preserve the possibility of another circuit. Equipment standards can make future expansion less bespoke. Planning can test several plausible demand and generation patterns instead of optimizing around one official projection.

The point is not to build every possible upgrade early. It is to notice when a small decision now preserves a much cheaper decision later.

This is where cost allocation becomes more than an accounting fight.

A narrowly assigned upgrade charge can make sense when one project clearly causes one local need. It becomes harder when the upgrade creates capacity that several future users may share, improves reliability beyond the first customer, or changes the feasible geography of later projects. The first entrant can end up being asked to pay for infrastructure whose benefits will outlive it.

That can create a rational reason to wait.

If every developer hopes someone else will trigger the network expansion first, a system can remain underbuilt even when many participants would benefit from the finished asset. The coordination problem then resembles other public infrastructure: the value is collective, the initiating decision is local, and the timing of who pays can determine whether anything happens.

There is no universal solution. Socializing too much cost can reward speculative projects and shift risk onto customers. Assigning too much cost to the first mover can suppress useful investment. The design problem is to make beneficiaries bear costs without pretending that network benefits always belong to one beneficiary.

This is not a side issue to energy abundance.

It is one of the mechanisms that decides whether abundance is allowed to arrive before the queue proves, one project at a time, that it was needed.

This shifts the meaning of energy abundance.

Abundance is often pictured as a field of solar panels, a nuclear plant, a geothermal well, or some future machine producing huge amounts of power. Generation is the visible symbol because generation makes the energy.

Networks make the energy available.

That second verb is quieter but decisive.

A civilization can know how to generate cheap electricity and still ration connection dates. It can have terawatts of proposed projects and spend years deciding which can interconnect. It can manufacture efficient machines faster than it upgrades the wires feeding them.

At that point the scarce resource is not necessarily energy.

It is coordinated capacity.

That means studies completed in sequence. Transformers manufactured to useful specifications. Rights of way assembled. Costs allocated. Protection systems designed. Construction crews scheduled. Local concerns negotiated. Operators trained. Equipment energized in an order that keeps the rest of the system reliable.

None of those tasks sounds like a breakthrough.

Together they determine whether breakthroughs arrive.

The progression matters for the argument of this book. Humanity learned to release energy, store it, mine it, convert it, route it, and use it more efficiently. Each gain widened the set of things that could be attempted.

As that set widens, coordination becomes more valuable because more machines are competing to join the same systems.

The network is where possibilities become compatible.

That is why the constraint can move from the spectacular object to the boring one.

The solar module falls in price. The server improves. The battery factory scales. The reactor design matures.

And the schedule waits for a transformer.

The deepest infrastructure problem is not that society has run out of technology. It is that technologies arrive on different clocks.

The network has to make those clocks agree.

That is also why the next question is not simply how much more electricity we can generate.

It is what happens when the cost of useful energy falls far enough that energy itself stops being the first reason to say no.
