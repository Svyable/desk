# The Constraint Is the Network

At the end of 2025, the United States had more than two terawatts of proposed generation and storage waiting in transmission interconnection queues.

The number is so large that it invites the wrong conclusion. It does not mean two terawatts of new power plants are about to appear. Berkeley Lab’s 2026 *Queued Up* analysis found about 8,200 active projects representing 1,312 gigawatts of generation and 749 gigawatts of storage. Yet only 13 percent of the capacity that entered queues from 2000 through 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025. Three quarters had withdrawn. Projects that did get built in 2025 had spent a median of more than five years moving from interconnection request to commercial operation in the regions where the laboratory had sufficient data.

The queue is not the grid.

It is closer to a map of ambition meeting a network that was not designed to absorb ambition at this speed.

For much of the history told in this book, the hard problem was obtaining energy. Fire made stored chemical energy controllable. Agriculture concentrated solar flows into harvests and animals. Coal opened a geological stockpile. Steam converted heat into mechanical work. Electricity made power transmissible and divisible. In the current system, the cost of producing another unit of electricity can sometimes be surprisingly low. Solar modules, wind turbines, batteries, gas turbines, and other generating equipment can be ordered from mature industries. The harder question is increasingly whether the system around them can accept, move, balance, and deliver what they produce.

A power plant without a connection is not a power plant in the economic sense that matters. It is equipment waiting for a network.

Interconnection is the process that turns a proposed generator into a participant in that network. A developer submits a request. The grid operator studies how the project would affect power flows and reliability. Engineers identify upgrades that may be required: a new substation, reconductored line, transformer, protection equipment, or larger transmission reinforcement. Costs are assigned. Studies are revised as other projects enter or leave the queue. Financing and permits have to survive the wait.

The process exists for a reason. Electricity does not behave like freight placed on a road and directed along a chosen lane. Power flows through an interconnected AC network according to physical laws. Adding a large generator in one location can alter flows elsewhere. A project that looks local on a map can create thermal, voltage, stability, or fault-duty problems on equipment far away. The studies are not bureaucratic decoration. They are one of the ways a tightly coupled machine protects itself from being modified carelessly.

But a necessary process can still become a bottleneck.

The latest Berkeley Lab data make that bottleneck visible. As of the end of 2025, solar remained the largest generation technology in active U.S. queues at 773 gigawatts, followed by 253 gigawatts of natural gas and 220 gigawatts of wind, alongside 749 gigawatts of storage. Total active queue volume actually fell about 10 percent during 2025 because withdrawals remained high and fewer new requests entered. Even after that decline, the backlog was enormous relative to the existing power system.

A more revealing number is 549 gigawatts. That much queued capacity already had a draft or executed interconnection agreement by the end of 2025 but had not yet reached commercial operation. An agreement is not a turbine turning or a battery charging. Construction, financing, equipment delivery, permitting, and other work still stand between paper and electrons.

This is why queue capacity must be handled carefully. A headline that says “two terawatts waiting to connect” can sound like a forecast. It is not. Many projects are competing alternatives for the same market opportunity. Some are speculative. Some will become uneconomic when upgrade costs arrive. Some will lose financing or permits. Some will be replaced by later projects. The high withdrawal rate is not a footnote to the queue data; it is part of what the queue is.

Yet dismissing the queue because most projects withdraw would make the opposite mistake. The backlog records a real mismatch between the speed at which developers can propose resources and the speed at which the network can study, upgrade, and connect them. When projects that eventually succeed take more than five years to move through the process, connection time becomes part of the economics of generation.

The cheap machine can wait for the expensive complement.

This pattern appears elsewhere in technology. A fast processor is useless without memory and data movement fast enough to feed it. A port can own enormous cranes and still be constrained by rail capacity leaving the terminal. A factory can add machines until the slowest stage determines output. Complex systems are governed less by the abundance of their best component than by the scarcity of whatever complement cannot be substituted away.

Electricity makes this unusually literal.

Generation, transmission, distribution, storage, fuel, balancing, protection, transformers, and demand all have to meet in real time. Improving one layer moves pressure toward another. Build generation faster than transmission and congestion grows. Build transmission without enough firm resources and reliability can still fail. Add storage without enough charging energy or network access and the battery cannot solve the missing constraint. Add large loads faster than generation and wires can serve them and the bottleneck moves to resource adequacy.

The constraint migrates.

That migration is now visible from both sides of the meter. Chapter 8 followed data centers whose developers may have chips, capital, land, and customers before they have a date when hundreds of megawatts can be energized. On the supply side, generation developers can have panels, turbines, batteries, land rights, and financing before the grid can tell them what connection will cost and when it can occur.

The network sits between two industries that can move faster than it does.

In July 2026, the U.S. Department of Energy released a draft *National Transmission Needs Study* that described a sharp change from the long period of relatively stagnant electricity demand. The study points to data centers, domestic manufacturing, large industrial loads, and electrification as drivers of new transmission need. It also identifies congestion that is concentrated rather than uniform: much of the observed congestion occurs in a small share of hours, especially around high net load, cold weather, large day-ahead to real-time price differences, and periods of high intermittent generation.

That detail matters. A grid can look adequate most of the year and still be constrained when the system most needs flexibility.

Transmission is built for difficult hours, not average ones.

The same logic applies to reliability. A city does not need a water system sized only for its average minute if morning peaks, fires, and equipment failures matter. An electrical system cannot be planned around annual energy totals alone. It needs enough capacity, transfer capability, and operational flexibility for the hours when demand is high, resources are unavailable, weather is severe, or a line or generator has failed.

This is why the grid is not merely a delivery mechanism attached after generation. It is part of the productive capability of the energy system itself.

A megawatt in the wrong place at the wrong hour may be much less useful than a megawatt that can reach the load. A cheap generator behind a congested interface can be curtailed while a more expensive generator closer to demand runs. A battery can be extremely valuable where it relieves a local constraint and less valuable where the network is already uncongested. Transmission can allow regions with different weather, demand patterns, and generation fleets to share resources, reducing the amount of local redundancy each would otherwise need.

Location and time change the value of the same electricity.

The draft DOE study argues that additional interregional transmission can provide reliability, resilience, and congestion benefits, particularly where regions experience different system conditions. This is not a new insight. Power systems have long benefited from interconnection. What is changing is the scale of the question. A grid built around twentieth-century load growth and generation geography is being asked to connect new generators in resource-rich areas while serving large new loads that may appear faster than traditional planning cycles expected.

The network has memory. Its routes reflect old power plants, old cities, old industrial patterns, old regulatory boundaries, and decades of incremental investment.

New energy technologies do not arrive on a blank map.

A solar project goes where sunlight, land, interconnection, permitting, and economics line up. Wind follows resource quality and land. Nuclear plants require sites that satisfy different engineering and regulatory conditions. Data centers care about fiber, land, tax policy, water or cooling conditions, and power. Manufacturing clusters around labor, logistics, suppliers, and incentives. The resulting geography of supply and demand can diverge from the geography embedded in the existing grid.

Transmission is the negotiation between those maps.

It is also slow for reasons that cannot be reduced to one villain. Large lines cross jurisdictions and properties. Planning has to decide which future loads and generators are credible. Cost allocation asks who should pay when a line benefits many customers differently over decades. Permitting and siting involve communities that bear local effects even when benefits are regional. Equipment has to be manufactured. Skilled labor has to be available. Utilities and system operators must coordinate construction with an operating grid that cannot simply be turned off while it is rebuilt.

Some delay is the price of legitimate scrutiny. Some is institutional fragmentation. Some is engineering. Some is supply chain. The proportions differ by project.

That is why “permitting” is too small a word for the network constraint.

Consider the transformer. It is not an emblem of the energy transition. It has no visible flame, spinning blade, or futuristic interface. Most people pass transformers every day without thinking about them. Yet the grid cannot use electricity at arbitrary voltage. Transformers step voltage up for efficient transmission and down again for distribution and end use. Large power transformers are specialized industrial equipment; distribution transformers exist in enormous numbers across local networks.

When transformer supply tightens, the glamorous parts of the system wait.

DOE’s Office of Electricity has documented how distribution-transformer lead times expanded from roughly three to six months in 2019 to roughly twelve to thirty months in 2023 during recent supply-chain stress. The dates matter: this is not a 2026 measurement of current lead time, and conditions can change. But the episode demonstrates the structure of the problem. A relatively old technology can become the pacing item for a system filled with newer ones.

The binding constraint does not have to be technologically exciting.

It only has to be scarce.

The same is true of transmission conductors, breakers, switchgear, substations, engineering studies, line crews, rights of way, and regulatory attention. A civilization can possess the scientific knowledge to generate cheap electricity while lacking the industrial and institutional throughput to build the network around it quickly.

That distinction becomes important whenever technology forecasts assume that cost decline in one component automatically scales the whole system.

Solar modules offer the clearest example. Decades of improvements in efficiency, manufacturing, materials, scale, and supply chains drove module costs downward. But a solar plant is not a module price multiplied by the number of modules. It includes land, structures, inverters, labor, financing, interconnection, and often storage or network upgrades. As the module becomes cheaper, the non-module parts occupy a larger share of the project’s economics.

Success changes what matters.

This is a recurring pattern in engineering. Reduce one source of loss and another becomes visible. Accelerate one stage of production and the next stage becomes the bottleneck. Make computation cheap and memory bandwidth matters more. Make generation cheap and the network matters more.

The mistake is to interpret the new bottleneck as evidence that the earlier improvement was unimportant. The opposite is often true. Constraints migrate because something else stopped being the dominant constraint.

The American interconnection queue is therefore evidence of both progress and failure.

It is evidence of progress because developers can plausibly propose enormous amounts of new generation and storage. Technologies and capital markets have made that possible. It is evidence of failure because the surrounding institutions and infrastructure cannot convert proposals into operating capacity at the same pace.

Those two facts coexist.

FERC has tried to change the process. Order No. 2023, issued in 2023, shifted generator interconnection toward cluster studies and introduced other reforms intended to reduce backlog and improve certainty. The 2026 Berkeley Lab report notes that Order 2023 and related reforms are being implemented but says it is still too early to measure their full effect.

That is an important discipline for this chapter. A new rule is not an observed outcome.

The same distinction applies to transmission planning. FERC’s Order No. 1920 series requires longer-term regional transmission planning and changes how planners consider future needs and allocate costs. Regional operators have also begun approving unusually large portfolios. DOE’s 2026 draft Needs Study points to MISO’s 2024 approval of $21.8 billion in transmission investment, including a 765-kilovolt backbone, and to Southwest Power Pool’s identification of $7.7 billion in investments with more than 2,000 new and upgraded transmission lines.

These are commitments and plans, not completed wires.

The physical grid is built in steel, aluminum, copper, concrete, ceramics, electronics, and cleared rights of way. Financial approval is a necessary step toward that grid, not the grid itself.

This difference between planned and operating infrastructure is easy to lose in an era of announcements. Gigawatts are announced. Billions of dollars are approved. Queue positions are filed. Factories are proposed. The future can look physically present on a spreadsheet years before the equipment carries current.

Energy systems punish that confusion because their service is delivered only by assets that actually operate together.

The strongest counterargument to the network-constraint thesis is straightforward: perhaps today’s queues and congestion are temporary administrative artifacts rather than a durable physical bottleneck. Interconnection rules were built for a different era. Developers have incentives to submit multiple projects. Queue reform can remove speculative requests. Existing lines can be reconductored. Grid-enhancing technologies can increase transfer capability without waiting for entirely new corridors. Batteries and flexible demand can reduce some peaks. Distributed generation can sometimes avoid bulk transmission. Better forecasting and software can use existing infrastructure more efficiently.

All of that is true enough to narrow the claim.

The grid is not always the constraint, and “build more transmission” is not the answer to every power-system problem. Some queue volume is noise. Some congestion can be relieved operationally. Some new load can be located where capacity already exists. Some generation can be built near demand. Storage can move energy across hours. Efficiency can reduce the amount of new infrastructure needed.

The serious claim is not that every electron requires a new long-distance line.

It is that cheap generation does not remove the need for complementary network capability. If reform, reconductoring, storage, flexible load, and better planning release enough capacity, then they are solving the network constraint rather than disproving its existence.

The evidence that would force this chapter to narrow further is measurable. If interconnection times fall sharply while completed-project rates rise, if large new loads connect without growing congestion or reliability risk, if transmission utilization improves enough that major planned expansions become unnecessary, and if transformer and equipment supply cease to delay projects, then the network may prove to be a transitional bottleneck rather than the defining constraint of the next energy era.

That outcome would be welcome.

For now, the opposite pressure is visible. North American reliability planners are preparing for much faster demand growth than they expected only a few years ago. NERC’s 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment forecasts summer peak demand growing by 224 gigawatts over ten years and winter demand by 246 gigawatts, with new AI and digital-economy data centers accounting for most of the increase in the summer forecast. NERC also warns that uncertainty and lag in resource additions raise the risk that supply will not keep pace.

A forecast is not destiny. Large loads can be delayed or canceled, and Chapter 8 treated announced demand accordingly. But planning a reliable grid requires acting before uncertainty disappears. By the time a load is certain, the infrastructure needed to serve it may be years behind.

Networks have to make commitments under uncertainty because networks take time.

That is the deeper constraint.

Generation technology can improve in factories. Software can be updated overnight. A chip generation can arrive within a few years. A transmission corridor has to cross a landscape, an economy, a regulatory system, and often several political jurisdictions. The pace of the whole energy system is therefore not set only by the fastest improving technology inside it.

It is set partly by the slowest complement that cannot be skipped.

This changes the meaning of energy abundance. A world of very cheap generation is not abundant if power cannot reach the place and time where it is valuable. A region with enormous theoretical wind and solar resources is not electrically rich merely because the resource exists. A data center with servers is not computationally abundant if it cannot be energized. A factory cannot consume a forecast.

Abundance is a system property.

The grid made electricity powerful by hiding complexity behind a standardized interface. Chapter 4 described the triumph of that abstraction: the user flips a switch without needing to know the generator. The present bottleneck is what happens when the hidden layer can no longer be taken for granted.

The network becomes visible again.

Not because it has failed, but because everything on both sides of it is asking more from it.
