# Chapter 8 — semiconductor roadmaps as coordination evidence

## Claim under test

Chapter 8 argues that Moore's Law became more than a retrospective description of transistor growth: shared expectations about future semiconductor capabilities helped coordinate suppliers, manufacturers, researchers, capital, and technical schedules.

That is plausible, but the manuscript should distinguish evidence that roadmaps coordinated an ecosystem from the stronger claim that roadmapping itself *caused* Moore's Law to continue.

## Primary-source evidence

The Semiconductor Industry Association's 2001 *International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors* is unusually direct about the coordination function. Its foreword describes the ITRS as the product of a worldwide consensus-building process sponsored by semiconductor organizations in Europe, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, and the United States. It says the earlier U.S. roadmaps supplied useful guidance to equipment, materials, and software suppliers and clear targets for researchers, and explains that globalization of manufacturers, suppliers, alliances, and joint ventures motivated a roadmap intended to guide the whole industry.

Source: Semiconductor Industry Association et al., *International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors, 2001 Edition — Executive Summary*.
https://www.semiconductors.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/2001-Executive-Summary.pdf

A historical account by International SEMATECH leaders W. J. Spencer and T. E. Seidel adds mechanism. They describe hundreds of scientists and engineers from industry, universities, and government jointly developing industry requirements; early SEMATECH roadmap workshops directly shaped operational plans and specific technology priorities; and the 1992 SIA roadmap used technical working groups with cross-coordination among areas such as lithography, interconnect, and materials. The workshop charter explicitly included identifying resources needed so the industry would have necessary technology for future competitive markets.

Source: W. J. Spencer and T. E. Seidel, “International Technology Roadmaps: The U.S. Semiconductor Experience,” in National Research Council, *Productivity and Cyclicality in Semiconductors: Trends, Implications, and Questions*, National Academies Press, 2004.
https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11134/chapter/11

## Strongest counterargument and boundary conditions

These sources do not prove that a roadmap made the transistor-density trajectory happen, nor that semiconductor firms subordinated commercial decisions to one central plan. Physics, process invention, competition, customer demand, firm strategy, capital cycles, and independent technical discoveries all mattered.

The ITRS itself states that it was intended for technology assessment and not commercial considerations concerning individual products or equipment. That is an important constraint on the manuscript's rhetoric: the roadmap supplied shared expectations, targets, problem definitions, and timing signals; it was not an industrial command economy.

Nor should every period of semiconductor progress be attributed to the same mechanism. The technical route changed repeatedly, and later gains increasingly depended on architecture, packaging, accelerators, memory systems, software, and forms of scaling not reducible to feature shrinkage.

## Editorial implication

Chapter 8 can keep the central idea that a scaling trajectory became a coordination device, because the industry's own roadmap documents explicitly describe consensus-building, supplier guidance, research targets, cross-functional coordination, and resource planning.

The strongest defensible formulation is narrower than “the industry made Moore's Law come true.” A better claim is that once expected progress became credible, formal roadmaps helped convert that expectation into mutually legible technical targets and investment horizons across a distributed ecosystem. They likely reinforced the trajectory without being sufficient to cause it.

That distinction improves the chapter's broader argument: a forecast can become part of a system's control logic without becoming the sole cause of the outcome it forecasts.
