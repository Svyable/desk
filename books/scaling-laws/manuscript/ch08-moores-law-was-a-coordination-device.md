# Chapter 8 — Moore's Law Was a Coordination Device

A prediction can describe a future.

Sometimes it can also help organize one.

In 1965, Gordon Moore looked at the rapidly increasing complexity of integrated circuits and projected the trend forward. The observation later acquired a name, a cadence and a mythology. What began as an empirical extrapolation became one of the most consequential expectations in industrial history.

Moore's Law is usually told as a story about transistors.

It is also a story about coordination.

## A law that was not a law

The word *law* invites the wrong mental model.

Gravity does not need a quarterly planning meeting.

The semiconductor industry did.

Moore's original observation was not a physical commandment forcing transistor density to improve at a fixed rate forever. It described a remarkable technological trajectory inside a particular industry, under particular economic and engineering conditions.

That trajectory persisted because millions of decisions kept making it persist.

Engineers changed lithography.

Materials changed.

Device structures changed.

Factories became vastly more expensive.

Design tools improved.

Equipment suppliers coordinated with chipmakers.

Software developers assumed more compute would arrive.

Customers designed products around expected improvements.

Capital flowed toward firms promising to stay on the curve.

The curve did not merely happen to the industry.

The industry organized around the curve.

## Forecasts can become interfaces

A useful forecast does more than predict.

It can become an interface between organizations.

A chip designer needs to know what manufacturing process may exist when the design is ready.

A lithography supplier needs to know what resolution customers will demand years ahead.

A chemicals company needs to know what purity and materials requirements fabs will impose.

A computer manufacturer needs to know what performance, power and cost envelopes future processors might occupy.

A software team needs to know whether algorithms that are impractical today may become cheap enough tomorrow.

Each actor faces uncertainty.

A shared expectation reduces some of it.

If everyone believes capability will improve on a recognizable cadence, they can align investment horizons.

Roadmaps become mutually legible.

The prediction becomes a planning language.

## Coordination creates its own causality

This is where the story gets interesting.

Suppose a technology trend is expected to continue.

That expectation encourages investment.

Investment funds research, fabs, suppliers, tooling and talent.

Those investments increase the probability that the trend continues.

Continued progress strengthens belief in the expectation.

The expectation then attracts more investment.

This is a feedback loop:

**forecast → coordination → investment → capability → validated forecast**.

The forecast is not therefore *true because people believed it*.

Physics still matters.

Manufacturing still has to work.

Yield still has to improve.

But belief can affect how much coordinated effort is applied to overcoming the next constraint.

A scaling law can become reflexive.

## The difference between prophecy and roadmap

Calling Moore's Law a self-fulfilling prophecy is tempting but incomplete.

Prophecy sounds passive.

Roadmapping is active.

A roadmap decomposes a desired trajectory into intermediate technical problems.

If transistor density must keep improving, what has to happen to feature size?

What has to happen to overlay accuracy?

What has to happen to defect density?

What new materials are needed?

What happens to power?

What happens to interconnect delay?

What happens to manufacturing cost?

A trajectory can therefore act as an organizing constraint.

It tells an ecosystem not merely where it hopes to arrive, but which bottlenecks must be attacked next.

The curve becomes a queue of engineering problems.

## The miracle was distributed

No single company manufactured Moore's Law.

That is precisely why the coordination story matters.

Semiconductor progress depended on a distributed industrial system: chip designers, foundries, equipment vendors, materials suppliers, metrology companies, packaging firms, universities, standards bodies, software-tool makers and customers.

The frontier depended on specialized organizations making complementary advances at compatible times.

A lithography breakthrough is less useful if resist chemistry is not ready.

A new process node is less useful if design tools cannot target it.

A denser chip is less attractive if packaging cannot remove the heat.

A powerful processor has limited value if software cannot exploit it.

The scaling unit was not a transistor.

It was an ecosystem.

## Expectations compress negotiation

Imagine every supplier having to renegotiate the industry's future from scratch.

How dense should chips become?

When?

At what cost?

With what power budget?

What manufacturing capabilities should suppliers prepare for?

Shared roadmaps compress these negotiations.

They create focal points.

Economists use the term *coordination equilibrium* for situations where outcomes depend partly on expectations about what others will do.

The semiconductor industry repeatedly faced this structure.

A supplier invests because customers are expected to need a capability.

Customers design for that capability because suppliers are expected to deliver it.

Capital providers fund both because the market expects the cadence to continue.

The roadmap coordinates commitments across time.

## A curve can create a deadline

Once a scaling trajectory becomes expected, falling behind acquires meaning.

A company is not merely slower.

It is off cadence.

A supplier is not merely late.

It risks blocking a node.

A product is not merely expensive.

It may be economically obsolete before launch.

This creates temporal discipline.

A shared scaling expectation turns technological improvement into a schedule.

Schedules create deadlines.

Deadlines reorganize organizations.

They influence hiring, capital expenditure, portfolio decisions, partnerships and risk tolerance.

This is one way a curve reaches inside institutions.

## When the target changes the system

Measurement has a peculiar property.

Once a metric becomes a target, behavior reorganizes around it.

Semiconductor density was not the only thing that mattered, but the industry learned to orient around process generations and expected improvements.

This brought enormous benefits.

It also created pressure to preserve a visible trajectory even as the underlying engineering regime changed.

The meaning of a "node" became less literal over time.

Performance improvement increasingly depended on architecture, packaging, accelerators, memory systems, specialized compute and software as well as simple feature shrinkage.

The headline curve could survive while the mechanism underneath it migrated.

That is a recurring pattern in scaling systems.

**A stable metric can hide a changing machine.**

## Dennard scaling was a different bargain

For a long period, transistor scaling brought an especially powerful companion effect: shrinking transistors could support improvements in density and performance without proportionally exploding power consumption.

This relationship is associated with Dennard scaling.

It is crucial not to collapse it into Moore's Law.

Component density and power-density behavior are different relationships.

When the old power-scaling regime stopped holding as neatly, the industry faced a new bottleneck.

Frequency scaling slowed.

Multicore processors, specialized accelerators, architectural changes and software parallelism became more important.

The visible scaling story persisted, but the route changed.

Again, bottlenecks move.

## A law can outlive its mechanism

This is one of the deepest lessons in the book.

A scaling regularity can persist even when the mechanisms producing it change.

At first, one fabrication innovation carries the curve.

Then another.

Then design automation matters more.

Then architectural specialization.

Then packaging.

Then system-level integration.

The apparent continuity of the graph can conceal discontinuity in the underlying industrial process.

This is why extrapolating the line is not enough.

You must ask what work is required to keep the line alive.

## The cost of staying on the curve

Scaling narratives often emphasize the output.

More transistors.

More performance.

Lower cost per function.

But maintaining the trajectory can require escalating inputs.

Leading-edge fabrication plants became extraordinarily capital intensive.

Process development became more complex.

Equipment became more specialized.

Supply chains became more strategically consequential.

The curve in capability can coexist with a curve in required coordination, capital and institutional sophistication.

This is a key pattern:

**the easier the output looks, the harder the hidden system may be working.**

Smooth technological progress can be the surface appearance of rising organizational difficulty.

## The industrial constitution

At its most powerful, Moore's Law functioned like an industrial constitution.

Not a law enforced by government.

A law enforced by expectation.

It told firms what future customers would consider normal.

It told investors what counted as competitive progress.

It told suppliers which capabilities would become necessary.

It told software developers that inefficiencies might eventually become affordable.

It told entrepreneurs that products impossible today could become routine tomorrow.

The expectation shaped the option space.

That is a different kind of power from prediction.

## The software side of the bargain

Hardware progress changed software culture.

When compute reliably becomes cheaper and more abundant, developers can spend future hardware gains on abstraction.

Higher-level languages become acceptable.

Graphical interfaces become ordinary.

Search becomes ubiquitous.

Video becomes software.

Cryptography becomes routine.

Machine learning becomes practical at scales previously reserved for laboratories.

The hardware curve expands the feasible software frontier.

Software then creates demand for more hardware.

Another feedback loop appears:

**cheaper compute → new applications → more demand → more investment → cheaper compute**.

The scaling law becomes an economic flywheel.

## Forecasting can create option value

A startup deciding what to build often depends on technologies that do not yet exist at the needed price.

If compute, storage, bandwidth or sensor cost is improving predictably, entrepreneurs can design for the future point where the economics cross a threshold.

A reliable scaling trajectory therefore creates option value.

It lets organizations act before the capability is fully available.

This is strategically important.

The future becomes partially financeable because the trend is credible.

Teams can begin building software, distribution and ecosystems in anticipation of hardware catching up.

A scaling law can pull complementary innovation forward in time.

## Coordination can be a competitive advantage

Countries and industries often talk about technological leadership as if it were a race between isolated inventions.

But frontier technologies are frequently coordination problems.

They require aligned capital, supply chains, training pipelines, infrastructure, standards, manufacturing know-how and demand.

The advantage may not belong simply to whoever invents first.

It may belong to whoever coordinates the fastest learning ecosystem.

Moore's Law demonstrates the power of synchronized expectation.

A society that can coordinate around credible technological roadmaps can convert uncertainty into investment.

That is industrial capability.

## But coordination can also trap

Shared expectations are not automatically good.

A roadmap can focus attention so strongly that alternatives become underexplored.

Capital can overcommit to one architecture.

Suppliers can optimize for one trajectory.

Standards can harden.

Talent can cluster around the dominant path.

The same mechanism that accelerates progress can create path dependence.

Coordination is powerful because it narrows possibilities.

That is also its danger.

A civilization needs shared roadmaps without becoming unable to leave them.

## The end of a law is not the end of progress

Popular discussions often ask whether Moore's Law is dead.

The more useful question is: **which scaling relationship is carrying progress now?**

Density may improve differently.

Performance may come from specialized architectures.

System capability may come from chiplets and advanced packaging.

Efficiency may come from software-hardware co-design.

AI capability may scale with compute, data, model architecture and inference-time resources in new combinations.

The old law can weaken while the broader system finds another curve.

A regime can end without technological history ending.

## Scaling laws as institutions

We can now generalize.

Some scaling laws are merely observed.

Others become institutionalized.

They enter budgets.

They enter roadmaps.

They enter investor expectations.

They enter procurement specifications.

They enter product plans.

They enter organizational identity.

At that point, the curve is no longer only a description of the system.

It has become part of the system's control logic.

This matters far beyond semiconductors.

Renewable-energy cost curves can shape deployment policy.

Battery roadmaps can shape vehicle strategy.

AI scaling curves can shape data-center investment and model-development plans.

Carbon targets can shape industrial capital allocation.

Performance benchmarks can shape research agendas.

Whenever a measured trend becomes a shared expectation, ask whether the expectation is now helping produce the trend.

## The reflexive exponent

Earlier chapters treated exponents as descriptions of how one quantity changes with another.

Here the exponent acquires a second role.

It can become a target.

Once a community believes a relationship should continue, organizations may invest specifically to preserve it.

The scaling law becomes reflexive.

That does not make it fake.

It makes causality more interesting.

The curve is partly generated by physical possibility, partly by accumulated knowledge, partly by economics and partly by coordinated human effort.

The exponent lives inside an institution.

## The next coordination device

The semiconductor era teaches a question worth carrying into artificial intelligence.

What happens when a field begins organizing around its own scaling curves?

If model developers expect loss to decline predictably with compute, data and parameters, those expectations can influence capital expenditure, data acquisition, hardware roadmaps and research strategy.

A descriptive relationship can again become a coordination device.

That does not guarantee that the relationship will hold.

It may instead increase the amount of effort devoted to keeping it alive.

The distinction matters.

A curve can be both empirically real and institutionally reinforced.

## The law underneath the law

Moore's Law was never just about the number of components on a chip.

Its greater significance was that an entire industrial civilization learned to coordinate around expected exponential improvement.

The curve disciplined time.

It synchronized investment.

It created deadlines.

It shaped software.

It moved bottlenecks.

It made future capability legible enough to plan around.

That is a deeper form of scaling.

The prediction became infrastructure.

And once a prediction becomes infrastructure, the most important question is no longer only whether it is accurate.

It is what the world is building in order to make it come true.
