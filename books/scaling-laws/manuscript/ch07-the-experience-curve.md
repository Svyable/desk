# Chapter 7 — The Experience Curve

The first airplane is expensive because it has to teach you how to make the second.

The second teaches you how to make the third.

By the thousandth, the factory is no longer merely repeating itself. It has become a different production system.

Fixtures improve. Workers sequence tasks better. Suppliers specialize. Designs simplify. Waste becomes visible. Quality control moves upstream. Tooling changes. Standards harden. Volumes justify automation that would have been absurd at the beginning.

The product may look similar.

The organization that produces it is not.

This is the intuition behind one of the most consequential scaling relationships in industrial history: the experience curve.

As cumulative production rises, unit cost often falls.

Not because time passes.

Because production creates knowledge.

## The curve made of memory

Theodore Wright, studying aircraft production in the early twentieth century, noticed that labor requirements tended to decline as cumulative output increased. The relationship could be expressed as a power law between unit cost and accumulated production.

In simplified form:

`C = aQ^b`

where `C` is unit cost, `Q` is cumulative production, `a` is a constant and `b` is negative.

The negative exponent means cost falls as experience accumulates.

A common way to describe the curve is with a *progress ratio*: after each doubling of cumulative production, cost falls to some fraction of its previous level.

The exact rate varies enormously by technology, company, period, cost definition and production regime.

The robust idea is more important than any one percentage:

**repetition can change the economics of what is being repeated.**

That makes cumulative production a strange variable.

It is not merely quantity.

It is quantity with memory.

## Production is an experiment

A factory is often imagined as the place where invention stops and replication begins.

That picture is too clean.

Production itself generates information.

A weld fails.

A supplier misses tolerance.

An assembly step takes longer than expected.

A component can be redesigned to eliminate three fasteners.

A machine can be rearranged to shorten travel.

A test can be automated.

A defect can be prevented rather than inspected.

Every unit exposes the system to another opportunity to notice friction.

At low volume, many frictions are tolerable because redesign costs more than the friction.

At high volume, tiny inefficiencies become enormous pools of money.

A one-dollar saving on ten prototypes is trivia.

A one-dollar saving on one hundred million units is a business model.

Scale changes which improvements are worth discovering.

## The cost decline has many parents

The phrase *learning curve* makes it sound as though workers simply become more skilled through repetition.

That happens.

But industrial cost decline can come from many mechanisms:

learning by workers;

design for manufacturability;

specialized tooling;

supplier learning;

higher equipment utilization;

better yields;

standardization;

larger purchasing volumes;

process automation;

capital investment;

product redesign;

quality improvement;

organizational routines;

and accumulated engineering knowledge.

Some of these are learning effects.

Some are economies of scale.

Some are technological change.

Some are bargaining power.

Some are redesign.

A falling curve does not tell us which mechanism caused the fall.

Again: an exponent is not a mechanism.

## Wright versus Moore

Experience curves are especially easy to misunderstand because cumulative production and time often move together.

Production grows year after year.

Technology improves year after year.

Costs fall year after year.

Was cost falling because factories produced more?

Because researchers invented better methods?

Because capital equipment improved?

Because an industry matured?

Because time happened to proxy all of these things?

Empirical comparisons across many technologies have found that Wright-style cumulative-production laws can forecast technological cost decline surprisingly well, while time-based relationships similar to Moore's Law can sometimes perform nearly as well.

That is not a trivial statistical nuisance.

It changes strategy.

If improvement is mainly a function of time, waiting can be rational.

If improvement is strongly connected to cumulative deployment, waiting can delay the very learning that would make the technology cheap.

One theory says progress arrives.

The other says progress is partly manufactured by use.

## The deployment paradox

New technologies are often rejected because they are expensive.

But if their costs fall with cumulative deployment, refusing to deploy them can help keep them expensive.

This creates a feedback loop:

high cost → low deployment → little experience → slow cost decline → high cost.

The loop can also run forward:

deployment → experience → lower cost → more deployment → more experience.

That is an industrial flywheel.

The hard question is who pays for the early turns.

A private customer may prefer to wait for the cheaper generation.

A company may not want to subsidize learning that competitors can copy.

A country may invest because the spillovers benefit its domestic industrial base.

A government procurement program may tolerate high early costs because it values the future capability created by repetition.

A climate policy may make deployment attractive before the technology is independently cost-competitive.

The experience curve therefore crosses the boundary between engineering and political economy.

The curve asks not only what happens when we build more.

It asks who has the incentive to build the expensive first units that make later units cheap.

## The first mover can manufacture the future

Traditional first-mover advantage is often explained through brand, distribution or customer lock-in.

Experience curves add another mechanism.

A company that accumulates production faster can potentially move farther down the cost curve.

Lower cost can support lower prices.

Lower prices can increase volume.

Higher volume can produce more experience.

More experience can lower cost again.

This creates a positive feedback loop between scale and learning.

Under the right conditions, cost advantage is not merely possessed.

It is produced by production.

This is why cumulative volume can become strategically important even when current margins look unattractive.

The company that appears to be losing money on early units may be buying a position on tomorrow's cost curve.

Of course, this can become a dangerous story executives tell themselves while destroying capital.

The learning must be real.

The knowledge must be retainable.

Demand must exist.

Competitors must not copy every gain instantly.

The relevant process must remain stable long enough for accumulated experience to matter.

A curve is not permission to subsidize failure forever.

## Learning can spill across company boundaries

Experience is rarely contained perfectly inside one firm.

Workers move.

Suppliers serve multiple customers.

Equipment vendors improve their tools.

Standards diffuse.

Patents expire.

Competitors reverse-engineer.

Universities publish.

Consultants carry routines between organizations.

This means the unit doing the learning may not capture all the value created by learning.

At the firm level, that can weaken incentives to invest in early deployment.

At the national or industry level, it can strengthen the case for coordinated investment.

A semiconductor cluster, battery ecosystem or aerospace supply chain can accumulate collective experience that no single balance sheet fully owns.

Learning curves can therefore have geography.

They can create industrial regions where capability compounds locally even when knowledge is partially global.

The curve can become a map of power.

## When cheap becomes inevitable

Once a technology has descended far enough down an experience curve, strategic narratives can flip.

Early arguments focus on whether the technology can ever compete.

Later arguments focus on how quickly incumbents will be displaced.

The transition can feel sudden even when the cost decline was gradual.

Suppose a new technology remains more expensive than the incumbent for years.

Its cost falls with deployment.

Then it crosses a threshold.

Now adoption accelerates, which increases cumulative production, which may drive further cost decline.

A smooth curve can create a discontinuous market event.

This is one way scaling laws generate thresholds that look like magic.

The threshold was not magic.

It was the point where a continuous cost relationship crossed a discrete decision boundary.

Buyers do not purchase logarithms.

They choose one product over another.

## Demand pulls the curve

Experience curves are often drawn as if production were an independent variable we can turn like a dial.

But production is caused by demand, finance, policy, capacity and expectations.

A product must be bought before cumulative volume can rise.

That creates endogeneity.

Technologies that improve quickly may attract more demand.

Technologies with more demand may accumulate more production and therefore improve more quickly.

Cause runs both ways.

A beautiful experience curve can therefore reflect a successful technology rather than explain its success completely.

This is a recurring challenge in scaling analysis.

The variable that appears to drive the curve may itself be selected by the system.

## The curve can break upward

Experience curves encourage a pleasing story: build more, get cheaper.

Reality is less polite.

Input prices can rise.

Scarce materials can become bottlenecks.

Labor markets can tighten.

Regulation can add cost.

Quality requirements can increase.

Supply chains can fragment.

A technology can run into physical limits.

Geopolitical shocks can reverse globalization advantages.

A design generation can change so radically that old production experience transfers only partially.

The curve can flatten.

It can kink.

It can reset.

It can temporarily reverse.

Cumulative experience is not a universal solvent for scarcity.

Learning reduces some costs while scale exposes others.

Bottlenecks move.

## The curve can become a weapon

Now the idea becomes more ambitious.

If cumulative production shapes future cost, then market share today can influence cost competitiveness tomorrow.

That turns deployment into strategic terrain.

A company may accept low margins to accelerate cumulative output.

A government may subsidize domestic manufacturing to move national producers down the curve.

An incumbent may cut prices to deny a challenger the volume it needs to learn.

A platform may bundle a new technology into an existing product to manufacture usage.

A military may procure systems not only for immediate capability but to sustain production knowledge and supplier competence.

A climate policy may aim not merely to reduce present emissions but to move a clean technology far enough down its cost curve that future adoption becomes self-reinforcing.

Under these conditions, cost is not merely an outcome of competition.

Cost becomes something competitors fight to shape through accumulated experience.

## Industrial policy is partly a race down curves

Nations talk about strategic industries using nouns: chips, batteries, aircraft, robots, energy systems.

Experience curves suggest a more dynamic way to think.

The real asset may be the rate at which an ecosystem can accumulate production experience.

Who is building enough units to uncover defects?

Who has suppliers seeing enough volume to justify specialized equipment?

Who has engineers solving the thousandth production problem rather than the tenth?

Who has factories dense enough for tacit knowledge to circulate?

Who owns the demand that keeps the learning system alive?

A country can import cheap products and still lose the production experience required to make the next generation.

Conversely, protecting domestic production at any cost can preserve inefficient systems that never become globally competitive.

The policy challenge is not simply to maximize local output.

It is to distinguish **productive accumulation of capability** from expensive repetition.

Not every factory learns at the same rate.

Not every doubling creates equal knowledge.

## AI may have experience curves too—but be precise

Modern artificial intelligence introduces several different kinds of accumulated experience that are easy to blur together.

Training more models can improve organizational knowledge about architectures, data pipelines and optimization.

Deploying models can generate feedback about user behavior, failure modes and product design.

Producing more accelerators can improve yields and manufacturing processes.

Serving more inference can justify software and hardware optimizations.

But these are distinct curves.

Training compute is not cumulative hardware production.

User interactions are not manufacturing experience.

Algorithmic improvements are not automatically functions of deployment volume.

The temptation will be to call all improvement a learning curve.

Resist it.

Name the unit that accumulates.

Name the cost or performance variable that changes.

Name the mechanism that carries experience from one unit to the next.

Otherwise the metaphor outruns the evidence.

## Learning is capital you cannot see

A factory appears on a balance sheet.

Accumulated production knowledge often does not.

Yet two factories with similar equipment can perform very differently because one organization has learned how to make the process work reliably.

Tacit knowledge hides in sequencing, maintenance routines, supplier relationships, exception handling, fixture design, yield management and thousands of micro-decisions that never become patents.

This makes experience a peculiar form of capital.

It is created through activity.

It depreciates when teams disperse.

It can be difficult to transfer.

It may be embedded in institutions rather than individuals.

And it is easiest to see indirectly, through the performance gap between organizations that have done the thing many times and those that have not.

## The strategic question changes

A static cost comparison asks:

Which technology is cheapest today?

An experience-curve comparison asks:

Which technology becomes cheaper fastest as we build it?

A static investment question asks:

What return does this factory earn now?

A dynamic question asks:

What capability does this production create that changes future economics?

A static procurement question asks:

Which supplier offers the lowest bid?

A dynamic question asks:

Which production path preserves or compounds the capability we will need later?

These are not excuses to ignore current economics.

They are reminders that current cost can be a state variable, not a permanent attribute.

## The experience trap

Learning has a dark side.

The more experience an organization accumulates with one architecture, the more costly it can become to abandon it.

Factories are optimized around existing processes.

Suppliers specialize.

Workers acquire specific skills.

Standards solidify.

Managers learn which metrics matter.

The experience curve can create path dependence.

The organization becomes extraordinarily good at descending the wrong curve.

A radically different technology may begin at a higher cost because it has not accumulated experience yet.

Comparing today's mature incumbent with tomorrow's immature challenger can therefore be unfair.

But assuming every challenger will eventually descend faster is equally dangerous.

The strategic task is to compare curves, not just points.

And then ask whether the curves belong to the same future.

## Build to learn

The deepest lesson of the experience curve is not that mass production makes things cheap.

It is that action can change the cost of future action.

That is a more profound form of nonlinearity.

The system remembers what it has done.

Each unit can leave behind knowledge that changes the next unit.

This creates feedback between scale and capability.

It can produce industrial dominance.

It can justify early deployment.

It can trap organizations in legacy architectures.

It can turn procurement into capability policy and market share into accumulated know-how.

It can make the expensive first generation the cause of the cheap tenth generation.

So when someone says a technology is too expensive, ask a second question.

Too expensive at what cumulative volume?

And when someone promises that costs will fall, ask the question underneath the curve.

What, exactly, is being learned?

The future does not always get cheaper because time passes.

Sometimes it gets cheaper because someone built enough of it to learn how.
