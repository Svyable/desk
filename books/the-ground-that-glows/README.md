## Reader promise

Earthquake lights sit in an unusually productive gap between ridicule and overclaim. People have reported flashes, glows, streamers, luminous balls, and near-ground light around earthquakes for centuries. Some modern cases are instrumented well enough to resist casual dismissal; laboratory and modeling work also shows that stressed or fractured rock can generate electrical effects by several routes. But the stronger claim—that such electrical phenomena provide a reliable warning before earthquakes—has repeatedly failed to become an operational forecasting method.

This book follows that split from eyewitness catalogs to the 2007 Pisco camera record, stress-activated charge in rock, competing electrical mechanisms, DEMETER and Swarm satellite studies, the retrospective precursor problem, L'Aquila, and a prospective experiment designed to let the phenomenon fail cleanly.

## Distinct contribution

The manuscript refuses to use evidence for one burden to certify another. It separates: (a) whether luminous events occur around some earthquakes, (b) whether geological rather than infrastructure or atmospheric sources produced particular events, (c) whether any proposed rock-electrical mechanism scales from laboratory specimens to real faults, and (d) whether a measurable anomaly has prospective forecasting value after false alarms, missed events, base rates, and action thresholds are counted.

The central thesis is deliberately asymmetric: **earthquake-related luminous and electromagnetic phenomena may be physically real in a subset of settings while remaining poor or useless earthquake predictors.** A real anomaly does not automatically imply an oracle.

## Strongest competing explanation

The strongest skeptical explanation is that the earthquake-light literature combines several unrelated phenomena—power-system arcing, lightning, meteors, camera artifacts, fires, retrospective witness association, and a smaller number of real but coseismic geophysical electrical effects—while precursor studies repeatedly overfit natural ionospheric and geomagnetic variability after large earthquakes are already known. On this account, the laboratory physics is real but does not establish a field-scale precursor channel.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified / documented:** major earthquakes can coincide with instrumentally recorded luminous events; stressed/fractured rocks and fault materials can show measurable electrical behavior; Pisco 2007 produced timed camera/seismic records; DEMETER was purpose-built in part to study seismo-electromagnetic effects; USGS currently finds no convincing evidence for reliable electromagnetic earthquake precursors.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** some Pisco flashes may have had a geological electrical source; positive-hole charge carriers or other mechanisms may transport earthquake-related charge under favorable geology; some statistical satellite studies report pre-earthquake ionospheric or magnetic anomalies above matched background.
3. **Disputed / not independently established:** a particular mechanism explains most earthquake lights; the historical rift association is physical rather than observational bias; satellite/ground electromagnetic anomalies currently enable useful short-term earthquake prediction; dramatic pre-earthquake videos can be attributed to crustal electricity without grid/lightning provenance.
4. **Speculative synthesis / open conjecture:** “earthquake lights” may be a family in which infrastructure events, atmospheric effects, coseismic local rock-electrical discharges, and rarer preseismic phenomena coexist; the scientific phenomenon may survive even if the prediction promise fails.
