# L'Aquila and the Cost of a Warning

Prediction research becomes ethically different when people might act on it.

The 2009 L'Aquila earthquake in Italy is often dragged into arguments about prediction, sometimes carelessly. In the months before the magnitude 6.3 mainshock, the region experienced an earthquake swarm. Technician Giampaolo Giuliani publicly promoted radon-based methods and made earthquake predictions that became part of a bitter public controversy. After the earthquake killed more than 300 people, scientists and officials were prosecuted over communication surrounding seismic risk. The legal story was complicated and changed on appeal; it should not be flattened into the familiar myth that Italian seismologists were convicted merely for failing to predict an earthquake.

What matters here is the environment the case exposed.

An earthquake warning is not a paper result. It is an intervention into behavior.

If a precursor scientist tells a town that an earthquake is imminent, schools may close. Hospitals may alter operations. People may sleep outside. Businesses may stop. Roads may fill. If nothing happens, trust changes. If repeated false alarms occur, compliance decays. If the scientist remains silent and an earthquake happens, the same signal will look obvious afterward.

This is why precursor sensitivity alone is not enough.

Warnings require calibrated probabilities and explicit action thresholds. A signal that shifts the probability of a damaging earthquake from one chance in ten thousand to one chance in one thousand has multiplied risk by ten. That sounds enormous. It may still be too low to justify evacuation.

The political pressure pushes in the opposite direction. People do not want a likelihood ratio. They want to know whether to leave the house.

Earthquake lights are especially dangerous in this environment because they are vivid. A photograph or personal sighting creates a sense of immediacy that a probabilistic seismic hazard map does not. If the phenomenon is rare, poorly specified, and vulnerable to mundane imitation, turning it into public warning advice before prospective validation would be reckless.

This does not mean the public should be told that unusual phenomena are impossible. It means the epistemic label has to travel with the claim.

A well-recorded coseismic light is an observation. A pre-earthquake glow with independent documentation is a candidate precursor. A mechanism that could produce such a glow is a mechanism. None of these, alone, is a warning rule.

L'Aquila is useful here because it shows what happens when uncertain precursor claims enter an environment already saturated with fear, institutional mistrust, and genuine seismic risk. The scientific question and the communication question become inseparable.

There is a strange asymmetry in prediction culture. A missed earthquake is memorable. A false alarm that disrupted nothing spectacular disappears.

Prospective evaluation has to remember both.
