# The Rift Problem

In 2014 a group led by Robert Thériault tried to do something more ambitious than explain one flash. They asked where earthquake lights happen.

Their catalog contained 65 reported cases spanning centuries. The striking result was tectonic. The cases appeared disproportionately associated with rift environments and steep, subvertical faults rather than with the subduction zones that generate many of the world's largest earthquakes.

If that pattern is physical, it is a clue of unusual value.

A mechanism involving upward migration of electrical charge should care about geometry, lithology, conductivity, and the path to the surface. Steep faults in continental settings could provide a more favorable route than a seismogenic interface buried beneath kilometers of seawater and sediment. Ocean water is conductive and hostile to large atmospheric electric fields. A continental rift also exposes rocks that may have suitable defect chemistry closer to the surface.

The catalog therefore turns a strange light into a geological prediction.

It also inherits every weakness of a historical catalog.

Human beings live on land. They see the sky over land. Written records are denser near settlements. A light emitted under the ocean is unlikely to become a witness statement. Subduction earthquakes often occur offshore. Rift earthquakes, strike-slip earthquakes, and intraplate events can occur beneath populated continental regions. The apparent tectonic selectivity may be partly the selectivity of observation.

The correct response is not to choose between mechanism and bias by taste. It is to build a denominator.

Take a defined period with reliable global earthquake catalogs. Select all earthquakes above a magnitude threshold. Classify their tectonic environment before examining light reports. Estimate nighttime observation opportunity, cloud cover, population density, electrification, and camera coverage. Then search for luminous reports using a fixed protocol and include explicit negative cases. The question is no longer 'where have earthquake lights been reported?' It becomes 'given comparable opportunity to observe them, are luminous anomalies enriched in particular geological settings?'

That experiment would be tedious. It would also be decisive in a way another famous anecdote cannot be.

The rift hypothesis is especially useful because it can lose. If careful prospective or denominator-aware work finds that apparent rift enrichment disappears after observation conditions are accounted for, the geological claim should weaken. If enrichment remains and correlates with mapped lithology or fault dip, the mechanism problem becomes narrower and more interesting.

This is the kind of asymmetry the field needs. A theory should not merely accommodate cases after they occur. It should place some earthquakes in a high-probability bin and others in a low-probability bin before anyone looks at the videos.

The 2014 paper did not provide that prospective test. It provided the map for one.

There is a temptation to call such work 'proof of earthquake lights' because the historical survey is unusually systematic for an anomalous subject. That oversells it. Its stronger contribution may be methodological. It converts a folklore category into a set of variables: tectonic regime, fault geometry, timing, distance, phenomenology.

Once those variables exist, the subject can be wrong in more informative ways.

That is progress.
