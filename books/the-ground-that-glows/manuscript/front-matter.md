# The Ground That Glows

*Earthquake Lights, Electric Rocks, and the Prediction Problem*

Sven Hardy Benson

There are observations science does not reject because they are impossible. It rejects them because they are expensive.

An observation can be expensive in several ways. It may require instruments that were not pointed in the right direction. It may arrive attached to folklore. It may be made by frightened people at precisely the moment when transformers are exploding, buildings are failing, clouds are moving, and a hundred ordinary sources of light have become unusual. It may implicate a mechanism that sounds embarrassing. Worst of all, it may carry a promise the evidence cannot cash.

Earthquake lights carry all of those costs.

People have reported luminous phenomena around earthquakes for centuries: flashes, glows, floating balls, flames close to the ground, sheets of light in the sky. Some reports are plainly weak. Some are probably electrical infrastructure failing during strong shaking. A smaller set is difficult to dispose of so easily. In 2007, cameras in Lima recorded flashes during the magnitude 8 Pisco earthquake, and researchers compared their timing with local ground acceleration. Laboratory work has shown that stressed and fractured rock can generate charge by more than one physical route. Other experiments and models ask whether stress-activated charge can move through rock, accumulate near the surface, and produce atmospheric discharge.

None of that means an earthquake can be predicted from a light in the sky.

That sentence is the hinge of this book.

Earthquake science has suffered for decades from a category error that appears everywhere frontier evidence meets human need. A phenomenon can be real without being specific. A mechanism can be plausible without being common. A signal can correlate with an event after the fact and still be useless before the fact. A detector can find an anomaly in the week before an earthquake and yet fail as a forecasting system because similar anomalies occur during weeks when no earthquake follows. If a signal cannot survive that denominator, it is not a warning system. It is an anecdote with instrumentation.

The evidence in this book is kept in four bins. **Verified/documented facts** include published measurements, instrument records, established earthquake physics, official assessments, and the existence and design of research programs. **Credible but incomplete evidence** includes well-documented observations and statistical associations whose causal interpretation remains unsettled. **Disputed allegations or claims** include purported precursors that have not survived strong prospective testing, overconfident interpretations of historical reports, and specific causal assignments not independently established. **Speculative synthesis** is where I connect the pieces, explicitly as conjecture, and ask what experiment would force the story to change.

The strongest skeptical position is not that every earthquake light report is nonsense. It is that the world contains enough lightning, power-system arcs, vehicle lights, clouds, camera artifacts, geomagnetic variation, ionospheric weather, human pattern-finding, and retrospective degrees of freedom that a researcher can nearly always find something odd before a large earthquake if the search begins after the earthquake. The U.S. Geological Survey states the problem without romance: some earthquake-light reports may be plausible, some are attributable to power-line arcing, and decades of work have not produced convincing evidence for electromagnetic precursors that reliably predict earthquakes.

The strongest positive position is narrower than popular accounts usually make it. Rock under extreme stress is not electrically inert. Charge separation, electrokinetic effects, piezoelectric effects in suitable minerals, fracture electrification, triboelectric charging, and proposed stress-activated electronic carriers give the solid Earth several ways to couple mechanical deformation to electrical phenomena. Under particular geology and near-surface geometry, some of those effects might become optically visible. The Pisco observations and a small number of other documented cases deserve better than ridicule.

Between those positions is a book.

It is a book about the ground as an electrical material, about the seduction of precursors, about why satellites were built to look for earthquake-related disturbances, about why retrospective anomaly hunting repeatedly produces more confidence than prospective forecasting, and about the strange possibility that earthquake lights can be physically real while remaining nearly worthless as an oracle.

That possibility is not a compromise. It may be the most interesting answer.
