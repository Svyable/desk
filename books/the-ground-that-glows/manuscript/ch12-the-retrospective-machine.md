# The Retrospective Machine

Large earthquakes are followed by a peculiar scientific weather pattern.

First comes the event. Then comes the archive search.

Satellite data are inspected. Magnetometers are revisited. Total electron content maps are plotted. Thermal infrared products are examined. Radon measurements are checked. Animal reports surface. Someone finds an anomaly two days earlier. Another group finds one five days earlier. A mechanism is proposed to connect it to the fault.

This is not fraud. It is the natural behavior of curious people after a rare event.

It is also a machine for producing precursors.

If the search space is large enough, something unusual will precede almost every major earthquake. The relevant question is not whether an anomaly existed. It is whether the same rule would have identified the anomaly as important before the earthquake, and how often that rule fires when no damaging earthquake follows.

The distinction sounds elementary. Entire literatures struggle with it.

Fabrizio Masci and other critics of ionospheric precursor work have repeatedly pointed out that purported precursors can emerge from natural variability, analysis choices, or incomplete control comparisons. Jeffrey Love of the USGS has made the prospective standard bluntly: the acid test is predicting something that has not happened yet.

A good precursor study should therefore resemble a clinical trial more than an autopsy.

Define the population of earthquakes. Define the sensor. Define the anomaly threshold. Define the spatial and temporal warning window. Freeze the rule. Run it continuously. Count every alarm, every missed earthquake, every correct rejection, and every hit. Do not change the rule after an especially interesting failure unless the next evaluation occurs on new data.

This sounds punitive because the underlying problem is punitive.

Earthquakes are rare in any specific place. A detector with a small daily false-alarm probability can still generate far more false alarms than successful warnings. If a region has one damaging earthquake in several years, even a system that looks impressive in a case-control paper may spend most of its life being wrong.

Base rates are merciless.

Suppose an anomaly appears on ten percent of days and on fifty percent of days immediately preceding large earthquakes. That is a large enrichment. It is scientifically interesting. But if large earthquakes occur on one day in several thousand, almost every anomaly day will still not precede one. The signal can be real and the alarm nearly useless.

This is why papers that report 'accuracy' without operational base rates should make readers nervous.

The retrospective machine is especially powerful when anomalies have no fixed morphology. If a precursor can be a rise or fall in electron density, a magnetic excursion in any component, a thermal patch, a radon spike, a luminous report, or an animal disturbance appearing anywhere from hours to weeks before a quake, the hypothesis can rarely lose.

A hypothesis that cannot lose is not robust. It is hospitable.

Earthquake science has enough hospitality already.
