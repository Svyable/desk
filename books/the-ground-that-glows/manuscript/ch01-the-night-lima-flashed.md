# The Night Lima Flashed

At 6:40 in the evening on August 15, 2007, the coast of Peru began to move.

The earthquake that followed ruptured offshore near Pisco and reached moment magnitude 8.0. In Lima, roughly 150 kilometers from the epicentral region, people saw flashes. The useful detail is not that people saw flashes. People see extraordinary things during earthquakes, and an earthquake is almost purpose-built to corrupt testimony. The useful detail is that at least some of the flashes were recorded.

A security camera at the Pontificia Universidad Católica del Perú caught luminous events while a seismological station on the same campus recorded ground motion. J. A. Heraud and J. A. Lira later compared the timing. Their 2011 paper did not solve earthquake lights, but it improved the quality of the argument. The lights were no longer only recollections collected after disaster. They could be placed against an instrumental timeline.

The sequence mattered. In the authors' analysis, the first strong luminous events did not simply coincide with the earthquake's origin time hundreds of kilometers away. They appeared during strong local shaking in Lima. Later modeling work seized on this distinction. If seismic waves arriving in Lima stressed local rock and produced a local electrical discharge, the light did not have to travel from the fault. The earthquake supplied the mechanical disturbance; the luminous event could be generated near the observer.

That is a modest idea, but it changes the geometry completely.

An earthquake light generated locally by passing seismic waves belongs to a different evidentiary class from a glow seen hours before rupture. One is potentially a coseismic effect. The other is a candidate precursor. The physics required to explain them may overlap, but the burden of proof does not. A coseismic flash only has to show that strong deformation can couple into electricity and light. A precursor has to show that a fault preparing to rupture produces a signal that escapes the crust early enough, strongly enough, and specifically enough to be distinguished from the rest of Earth's electrical noise.

Those are not neighboring claims. They are separated by an entire forecasting problem.

Pisco also contains the first trap. The cameras recorded the sky of a major city during a major earthquake. Electrical infrastructure was failing. Distribution lines can arc. Transformers can flash. A bright event above a city in violent motion is not automatically geological just because a seismogram sits beside the video.

This is why the argument has lasted. The most interesting earthquake-light cases improve the evidence without closing the case. They replace folklore with timing, then expose a harder problem of source attribution.

The U.S. Geological Survey's public description is appropriately awkward. Earthquake lights are reported as sheet lightning, balls, streamers, and glows. Some geophysicists doubt that individual reports amount to solid evidence. Others think at least some are plausible. Some events have straightforward electrical-infrastructure explanations. Physics-based hypotheses exist for a narrower class near faults and strong earthquakes.

That is almost exactly where a serious book should begin: not with belief, but with a badly sorted drawer.

One item in the drawer is an eyewitness account. Another is a security-camera frame. Another is a power-grid failure. Another is a laboratory current from stressed rock. Another is a satellite anomaly discovered three days before a quake only after someone decided where to look. They resemble one another because they have all been given the same name. They may not be the same phenomenon.

During Pisco, the crucial measurable quantity was not mystery. It was timing. The camera clock could be compared with ground acceleration. The strongest flashes appeared in relation to the arrival of damaging seismic waves. That fact supports a local coupling hypothesis more naturally than a signal racing out from the hypocenter and illuminating Lima at the origin time.

It also makes Pisco much less useful for prediction.

This is a recurring humiliation in precursor science. The better the temporal resolution becomes, the more an impressive anomaly can migrate from the category of warning into the category of consequence. A flash seconds after a shear wave arrives may tell us something fascinating about crustal electrodynamics. It tells the person beneath the falling ceiling almost nothing about what to do next.

The light can be real and still arrive too late.
