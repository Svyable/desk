# The Grid Is a Terrible Witness

A city during a strong earthquake is an electrical experiment nobody designed.

Poles sway. Conductors slap together. Insulators fail. Substations trip. Transformers arc. Fires begin. Backup systems engage. Vehicle headlights move in ways they normally do not. Cameras auto-expose against a changing sky. Then videos are clipped, reposted, compressed, slowed down, and given titles by people who already know what happened.

If you wanted to manufacture false earthquake lights without falsifying a single video, you could hardly build a better machine.

This is why electrical infrastructure has to be treated as an adversarial explanation, not as a footnote. The U.S. Geological Survey explicitly notes that some supposed earthquake lights have turned out to be power-line arcing during shaking. That fact does more than eliminate a few weak cases. It sets the standard for strong ones.

A convincing modern earthquake-light observation should ideally include a mapped camera location, calibrated time, field of view, weather, lightning data, local grid topology, outage and protection logs, and seismic motion at the site. If a flash begins exactly when a nearby feeder faults, geology has lost that round. If the same luminous structure appears across cameras with geometry inconsistent with the distribution network, the case becomes more interesting.

The problem is that the most spectacular videos usually arrive without this infrastructure autopsy.

After the 2023 earthquakes in Türkiye and Syria, for example, social media filled quickly with footage labeled as earthquake lights. Some clips may show genuinely unusual atmospheric or electrical phenomena. Some show the kind of blue-green flashes familiar from power-system faults. Without provenance, timestamp integrity, location, and grid information, the video is closer to a lead than a measurement.

There is a psychological reason this boundary is difficult. Geological light and electrical-grid light are not opposites. The grid can be responding to the earthquake. A transformer flash is therefore both caused by the earthquake and irrelevant to the claim that stressed rock produced the light. The phrase *earthquake light* quietly slides between those meanings.

That slide is fatal if the goal is mechanism.

Imagine a surveillance network around a fault. Every camera frame is timestamped. Utility fault logs are available. Lightning mapping is available. Magnetometers and atmospheric-electric-field mills are colocated. Seismic instruments record local acceleration. Now a luminous event appears during strong shaking at three cameras. If no grid fault, lightning discharge, vehicle source, or camera artifact explains it, and an electric-field transient appears at the same time, the evidentiary posture changes. A strange story becomes a multi-instrument event.

This is exactly what rare-event science needs.

The temptation in anomalous research is to defend the anomaly from mundane explanation. Good research should do the opposite. It should make mundane explanations as powerful as possible. A mechanism worth keeping is one that remains after the city has been given every opportunity to fake it.

The same rule applies to historical accounts. Before rural electrification, power-line arcs are unavailable as an explanation, but fire, lightning, aurora, meteors, volcanic glow, and observational uncertainty remain. The candidate set changes; the burden does not disappear.

One of the useful things about earthquake lights is that they force epistemology into engineering. You cannot resolve the question by asking witnesses whether the light looked like a transformer. You need the transformer's protection log.

That is an unromantic document.

It may also be worth more than a thousand eyewitnesses.
