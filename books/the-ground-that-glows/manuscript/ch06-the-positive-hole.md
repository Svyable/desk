# The Positive Hole

The positive-hole theory is easy to caricature because its name sounds like a placeholder invented after the fact.

It is not.

In semiconductor language, a hole is not a little object burrowing through rock. It is a way of describing the absence of an electron in a structure where that absence behaves as a mobile positive charge carrier. Friedemann Freund's work argues that peroxy defects in oxygen-bearing minerals can break under stress in a way that activates positive holes in the oxygen sublattice. Those carriers, in the laboratory picture, can move out of a stressed rock volume.

The leap from laboratory slab to earthquake is large.

Freund and collaborators have reported currents, surface potentials, infrared effects, and air ionization associated with stressed rock. St-Laurent, Derr, and Freund proposed in 2006 that sufficiently concentrated positive-hole carriers reaching the surface could produce dielectric breakdown and luminous discharge. Later earthquake-light papers used this mechanism to explain why steep faults and rift geometries might matter: a suitable structure could provide a path from stressed igneous material toward the surface.

There is an appealing coherence to the story. Stress deep in rock activates charge. Charge propagates. Surface fields rise. Air ionizes. Light appears. A single microscopic defect becomes a bridge from tectonics to the atmosphere.

Coherence is not confirmation.

At each arrow, scale changes. Laboratory specimens are centimeters. Seismogenic volumes are kilometers. A dry rock experiment is not a fluid-filled fault. A measured current at an electrode is not proof that charge traverses heterogeneous crust without being neutralized. A surface potential is not automatically a field strong enough to sustain visible discharge in humid, conductive, irregular terrain.

The 2018 Jánský modeling is particularly useful here. Rather than asking whether earthquake-generated currents could exist, it asked what currents and energy would be needed to produce atmospheric electric fields associated with visible discharge. A current source deep in the crust performed badly. A small source tens of meters beneath the surface performed much better. The model does not disprove positive-hole transport. It tells any positive-hole account what it must accomplish: deliver charge near the surface efficiently enough that the atmosphere can be affected without an impossible energy budget.

That is a healthy constraint.

Another constraint is geology. If the relevant defect chemistry is concentrated in particular igneous and metamorphic rocks, the mechanism should not predict identical behavior everywhere. The reported rift association may therefore be more than a catalog curiosity. It could be a mechanism signature. But the same pattern could emerge because continental rifts are easier places for humans to observe lights than offshore subduction zones. The geological prediction has to be tested against observation bias.

The most interesting experiment would not begin with an earthquake at all. It would begin with a mapped volume of rock whose mineralogy and electrical properties are known, instrument it densely, stress it under controlled conditions, and measure current propagation away from the stressed zone through realistic fractures, fluids, and contacts. Then scale the observed transport using parameters that can be checked in the field.

This is less dramatic than pointing a satellite at a fault after a magnitude 7 event.

It is also how a mechanism earns the right to leave the laboratory.

Positive holes belong in the second evidence class of this book: a physically developed, experimentally motivated mechanism with serious unresolved scaling questions. They are not folklore. They are not yet a general explanation for earthquake lights.

The distinction is worth protecting because frontier subjects are often damaged by their defenders. If every pre-earthquake oddity is fed into the same mechanism, the mechanism gains narrative range while losing scientific risk.

A good theory should tell us where the ground will not glow.
