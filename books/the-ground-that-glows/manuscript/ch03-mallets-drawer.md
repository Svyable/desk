# Mallet's Drawer

Long before a satellite had a plasma probe, earthquake researchers were already collecting lights.

Robert Mallet, the nineteenth-century engineer often described as one of the founders of modern seismology, compiled historical reports of earthquake phenomena. Later investigators found luminous descriptions scattered through such catalogs: flames, glows, flashes, strange brightness in the sky. The old material is seductive because it seems to push the phenomenon backward through centuries, away from television transformers and digital cameras.

It also pushes us backward into worse evidence.

Historical catalogs are not data in the modern instrumental sense. They are records of records. A chronicler may be summarizing a witness who summarized another witness. The date can be firm while the time is not. A luminous event may be associated with an earthquake because the compiler already knew an earthquake occurred. A storm, fire, aurora, comet, lantern, volcanic phenomenon, or electrical event can acquire a seismic identity in retelling.

None of this makes the catalogs worthless. It changes what they are good for.

They can establish that reports of unusual luminosity around earthquakes did not begin with a twentieth-century theory. They can reveal recurring descriptive families. They can identify locations and events worth reconstructing from independent archives. They can show that scientists have been annoyed by this problem for a very long time.

They cannot, by themselves, establish a mechanism.

This distinction becomes important in modern earthquake-light literature because case catalogs often do double duty. First they are offered as evidence that the phenomenon exists. Then the geographical pattern of the selected cases is analyzed to infer where the mechanism operates. A 2014 paper by Robert Thériault, France St-Laurent, Friedemann Freund, and John Derr examined 65 reported cases and argued that earthquake lights disproportionately occurred in rift environments or along steep faults that might permit electrical charge to travel toward the surface.

That result is fascinating if the case set is representative.

But historical event selection is never a neutral act. The world is not covered by equally literate observers, equally dark skies, equally dense power grids, equally visible faults, or equally energetic archival traditions. Large offshore subduction earthquakes may occur beneath water where any near-surface electrical discharge is suppressed or unseen. Continental rifts may place earthquakes beneath populated land. A catalog can therefore contain a real geological pattern, an observation pattern, or both.

The only defensible response is not to dismiss the pattern but to make the denominator explicit.

How many large earthquakes occurred in each tectonic setting during the same period? How many had plausible observation conditions? How many produced no reported luminosity? How did the authors decide which historical reports were reliable enough to include? Could an investigator blinded to the tectonic environment make the same inclusion decisions from the source material?

These questions are harder than counting lights. They are also where the book's epistemology begins to matter.

A frontier field often advances by accumulating oddities until someone notices a structure. That is legitimate exploratory science. Trouble begins when the exploratory structure is spoken of as though it emerged from a preregistered test.

The Thériault catalog gives us a hypothesis worth risking: certain geological settings may favor the transport of stress-generated charge to the surface. That hypothesis can be tested prospectively by instrumenting comparable faults in different settings and asking whether electrical and luminous anomalies occur at different rates under defined stress conditions. The historical cases help choose the experiment. They should not be allowed to impersonate it.

Mallet's drawer is therefore valuable for exactly the reason old drawers usually are. It contains things worth looking at again.

The scientific mistake would be to confuse the drawer with the laboratory.
