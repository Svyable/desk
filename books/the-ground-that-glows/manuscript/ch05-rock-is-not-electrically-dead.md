# Rock Is Not Electrically Dead

The easy skeptical story says that rocks do not make light.

That story is too easy.

Mechanical stress and fracture can produce electrical effects in solids by several established or at least physically serious routes. Quartz-bearing material can exhibit piezoelectric behavior. Fracture can separate charge. Grain contacts can generate triboelectric effects. Fluids forced through porous material can generate streaming potentials. Changing pore networks can alter conductivity. Fault gouge has frequency-dependent electrical properties. None of these facts demonstrates that a particular flash over a fault was produced by rock. They remove the permission to dismiss the subject because stone is supposedly electrically inert.

This matters because earthquake science inherited a disciplinary boundary that the crust does not respect. Seismology is organized around stress, strain, rupture, and elastic waves. Atmospheric electricity is organized around fields, ions, discharges, and weather. Solid-state physics is organized around charge carriers and defects. Hydrogeology cares about fluids. A fault can involve all of them at once.

In 1983 David Lockner, Mark Johnston, and James Byerlee published laboratory work on electrical signals associated with rock fracture. Lockner and Byerlee later examined complex resistivity in water-saturated San Andreas fault gouge and noted that large low-frequency permittivities could permit charge to persist following earthquakes, with possible relevance to reported electromagnetic effects. These studies do not give us an earthquake-lamp switch. They establish that the electrical state of fault material is measurable, complicated, and responsive to mechanical conditions.

The most controversial modern mechanism is associated with Friedemann Freund and colleagues: stress-activated positive-hole charge carriers in oxygen anion sublattices of certain rocks. In this picture, dormant defect states can be activated by stress, producing mobile electronic carriers that can propagate beyond the stressed volume. At sufficiently high concentrations near the surface, the mechanism is proposed to alter surface potential, ionize air, and perhaps contribute to corona-like luminosity.

The mechanism has been developed in laboratory papers and invoked to connect several claimed earthquake precursors: ground potentials, air ionization, infrared anomalies, ionospheric effects, and lights. That breadth is both its appeal and its danger. A mechanism that explains too many poorly constrained observations can become difficult to falsify.

So the right question is not whether positive holes sound strange. The right questions are quantitative. Which rock types host the relevant defects? What stress states activate them? What carrier density and mobility are measured? How far can charge propagate through realistic fractured, wet, heterogeneous crust? What losses occur? Can the resulting surface field reach discharge thresholds without demanding an energy budget larger than the earthquake provides?

A 2018 modeling paper by Jaroslav Jánský and colleagues is useful because it imposed exactly this kind of embarrassment. Their calculations showed that a deep, large-scale current dipole capable of producing atmospheric breakdown could require absurdly large currents and energy—greater than the seismic-wave energy of a major earthquake in one modeled geometry. Moving the electrical source close to the surface made the requirements far more plausible.

That result narrows the story. It favors local generation near the surface or electrical transport that reaches close to the surface before producing light. It makes a flash generated locally by passing seismic waves easier to accommodate than a gigantic buried battery illuminating the sky from ten kilometers down.

This is what mechanism work should do. It should kill versions of the story.

Rock is not electrically dead. That fact opens the door.

Energy accounting decides how far we get to walk through it.
