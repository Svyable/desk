# Pisco Under Cross-Examination

The Pisco case is valuable because it can survive being made less magical.

Start with what is documented. A magnitude 8.0 earthquake struck offshore Peru on August 15, 2007. Luminous flashes were widely reported along the coast and in Lima. A camera at the Pontificia Universidad Católica del Perú recorded flashes, and a colocated seismic record allowed Heraud and Lira to compare their timing with local shaking. Their published analysis reported that the luminosity was associated with strong ground motion, including the interval after the arrival of S waves.

That is evidence class one for the existence of recorded light during the earthquake.

It is not evidence class one for a geological source.

Lima had electrical infrastructure. The camera did not perform spectroscopy. The paper's correlation between flashes and seismic acceleration is compatible with a geological mechanism, but it is also compatible with shaking-induced electrical faults unless those are independently excluded. The distance from the earthquake source becomes less problematic if the light is locally generated, but local generation makes the urban grid even more relevant as a competitor.

The case therefore turns on details that are less cinematic than the footage.

Where in the frame did each flash originate? Did multiple cameras triangulate a common source region? Were utility faults recorded at matching times? What was the local lightning environment? Did any flash occur over terrain without electrical infrastructure? Were there simultaneous changes in atmospheric electric field or magnetic field? Could camera blooming turn a compact source into apparent sky illumination?

A strong anomaly should invite hostile reconstruction.

Jánský and colleagues later modeled earthquake-light generation through electrical sources in the crust and discussed Pisco explicitly. Their work found that a deep source capable of producing atmospheric breakdown could require unrealistic energy, whereas current sources tens of meters beneath the surface were much less demanding. They noted the Pisco timing as consistent with local generation by passing seismic waves.

This is an elegant rescue of the geometry, but it is not independent proof of the source. A model can show that a mechanism is energetically possible. It cannot reach backward through a camera and identify what emitted the photons.

Pisco is therefore best treated as a benchmark case, not a solved case.

It gives future researchers a target level of evidence to exceed. A new event should have the camera timing Pisco had, plus optical spectra, calibrated radiance, multiple viewing angles, grid logs, lightning data, local field measurements, and dense seismic data. The aim should not be to collect another intriguing video. It should be to close an attribution loop.

There is a broader lesson here about anomalous evidence. Weak cases tempt believers to defend every detail. Strong cases can tolerate subtraction.

Take away the claim that Pisco proves positive holes. Take away the claim that every flash was geological. Take away the predictive aura—the observed flashes were largely coseismic and therefore not a practical warning. Something interesting remains: unusual luminous events were instrumentally recorded during a major earthquake and their timing can be compared with local seismic motion.

That surviving core is enough to justify more work.

It is also small enough to fit inside the evidence.
