# Other Ways to Make Voltage

Even if the positive-hole mechanism vanished tomorrow, the electrical earthquake problem would remain.

There are too many other ways to make voltage.

Quartz is piezoelectric: deform the right crystal orientation and electrical polarization appears. The crust contains quartz, but a polycrystalline rock mass does not behave like a neatly cut sensor. Crystal orientations can cancel. Conductive fluids leak charge away. Still, piezoelectricity is physically real and has long been invoked in earthquake-electromagnetic discussions.

Fracture electrification is less elegant and perhaps more relevant. Break materials and newly created surfaces can carry charge. Crush grains and charge separation can occur. Rub and separate dissimilar or even similar materials under certain conditions and triboelectric potentials emerge. Fault zones are extraordinarily good machines for creating fresh surface area.

Then there are fluids.

When electrolyte moves through charged pore surfaces, it can produce electrokinetic or streaming potentials. Earthquake preparation changes cracks, permeability, and pressure. Seismic waves pump pore space dynamically. Hydrogeological systems respond to large earthquakes over surprising distances. None of this requires exotic solid-state carriers.

This abundance of mechanisms is not purely good news for earthquake-light research. It creates an inverse problem.

If an electrode near a fault records a transient, which mechanism produced it? If the transient occurs during shaking, was it electrokinetic, piezoelectric, fracture-generated, electrode motion in soil, cable motion, or cultural noise? If a camera records light, was the source a corona discharge driven by geological current, or ordinary power infrastructure reacting to the same ground motion?

Multiple physically possible channels make the phenomenon easier to believe and harder to attribute.

This is a pattern worth noticing beyond earthquakes. A subject often becomes scientifically respectable when someone produces a plausible mechanism. But when several mechanisms become available, mechanism plausibility stops doing much evidentiary work. The problem shifts to signatures.

Each candidate process should leave a different fingerprint. Piezoelectric effects should depend strongly on mineralogy and orientation. Electrokinetic signals should track fluid pressure, permeability, and conductivity. Fracture electrification should cluster around active cracking and surface creation. Positive-hole transport should show material-dependent activation and carrier behavior. Power-grid arcs should align with utility faults. Atmospheric lightning should appear in lightning networks.

A mature earthquake-light experiment would therefore be designed less like ghost hunting and more like fault diagnosis.

The sensor suite matters. Electric field alone is ambiguous. Add magnetic field, atmospheric ions, acoustic emission, pore pressure, strain, resistivity, broadband seismic motion, optical spectra, lightning detection, meteorology, and utility logs, and ambiguity begins to shrink. A visible discharge has a spectrum. A transformer arc has electrical companions. A corona has characteristic field conditions. If a geophysical mechanism is real, it should eventually produce a multichannel pattern that ordinary sources have difficulty imitating.

The word *eventually* is doing work there.

Most earthquake-light reports were never observed by such a system. They were seen by people. The modern cases were often caught accidentally by cameras designed for security, traffic, or personal recording. We are trying to infer source physics from an archive optimized for everything except source physics.

The abundance of electrical mechanisms therefore supports a cautious conclusion. Mechanical deformation of the crust can plausibly produce electrical phenomena. It does not follow that one channel dominates in nature, or that a luminous manifestation should be common.

A fault is not one electrical machine.

It is an argument among several.
