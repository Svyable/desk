# The Experiment That Would Count

The decisive earthquake-light experiment is not difficult to describe.

It is difficult to fund for long enough.

Choose several tectonic regions before any target earthquake occurs. Include at least one rift or steep continental fault environment favored by the historical earthquake-light hypothesis and at least one comparison environment. Build identical stations with synchronized all-sky low-light cameras, narrow-field cameras, optical spectrometers, electric-field mills, air-ion counters, broadband magnetometers, seismic sensors, infrasound, weather instruments, and lightning-network access. Obtain agreements with local utilities for timestamped fault and outage logs.

Then leave the stations running.

Do not trigger recording only after seismic unrest. Do not save only interesting nights. The boring denominator is the experiment.

Before data collection, define a luminous event algorithm that can be audited. Preserve raw video. Require multi-camera confirmation where geometry permits. Classify known aircraft, meteors, lightning, grid arcs, fires, and camera artifacts using independent data. Publish uncertain events as uncertain rather than assigning them to the earthquake bin.

For the electrical side, define anomaly thresholds in advance and use matched control windows drawn from the same season, local time, weather, and geomagnetic conditions. If the stations detect unusual currents or fields, test whether they have optical companions and whether their timing is preseismic, coseismic, or postseismic.

The satellite component should be equally unforgiving.

Freeze an ionospheric anomaly detector using historical training data. Run it prospectively on Swarm, GNSS total electron content, radio-occultation, and space-weather inputs. Let the system issue alerts automatically without knowing future epicenters. Score the alert volume, geographic area, lead time, false positives, and missed earthquakes.

Most important, keep the mechanism and forecast tests separate.

A local coseismic luminous event could validate crust-atmosphere electrical coupling even if the forecasting model fails completely. A weak ionospheric forecasting signal could be statistically useful even if the optical mechanism turns out to be unrelated. The project should be designed so one result cannot rescue another.

There should also be defeat conditions.

If years of continuous observation across favorable faults produce no non-infrastructure luminous events despite many strong nearby earthquakes, the frequency claim should shrink. If anomalies occur equally often in control periods, the precursor claim should shrink. If rift enrichment vanishes after observation opportunity is controlled, the geological hypothesis should shrink. If a proposed positive-hole signal cannot propagate through realistic wet fractured rock at required scales, that mechanism should shrink.

This sounds obvious.

Frontier research rarely fails because nobody can imagine a positive result. It fails because nobody specifies what negative result would be allowed to matter.

The experiment that would count is the one built to disappoint us.
