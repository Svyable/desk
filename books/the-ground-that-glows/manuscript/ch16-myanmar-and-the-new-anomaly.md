# Myanmar and the New Anomaly

The precursor argument is not a historical curiosity. It is still being published in real time.

On March 28, 2025, a magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck central Myanmar along the Sagaing Fault. In 2025, researchers published an analysis of European Space Agency Swarm satellite magnetic-field data from the ten days before the earthquake. They reported significant anomalies in multiple field components, with the Y component especially pronounced, and compared the measurements with empirical relations developed in earlier work.

This is exactly the kind of study that keeps the field alive.

It is also exactly the kind that demands restraint.

A case study can ask whether a later earthquake was preceded by features consistent with a proposed precursor model. It cannot establish the model's forecasting performance unless the anomaly rule was fixed and evaluated prospectively or against a rigorous, independent denominator.

The Myanmar paper's value lies partly in its test of an existing empirical framework on a new large event. That is better than inventing a bespoke anomaly after the fact. But the deeper questions remain. How often do similar Swarm anomalies occur near the Sagaing region without a major earthquake? How sensitive are the results to geomagnetic filtering and baseline choice? Would the system have issued an alert before March 28 without knowledge of the future epicenter? What spatial area and time window would that alert have covered?

Those operational questions are not attacks on the paper. They are the next experiment.

A 2026 study using COSMIC-2 radio-occultation data to examine ionospheric scintillation anomalies similarly reported measurable detection performance across earthquake populations, including nontrivial false-positive rates. This kind of work is more useful than a gallery of spectacular precursors because it exposes the denominator.

Once false positives are visible, rhetoric changes.

A true-positive rate that sounds impressive in isolation may coexist with enough false alarms to make deployment impractical. Conversely, modest forecast skill may still teach us something real about coupling between the lower atmosphere and ionosphere. Scientific value and warning value need not move together.

There is another reason the new satellite literature matters. It shows that skepticism has not ended the empirical program. Better magnetic-field missions, GNSS constellations, radio occultation, machine learning, and larger archives make tests possible that early researchers could not perform.

That should make the standards stricter, not looser.

The era of 'an anomaly was found before the earthquake' should be ending. The interesting questions now are prospective: how much probability does the signal add, over what baseline, with what false-alarm burden, under what geomagnetic conditions, and across how many independent tectonic settings?

Myanmar is not the conclusion.

It is a chance to stop repeating the same experiment.
