# Forecasting Is Not Prophecy

Seismology already predicts earthquakes.

It just does not usually predict them in the way people mean when they ask.

Probabilistic seismic hazard models estimate the chance of damaging shaking over years or decades. Operational earthquake forecasting can update probabilities after swarms or large events. Earthquake early-warning systems detect rupture after it begins and send alerts ahead of the strongest shaking to places the waves have not yet reached.

None of these tells you on Tuesday that a magnitude 7.1 earthquake will strike a particular fault at 3:17 Thursday afternoon.

That narrower ability is what most precursor claims implicitly promise.

The distinction matters because established systems succeed by accepting uncertainty. A seismic hazard map does not pretend to know the date. Early warning does not pretend to know before rupture. It exploits the fact that electronic communication is faster than seismic waves. Both are useful because their claims fit what the physics and data can support.

A precursor system has to outperform that honesty.

It needs a defined lead time and spatial window. It needs enough specificity to justify an action that existing forecasts would not already recommend. If it only tells us that a tectonically active region is under stress, we knew that. If it fires after rupture begins, early warning may be faster and more reliable. If it produces one alert every few days for an earthquake that occurs once a decade, people will stop listening.

This is why the phrase *earthquake prediction* can damage good anomaly research. It forces a promising measurement to carry a public-service burden long before the measurement is understood.

Earthquake lights may be a particularly clean example. Suppose future cameras and field sensors establish that a certain class of blue corona-like discharge is genuinely generated by seismic stress in a subset of rift environments. That would be a discovery. If ninety percent of those discharges occur during or after strong shaking, it would still be almost useless for forecasting.

Science would have gained a phenomenon and failed to gain an oracle.

There is no contradiction in that outcome.

The same applies to satellite signals. A small statistical shift in ionospheric parameters before large earthquakes could reveal real lithosphere-atmosphere coupling while providing poor event-level classification. The mechanism paper and the forecasting paper would be answering different questions.

The mistake is insisting that the second result is the purpose of the first.

A planet can reveal itself without helping us schedule it.
