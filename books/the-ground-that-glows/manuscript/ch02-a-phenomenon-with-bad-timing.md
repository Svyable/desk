# A Phenomenon with Bad Timing

The phrase *earthquake light* encourages a mistake before the evidence has even been examined. It sounds like a species.

It is closer to a box label.

Into that box have gone blue-white flashes during shaking, reddish glows near horizons, luminous balls reported before earthquakes, flame-like emissions near the ground, diffuse sky brightness, and lights seen days before a later rupture. Some last less than a second. Others, in historical accounts, persist. Some occur near the causative fault. Some are reported far away. Some are photographed. Most are not.

A single mechanism would be convenient. Nature is not obliged to be convenient.

There are at least three timing regimes that should be treated separately. **Preseismic** reports occur before the main rupture. **Coseismic** reports occur during fault rupture or during the passage of seismic waves. **Postseismic** reports follow. The timing is not a filing detail. It determines which causal stories are even available.

For a coseismic light, there is abundant mechanical energy moving through the crust. Rock is fracturing. Pore pressure is changing. Conductive fluids are being forced through cracks. Seismic waves impose rapid stress changes far from the source. Power grids are also being shaken apart above ground. A coseismic flash therefore has many ordinary and geophysical candidate causes.

A light two days before the mainshock is a more extraordinary claim, but not because light itself is strange. The problem is causality across time. The preparatory process has to be sufficiently developed before dynamic rupture to produce the effect. Then that effect has to escape the source region and reach the observer or instrument. Then the observer has to know that the anomaly is connected to an impending earthquake rather than to everything else that can produce an anomaly.

The final step is where almost every public conversation becomes careless.

Suppose a satellite sees an unusual ionospheric patch three days before a magnitude 7 earthquake. The result is interesting only after three questions are answered. How often do similar patches occur without a nearby earthquake? How often do comparable earthquakes occur without the patch? Was the rule defining the patch fixed before the earthquake, or tuned afterward until this event looked special?

Without those questions, precursor science becomes a machine for turning coincidence into mechanism.

This is not a theoretical complaint. Earth's electromagnetic environment is violently variable. Solar activity changes the ionosphere. Geomagnetic storms move it. Thunderstorms couple energy upward. Human transmitters contaminate measurements. Day-night cycles, seasons, latitude, atmospheric tides, and plasma instabilities all alter the background. The same is true on the ground: soil moisture, rainfall, temperature, electrode chemistry, cultural noise, and nearby infrastructure can move an electrical trace.

The existence of noise does not prove the signal is absent. It changes the experiment required to find it.

A useful earthquake precursor must earn four things at once: a repeatable anomaly, a defensible physical link to earthquake preparation, a low enough false-alarm rate, and enough lead time to matter. That last condition is often forgotten. A gorgeous signal thirty seconds before rupture at a station sitting on the fault might be scientifically important while adding little beyond seismic early warning triggered after rupture has already begun.

Earthquake lights make this distinction unusually visible. A luminous event is psychologically irresistible. It is hard to look at a glowing sky near an earthquake and not feel that the planet has announced what it is doing. But announcement is not prediction.

The safest way to proceed is to make the timing carry the burden. A case that occurred during strong shaking belongs first in the coseismic drawer. A case hours before a mainshock belongs in the precursor drawer, but only provisionally until ordinary sources and retrospective selection are addressed. A historical light with uncertain timing belongs in a third drawer marked *phenomenology*, not proof.

There is no shame in these distinctions. They are what keeps a strange subject from dissolving into folklore.

The irony is that earthquake lights may become more scientifically respectable when their predictive glamour is stripped away. A real electrical consequence of rapid stress change in rock would already be remarkable. It would connect solid-state physics, fault mechanics, atmospheric electricity, and human observation in a way standard seismology rarely needs to consider.

We do not have to make the light prophetic to make it worth understanding.
