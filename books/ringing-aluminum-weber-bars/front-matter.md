# Ringing Aluminum

## Joseph Weber and the Gravity Waves That Wouldn't Repeat

Sven Hardy Benson

A scientist can be wrong in a way that leaves nothing behind. A bad calculation is corrected. A broken apparatus goes to a storeroom. A fashionable claim becomes an embarrassing citation and then disappears.

Joseph Weber was wrong in a more consequential way.

He built a machine for a phenomenon many physicists still regarded as nearly metaphysical, put a second machine a thousand kilometers away, looked for both to ring at once, and announced that the universe had answered. Other physicists then had to decide whether the answer belonged to the universe, the aluminum, the electronics, the statistics, or the man interpreting all four.

They built their own bars.

They found nothing.

That is usually where the anecdote ends. Then LIGO appears half a century later, heroic and correct, and history becomes a clean before-and-after photograph. The aluminum cylinder is the primitive ancestor. The laser interferometer is modernity. Science improves.

The real story is less hygienic. Weber's bars were not foolish machines. His coincidence idea was not foolish. His insistence on environmental controls was not foolish. The problem was that the distance between a plausible detector and a justified discovery is much larger than the distance between two laboratories.

That distance is the subject of this book.

The sources here are public: Weber's papers, the negative replication literature, later histories by participants, gravitational-wave detector literature, and the LIGO/Virgo record. Where participants disagree about what happened, the disagreement stays visible. Where later historians infer motives, those inferences are not promoted into facts. Weber's failed claim is interesting enough without turning him into either a martyr or a fool.
