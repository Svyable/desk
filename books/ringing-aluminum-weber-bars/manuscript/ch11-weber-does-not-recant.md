# Weber Does Not Recant

Joseph Weber did not solve the historical discomfort by changing his mind.

As the null results accumulated and the community's confidence in his detections collapsed, Weber continued to defend his work. He kept operating detectors. He published further claims. In later years he associated signals with events such as Supernova 1987A, claims that did not restore confidence among gravitational-wave experimentalists.

The easy psychological story writes itself: pioneer becomes attached to discovery, criticism becomes identity threat, and stubbornness replaces inference. Some of that may be true. It is also dangerously convenient because it lets everyone who eventually agreed with the consensus imagine that they would have known exactly when to abandon the result.

Scientists rarely experience consensus as a timestamp.

From inside a disputed experiment, each negative replication has details. Frequencies differ. Bandwidths differ. thresholds differ. Detectors are not co-located. Data cuts differ. Critics may misunderstand the original hardware. Some criticisms are poor. Some published nulls later need correction. Personality conflict can make legitimate technical points easier to dismiss.

There is no instrument on the laboratory bench labeled "consensus now valid."

This does not rescue Weber's gravitational-wave interpretation. It makes his persistence more scientifically interesting.

A discovery claim generates sunk intellectual capital. The claimant has spent years learning the apparatus. He knows failure modes that outsiders do not. That expertise creates a genuine reason to discount superficial criticism. Unfortunately, the same expertise can also make every contradiction explainable.

This is the expert's trap: the person best equipped to find a subtle reason the result survives is also best equipped to keep finding one.

Fields need social mechanisms to break that loop. Independent laboratories matter because they do not share the original team's tacit commitments. Shared data can matter. Predefined analysis can matter. External calibration can matter. Multi-instrument observation can matter. None is perfect, but all redistribute interpretive authority.

Weber's isolation therefore became evidentiary. At first, being the only laboratory seeing the effect could be a marker of technical leadership. Later, as competitors reached relevant sensitivity and still saw nothing, uniqueness became a liability.

This reversal happens in many anomaly domains. The first exclusive observation is exciting. The hundredth exclusive observation is a methodological problem.

There is also a moral temptation to punish Weber retrospectively because he did not provide the tidy scientific virtue of recantation. Yet the history of physics is full of stubborn people who were eventually right and stubborn people who were eventually wrong. Stubbornness itself cannot adjudicate a signal.

The data have to do that work.

By the end of the 1970s, the community's practical judgment was clear enough: Weber's claimed event population was not accepted as gravitational radiation. Research moved on without waiting for its founder to agree.

Science does not require unanimous psychological closure.

It requires an evidentiary architecture capable of functioning without it.
