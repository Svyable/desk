# A Coincidence Is Not Yet a Cause

Coincidence is one of science's most seductive words because it sounds almost like causality without having to say so.

Two bars ring within the permitted interval. The event is rare under a background model. Local seismic disturbances have been monitored. Electromagnetic tests have been performed. At some point the analyst must decide whether the remaining excess belongs to the sky.

The difficulty is that a coincidence probability answers a narrower question than discovery language usually implies. It estimates how often events satisfying a particular definition should align under a particular model of accidentals. It does not, by itself, establish that every other source of correlation, selection, threshold drift, nonstationarity or analysis flexibility has been exhausted.

Weber's critics increasingly concentrated on exactly this gap. The literature of the early 1970s became an argument not merely about whether events existed but about how an event came into existence on paper.

Suppose each bar generates candidate pulses at some rate. If those candidates are independent and stationary, the expected accidental coincidence rate can be estimated from the individual rates and the coincidence window. But real instruments are not obliging Poisson machines. Their noise changes with time. Threshold crossings cluster. Electronics drift. Maintenance creates gaps. Data quality changes. An analyst may respond by discarding bad intervals, changing thresholds or adopting filters that better match the apparent events.

Every one of those actions can be reasonable. The problem is cumulative freedom.

A probability calculation performed at the end may treat the final rules as though they had been fixed before the data were seen. If they were not, the nominal improbability can exaggerate the evidence. This is not an accusation of fraud. It is one of the ordinary ways discovery statistics become too optimistic.

Modern fields have formal names for parts of this problem: trials factors, look-elsewhere effects, p-hacking, overfitting, data snooping. Those labels came from many disciplines and should not be back-projected carelessly onto Weber. What matters is the structure. A detector is also an analysis space. The more ways an event can be defined after inspection, the more ways noise can be taught to qualify.

There is a second complication. Coincidences can be genuine and still not be gravitational waves. Two instruments may share environmental influences on larger scales than expected. They may share timekeeping artifacts. They may share processing assumptions that produce similar temporal structures. Or the coincidence may arise from a high background rate interacting with a generous window.

This is why independent detectors are necessary but not sufficient.

The phrase sounds almost disappointingly modest after half a century of heroic discovery imagery. Yet it is one of the most durable experimental lessons. Multiple instruments reduce some classes of uncertainty while leaving others untouched. They change the shape of skepticism; they do not abolish it.

Weber's great insight was to realize that separation in space could attack local noise. His mistake, or one of them, was treating the surviving coincidence structure as carrying more causal specificity than it did.

The bars did not have to be lying for the inference to fail.

They only had to be answering a different question.
