# The Filter Decides What Exists

A gravitational-wave detector never hands the scientist a gravitational wave. It hands over voltage.

Between voltage and astronomy sits a filter.

This is obvious in modern data analysis, where pipelines are explicit software systems with documented transfer functions, templates, vetoes, ranking statistics and background estimates. In Weber's era the same conceptual machinery existed in more compact and often more hands-on forms. The bar vibrated. A transducer produced an electrical signal. Electronics and analysis extracted an estimate of the relevant mode energy or amplitude. Thresholds created events from continuous data.

James Levine later argued that a crucial difference between Weber's treatment and the replication experiments lay in the signal processing. In his 2004 historical account, Levine emphasized Weber's use of a quadratic detector and contrasted it with the linear processing adopted by other groups. He also described what he regarded as artifacts of extremely hands-on data analysis.

That is a participant's retrospective account and should be treated as such, not as a court judgment that identifies one magical bug. But it points to a fundamental feature of frontier instrumentation: the processing chain is part of the detector.

A resonant bar has a narrow mechanical response. Thermal motion dominates. The analyst wants to estimate whether an impulsive excitation occurred and how energetic it was. Different filters weight the time series differently. Different assumptions about the signal shape lead to different optimum strategies. A threshold placed on a processed statistic can create a candidate population whose properties depend as much on the filter as on the raw motion.

This is not suspicious. It is measurement.

The trouble begins when the transformation from raw sensor output to reported event is treated as neutral. It never is.

Every mature observatory eventually learns to characterize the entire measurement chain. Signals are injected. Calibration lines are tracked. Instrument state is recorded. Different pipelines analyze overlapping data. Software versions matter. Environmental monitors become data streams in their own right. Analysts spend astonishing amounts of effort proving that an event was not produced by a detector subsystem nobody outside the collaboration has heard of.

Again, the historical claim here must be bounded. LIGO did not invent detector characterization because Joseph Weber once used a quadratic demodulator. The modern architecture grew from decades of experimental physics, statistics, controls engineering and gravitational-wave work across multiple technologies.

But Weber's controversy supplies a brutally clear early example of why the processing chain cannot remain invisible.

If two groups build mechanically similar bars but analyze them differently, have they replicated the experiment?

There is no yes-or-no answer until the expected signal and noise are passed through both pipelines. Replication requires equivalence at the level of detectability, not visual resemblance of hardware.

This is why Garwin and Levine emphasized demonstrated detection efficiency. A calibration impulse was not decorative. It asked the apparatus, electronics and analysis to recover a known event. The detector had to prove it could hear the kind of thing whose absence it reported.

A claim becomes much harder to defend when the critics can say both: we would have heard your events, and we did not.

The filter does not decide what exists in nature.

It decides what the experiment is capable of calling real.
