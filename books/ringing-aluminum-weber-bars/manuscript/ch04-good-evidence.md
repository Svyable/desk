# Good Evidence

On June 16, 1969, Physical Review Letters published Weber's paper titled "Evidence for Discovery of Gravitational Radiation." The title now reads like a warning label. At the time it was an invitation.

The paper reported coincident events from Maryland and Argonne. Weber argued that the accidental-coincidence probability was extremely small and that seismic and electromagnetic explanations had been constrained. The inference was explicit: gravitational radiation had been discovered.

A result like that does two things at once. It announces a phenomenon and advertises an experiment.

If the phenomenon is real, the scientific reward for confirmation is enormous. If the experiment is flawed, the scientific reward for finding the flaw is also substantial. Either way, other laboratories suddenly have a reason to build heavy aluminum cylinders.

Weber's result also had an advantage many extraordinary claims lack: it was legible to mainstream physics. Nothing in the hypothesis required a new force, a paranormal faculty or a secret material. General relativity predicted gravitational waves. The controversy was whether this apparatus had seen them at this intensity and rate.

That makes the episode unusually clean for thinking about scientific credibility. The underlying phenomenon was real. We know that now. A correct theory can therefore sit behind an incorrect discovery claim.

This should make us cautious whenever people use later vindication of a broad phenomenon to rehabilitate an earlier specific detection. LIGO's 2015 observation does not make Weber's 1969 events more likely to have been gravitational waves merely because both carry the same category name. The source populations, amplitudes, frequencies, detectors and analysis contexts matter.

The same error appears throughout science. A disease mechanism is later validated, so every early anecdotal treatment claim is remembered as prescient. An exoplanet class is later found, so a disputed photographic speck acquires retroactive glamour. A real intelligence program is declassified, so unrelated allegations are said to have been confirmed "in principle."

Reality does not backdate evidence.

Weber's own papers grew more ambitious. In 1970 he reported anisotropy in detector intensity with a sidereal-time pattern he associated with the galactic center and the opposite direction expected from the antenna symmetry. This was seductive because it seemed to add astronomy to instrumentation. Noise might make a bar ring. Noise that knew where the Milky Way was would be harder to explain.

Again, the same warning applies. A directional pattern is downstream of analysis choices, duty cycle, threshold behavior and detector response. A pattern can strengthen a signal hypothesis only to the extent that its construction was not itself flexible enough to manufacture structure.

The best version of Weber's case in 1970 was not absurd: two distant detectors, coincident events, environmental checks, and an apparent sidereal anisotropy. This is precisely why the story matters. Bad evidence that looks obviously bad teaches little about the frontier.

The frontier is where competent people can assemble a chain of individually reasonable moves and still arrive at the wrong sky.

The field now had to test that chain one link at a time.
