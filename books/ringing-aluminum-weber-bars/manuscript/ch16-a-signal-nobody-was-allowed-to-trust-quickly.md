# A Signal Nobody Was Allowed to Trust Quickly

On September 14, 2015, the two Advanced LIGO detectors recorded a transient signal separated by milliseconds.

It was loud by gravitational-wave standards. The waveform swept upward in frequency in the characteristic manner expected from a compact binary coalescence. The Livingston and Hanford data were mutually consistent. The inferred source was a pair of stellar-mass black holes merging more than a billion light-years away.

The event would become GW150914.

The public announcement did not come until February 11, 2016.

That delay is often narrated as scientific caution, which is true but incomplete. The collaboration was not merely waiting to feel emotionally certain. It was executing an evidentiary program.

The detector state had to be checked. Calibration had to be validated. Environmental channels had to be inspected. Independent search pipelines had to estimate the event's significance. Waveform consistency had to be tested. The background distribution had to be built from data. Instrumental explanations had to be attacked.

Crucially, the result was seen at two sites. But now coincidence was only the beginning.

The event's signal-to-noise ratio was high, and its morphology matched general-relativistic binary-merger expectations. Less-modeled transient searches also recovered it. The significance assessment depended on extensive time-shift analyses and understanding of the detectors' coincident observing time. Companion papers examined detector performance, source properties and tests of general relativity.

This was not an experiment free of judgment. No important experiment is. Analysis choices, waveform models, data-quality decisions and calibration uncertainties all remained. The difference was that those choices had been multiplied into explicit systems that could be inspected, challenged and cross-checked.

GW150914 therefore makes a useful counterimage to Weber without turning history into a fable.

Both involved two distant instruments.

Both involved a rare coincidence.

Both required an interpretation of continuous noisy data.

But the density of constraints surrounding the later event was radically different.

The shape of the waveform carried source information. The rate was astrophysically plausible. The detector network's background could be empirically estimated. Environmental and instrumental channels were deeply monitored. Calibration was continuously modeled. Multiple pipelines with different assumptions found the event.

Most importantly, the field had spent decades learning how a detector can manufacture conviction.

When LIGO announced discovery, it was not vindicating every earlier gravitational-wave claim.

It was demonstrating what the word could be made to cost.
