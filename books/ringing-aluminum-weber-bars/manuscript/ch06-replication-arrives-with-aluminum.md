# Replication Arrives with Aluminum

The most consequential response to Weber was not an editorial. It was procurement.

Laboratories built bars.

This is science at its most expensive form of disagreement. A critic can always say an experiment is wrong. A serious replication group accepts the machinery of the claim, spends money, hires people, fights its own noise, calibrates its own detector, and asks nature the same question with different hands.

By the early 1970s multiple groups were doing exactly that. IBM, Bell Labs, Stanford, Glasgow, Munich-Frascati and others entered the field. The details differed, which became both strength and complication. A nominal replication is never truly identical. Bars had different masses, resonance frequencies, transducers, filtering schemes, temperatures, bandwidths and analysis pipelines.

This gives a claimant room to survive negative results. Perhaps the replication was not sensitive to the same signals. Perhaps its bandwidth missed them. Perhaps the signal population varied. Perhaps calibration differed. Perhaps the original instrument had a property the replicas inadvertently removed.

Some of these objections can be legitimate. Replication is not magic. An experiment that asks a slightly different question can obtain a null result without contradicting the original.

That is why the argument around Weber became increasingly technical. Critics did not merely report silence. They worked to show that their detectors would have seen events like Weber's if those events were arriving with the claimed properties.

James Levine and Richard Garwin at IBM became central to this phase. Their 1973 Physical Review Letters work reported absence of gravity-wave signals in a bar whose detection efficiency could be demonstrated with calibration impulses. The next year they published another negative result with a larger detector and explicitly compared it with Weber's reported detections, concluding that Weber's events did not arise from gravitational radiation.

The calibration detail is the hinge.

A null experiment has to prove it knows how to hear. Otherwise silence means nothing.

This is one of the deepest legacies of replication controversies. Sensitivity cannot remain a specification on a schematic. It has to be demonstrated end to end. Inject something signal-like. Show that the pipeline recovers it. Measure efficiency as a function of amplitude. Characterize dead periods. Estimate the background from the actual data rather than from an idealized noise assumption.

Modern gravitational-wave collaborations do these things with vastly more sophisticated tools, including software and hardware injections. It would be historically reckless to claim they all descend directly from one quarrel in the 1970s. But the family resemblance is unmistakable because the problem is the same.

How do you know your detector would recognize the thing you say is absent?

Weber had converted gravitational waves from a theoretical possibility into an experimental target. His replicators converted his target into a testable dispute.

The field was becoming real even as his discovery was becoming less so.
