# Garwin Builds the Argument

Richard Garwin could have criticized Weber from a blackboard. Instead he helped build a bar.

That decision gave the dispute a different character. Garwin was not merely saying that Weber's astrophysics looked implausible. With James Levine at IBM, he participated in an experiment designed to be sensitive to the kind of impulses Weber was reporting and then looked for them.

The 1973 IBM result described a 118-kilogram bar at 1695 hertz. Calibration impulses were used to demonstrate detection efficiency above a chosen threshold. During the reported observing period the team did not see the population Weber's claims seemed to require. In 1974 Garwin and Levine reported a second negative result with a 500-kilogram detector and concluded explicitly that Weber's detections did not arise from gravitational radiation.

This is stronger than saying, "we saw nothing."

A negative experiment must earn its negation.

Imagine a microphone pointed at a forest where someone claims to hear an ivory-billed woodpecker. Silence from your microphone tells us little if the microphone cannot pick up a known woodpecker at the relevant distance. The experimental analog is calibration: inject a disturbance with known properties and measure how often the system recovers it.

This turns a null result into a statement about excluded signal space.

Garwin also attacked the astrophysical consequences of Weber's event rate. The famous rhetoric about the universe exhausting its energy if the signals were real was memorable because it joined detector criticism to source physics. The problem was no longer just that other bars were quiet. Weber's loud sky had to make sense as a universe.

Public disagreement became sharp. In retrospective accounts, the 1974 MIT gravitational-wave meeting is often treated as a point at which Garwin's criticism became devastatingly explicit. It is tempting to write the scene as a duel between personalities. That would be cheap. The real asymmetry lay in accumulated evidence.

By then there were multiple independent experiments. Bell Labs and other groups were also reporting null results. The center of gravity had moved from "Weber has an extraordinary claim" to "why does Weber alone continue to see it?"

That is a dangerous transition for any experimentalist. Early on, a nonreplication can be explained by differences in sensitivity. A second may be bad luck. A third may reveal unknown source intermittency. But as competent groups accumulate calibrated nulls, the special pleading required to preserve the original result grows.

One can imagine a world in which Weber was right and everyone else built the wrong machines. Science has known cases where a difficult effect vanished outside a single laboratory because tacit craft mattered. The possibility cannot be eliminated by sociology.

It can, however, become quantitatively expensive.

To preserve Weber's interpretation, one had to believe a source population intense enough to excite his antennas was repeatedly escaping detectors built to test exactly that proposition. The burden shifted.

This burden shift is one of the least formal but most important operations in science. No committee votes it into existence. It happens when the alternative explanation has to acquire more exceptions than the skeptical one.

Garwin's bar mattered because it made skepticism expensive too. He could not simply say Weber was mistaken. He had to construct enough of Weber's world to show that the predicted sky did not arrive.
