# The Bell That Heard the Universe

An aluminum cylinder does not look like an astronomical instrument. It has no mirror, no eyepiece, no dish aimed at a patch of sky. Joseph Weber's detector looked closer to a piece of industrial stock someone had forgotten to machine. That visual awkwardness matters because the experiment depended on asking the reader to accept something conceptually violent: the universe might pass through a metal bar and make it ring.

Not ring in the ordinary sense. No human ear was going to hear spacetime. The cylinder was a resonant mass, suspended and instrumented so that a passing gravitational wave might deposit a minute amount of energy into one of its mechanical modes. Piezoelectric transducers converted the motion into an electrical signal. The bar was an antenna only because general relativity said matter should respond to changing spacetime geometry.

By the mid-1960s, Weber had moved beyond proposing such an antenna and was operating one. This was itself a conceptual achievement. Gravitational radiation had occupied an uncomfortable place in relativity for decades. Physicists had argued about coordinates, energy, and whether the waves were physically meaningful or partly artifacts of description. Theoretical clarity improved during the 1950s. Weber's instinct was to treat the argument as unfinished until somebody built hardware.

Hardware is rude to metaphysics. It asks how many kilograms, what resonance frequency, what thermal noise, what transducer, what bandwidth, what isolation, what threshold. A question about whether spacetime can carry radiation becomes a question about bolts and vacuum systems.

Weber had been an electrical engineer before becoming a gravitational physicist. He had worked with radar in the Navy. That biography can be made too neat after the fact, but there is an engineering cast to the whole program. He was not waiting for gravity to become observationally convenient. He wanted a receiver.

The difficult part was that a receiver this sensitive also receives itself. Aluminum at finite temperature moves. Supports move. The ground moves. Electronics make noise. The detector is never silent enough for a scientist to point at a pulse and say: there, gravity. A signal has to be distinguished from a population of events generated by the apparatus and its environment.

Weber's solution was partly physical and partly statistical. Build a high-Q resonant bar. Isolate it. Measure its thermal behavior. Then operate more than one detector. Local disturbances should not strike two distant antennas at the same time. A true gravitational wave could.

This is the first important fact to keep: Weber's eventual error did not begin with a contempt for controls. It grew inside a serious attempt to create them.

In 1967 he published a Physical Review Letters paper reporting early operation of his detector. The wording was still careful. Some gravitational signals, he wrote in effect, could not be completely excluded. Two years later the claim hardened. Coincident events had been observed between detectors at the University of Maryland and Argonne National Laboratory, roughly a thousand kilometers apart. Weber calculated that chance was an extraordinarily implausible explanation. Seismic and electromagnetic explanations, he argued, had also been constrained.

The machine had become a telescope without an image.

That created a peculiar epistemic burden. Astronomers are accustomed to instruments whose artifacts can imitate celestial structure, but a resonant bar offered almost no humanly intuitive distinction between a cosmic event and a statistical accident. There was only a time series, a threshold, an analysis rule, another time series, and a coincidence.

Weber thought the coincidences were the universe.

The rest of the field was about to learn how expensive that sentence could become.
