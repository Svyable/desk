# Two Bars, a Thousand Kilometers

The second bar was not just another instrument. It was an argument.

Put two detectors far enough apart and the ordinary world should stop conspiring so effectively. A truck near College Park cannot shake Argonne at the same instant. A local electrical transient should not reproduce itself hundreds of miles away. A technician dropping something beside one bar is irrelevant to the other. If both detectors report an event within an appropriate time window, the coincidence is more interesting than either pulse alone.

This logic remains powerful. Modern gravitational-wave observatories use networks for reasons that include coincidence, localization and polarization. Particle physics, neutrino astronomy and high-energy astrophysics all live on variants of the same principle: independent channels can turn an ambiguous event into a credible one.

But independence is never bestowed by mileage.

The two bars can share an analysis procedure. They can share thresholds. They can share assumptions about the shape and duration of a signal. They can share the same code—or, in Weber's era, the same human choices about what qualifies as an event. Their errors can be correlated conceptually even when the laboratories are geographically separate.

There is also the problem of the coincidence window. Make the allowed window too narrow and a real signal can be lost if timing is imperfect. Make it too wide and accidental pairings multiply. Change the event threshold and the background rate changes, which changes how surprising the coincidences are. Decide after looking at the data which cuts seem sensible and a nominal probability can become more impressive than the full analysis history warrants.

None of this proves what happened in Weber's pipeline. The exact causal anatomy of his excess is still a historical and technical question, and participant accounts deserve to be separated from demonstrated errors. But the larger lesson does not require a single smoking bug.

A coincidence statistic is downstream of a detection definition.

That sounds obvious now because entire subfields have been built around it. It is less obvious when the instrument is new and the analysis is being invented by the same small group that designed the hardware. Every boundary in the pipeline contains judgment: how to filter, how to estimate background, how to treat nonstationary periods, how to reject local disturbances, how to define dead time, how to quantify efficiency.

Weber tried to address some of these problems directly. His 1969 paper described seismic monitoring and electromagnetic shielding tests. The paper's conclusion was strong: the coincidences were, in his assessment, neither accidental nor attributable to the tested seismic and electromagnetic mechanisms. He called that good evidence for gravitational radiation.

The phrase 'good evidence' is more interesting than 'discovery.' It reveals the actual contest. Weber believed he had moved enough alternative explanations out of the way that gravitational radiation was the remaining serious cause.

His critics did not need to know the exact cause of his events to disagree.

That asymmetry is fundamental in anomaly research. The claimant may say: I have ruled out A, B and C, therefore X is supported. The skeptic can answer: your instrument has more failure modes than A, B and C, and your analysis has more flexibility than your probability calculation admits. The skeptic is not required to name the hidden variable before refusing the extraordinary conclusion.

This can feel unfair to the experimentalist. A new phenomenon is always surrounded by unknown unknowns. If every unexplained event is presumed instrumental until its instrument is omniscient, discovery becomes impossible.

The answer is replication.

Not another bar run by the same hands. Another group. Another apparatus. Another pipeline. Another set of mistakes.

Weber had built the argument that would eventually be used against him.
