# Notes on Evidence

This book distinguishes the reality of gravitational waves from the validity of Joseph Weber's reported detections. The former is established by modern observations. The latter is not rescued retroactively by those observations.

The strongest documentary core is the contemporary literature: Weber's detector and detection papers, the independent null experiments, theoretical criticism of the implied flux and source population, and later resonant-mass work. Retrospective histories by participants are valuable but treated as participant evidence, particularly when assigning responsibility for analysis failure.

The comparison with LIGO is structural, not a claim of direct institutional descent. Modern calibration, data-quality systems, injection practice, background estimation and multi-pipeline analysis grew from many sources over decades. The book argues that Weber's controversy exposed an early version of the same epistemic problem, not that every modern solution was invented because of him.

## Defeat conditions

The book's synthesis would need substantial revision if primary records showed that the major independent experiments were not actually sensitive to Weber-like signals; if reanalysis of preserved Weber data with transparent modern methods recovered a statistically robust, astrophysically plausible coincident population; or if archival evidence showed that the apparent methodological parallels to later gravitational-wave practice were retrospective inventions unsupported by the field's actual development.

None of those possibilities should be excluded by rhetoric.
