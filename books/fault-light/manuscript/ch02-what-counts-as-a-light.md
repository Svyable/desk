# What Counts as a Light

“Earthquake light” sounds like a mechanism. It is only a bucket.

The USGS description is admirably untidy: sheet lightning, balls of light, streamers, steady glows. Those are not interchangeable observations. Some resemble atmospheric discharge. Some resemble electrical infrastructure failing. Some resemble luminous phenomena reported in settings with no obvious electrical network. A category this broad can manufacture agreement by vocabulary alone.

So the book treats each report as a small forensic case. Time is the first axis. A light that appears only after strong ground motion has begun competes immediately with power-line arcing and transformer failure. A light minutes or hours before detectable shaking faces a different evidentiary problem: it avoids the easiest infrastructure explanation but becomes vulnerable to retrospective timing error and the vast denominator of nights on which people see odd lights and no earthquake follows.

Space is next. A luminous event directly above a damaged substation is not evidentially equivalent to a glow reported along a sparsely electrified fault valley. Geometry matters. So do weather, cloud base, topography and line of sight.

The result is less romantic than a catalog of glowing orbs and more useful. The question is not whether “earthquake lights” exist as one thing. The question is whether, after classification, there remains one or more reproducible luminous classes whose occurrence rate changes around specific kinds of crustal stress or rupture.

That is a much harder claim. It is also one that can fail cleanly.
