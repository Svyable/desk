# L'Aquila

After the 6 April 2009 L'Aquila earthquake in central Italy, Cristiano Fidani collected testimony about non-seismic phenomena, including luminous reports. The resulting 2010 paper cataloged observations extending across a broad area and over a long time window.

L'Aquila is valuable and dangerous for exactly the same reason: it contains a lot of testimony. Large testimonial datasets invite pattern recognition. They also invite selection effects, contaminated memory and flexible temporal windows.

Reports said to begin months before the main shock are not automatically precursors. Widen the window far enough and ordinary luminous events accumulate. The denominator becomes the missing object: how many comparable lights were seen in the same region during periods without a damaging earthquake? How many residents noticed something odd but never reported it? How did reporting intensity change after the catastrophe?

Fidani's work remains useful because it records characteristics and locations rather than erasing them into a single claim. It provides hypotheses about relationships to local landscape and faulting. Those hypotheses deserve prospective tests, not retrospective applause.

L'Aquila also carries a public-safety warning. Earthquake prediction in Italy is not an abstract topic; communication around seismic risk has had legal and political consequences. Turning an uncertain light report into an alarm can cause harm. Refusing to investigate a genuine precursor can also have cost. The standard has to be higher than intuition in either direction.
