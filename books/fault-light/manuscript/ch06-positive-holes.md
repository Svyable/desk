# Positive Holes

“Positive holes” sound invented for a science-fiction prop. They belong to solid-state physics.

In Freund's formulation, dormant electronic defects in common rock-forming minerals can be activated when rocks are stressed, producing defect electrons that behave as positive charge carriers. Experiments have reported currents flowing out of stressed rock volumes. The proposed earthquake connection is that a sufficiently large stressed crustal volume might release charge that propagates through rock and accumulates near the surface.

The attractive part of the idea is that it connects several classes of reported anomaly without requiring a different miracle for each one: surface potentials, air ionization, some electromagnetic effects, perhaps luminous discharge. That unification is also the danger. A mechanism broad enough to explain many poorly measured observations can become hard to falsify.

The right test is numerical. How much stressed volume is required? What carrier density? How far can charge travel through heterogeneous wet crust before recombination or leakage dominates? What currents should a surface array measure? At what distance from the stressed region? What changes under different lithologies? If light is produced, what spectrum and duration follow?

Without those numbers, positive holes are a plausible vocabulary. With them, they become a model that instruments can embarrass.

This distinction runs through the whole book. Anomalies become science when they accept the possibility of public humiliation.
