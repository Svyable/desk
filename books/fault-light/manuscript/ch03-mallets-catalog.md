# Mallet's Catalog

Long before cameras became cheap, earthquakes already came with light stories.

Robert Mallet, one of the nineteenth century's foundational figures in seismology, collected descriptions of earthquake phenomena that included luminosities. Older chronicles go back much farther. Their value is not that ancient witnesses were precision instruments. Their value is that the association between seismic events and anomalous light was not invented by social media, UFO culture or the modern electrical grid.

Historical continuity is weaker evidence than enthusiasts sometimes make it. A recurring story can recur because people inherit expectations. Religious interpretation, fire, lightning, aurora, meteors and urban conflagration all contaminate early accounts. Translation can flatten distinct words into “light.” Dates can drift. Epicenters can be reconstructed centuries later with false precision.

But old catalogs do something modern databases often fail to do: they remind us that observation starts messy. Mallet did not have the luxury of pretending earthquakes were only waveforms. People reported sounds, odors, changes in water, animal behavior, flame-like appearances and sky effects. Most would not become reliable precursors. Some would turn out to be secondary effects. A few might be physical signals whose mechanisms were not yet available.

A mature science does not owe every old report belief. It owes unusual recurring reports a fair attempt at discrimination. The archive becomes useful when it stops being a treasury of marvels and becomes a source of testable metadata: geology, fault style, timing, distance, infrastructure, weather, and independent witnesses.

The nineteenth century left us stories. The twenty-first has to decide which variables the stories were accidentally recording.
