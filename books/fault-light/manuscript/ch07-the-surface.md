# The Surface

Every subsurface precursor story eventually reaches the same border: rock ends, air begins.

A charge arriving at the ground is not yet a visible light. The proposed chain requires surface accumulation, atmospheric ionization or electrical breakdown, and enough emitted photons to be seen against whatever the sky and city are already doing. Each transition can fail independently.

Freund and colleagues have proposed corona-like discharge and high-frequency electromagnetic emission as consequences of intense surface charge. That provides a laboratory-informed route from stressed rock to luminous air effects. But crustal realism is brutal. Soil moisture changes conductivity. Vegetation changes surface geometry. Groundwater moves ions. Rain changes air conductivity. Topography concentrates electric fields. Human infrastructure adds its own electromagnetic noise.

A serious field experiment therefore cannot consist of a camera aimed at a fault. It needs electric-field mills, magnetometers, air-ion counters, weather stations, utility telemetry, all-sky cameras, spectrometers and ordinary seismology tied to one clock. Ideally it needs several sites far enough apart to distinguish local electrical failures from regional effects.

The surface is where elegant mechanisms encounter mud, wires and weather. That is not a reason to abandon them. It is the reason to instrument them properly.
