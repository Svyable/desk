# The Power-Line Problem

A shaking city is an electrical experiment nobody designed.

Conductors swing. Lines touch. Insulators fail. Transformers arc. Protective equipment trips. Fires begin. Low clouds turn local flashes into sky-wide illumination. Anyone studying earthquake lights who does not begin here is studying belief rather than evidence.

USGS explicitly notes that some supposed earthquake-light reports have turned out to be electricity arcing from shaking power lines. That single fact should shape the whole field. It creates a powerful confound exactly when the phenomenon of interest is most likely to be reported: during strong ground motion in populated areas.

The confound is useful because it forces better questions. If a luminous event begins after the arrival of strong waves, what electrical infrastructure was within line of sight? Do utility fault logs show a coincident trip? Do videos reveal a source near distribution equipment? Does the spectrum resemble an arc? Does the flash repeat with mechanical movement?

The harder cases are those that precede shaking or occur where infrastructure is sparse. They should not receive automatic promotion to geology. Vehicles, meteors, lightning beyond the observer's horizon, industrial activity, camera artifacts and fabricated stories remain available.

This is where anomaly research usually becomes emotional. Debunkers are accused of refusing the strange; believers are accused of refusing the ordinary. The better posture is procedural. Give conventional explanations first access to the evidence. Make them work. Record where they fit and where they do not.

A phenomenon that disappears under that pressure deserved to disappear. One that does not becomes more interesting precisely because the easy cases were removed.
