# Rocks That Carry Charge

The leap from folklore to physics begins with an impolite question: can stressed rock do anything electrical at all?

The answer is yes, though the implications are contested. Laboratory studies have measured electrical effects associated with stressed rocks. One influential line of work, associated especially with Friedemann Freund and collaborators, argues that defects in oxygen anion sublattices can produce mobile positive-hole charge carriers under stress in certain rocks. In this model, charge can migrate away from a stressed volume rather than remaining imprisoned at the crack where it originated.

That matters because earthquake-light hypotheses have a transport problem. A fault kilometers deep cannot simply “spark the sky.” Some process must couple stress or rupture to the surface and atmosphere. A charge-carrier mechanism offers one possible bridge.

Possible is carrying almost the whole sentence. Laboratory activation does not establish crustal scale. Current in a rock sample does not establish luminous air discharge over a fault. A mechanism that can produce an effect does not prove that it produced any historical sighting.

The temptation in anomaly research is to treat a plausible mechanism as retrospective confirmation. This book does the reverse. Mechanism raises the evidentiary bar. Once a pathway is proposed, it should predict materials, stress regimes, current densities, timing, spatial patterns, atmospheric conditions and electromagnetic signatures better than an unstructured story can.

Physics is useful here not because it makes earthquake lights respectable. It makes them easier to kill.
