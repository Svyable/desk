# Sixty-Five Cases

In 2014 Robert Thériault, France St-Laurent, Friedemann Freund and John Derr published an analysis of 65 reported earthquake-light cases.

The numbers are difficult to ignore.

About **85 percent** of the selected cases occurred on or near rift environments. **Sixty-three of the sixty-five—97 percent—were at or adjacent to regional subvertical faults**, including rifts, grabens, strike-slip and transform structures. Roughly **80 percent** of the earthquakes in the catalog were larger than magnitude 5.

If those percentages describe nature rather than the way the catalog was assembled, they are enormously informative. A phenomenon that preferentially appears in particular tectonic geometries is harder to dismiss as generic earthquake folklore. Geology would be acting like a filter.

But the percentages have a denominator problem.

The 65 events were not 65 earthquakes sampled at random and then checked for light. They were 65 earthquakes already associated with what the authors judged to be reliable earthquake-light reports, drawn from historical material in the Americas and Europe going back centuries.

That is a case series.

A case series can discover a pattern inside cases. It cannot, by itself, tell us how often the same geological setting occurs when the outcome is absent.

The distinction is easiest to see with a medical analogy. Suppose 85 percent of patients in a rare-disease clinic have a particular exposure. That looks important. But if 85 percent of otherwise comparable people without the disease have the same exposure, the exposure explains nothing. To estimate association, you need the controls.

Earthquake lights need the same discipline.

The relevant control is not simply "all earthquakes on Earth."

That denominator would be too crude in the other direction.

A magnitude-4 earthquake beneath an uninhabited ocean in 1700 did not have the same chance of generating a preserved luminous report as a magnitude-7 earthquake near an electrified city in 1988. A daytime event and a nighttime event have different visual detectability. A region with newspapers, cameras, astronomical observers, power infrastructure and dense settlement has a different evidence-production system from a remote fault with none of those things. Even the confounds differ: electrification makes luminous infrastructure failures more likely at exactly the same time that it makes observation and documentation more likely.

So the missing denominator has to be matched.

For every cataloged earthquake-light case, imagine a set of earthquakes that were similar in the variables that determine whether a light could have been seen and preserved:

- magnitude and depth;
- distance to population;
- local time and season;
- observation era;
- electrification and utility infrastructure;
- weather and cloud cover where recoverable;
- archival density and language coverage;
- tectonic setting and fault geometry assessed without knowing the light outcome.

Then ask the question the 65-case catalog cannot answer alone:

**among earthquakes with comparable opportunities to produce and preserve a report, are rift and subvertical-fault events still disproportionately likely to produce qualifying luminosities?**

That is the causal hinge.

The published result is still useful before that study exists. It tells us where to look. It generates a prospective prediction. It identifies a geological variable that a mechanism ought to explain. But it should not be narrated as though 97 percent were already a population-level risk estimate.

The classification process deserves the same scrutiny.

"Reliable earthquake light" is not an instrument reading. It is a judgment applied to heterogeneous evidence: historical descriptions, photographs, scientific observations and reports collected under very different conditions. Some reports may be excellent. Others may survive because they fit a recognizable narrative. A case catalog must decide which witnesses count, how close in time and space a luminosity must be, whether power-line events are excluded, and how to handle multiple reports around one earthquake.

Those rules are not bureaucratic detail. They define the phenomenon.

Geological classification also needs blinding where possible. A fault described as "subvertical" after investigators know that a famous light report occurred nearby can unintentionally inherit the hypothesis. The clean version is to have geologists classify the tectonic geometry from independent mapping under rules fixed before the light data are examined.

None of this makes the 2014 paper weak.

It makes it a strong **hypothesis-generating paper**.

That category has become unfairly pejorative in some areas of science, as though generating a good hypothesis were a lesser achievement. It is not. A pattern this sharp can justify an expensive prospective experiment. What it cannot do is serve as its own independent confirmation.

The right next experiment is almost embarrassingly straightforward.

Choose several rift/subvertical-fault regions and several matched non-rift regions with substantial earthquake rates. Instrument them continuously before the earthquakes occur. Use synchronized all-sky cameras, calibrated photometers, electric-field mills, magnetometers, weather stations and—critically—utility-grid telemetry. Freeze the detection algorithm before the target events. Preserve every luminous anomaly, including the thousands that occur when there is no earthquake.

Then the denominator finally exists.

The experiment can estimate two probabilities instead of one:

`P(light-class anomaly | target earthquake, geology)`

and

`P(light-class anomaly | no target earthquake, same observing system)`.

It can also estimate the reverse probability that matters for prediction:

`P(target earthquake | light-class anomaly, geology)`.

Those are very different objects.

A phenomenon can be genuinely enriched before earthquakes and still be useless as a warning because luminous anomalies are common for unrelated reasons. Conversely, a very rare, well-characterized optical/electrical signature could be scientifically valuable even if it precedes only a minority of earthquakes.

The 65 cases also contain a clue about scale. If 80 percent are above magnitude 5, either stronger earthquakes are physically more capable of producing the relevant process, or stronger earthquakes are more likely to generate witnesses, damage, news coverage and retrospective investigation—or both. A matched design can begin to separate those possibilities.

This is why spectacular percentages should make a scientist more specific, not more certain.

**Eighty-five percent near rifts** is not yet the probability of earthquake lights in rifts.

**Ninety-seven percent near subvertical faults** is not yet proof that subvertical faults generate the phenomenon.

They are conditional summaries of a selected case set.

Their scientific value is that they make a prediction dangerous enough to fail.

If a decade of preregistered, instrumented monitoring finds no excess luminous/electrical event rate around matched rift-zone earthquakes, the geological association should be demoted no matter how compelling the historical cases remain.

If the excess survives—with the same fault geometry, timing and signal morphology appearing under continuous instrumentation while utility and weather confounds are measured—the old catalog becomes much more important.

The historical cases would then have done exactly what a good anomaly catalog is supposed to do.

Not prove the mechanism.

Tell us where to build the instrument that can.
