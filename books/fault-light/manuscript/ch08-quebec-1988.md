# Quebec, 1988

The 1988 Saguenay earthquake in Quebec appears repeatedly in the earthquake-light literature because luminous phenomena were reported and, unusually, some photographic evidence entered the discussion. The event later became one of the cases considered in attempts to identify geological patterns among reported earthquake lights.

The temptation is to make Quebec the photograph that proves the phenomenon. It does not. A photograph proves that a camera recorded light. Attribution still requires location, exposure behavior, timing, weather, electrical infrastructure and the geometry between camera and source.

What Quebec contributes is different. It helps move the subject from purely verbal testimony toward inspectable media. Once an image exists, critics can ask whether it resembles arc light, reflection, motion smear, lens artifact or an extended atmospheric glow. Competing explanations can touch the same object.

That sounds modest. It is a methodological revolution compared with a witness remembering a flash years later.

The deeper lesson is that anomaly quality depends less on drama than on metadata. A dull, time-stamped frame with known camera parameters and synchronized grid telemetry is worth more than a spectacular anonymous clip. The field's future belongs to boring evidence.
