# Better Than Human

Julian Huxley needed a word for a peculiar ambition.

By the middle of the twentieth century, industrial civilization had acquired a new relationship with limits. Aircraft had turned distances that once took weeks into hours. Antibiotics had converted some fatal infections into treatable events. Nuclear physics had shown that matter contained stores of energy far beyond ordinary intuition. Computing was beginning its migration from wartime machinery into science and administration. Biology had not yet acquired the molecular tools that would come later, but evolution had already changed the way educated people could imagine the human animal.

Huxley, an evolutionary biologist and the first director-general of UNESCO, wrote in 1957 about the possibility that the human species might deliberately transcend itself. He called the idea transhumanism. Nick Bostrom’s later history of transhumanist thought treats Huxley as an important source for the modern term, while also tracing older ambitions to defeat aging, expand capability, and escape ordinary human limitations.

The distinction worth keeping is in Huxley’s formulation itself.

Man remains man.

The limits change.

That is the original emotional bargain at the center of much transhumanism. The project can become radical enough to include altered bodies, engineered cognition, indefinite life extension, artificial organs, genetic intervention, cryonics, brain-computer integration, machine intelligence, digital copies, and settlement beyond Earth. The object moving through all those transformations remains recognizably connected to a human interest.

Transhumanism is therefore not simply a belief that technology will improve. It is a claim about who improvement is for.

That beneficiary becomes easier to see in the movement that formed around Extropy in the late 1980s and early 1990s. In 1990, Max More published a version of “The Extropian Principles” that joined technological ambition to personal transformation. The language was deliberately maximalist: longer life, greater intelligence, biological and neurological augmentation, expansion beyond Earth, resistance to fixed limits. Technology was not presented as an external force advancing on its own. It was something intelligent beings could use to increase their options and remake themselves.

The self mattered.

So did choice.

Those themes survived into the Transhumanist Declaration, originally assembled in 1998 and later adopted by Humanity+ in 2009. The declaration imagines science and technology broadening human potential by overcoming aging, cognitive shortcomings, involuntary suffering, and confinement to Earth. It also does something that critics of transhumanism sometimes forget: it acknowledges serious risks, including the possible loss of much or all of what humans value, and extends moral concern beyond present human beings to future artificial intellects and other forms of sentience.

That does not make every transhumanist argument careful. A movement can contain a risk paragraph and still attract people who underestimate risk. It does make the simple caricature inaccurate.

The humanistic strand of transhumanism is not a death cult for Homo sapiens.

It is closer to a refusal to treat Homo sapiens as finished.

Aging is contingent. Memory can be augmented. Disease can be attacked. Bodies can be repaired and altered. Reproduction can become a domain of choice rather than pure inheritance. Intelligence can be extended through tools. The planet need not define the boundary of human habitation. Even mortality, the oldest limit, can be treated as a technical problem rather than a moral instruction.

There is something almost conservative inside the radicalism.

The thing being conserved is the person.

Consider cryonics. Whatever its technical prospects, the desire is continuity: the person who dies wants some meaningful version of that person to exist later. Consider cognitive enhancement. The student takes a drug, receives an implant, uses a model, or modifies a neural system because she expects the resulting capability to be hers. Consider longevity. The point is not that a future organism exists. The point is that I get more life.

The pronouns are doing real philosophical work.

The emphasis on choice produces a second tension that matters later in the book. Enhancement can be voluntary at the level of one person and coercive at the level of a competitive system.

Suppose a cognitive enhancement is safe, effective, and optional. The first people to use it perform difficult work faster. Employers begin to prefer them. Professional schools redesign expectations around the new capability. Deadlines shrink. A student who would rather remain unenhanced can still refuse, but the meaning of refusal has changed. Nothing in the technology has forced her to adopt it. The environment has.

This problem is not unique to speculative implants. Smartphones already created milder versions. A worker can choose not to carry one, but many jobs are organized around the assumption of immediate reachability. A programmer can choose not to use a new development tool until competitors and colleagues reorganize their pace around it. The individual retains formal freedom while the baseline moves.

Transhumanist writing has language for one side of this problem: morphological freedom, the ability to modify one’s body and mind according to one’s values, coupled in serious versions with the right not to be coerced. The economic system has its own language: competition.

They are compatible until they are not.

If enhancement provides a large enough advantage, the decision to remain biologically ordinary can begin to resemble the decision to compete in a motor race on foot. The person is free. The outcome is not mysterious.

This creates a bridge from transhumanism to acceleration that does not require abandoning human welfare. Even a movement committed to individual agency can participate in a system whose competitive dynamics make successive augmentation difficult to refuse. The future can remain full of humans while the definition of an economically viable human moves underneath them.

The possibility becomes sharper with external AI because adoption is easier. A neural implant would require surgery, regulation, manufacturing, medical skill, and a person willing to put hardware in a body. A software system can spread through an organization in an afternoon. The enhancement is less intimate, but the competitive pressure can arrive sooner.

This also complicates the word natural. Eyeglasses, anesthesia, vaccination, pacemakers, hormonal contraception, joint replacements, organ transplantation, and antidepressants already alter the package evolution handed us. Few people need a theory of transhumanism before accepting a titanium hip. The dispute usually begins when an intervention moves from repairing a recognized impairment toward increasing a capacity beyond some contested baseline.

That line refuses to stay still.

A cochlear implant can be described as restoration by one person and as a threat to a linguistic culture by another. A stimulant can be treatment in one context and enhancement in another. Genetic screening can reduce suffering and also create pressure around which traits are considered acceptable. The technology does not settle the category.

This is one reason the best transhumanist writing is more interesting than the cartoon version. The subject is not simply how much technology can be added to a body. It is who gets to define an improvement, who gets access, who bears the risk, and whether transformation expands a person’s possible lives or narrows them toward whatever the surrounding system rewards.

Land keeps the last part and drops the first-person veto.

They become most strained in the idea of mind uploading.

Whole-brain emulation has long occupied an unusual place in transhumanist thought because it offers the most dramatic escape from biology while making the question of continuity impossible to avoid. In 2008, Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom published a technical roadmap for whole-brain emulation. The report did not claim that uploading was imminent. It tried to decompose the problem into prerequisites: scanning, modeling, computing, neuroscience, and the levels of detail that might be required to reproduce the relevant functions of a brain.

The engineering question is difficult.

The identity question is worse.

Suppose a future system scans a brain with enough detail to instantiate a software process that behaves exactly like the person who was scanned. It knows the childhood stories. It recognizes friends. It finishes old jokes. It hates the same foods. It remembers deciding to undergo the procedure.

Is that survival?

If the biological person remains alive beside the copy, the problem becomes immediate. There are now two claimants to one continuity. Both remember being the original. Neither can inherit exclusivity from memory alone.

If the biological person is destroyed during scanning, the ambiguity becomes easier to hide and harder to resolve.

Transhumanist discussions have never had one answer to this. Some versions care about pattern continuity more than biological continuity. Others treat destructive uploading as copying followed by death. The disagreement matters for this book because it exposes a boundary between transhumanism and succession.

A copied human mind might be post-biological while remaining intensely human in purpose.

A machine intelligence that never shared human memory, embodiment, development, emotion, mortality, or social dependence might be something else entirely.

Both can be called posthuman.

The word conceals the difference.

That difference was easier to ignore when the technology was hypothetical. A future of engineered bodies, neural implants, artificial organs, and uploaded minds could be imagined as one broad arc: humans using science to become more than current humans. Artificial intelligence sat somewhere inside the arc as a tool, collaborator, or possible new form of mind.

Modern AI changes the geometry.

The machine does not have to wait for the human body to be upgraded.

A language model can already perform cognitive work outside any individual human nervous system. An agent can operate software through credentials rather than muscles. A scientific model can search chemical or mathematical spaces at machine speed. None of these capabilities proves that the machine is conscious or generally intelligent. The point is structural. Enhancement can now occur on the other side of the interface.

The tool can improve independently of the user’s biology.

This creates two possible acceleration paths.

One path upgrades the person. Better medicine, better interfaces, implants, genetic interventions, prosthetics, exoskeletons, cognitive aids, and perhaps eventually more radical changes keep human beings inside the capability loop.

The other path upgrades the external system. Models become more capable. Robots become more dexterous. laboratories automate. Factories require fewer people. Software agents receive broader permissions. The environment becomes smarter faster than the person inside it.

These paths can reinforce one another. AI can design therapies. Neural interfaces can make AI easier to use. Machine intelligence can help decode brain activity. Better automation can lower the cost of physical augmentation.

They can also race.

The relative rate matters more than the science-fiction category.

If external machine capability compounds faster than humans can integrate with it, the future may become posthuman before people become transhuman in the strong sense. A person using an extraordinary AI through a screen has more effective capability than the same person without it, but the capability belongs partly to an external service that can be upgraded, copied, restricted, priced, or reassigned without changing the person’s nervous system.

Calling that person enhanced is reasonable.

Calling the enhancement hers is more complicated.

A pair of glasses becomes part of ordinary human capability partly because it is simple, local, reliable, personally controlled, and legible. A cloud model is different. It depends on distant infrastructure. Its behavior can change overnight. The user may not own the system, know its internal state, or control its future versions. The enhancement is real but institutionally mediated.

This is not an argument against using it.

It is an argument about where the upgraded intelligence lives.

Transhumanism developed when the most exciting technologies could still be imagined as modifications to the human being. Even cyberspace often preserved a human traveler. The person entered the virtual world, adopted an avatar, expanded cognition, or projected identity into a digital domain.

Land takes the opposite perspective. The network is not a place humans enter. Humans are components inside the network.

That reversal can sound like semantics until ownership, agency, and speed enter the picture.

A human can use an AI agent to run a business. At first, the agent is an extension: it handles customer support, writes code, prepares invoices, schedules work, purchases ads, and watches inventory. The owner defines the goals and receives the profit.

Now imagine successive versions require fewer interventions. The agent negotiates with suppliers, experiments with pricing, hires services from other agents, changes its software, opens new markets, and allocates capital under standing permissions. The human owner still matters legally and economically. But the number of moments at which human cognition changes the trajectory declines.

That person may feel radically enhanced.

The firm may simultaneously become less human.

Both descriptions can be true.

This is why the language of merger needs to be handled carefully. People sometimes speak as though human and machine intelligence will naturally converge because the boundary between them is already porous. That can happen at the level of function without happening at the level of identity or control.

A spreadsheet became part of accounting without accountants becoming spreadsheets.

Search engines became part of memory without users owning the index.

GPS became part of navigation without satellite infrastructure becoming part of the driver’s body.

Technologies can be cognitively intimate and institutionally external at the same time.

The strongest transhumanist answer is that this distinction may eventually disappear. Brain-computer interfaces could increase bandwidth. Prosthetic systems could become more integrated. Biological and artificial components could form coupled cognitive systems. Medicine could slow or reverse aspects of aging. Synthetic biology could alter what bodies can do. The endpoint might not be a biological human commanding a separate machine but a continuum of mixed systems.

That possibility deserves more than mockery and less than faith.

The neuroscience alone imposes humility.

In 2025, the MICrONS consortium published a functional connectomics dataset spanning roughly a cubic millimeter of mouse visual cortex. That tiny volume contained more than 200,000 reconstructed cells and about half a billion synapses. The work is extraordinary precisely because the scale is so small relative to a whole mammalian brain. A cubic millimeter can require an immense experimental and computational project.

This does not show that whole-brain emulation is impossible.

It shows what the word whole is hiding.

A human brain is not a static wiring diagram. Synapses change. Neurons depend on biochemical states. Glia participate in function. Hormones, sensory systems, the body, and environment influence cognition. Researchers do not yet know which levels of biological detail would be necessary for an emulation that preserves the functions a person cares about.

A roadmap is not a schedule.

That point matters because merger is sometimes used as an escape hatch from uncomfortable AI futures. Machines become more capable, but that is fine because people will merge with them. The statement can smuggle a second speculative technology into the argument to neutralize the uncertainty of the first.

Perhaps integration wins.

Perhaps external AI moves much faster.

Perhaps medicine extends healthy life while cognition remains mostly biological. Perhaps neural interfaces become useful without producing anything like mind uploading. Perhaps people reject invasive enhancement while happily using external systems. Perhaps economic inequality determines who can integrate and who cannot. Perhaps the category fractures into many forms of human and machine rather than converging into one.

Transhumanism does not supply one destination.

It supplies a refusal to accept the present human package as sacred.

That refusal can point in opposite directions.

One direction protects continuity of the human subject while changing almost everything about the subject’s capacities. The other becomes increasingly indifferent to continuity. Intelligence matters. Capability matters. Complexity matters. Expansion matters. Whether a particular biological lineage remains at the center may not.

The intellectual break is easier to see by comparing two attitudes toward death.

For a humanistic transhumanist, death is often the enemy because a person loses possible life. The technical project exists to preserve or expand that life.

For an impersonal acceleration process, individual death can be irrelevant if the system continues to become more capable. Companies die. Technologies disappear. Industries reorganize. Old skills become worthless. The process does not mourn its discarded components.

That does not make capitalism a creature.

It reveals the danger in using the same word progress for two different things.

A process can progress by its own metric while making particular participants worse off. Evolution produces extraordinary adaptation without promising comfort to organisms. A market can become more productive while destroying firms that once dominated it. A scientific field can advance by proving its founders wrong. A technical system can improve while reducing the status of the people who built the previous version.

Transhumanism tries to align technological progress with human development.

Land asks what happens if alignment is temporary.

The difference is not that one side likes technology and the other fears it. Land is not a conventional critic warning that the machine must be stopped. His writing is fascinated by acceleration, escape, and processes that exceed human control. The disturbing part is not opposition to technological progress. It is enthusiasm stripped of the guarantee that progress belongs to us.

That makes him unusually relevant to the present.

Artificial intelligence has created a technology whose most commercially important property is the ability to perform parts of cognition without being part of a human mind. It can augment people, certainly. It can also make some human cognitive labor less necessary.

Robotics extends the same possibility into action.

Autonomous science extends it into discovery.

Machine design extends it into technical improvement.

None of these has to end in succession. Each creates one more domain in which the human can be enhanced or bypassed.

The difference may depend less on philosophical intention than on architecture.

If a person retains meaningful control, understands enough to redirect the system, owns the relevant tools, and receives the capability as an extension of agency, the transhumanist description remains strong.

If the system increasingly sets intermediate goals, allocates resources, produces improvements, and operates through infrastructures no person can meaningfully supervise, the Landian description gains ground even if every company involved still uses the language of serving customers.

There is no clean threshold between the two.

There are dependencies.

That is useful because dependencies can be inspected.

Who defines the objective?

Who can change it?

Who knows whether the system succeeded?

Who supplies the energy?

Who repairs the hardware?

Who owns the output?

Who can stop the process?

Who benefits if it continues?

These are not philosophical decorations around technology. They determine whether enhancement belongs to the human or whether the human belongs to the process.

The transhumanist dream remains one of the most ambitious human projects ever proposed: use knowledge to turn inherited limits into choices.

Land’s provocation begins one step later.

What if the first limit the system learns to route around is us?
