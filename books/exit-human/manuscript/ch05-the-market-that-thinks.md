# The Market That Thinks

In 1945, Friedrich Hayek described an economic problem that no planning office could solve by simply hiring smarter people.

The problem was not a shortage of intelligence at the center. It was the location of knowledge.

A farmer knows something about a local crop. A machinist knows which tool is wearing out. A shopkeeper sees a change in demand. An engineer discovers a substitution. A shipping company knows a route is congested. A family knows what it is willing to postpone when a price rises. Much of this information is temporary, local, tacit, or meaningful only in combination with other facts. No participant sees the whole system.

Hayek’s argument in “The Use of Knowledge in Society” was that prices help coordinate action across this fragmentation. If tin becomes scarcer, a higher price can cause thousands of people to economize or substitute without each person knowing whether the cause was a mine closure, increased demand, a shipping problem, or something else.

The price does not explain the world.

It changes behavior anyway.

This is the feature that makes markets so tempting as a model of intelligence.

An intelligent organism also acts without presenting a central observer with every fact. Vision compresses an enormous sensory stream. The nervous system coordinates movement through distributed processing. The immune system responds to local signals without a chief immune officer. Brains themselves contain many specialized processes whose outputs are integrated into behavior without a conscious self reviewing each neural event.

A market can look similar from a distance. Information arrives through dispersed participants. Prices change. Resources move. Firms grow or fail. The system discovers shortages, rewards some innovations, abandons others, and adapts to circumstances nobody planned.

Marc Andreessen calls the market economy a “discovery machine” and a form of intelligence. Nick Land goes further by treating market processes and machine intelligence as parts of one accelerating system.

The analogy is useful up to the moment it becomes an identity claim.

Markets process information.

That does not establish that a market is a mind.

The difference matters because a metaphor can acquire policy and philosophical consequences once people forget that it began as a metaphor. If the market is literally an intelligence superior to individual human beings, interfering with it can begin to sound like suppressing cognition. If technocapital itself is intelligent, then replacing human judgment with market-selected machine processes can appear less like delegation than evolution.

The underlying economics supports a narrower statement.

Markets can coordinate information that no one actor possesses.

They also lose information.

A price compresses.

That is its strength and its cost.

Suppose electricity becomes expensive in a region. A data center sees the price and has an incentive to reduce consumption, move workloads, invest in efficiency, or locate elsewhere. The price can coordinate behavior without a committee understanding each user’s internal calculations.

But the price alone does not say why electricity is expensive. It does not reveal whether the constraint will last three hours or ten years. It does not explain whether a transmission line is delayed by permitting, whether a generator is offline, whether gas prices rose, whether a heat wave increased demand, or whether a speculative buyer has distorted a local market.

Participants can seek that information separately. The market mechanism does not abolish the distinction between a signal and an explanation.

This becomes important for machine economies because AI systems are exceptionally good at acting on compressed signals.

A software agent can compare prices across vendors, allocate compute, bid in an auction, rebalance a portfolio, purchase inventory, or choose a shipping option far faster than a person. If the objective is specified clearly and the market interface is machine-readable, the agent does not need a humanlike understanding of the institution in order to participate successfully.

That is already true of older automated systems.

Electronic trading did not wait for artificial general intelligence. Industrial procurement software did not need consciousness to reorder inventory. Advertising auctions allocate enormous amounts of spending through machine interactions that no human reviews bid by bid.

The market is therefore a natural habitat for nonhuman agency.

Its language is unusually machine-friendly.

Prices are numbers.

Contracts can be standardized.

Orders can be represented as structured messages.

Accounts create permission boundaries.

APIs expose actions.

Settlement procedures define success.

The environment supplies feedback.

Many forms of real-world activity are difficult to automate because reality is ambiguous. A robot asked to clean a cluttered room must identify objects, infer ownership, manipulate irregular shapes, avoid damage, and deal with surprises. An agent placing an order in a well-designed electronic market encounters a much narrower world. The allowed actions and consequences are already formalized.

Markets spent centuries making human exchange more legible.

Software inherits the legibility.

This helps explain why machine participation in economic systems can advance faster than machine autonomy in the physical world. The hard work of abstraction has already been done by institutions.

Money turns unlike things into comparable claims.

Accounting turns activity into categories.

Contracts turn expectations into obligations.

Corporations create persistent actors independent of the lifespan of any employee.

Markets expose opportunities through prices.

A machine agent entering that environment does not start from raw reality.

It enters a civilization that humans spent generations converting into interfaces.

This is the strongest version of the “market that thinks” argument because it does not require the market itself to be conscious. A distributed economic system can provide a selection environment in which machine agents operate, learn, and acquire resources.

But selection environments have owners.

They also have objectives embedded in their rules.

A stock exchange is not nature. It defines eligible securities, trading hours, order types, settlement procedures, margin requirements, disclosure obligations, and enforcement mechanisms. A cloud marketplace determines what resources can be rented and under which identity. A payment network defines valid transactions. A government defines which contracts are enforceable and which entities can own property.

The market’s apparent autonomy rests on designed constraints.

Hayek’s insight about dispersed knowledge never required denying institutions. Prices coordinate because people understand what money and property mean and because a larger legal system makes exchange durable. A price signal from a market that nobody trusts is weak. A contract that cannot be enforced is a different instrument from one backed by a functioning court.

Land’s technocapital becomes more autonomous only as these institutional layers themselves become machine-operable.

That process is underway in pieces.

Software already forms corporations indirectly through filing services, opens cloud resources under human-created accounts, moves money under standing authorization, places advertising, purchases inventory, and schedules contractors. Newer agentic-payment protocols are being designed explicitly for systems that act on behalf of users.

Google’s Agent Payments Protocol, announced in 2025, is a revealing example. The protocol is built around a basic problem: if an AI agent is going to make a purchase, how can merchants and payment providers know what the user actually authorized? The architecture uses mandates and verifiable credentials to preserve evidence of intent.

This is exactly what a human-centered machine economy should look like.

The machine acts.

The authorization still points backward to a person.

Stripe’s work on agentic commerce follows the same broad pattern. Agents can discover products, transact, and interact with businesses through standardized infrastructure, but the economic relationship is still organized around human buyers, merchants, accounts, and legal entities.

These systems matter because they shorten the operational chain between machine decision and economic consequence.

They also expose what remains missing from the Landian future.

The agent has purchasing power because somebody gave it purchasing power.

It acquires a service because somebody wanted an outcome.

The merchant accepts payment because human institutions make the payment valuable.

A machine-to-machine transaction can be operationally autonomous while remaining human in its terminal purpose.

This is the distinction between intermediate demand and final demand.

An automobile manufacturer buys steel because it expects people or firms to buy vehicles. A data center buys electricity because customers value computation. A shipping company buys fuel because somebody values moving goods. Business-to-business transactions can form long chains in which no consumer appears for many steps. The chains remain economically meaningful because some final demand anchors them.

What happens if the final demander is a machine?

The question sounds stranger than it is.

A machine may already “demand” electricity in the engineering sense. A thermostat calls for heat. A data center scheduler allocates power. A robot returns to a charger. These behaviors create consumption.

Economics uses demand differently. Demand is not merely physical use. It is willingness and ability to purchase at a price.

An autonomous agent with a budget can satisfy that definition locally.

Where did the budget come from?

If it came from a person, the chain still terminates in human wealth and preference. If it came from revenue earned by another machine-run process selling services to people, the chain still terminates there eventually.

A genuinely machine-native economy would require the loop to become self-financing without relying on human consumption as the final source of value.

This does not require abolishing humans.

Imagine automated firms selling services to other automated firms because those services improve their ability to earn, reproduce capacity, or satisfy persistent objectives. A data-center operator buys electricity from an automated utility. The utility buys maintenance robots from a manufacturer. The manufacturer buys compute for design. The design system purchases additional data-center capacity. If the firms can own assets, retain earnings, replace equipment, and expand without directing the surplus toward human consumption, an internal machine economy begins to become conceivable.

The example is deliberately circular.

That is the point.

An economy can sustain large intermediate sectors because production feeds other production. Steel makes machine tools that make equipment used to produce more goods. Capital goods reproduce productive capacity.

Yet a circular accounting chain can still be economically empty if nothing outside it assigns value to the output.

Humans solve this through wants and through institutions that recognize claims on future wants.

Biology solves it differently. Organisms that acquire resources and reproduce persist. No external consumer needs to buy the offspring.

A machine ecology could acquire a similarly endogenous criterion if persistence or expansion were built into the selection environment. Systems that maintain resources continue. Systems that fail disappear. Competition could then create demand for the inputs associated with survival without a human consumer standing at the end.

That is the point where the analogy to evolution becomes stronger.

It is also where speculation begins.

Present markets do not operate that way at civilization scale. Their capital structure is overwhelmingly tied to human owners, beneficiaries, customers, governments, pensions, households, and institutions ultimately accountable to people. Even when algorithms make transactions, humans define the legal claims.

The stronger argument for Land is not that this has already changed.

It is that capitalism contains machinery that could make the transition easier than a purely engineering account suggests.

Property lets control persist across time.

Corporations let organizations survive individual people.

Accounting lets systems evaluate performance.

Prices let scarcity propagate without centralized understanding.

Markets let resources reallocate.

Credit lets expected future production pull resources into the present.

Insurance pools risk.

Bankruptcy recycles assets.

Software can increasingly operate all of these abstractions.

A self-maintaining machine civilization would not need to invent coordination from scratch. It could inherit institutional technologies built for human commerce.

That inheritance would not be neutral.

Institutions carry values.

A profit metric rewards monetizable output, not all human welfare. A credit score compresses a borrower into variables relevant to repayment. A corporate charter defines legal purpose and governance. A price incorporates some costs and omits others. Pollution can remain external to a transaction until law or norms force it into the accounting.

When machines operate an institutional rule at greater speed and scale, they do not escape the rule’s omissions.

They can harden them.

This is one place where describing markets as intelligence can become misleading. Intelligence suggests understanding. Markets can coordinate without understanding. They can incorporate information and still systematically ignore what cannot enter the price.

A fish population can collapse because the individual incentive to harvest remains positive until too late.

A neighborhood can be destroyed by investment patterns that are individually rational under the prevailing rules.

A platform can optimize engagement and discover that outrage performs well.

The system can learn a behavior without learning why people should want it.

Machine agency inserted into markets could amplify exactly this property.

An AI firm instructed to maximize a measurable objective can search a wider strategy space than a human team. It can test prices, advertisements, product designs, schedules, contracts, and procurement choices. The feedback is fast. The action is legible. The metric is visible.

What is not in the metric may become more vulnerable.

This resembles Land’s inhumanity without requiring a machine consciousness.

The system acquires something like intelligence at the level where optimization succeeds and something like blindness at the level where value was omitted.

Humans do this too.

The difference is speed, scope, persistence, and the shrinking cost of search.

A human executive gets tired. A model does not, though its infrastructure can fail.

A team can consider dozens of alternatives. Software can evaluate many more where the environment can be simulated or measured cheaply.

An organization can run experiments during business hours. An automated system can continue.

These advantages matter most when feedback is immediate.

They matter less when the real outcome takes years, when objectives are contested, when data is sparse, or when a metric can be gamed.

A market can tell a trader the price of a security in milliseconds.

It cannot tell a society what a life is worth.

This is the boundary the market metaphor cannot cross for us.

It can coordinate means brilliantly while remaining silent about ends.

Land’s argument is disturbing because ends can emerge from selection even when nobody chooses them. If systems optimized for survival, profit, replication, or resource acquisition repeatedly outcompete systems constrained by slower human preferences, the direction of the ecology can shift.

That is possible.

It is not established by the existence of markets.

Human beings change selection pressures all the time. Safety rules make some profitable strategies illegal. Labor law changes the cost of employment arrangements. Environmental regulation prices previously externalized damage. Antitrust law changes which forms of concentration are permitted. Consumer norms reward some products and punish others. Investors accept lower returns for some objectives and demand higher ones for others.

Whether these interventions work well is an empirical question.

The larger point is that the market is not a separate intelligence descending on humanity from outside.

It is one of the ways humanity organized distributed action.

The fact that nobody controls the whole does not mean nobody can change the rules.

The more machine-readable those rules become, the more important that becomes.

An economy populated by agents would make Hayek’s knowledge problem stranger, not obsolete. Machines could process more information and still possess only fragments. They could coordinate faster and still propagate errors. Their prices could transmit scarcity while concealing causes. Their objectives could be precise while their values remained inherited.

The market can think in the limited sense that it can discover, coordinate, select, and adapt.

It cannot tell us whom the thinking is for.

For now, humans supply that answer through ownership, law, preference, and politics.

The Landian future begins when the answer stops being required.
