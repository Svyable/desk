# 1 — The Light Before the Earth Moves

The problem with a light in the sky is that everybody wants it to be a noun.

Aircraft. Meteor. Drone. Ball lightning. Transformer. Plasma. Spacecraft.

A noun ends the argument. The light becomes a thing.

But sometimes the light is a verb.

Rock is being stressed. Charge is moving. Air is breaking down. A power line is arcing. A camera sensor is saturating. A human visual system is solving distance with almost none of the information it normally uses. Something is happening, and the bright object we think we see may be the visible punctuation at the end of a process that begins kilometers away and, in some proposed models, kilometers down.

That possibility is stranger than it sounds. It means the object can be real without being an object.

The U.S. Geological Survey has a wonderfully uncomfortable answer to the question of earthquake lights. Geophysicists disagree about whether individual reports constitute solid evidence. Some doubt the category. Others think at least some reports plausibly belong in it. There are physics-based hypotheses. There are also cases where the spectacular light was electricity arcing from infrastructure shaken by the earthquake.

That is almost the whole epistemology of anomalous phenomena in miniature.

Real report. Uncertain category. Several mechanisms. Contamination by ordinary things that look extraordinary at exactly the moment everybody is looking up.

In 2006, Friedemann Freund, John Derr and France St-Laurent published a proposed mechanism in *Physics and Chemistry of the Earth*. Under stress, they argued, defects in igneous rocks could activate mobile positive-hole charge carriers. At sufficient concentrations, charge reaching the surface might produce corona discharge, visible light and high-frequency electromagnetic radiation.

Notice the verbs.

Activate. Propagate. Accumulate. Discharge.

The paper did not discover a little glowing vehicle hiding inside granite.

Then physics did what physics is supposed to do to an attractive story. It asked what the story costs.

A 2018 modeling paper by Jánský and Pasko examined electrical coupling between crust and atmosphere. A large-scale current dipole with poles five and fifteen kilometers deep required energy significantly exceeding the total seismic-wave energy of major earthquakes. Moving the upper pole close to the surface made the setup more plausible.

This is why I distrust explanations that arrive as labels. *Earthquake light* sounds like a solved thing. It is not. The mechanism has geometry. The geometry has an energy bill. The bill can bankrupt the explanation.

That does not make the reports disappear.

In southern Washington, Derr and Michael Persinger reported luminous phenomena associated with small earthquakes and compressional stress. Around the 2009 L'Aquila earthquake, Cristiano Fidani assembled a large testimonial catalogue of non-seismic phenomena, including reported lights. The catalogue is evidence that people reported lights. It is not automatically evidence that stressed rock made them. Those are two different sentences and they should never be allowed to merge simply because they sit next to each other on a page.

This distinction becomes more important when the earthquake leaves the story.

Go north to a valley in Norway called Hessdalen.

Beginning in the early 1980s, residents there reported recurrent luminous phenomena. Unlike the classic UFO encounter, the anomaly stayed put long enough for people to bring instruments. Project Hessdalen's 1984 campaign recorded dozens of observations. Later monitoring added cameras and other sensors. Some events have been reported visually and on radar. The lights have been described as flashes, persistent luminous forms and moving sources.

And still the answer did not fall out.

That is the part I care about.

A recurring anomaly in an accessible valley should be the easy version. The mountain does not sign a nondisclosure agreement. The light does not disappear forever after frightening one pilot. You can return. You can place cameras. You can log weather. You can measure magnetic fields. You can attempt spectroscopy. You can look for radar correspondence. You can make mistakes and come back next summer with better equipment.

Decades later, the category remains unsettled.

That should humble both camps.

The person who says *spacecraft* has skipped several floors. The person who says *plasma* may have done exactly the same thing in the opposite direction.

The useful question is not which noun wins. It is which measurements would force one model to lose.

That question matters far beyond Hessdalen because governments have occasionally wandered into the same territory. The British Ministry of Defence's classified UAP study later known as Project Condign proposed atmospheric and plasma-like explanations for some reports. The report has since acquired a peculiar afterlife. People who want official validation quote its willingness to treat anomalous phenomena as physically real. People who want dismissal quote its rejection of extraterrestrial craft as necessary explanation.

A declassified report is not a peer-reviewed law of nature. Classification tells us who was allowed to read a document, not whether the universe agreed with it.

Condign is valuable for another reason. It exposes an institutional possibility that the public UFO argument handles badly: the state can believe that a report describes a real physical event while rejecting the witness's implied ontology.

Something happened.

The pilot was not necessarily hallucinating.

The radar return was not necessarily fabricated.

And there still may have been no vehicle.

That gap—between event and object—is where this book lives.

It is also where sensor phenomenology gets vicious. Radar is excellent at giving an observer confidence that *something* exists. It is much worse at independently proving what that something is. Optical brightness destroys range intuition. A point source against a dark background offers almost no size information. Apparent acceleration can be manufactured by observer motion and geometry. Plasma and ionization can interact with electromagnetic systems. Instrument artifacts can coincide with real atmospheric structure. A witness can be competent, sober, truthful and wrong about distance.

None of those statements explain any particular UAP case.

They are the admission price for explaining one.

The book will therefore do something slightly unfashionable. It will treat mundane explanations as claims that need evidence.

If a case is called transformer arcing, show the grid failure and geometry.

If it is called earthquake light, show the seismic timing, fault relationship, atmospheric path and mechanism that can pay its energy bill.

If it is called plasma, define the plasma. Give the density, lifetime, power source, confinement problem, spectrum and expected radar behavior.

If it is called a vehicle, earn the vehicle: persistent geometry, trajectory, parallax, independent sensors, acceleration with uncertainty, interaction with the environment, whatever the case actually permits.

And if the answer is *unknown*, leave it there.

Unknown is not an embarrassment. Unknown is the little patch of ground science has not paved over yet.

There is a larger hypothesis behind this investigation, but it has to wait for the evidence. Geological stress, atmospheric electricity and luminous anomalies may form a poorly integrated family of processes that sometimes generates observations culturally classified as UFOs. The strong version—that a meaningful fraction of famous UAP reports can be assigned to such processes—is unproven. The stronger version—that geophysical anomalies explain apparently intelligent or structured craft reports—is further away still.

The hypothesis earns nothing from being naturalistic.

It has to survive the same knife we would use on aliens.

That means denominators. How many earthquakes produce credible lights? How many luminous reports occur without seismic activity? How often do instrumented sites capture events? How many supposed earthquake lights are grid failures? What spectra are recorded? What radar frequencies respond? How long do events persist? Do reported motions fit winds, electric fields or neither? Are hotspot locations actually enriched for faulting, mineralization, conductivity gradients or atmospheric electrical conditions after correcting for where people look?

There is an ugly possibility here that makes the book worth writing.

We may have divided one hard problem into separate disciplines and then congratulated ourselves for making each piece smaller.

Seismologists inherit earthquake lights and mostly do not care about UFO reports. Atmospheric physicists inherit plasma and electrical discharge. Radar engineers inherit propagation and clutter. Perception researchers inherit the witness. Intelligence agencies inherit unidentified tracks. UFO researchers inherit the story after all those people have gone home.

The anomaly is what remains in the cracks between departments.

That does not mean the cracks contain spacecraft.

It means we should inspect the cracks.

Start with the ground.
