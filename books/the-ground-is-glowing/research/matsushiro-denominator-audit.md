# Matsushiro denominator audit

This is a bounded research pass for *The Ground Is Glowing*. It does not revise Chapter 1 while the manuscript PR is open. Its job is to keep Chapter 3 from turning the Matsushiro photographs into cleaner evidence than the surviving record warrants.

## What the later technical record actually says

The Japan Meteorological Agency's Matsushiro Seismological Observatory describes the swarm as beginning on 3 August 1965 directly beneath an already well-equipped observatory. By early November 1965 the area was experiencing about 100 felt shocks a day. On 17 April 1966 the observatory recorded 6,780 shocks in one day, including 585 felt shocks and three reaching JMA seismic intensity V. The swarm is therefore unusually useful because the seismic denominator is enormous and unusually well observed.

Authoritative institutional background:
https://www.jma.go.jp/jma/kishou/know/jishin/matsushiro/en/index.html

A 2017 *Scientific Reports* reconstruction by Yuji Enomoto, Tsuneaki Yamabe and Nobuo Okumura is especially useful because it reaches backward into the Matsushiro Earthquake Center collection and Yasui's earlier light catalogue rather than merely repeating the famous photographs. The authors report that T. Kuribayashi photographed the luminous phenomena and summarize Yasui's classification of 34 candidate events: 18 were identified as earthquake-light events, while the other 16 carried risk of misidentification from lightning, Sun or Moon, artificial lights, and other sources.

Primary technical synthesis and source trail:
https://doi.org/10.1038/srep44774

The same paper gives an unusually concrete event: a fisheye photograph on 26 September 1966 was interpreted as showing a luminous zone about 8 km across, lasting 96 seconds. But no felt earthquake occurred at the time. Felt earthquakes occurred at 03:11 and 03:37 local time instead. The authors explicitly say that this lack of direct coincidence means the event cannot be explained by a model that requires coseismic stress change.

That detail should survive into the book. It is much more interesting than the generic sentence "earthquake lights were photographed at Matsushiro." The best-known photograph does not sit neatly on top of an earthquake. The case becomes a problem of state, timing, atmosphere and mechanism rather than a picture attached to a shock.

## The denominator changes the story

Matsushiro produced thousands of shocks in a day at peak activity and an enormous number across the swarm. Against that seismic activity, the inherited light catalogue contains 34 candidates, only 18 of which Yasui classified positively in the later reconstruction.

The manuscript should therefore avoid any formulation implying that the lights were a routine accompaniment to earthquakes. The interesting phenomenon is rare even inside an unusually active swarm.

A useful Chapter 3 extraction should place these quantities beside each other:

- seismic activity by day and stage;
- felt-shock count;
- M >= 4 event count;
- all 34 candidate light reports;
- the 18 accepted and 16 questionable cases;
- observation effort and camera availability, if recoverable;
- weather/cold-front timing;
- exact temporal distance from the nearest earthquake;
- artificial-light, lightning, astronomical and grid controls.

Without observation effort, 18/34 is not a prevalence estimate. Without the earthquake denominator, however, the photographs invite exactly the wrong intuition: that a visually memorable phenomenon was common because the seismic event was common.

## Mechanism boundary

Enomoto et al. propose a coupled hydro-mechanical/electromagnetic model involving CO2/water migration, electrification, atmospheric-electric-field variation and dielectric-barrier discharge. Their laboratory model produced pale bluish-purple discharge and nitrogen emission features under imposed electrical conditions.

This is useful mechanism work, but the field photographs do not by themselves establish that this mechanism produced the Matsushiro lights. The laboratory setup is a plausibility bridge, not a field identification. Chapter 3 should preserve that separation.

It should also avoid silently merging this mechanism with the positive-hole mechanism already introduced in Chapter 1. They are not interchangeable labels for "rock electricity." The book will be stronger if Matsushiro becomes the place where multiple naturalistic mechanisms begin competing with one another rather than where one preferred explanation is crowned.

## Strongest counterargument

The 18-versus-16 classification is itself inherited through later literature and needs recovery of Yasui's original 1968/1971 publications and the Matsushiro Earthquake Center records before publication-level confidence. A later paper's summary of an older catalogue is not a substitute for the catalogue.

Likewise, the absence of a felt shock at the instant of the 26 September photograph does not make a seismic connection disappear. Stress, fluid migration and atmospheric electrical effects could plausibly operate before or after discrete felt events. The timing instead defeats only the narrowest coseismic model.

The correct skeptical control is therefore not "no earthquake at that second, so not earthquake light." It is: what time window does each proposed mechanism predict, and how much background time would satisfy that window by chance during a swarm this active?

## A quantitative test Chapter 3 can earn

For each of the 34 candidates, recover the nearest-event time difference and calculate results under prospectively declared windows such as ±1 minute, ±10 minutes, ±1 hour and ±24 hours. Then compare those overlaps with the fraction of total observation time covered by the same windows. Because windows will overlap heavily during peak swarm activity, calculate union-of-time coverage rather than multiplying event count by window width.

If almost every minute during the peak is "near an earthquake," temporal proximity has little evidentiary value. A useful association would need to outperform that base rate and, ideally, covary with a physically motivated variable such as strain state, fluid outflow, atmospheric electric field or meteorology.

This turns Matsushiro from a famous-photo anecdote into a testable case.

## Narrative opportunity

Chapter 3 has a better opening available than "people saw strange lights during an earthquake swarm." On the peak day the observatory counted 6,780 shocks. Yet the photograph that later became emblematic of earthquake lights was taken on a night when no felt earthquake occurred at the moment the sky lit up.

That contradiction creates the chapter's pressure honestly. The ground was extraordinarily active. The light was photographed. The obvious temporal story still does not quite work.

Then the old catalogue complicates it again: 34 candidates become 18 accepted cases and 16 possible mistakes. The reader can watch the category shrink before any mechanism is introduced.

## Publication holds

- Do not call the Matsushiro photographs proof of a specific earthquake-light mechanism.
- Do not imply the 26 September 1966 light was simultaneous with a felt earthquake.
- Do not turn 18 accepted cases out of 34 candidates into a prevalence rate without observation-time and earthquake denominators.
- Do not treat the 2017 laboratory discharge model as a field replication.
- Do not merge the CO2/water dielectric-discharge model with the positive-hole model without explaining their different causal chains.
- Recover Yasui's original publications and, if possible, the Matsushiro Earthquake Center catalogue before final manuscript claims about classification counts or photographic provenance.
