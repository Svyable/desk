# Chapter 15 — 1910s: Lincoln School — America Turns the Curriculum Into a Laboratory

In 1917, Teachers College at Columbia University established the Lincoln School in New York City.

It was a private, coeducational laboratory school backed by substantial philanthropic support. Its students were not a representative cross-section of American children. Its resources were unusual. Its purpose was unusual too.

The school existed partly so adults could experiment on the curriculum.

That sentence sounds worse than the idea because the word *experiment* carries the memory of medical subjects who did not consent. Educational experimentation can be more ordinary: try a new sequence, observe student work, revise a lesson, compare approaches, build a curriculum around problems rather than recitation.

Still, the word matters.

The Lincoln School is the school of the 1910s because it made a modern claim about teaching: the curriculum is not inherited scripture. It can be designed, tested, and changed.

Progressive education had many sources and many forms. John Dewey's laboratory school at the University of Chicago had opened in the 1890s. Reformers across the country were questioning rigid recitation, narrow classical curricula, and schools organized around passive obedience. The Lincoln School did not invent experimentation.

It institutionalized the confidence of an era that believed expertise could redesign social systems.

That confidence appeared everywhere in Progressive Era America.

Cities created professional departments. Public-health reformers gathered data. Engineers built networks. Social workers classified needs. Managers studied efficiency. Psychologists developed tests. Government commissions investigated problems. The modern administrative state was taking shape around the proposition that trained experts could improve systems through systematic knowledge.

The school became one of the systems available for redesign.

Traditional curricula had accumulated over time through subjects, textbooks, examination requirements, and college expectations. A laboratory school could ask a more aggressive question: what should children learn if we begin with the needs of modern life rather than the authority of the inherited timetable?

The question sounds liberating.

It also gives enormous power to the people who define “modern life.”

This is the central tension inside curriculum reform.

Every curriculum excludes almost everything.

A child has a finite number of school hours. Add one subject and something else receives less time. Emphasize projects and direct instruction may shrink. Emphasize canonical texts and newer voices may be displaced. Add computing, financial literacy, media literacy, health, civics, or climate science and the schedule does not expand by magic.

Curriculum design is scarcity management with moral consequences.

The laboratory school makes those consequences visible because change is intentional.

When an old subject disappears through neglect, nobody seems responsible. When a curriculum committee removes it, responsibility acquires a name.

That is why curriculum fights become political fights so quickly.

The Lincoln School's progressive philosophy favored learning connected to experience, inquiry, projects, interdisciplinary work, and the development of students as active participants rather than passive receivers. The exact program changed over time and should not be reduced to a single method.

What matters is the institution's orientation toward revision.

The school assumed that education could improve by studying itself.

This is surprisingly radical.

Most mature institutions protect routines by calling them standards. A laboratory school begins with the possibility that the routine is wrong.

Teachers become researchers of practice.

Students' work becomes evidence.

A classroom can be treated as a site where an educational hypothesis meets actual learners.

The best version of this mindset produces humility.

We think this sequence will help students understand. Let's see.

The worst version produces fashion.

We have a new theory. Everyone should adopt it.

The distance between the two is evidence.

Education has struggled with that distance for more than a century.

Compared with medicine, schools often have weaker experimental conditions. Students cannot be randomly assigned to every educational experience without ethical or practical limits. Teachers differ. families differ. schools differ. Outcomes unfold over years. A curriculum can affect test scores, motivation, knowledge, behavior, identity, and later life in different directions.

What counts as success?

The question is not technical before it is philosophical.

A curriculum that raises reading scores but reduces students' interest in reading has produced two results. A project-based program that develops collaboration but leaves gaps in factual knowledge has produced two results. A classical curriculum that builds shared references but narrows whose history is represented has produced two results.

Educational evaluation requires choosing which results matter.

The laboratory school cannot escape values by becoming scientific.

That lesson would become increasingly important as the twentieth century embraced standardized testing and quantitative evaluation.

Measurement can make schooling less arbitrary. It can reveal whether adults' confidence matches students' learning. It can expose achievement gaps that institutions prefer not to see.

Measurement can also become a substitute for judgment.

The easiest educational outcomes to count are not necessarily the most important.

A test can assess some knowledge efficiently. It has more difficulty measuring whether a student has become intellectually curious, developed judgment under uncertainty, learned to collaborate across difference, or acquired the confidence to challenge a bad premise.

Laboratory schools often defend themselves by pointing toward those harder outcomes.

Critics reasonably ask how they know the outcomes occurred.

The argument never ends because education contains goods that resist a common unit.

The Lincoln School's unusual resources sharpen another problem: can elite experiments teach mass systems anything?

A well-funded private laboratory school can employ talented teachers, maintain favorable class sizes, provide materials, and work with families able to support a demanding program. A public district serving thousands of children across different languages, income levels, disabilities, and neighborhood conditions does not operate under the same constraints.

A reform can succeed because of its environment rather than its visible method.

This is the replicability problem introduced in Gary.

The Lincoln School makes it even more acute because it was designed to generate educational ideas that others might study.

If a project works because teachers receive unusual planning time, copying the project without the planning time is not replication.

If students arrive with extensive background knowledge from home, a curriculum that appears to produce broad understanding may be relying on knowledge the school did not teach.

If families can provide tutors or cultural experiences, gaps in the curriculum may be repaired outside the institution.

Privilege can hide instructional weakness.

This is one reason elite educational innovation should be treated with both interest and skepticism.

Rich schools can afford experimentation.

They can also afford the consequences of failed experiments.

Poor schools are often asked to adopt reforms under greater pressure and with fewer buffers.

That asymmetry persists.

A technology company pilots adaptive software in a well-resourced district. A philanthropic network introduces a new model in schools serving low-income students. Researchers study outcomes. The language of innovation can make children appear to be the site where adults prove ideas.

The ethical question is not whether experimentation should stop.

Every classroom already contains experimentation because no lesson works identically with every group.

The ethical question is who bears the risk and who gets the benefit.

Students deserve evidence-informed education without being denied the possibility of improvement simply because existing practice is familiar.

That balance is difficult.

The Lincoln School's greatest contribution may therefore be less a specific curriculum than the idea of disciplined revision.

Schools should have mechanisms for learning from their own work.

This sounds ordinary in organizations and remains surprisingly difficult in education.

Teachers often work in isolation. A lesson can succeed or fail behind a closed door with little systematic opportunity for colleagues to observe. Student work may be graded and returned without being used as institutional evidence. Curriculum materials can persist because replacement is expensive or politically contentious.

The system teaches students every day while learning slowly about itself.

Laboratory schools try to reverse that.

Observation, documentation, collaboration, and curriculum development become part of the institution's job.

That moves educational knowledge closer to practice.

Universities can study learning in controlled or theoretical settings. Classroom teachers encounter the messier reality of students who are tired, excited, confused, absent, multilingual, gifted, distracted, anxious, hungry, or carrying prior misconceptions.

Practice produces knowledge theory needs.

The best laboratory school creates a bridge between them.

The worst creates a showcase that proves only that exceptional conditions are exceptional.

Again, the difference is whether the institution preserves counter-evidence.

Progressive education has often been criticized for underestimating the importance of explicit knowledge. If students are asked to discover everything through projects, they may spend cognitive effort reinventing ideas a skilled teacher could explain efficiently. Novices do not organize knowledge like experts. Inquiry works differently when a student has enough background knowledge to ask productive questions.

These criticisms do not invalidate active learning.

They challenge a romantic version in which the teacher disappears and children naturally construct a complete education from experience.

The serious progressive insight is narrower and stronger: knowledge becomes more durable and meaningful when students use it, connect it, and understand why it matters.

The serious traditional insight is equally important: students cannot think critically about knowledge they do not possess.

A good curriculum needs both inheritance and inquiry.

Laboratory schools are valuable when they test the balance rather than declare one side victorious.

The Lincoln School existed during a period when the United States was becoming culturally and economically modern at extraordinary speed. World War I brought industrial mobilization and propaganda. Immigration transformed cities. Women's suffrage advanced toward constitutional victory. Psychology was entering education. Intelligence testing would be used during the war and later influence schools, often with exaggerated claims about what tests could measure.

The era trusted classification almost as much as experimentation.

That should make us cautious about progressive expertise.

Experts were not simply enlightened reformers freeing children from tradition. Progressive institutions also participated in movements for eugenics, Americanization, tracking, and administrative sorting.

Modernization expands the tools available to benevolence and prejudice alike.

A test can identify a child who needs support.

A test can also rationalize a hierarchy.

A curriculum can broaden experience.

A curriculum can also be designed around an elite's belief about what other people's children need.

The laboratory mindset requires a moral rule: students are not raw material for institutional ambition.

Their welfare is the purpose of the experiment, not a variable secondary to it.

The Lincoln School's social selectivity makes this particularly interesting because affluent families often have more power to resist educational practices they dislike.

That can constrain experimentation in ways poor families cannot.

Voice changes ethics.

An experiment conducted with participants capable of challenging the institution differs from one imposed on people with little political leverage.

This is another reason family engagement matters beyond public relations. Parents and students can provide forms of evidence professionals do not possess.

A child's experience is data, though not the only data.

The Progressive Era's faith in experts sometimes underestimated this.

Modern schools repeat the mistake when dashboards replace conversation.

The laboratory school also changed the role of the teacher.

A teacher following a fixed textbook can be evaluated partly on faithful delivery. A teacher inside an experimental curriculum has to exercise more judgment.

Judgment increases professional demand.

It requires knowledge of subject matter, students, assessment, and the purpose of the larger curriculum. It also requires time to plan and revise.

Progressive pedagogy can fail when systems adopt its surface practices without funding the professional conditions beneath them.

“Student-centered” becomes a slogan while teachers are given impossible class sizes. “Project-based” becomes a poster assignment. “Inquiry” becomes students searching the internet without enough background knowledge to judge sources.

Methods decay when copied cheaply.

This is not unique to progressive education. Direct instruction can decay into mindless worksheets. Classical education can decay into prestige attached to old books. Technology can decay into screen time.

Every educational idea has a low-cost imitation.

The job of a laboratory is to distinguish the method from the imitation.

That requires honest failure.

Institutions are terrible at publishing failure because reputation depends on success.

A laboratory school has a special responsibility to admit what did not work.

If only successful projects become public, the “research” becomes marketing.

This problem extends throughout modern science and innovation. Positive results are more publishable. Philanthropies announce successes. districts promote initiatives. Superintendents build careers on reform. Vendors sell outcomes.

Education needs institutions willing to preserve the embarrassing lesson.

The student did not understand.

The project took too long.

The curriculum assumed background knowledge that was not there.

The assessment measured the wrong thing.

The schedule defeated the idea.

Failure becomes valuable only when someone records it accurately enough that the next attempt changes.

That is what turns experimentation into learning.

The Lincoln School eventually closed in the 1940s after operating for decades. Its direct institutional life was shorter than the universities that surround it in this book.

Its influence belongs partly to a lineage of laboratory schools and progressive curriculum development that continued elsewhere.

A school does not have to survive forever to alter educational imagination.

Sometimes the temporary institution matters because it gives adults permission to ask whether the ordinary institution is inevitable.

That was Lincoln's role in the 1910s.

The school day, the textbook, the sequence of subjects, the relationship between teacher and student—none had to be inherited untouched.

They could become objects of inquiry.

That creates a permanent danger of fashionable reform.

It also creates the possibility of improvement.

The alternative to experimentation is not stability.

It is accidental experimentation without records.

Every year, every teacher changes something. Every class differs. Every new technology or cohort alters the conditions. The question is whether schools learn from the variation.

In 1917, the Lincoln School was built around the belief that they could.

America had turned the curriculum into a laboratory.

The Lincoln School’s laboratory identity matters because it made curriculum itself an object of experiment. A traditional school can revise what it teaches, but a laboratory school is founded on the premise that revision is part of the institution’s purpose. Teachers and researchers are not only delivering a program; they are observing, testing, documenting, and changing it.

That orientation reflected a larger progressive conviction that education should respond to a changing society rather than preserve inherited subject divisions simply because they were inherited. Industrialization, urbanization, new scientific knowledge, changing family life, and democratic participation all placed pressure on a curriculum shaped for an earlier elite. The question was no longer only which facts students should know. It was how school experience should be organized if learning was understood as active, developmental, and connected to life outside the classroom.

The Teachers College setting gave the experiment a second audience. Students at Lincoln School mattered in their own right, but the institution also existed within a teacher-education and research environment. Practices developed there could be observed by educators, described in publications, and carried into other schools. That makes the field-testing dimension important. A laboratory school is historically consequential only if its experiments enter a wider conversation.

The distance between prototype and public system, however, is enormous. An experimental school may have unusual resources, carefully selected staff, supportive families, smaller scale, or institutional freedom that ordinary districts do not possess. A curriculum that works in such a setting cannot simply be assumed to work everywhere. The translation problem is part of the history, not a reason to dismiss the experiment.

This is one of the recurring weaknesses of educational reform. Innovators often prove that something can be done under favorable conditions, then reformers mistake possibility for scalability. Public systems have to serve students with different needs, operate under law and collective bargaining, manage transportation and facilities, respond to elected boards, and survive political turnover. The laboratory can simplify some of those constraints in order to learn. The system cannot.

Lincoln School therefore helps separate invention from implementation. An educational idea can be intellectually persuasive yet institutionally fragile. It may depend on teacher judgment that is difficult to standardize, materials that are expensive to reproduce, or a culture that takes years to build. The real test of influence is not whether other schools copy the surface form, but whether the experiment changes what educators consider possible.

The school also illuminates a fundamental shift in authority. If curriculum can be scientifically or systematically studied, then expertise over what children should learn moves partly from tradition, textbook publishers, and local custom toward researchers and professional educators. That can improve curriculum by making evidence and child development relevant. It can also distance decisions from families and communities who may reject the values embedded in the experts’ definition of progress.

The result is a political paradox. Progressive education often presents itself as more democratic because it attends to the learner, encourages participation, and resists rote authority. Yet the reform movement can simultaneously strengthen professional authority by claiming that trained experts understand learning better than ordinary adults. The twentieth century will repeatedly oscillate between those two impulses.

Lincoln School sits at the center of that contradiction. Its experiments helped make curriculum dynamic rather than fixed, but a dynamic curriculum always raises the question of who gets to change it. Teachers? Researchers? Students? Parents? Boards? Legislatures? Courts? Markets? Every answer carries a theory of educational authority.

That is why the school belongs in the 1910s. It represents the moment when modern America increasingly treated the curriculum not as a list inherited from the past but as a technology that could be redesigned. Once curriculum becomes a technology, innovation accelerates—but so does conflict over who controls the design.

The archival record of influence beyond the campus is therefore as important as the founding date. A laboratory school becomes nationally significant when its experiments circulate through teacher education, curriculum projects, professional networks, and the expectations of educators who may never visit the original school. Influence can survive even when the institution itself changes or disappears because practices and questions have migrated elsewhere.

That form of afterlife is common in education. A school does not need to become a permanent mass model to matter. It can serve as a research-and-development site for ideas that are later modified, combined with other approaches, or absorbed so thoroughly that their experimental origin is forgotten. Lincoln School’s strongest historical claim is in that diffusion of the permission to experiment.

The harder task was making sure the students remained more important than the experiment.
