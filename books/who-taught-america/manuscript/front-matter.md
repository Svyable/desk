# Who Taught America?

*Twenty-Six Schools, 250 Years, and the Classrooms That Made a Nation*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a familiar way to tell the history of the United States. Begin with a declaration. Move through presidents, wars, elections, inventions, court decisions, depressions, booms, movements, assassinations, scandals, and technologies. Keep the camera mostly on adults who already possess power. Schools appear when a Supreme Court case requires one, when a protest spills onto a campus, or when a statistic about literacy or college attendance helps explain something happening elsewhere.

This book turns the camera around.

For each calendar decade touched by the American experiment, from the 1770s through the 2020s, I have chosen one school. The claim is not that the school was the best in America, the largest, the oldest, the most selective, or even the most influential in every defensible sense of the word. “Most important” is a deliberately dangerous phrase. It creates an argument where a ranking would create false precision.

The question I use instead is this: if you could walk into only one school to understand what America was becoming in that decade, where would you go?

In the 1770s, I would go to William & Mary, where a new professorship in law helped turn the ideals of a revolution into a profession for governing a republic. In the 1800s, I would go to West Point, where the young federal government trained engineers as well as officers because a country that wanted roads, forts, canals, maps, and continental reach needed technical expertise it did not yet possess in abundance. In the 1810s, I would go to the American School for the Deaf, where a new institution challenged the easy assumption that a person who could not hear could not fully participate in intellectual life.

The choices become less comfortable as the country grows.

In the 1870s, the school is Carlisle Indian Industrial School. Its importance is not honorable. Carlisle became a model for federal boarding-school policies designed to separate Native children from families, languages, customs, and identities. A history of American education that includes only institutions we now admire would be a history of self-congratulation. Schooling has been used to enlarge human possibility and to narrow it. It has offered refuge, status, language, credentials, food, safety, friendship, and a path into public life. It has also been used to sort people, discipline them, assimilate them, exclude them, and tell them that the culture they brought through the door should not come inside.

The same institution can do several of these things at once.

That is one reason schools are such good places to study a country. A school has to make abstract beliefs operational. A legislature can announce that education matters; a school has to decide who may enroll, who pays, what is taught, whose language is legitimate, how long the day lasts, which books enter the room, what counts as evidence, what behavior is punished, who gets to teach, what a diploma certifies, and what happens to the child who does not fit the plan.

The distance between national ideals and school rules is often the distance between rhetoric and reality.

The United States declared equality before it built anything close to equal schools. It praised self-government while denying large parts of the population meaningful access to the institutions that prepared people for public power. It celebrated local control until local control defended arrangements that federal courts found unconstitutional. It defended academic freedom and then discovered, repeatedly, that communities disagree intensely about what teachers should be free to teach. It praised merit while changing the definition of merit whenever the people arriving at the gate changed.

Schools make those contradictions visible because they force a society to schedule them.

At eight o’clock, students enter. Someone has to decide which students. A bell rings. Someone has to decide what knowledge receives the next forty-five minutes. A test is administered. Someone has to decide what the score means. A student speaks. Someone has to decide whether the speech is learning, disruption, politics, disrespect, or all four. A parent complains. A board meets. A court intervenes. A budget is cut. A new building opens. A neighborhood changes. A technology arrives. The ideal becomes a timetable.

This is not a comprehensive history of American education. It cannot be. Twenty-six schools leave out almost everything. Harvard could plausibly occupy many decades and appears only at the end. The great historically Black colleges and universities deserve volumes rather than a single chapter. The common-school movement cannot be reduced to one normal school. The history of Catholic schools, Jewish schools, tribal colleges, community colleges, land-grant universities, vocational education, special education, homeschooling, for-profit higher education, school finance, teachers’ unions, standardized testing, and student debt all exceed the frame.

The exclusions are part of the method. A forced choice sharpens attention. To nominate one school is to say what you think mattered most about the decade. The reader should disagree with me at least a few times. I would distrust a list this long that produced no argument.

I use five tests when making the choices.

First, the school must reveal a national tension, not merely a local curiosity. The story should open outward into questions about citizenship, authority, labor, race, gender, religion, technology, public finance, speech, or opportunity.

Second, the school should have changed something beyond itself. That change may be direct, as when a model is copied, or symbolic, as when one campus becomes the place where the country watches a conflict it had been avoiding.

Third, the school should permit a story. Institutions are made of people, rooms, timetables, budgets, ambitions, mistakes, and consequences. A chapter should not read like an encyclopedia entry with a thesis stapled to the top.

Fourth, the selection should complicate the usual hierarchy of educational importance. Universities matter enormously, but a high school can alter constitutional history. A tiny experimental college can shape art far beyond its enrollment. An obscure alternative program in a recreation center can become the first working example of an institutional form that spreads across a nation.

Fifth, the legacy must survive scrutiny. Some schools deserve admiration. Some deserve grief. Most deserve both praise and argument. I have tried to preserve the difference between what people hoped an institution would do, what it actually did, and what later generations decided the story meant.

The book covers twenty-six named decades even though the United States turns 250 in 2026. That is not a mathematical mistake. The span from 1776 through 2026 touches part of the 1770s, all of the decades in between, and part of the 2020s. History rarely agrees to begin on the first day of a tidy interval.

The opening and closing choices therefore face each other across the book.

William & Mary in 1779 asks how a republic trains people to govern. Harvard in the 2020s asks who gets through the gate of institutions that confer unusual access to power, and under what constitutional rules. Between them, the American school becomes public, professional, coeducational, interracial, compulsory, bureaucratic, experimental, segregated, desegregated, politicized, digitized, networked, and increasingly difficult to define by a physical campus alone.

There is another reason to end in the 2020s without pretending the decade has already supplied its verdict. The school itself is changing.

During the pandemic, classrooms migrated onto screens with astonishing speed and uneven success. A student could be physically absent from a school building while still formally present. Long before that, MIT had put course materials online for anyone to use. By the middle of the 2020s, generative artificial intelligence could explain a theorem, draft an essay, translate a paragraph, write code, produce a study guide, and imitate the surface features of competent schoolwork in seconds. The old institutional bargain—come to this place, encounter these teachers, complete these assignments, earn this credential—now sits beside tools that can separate instruction, practice, production, evaluation, and credentialing from one another.

That makes the historical record more useful, not less.

Every generation has been tempted to think its school crisis is unprecedented. Usually some part of it is new and some part is an old argument in new clothing. Americans have spent two and a half centuries asking whether education should serve democracy or employment, whether schools should transmit a culture or help students challenge it, whether local communities or distant authorities should decide what happens in a classroom, whether admission should reward inherited preparation or correct for unequal preparation, whether practical knowledge threatens intellectual depth, whether new media corrupt attention, and whether the people teaching the next generation possess too much authority or too little.

The arguments persist because they do not have final answers. They are conflicts among goods.

A school can be more open and become harder to fund. It can be more rigorous and less forgiving. It can preserve a tradition and exclude people who were never allowed to shape that tradition. It can give teachers autonomy and make accountability harder to standardize. It can measure achievement more consistently and narrow what gets taught. It can protect students from political pressure and become politically insulated. It can respond to labor markets and train people for jobs that disappear. It can resist markets and become unaffordable.

The interesting historical question is rarely whether one side was simply foolish. It is what problem people thought they were solving, what their solution made possible, and what new problem arrived with it.

That is the story behind these schools.

The American republic did not merely build classrooms. Again and again, it entered them to argue with itself.
