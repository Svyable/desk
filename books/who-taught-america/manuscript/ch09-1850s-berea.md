# Chapter 9 — 1850s: Berea College — An Interracial School in a Slaveholding State

In 1855, John Gregg Fee opened a one-room school in Kentucky.

The word *school* is almost too small for what the decision meant.

Kentucky was a slaveholding state. Fee was an abolitionist minister who argued not merely against slavery but for racial equality, a position that made him more radical than many white opponents of slavery. The school that developed into Berea College was interracial and coeducational. Its existence asked a question that the country was trying very hard not to answer peacefully: if Black and white students can learn together, what exactly is segregation protecting?

The school of the 1850s stands close to the fault line that would break the nation.

By the middle of the decade, the United States had accumulated compromises that no longer looked much like compromise. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 strengthened federal mechanisms for returning people who escaped slavery. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 reopened the territorial expansion of slavery to political struggle and helped produce bloodshed in Kansas. The Supreme Court's Dred Scott decision would arrive in 1857, declaring that Black people could not be citizens under the Constitution as the Court interpreted it and restricting Congress's power to prohibit slavery in the territories.

Against that national background, an interracial school in Kentucky was not a quaint reform experiment.

It contradicted the social order in its seating plan.

Berea began before it was a college in the modern sense. Fee's one-room school developed into an academy and then an institution of higher learning. The later college should not be projected backward fully formed onto 1855. What mattered at the beginning was the principle: education across the racial line.

That principle was dangerous because schools do more than transmit ideas about equality.

They create relationships that make hierarchy harder to maintain psychologically and administratively.

Segregation depends on distance. It tells groups that they are fundamentally different and then organizes institutions to prevent ordinary evidence from accumulating against the claim.

An interracial classroom disrupts the arrangement.

Students encounter the same texts. They perform the same exercises. They may be corrected by the same teacher. Academic success becomes comparable. Friendship becomes possible. Competition becomes possible. So does hostility. Shared space does not guarantee harmony, but it removes the institutional convenience of pretending the other group belongs to an entirely different order.

That is why segregationists have so often fought schools with unusual intensity.

The schoolhouse contains children, which allows political arguments to be framed as protection. It also contains the future, which makes every admission decision feel like a statement about the society to come.

An integrated school says the future is shared.

Berea's founders were saying that before the Civil War.

The institution's later history demonstrates how threatening the principle remained. Kentucky's 1904 Day Law prohibited interracial education. Berea challenged the statute but lost at the U.S. Supreme Court in 1908. The college was forced to segregate and created a separate institution for Black students before later reintegrating after the law changed.

That sequence belongs to a later decade, but it illuminates the 1850s choice.

The state eventually used law to dismantle an interracial institution that had existed for decades.

Segregation was not simply a cultural habit left over from slavery. It had to be built and enforced.

This is one of the central corrections that school history offers to national memory.

People often imagine racial separation as the absence of integration, as though communities simply developed apart until civil-rights reformers brought them together. Berea shows a different process. Interracial education existed. Law later prohibited it.

Exclusion can be a reaction to inclusion.

The timing matters because it complicates the common story of steady progress. The United States can widen a right and then narrow it. Institutions can become more inclusive and later be forced backward. A reform does not become irreversible merely because it is morally compelling.

Schools are particularly vulnerable because they depend on law for charters, funding, accreditation, taxation, and compulsory attendance.

The public power that can open a classroom can close it.

Berea was a private institution, but the state still possessed regulatory power over education within its borders. That fact would become important across the twentieth century as courts distinguished between private choice and state action while governments used licensing, taxation, and law to shape schooling.

The school therefore belongs in a long struggle over whether freedom of association can be used to defend inclusion as well as exclusion.

In 1855, the most immediate threat was not an abstract legal theory. It was violence and social hostility.

Abolitionists in slaveholding regions operated under serious danger. Fee faced opposition because his beliefs challenged economic interests, racial hierarchy, and community norms. The Berea project would be disrupted during the Civil War era, and its participants could not assume institutional survival.

This uncertainty matters.

Later history can make successful institutions look protected by their eventual reputation. The one-room school had no guarantee it would become a college known more than a century later.

It was a fragile claim with desks.

That is a useful description of many important schools.

The institution becomes historically consequential because someone is willing to make a principle routine before society has agreed to it.

Abolitionism itself often operated through such institutions: churches, newspapers, lecture societies, schools, vigilance committees, and networks assisting freedom seekers. Political movements require places where people can practice relationships that the wider society resists.

The school is unusually powerful because it includes time.

A meeting can gather people for an evening. A school asks them to return tomorrow.

Repetition turns principle into habit.

An interracial classroom does not announce equality once. It assumes enough equality to continue teaching on Wednesday.

That ordinariness can be more disruptive than rhetoric.

Berea also forces us to distinguish abolition from racial equality.

Many white Americans who opposed slavery did not believe in equal education, equal political rights, interracial institutions, or social equality. The end of legal enslavement did not logically guarantee the end of white supremacy because white supremacy had ideological and institutional life beyond slavery.

Berea's educational principle moved into that harder territory.

If Black students belong in the same classroom, then the school has to treat them as intellectual peers.

That is a stronger claim than saying slavery is economically inefficient or morally troubling.

Peerhood destabilizes paternalism.

A paternalistic reformer can argue that an oppressed group deserves protection while retaining authority over the group. A peer relationship requires the possibility that the other person may know more, perform better, disagree, lead, or judge.

Education can therefore become a test of whether equality is sentimental or operational.

Who marks the paper?

Who wins the prize?

Who becomes the teacher?

Who shares the dormitory, library, or laboratory?

Institutions convert moral language into these small distributions of authority.

Berea's story is also inseparable from class.

The college later developed a distinctive commitment to students of limited financial means and a work program in which students contribute labor. Those mature features should not be retroactively assigned to the one-room school in complete form. But the institution's development connected racial inclusion with an educational philosophy skeptical of inherited privilege.

That combination is important because American colleges have often widened one dimension of access while remaining exclusive on another.

A college can admit across race but remain unreachable to poor families. It can admit women but serve a narrow class. It can provide financial aid while requiring cultural knowledge that favors students from college-educated households.

Access is multidimensional.

Berea's later identity would make that visible by treating work and affordability as part of educational structure rather than as private problems students solved outside the classroom.

This raises a question that becomes central in American higher education: is work an obstacle to education or part of education?

Elite residential colleges historically gained status partly by separating study from manual labor. Students whose families could support them had time to read, debate, and prepare. Working students had less time and could be marked as less committed when they were simply less subsidized.

Institutions that integrate work challenge the hierarchy between intellectual and manual activity.

That hierarchy had racial meaning in a slave society.

Enslavement associated forced manual labor with Blackness while white elites claimed intellectual and political authority. After emancipation, debates over Black education would repeatedly turn on whether schooling should emphasize industrial labor or liberal learning. Tuskegee, the school of the 1880s in this book, would become central to that argument.

Berea arrives earlier and offers a different combination: interracial education linked to the dignity of labor without accepting racial subordination as the organizing principle.

The distinction matters.

Work can be educational when chosen, compensated, structured, and connected to institutional membership. Forced labor is something else entirely. American rhetoric has sometimes blurred them by praising discipline in ways that obscure coercion.

Schools are especially capable of moralizing labor because they control students' time.

The line between education and exploitation can become thin when institutions require work from people with little power.

A serious history must ask who benefits.

Berea's importance does not depend on claiming every practice was equitable. It depends on the institution's attempt to make interracial education sustainable in a region where the racial order was enforced by law and violence.

That attempt created opponents precisely because it worked enough to matter.

The later Day Law is evidence of the threat. Legislators did not need to prohibit interracial education as an abstraction. They were responding to institutions including Berea that made it real.

This pattern recurs throughout civil-rights history.

Law often arrives after practice begins.

People form interracial organizations, unions, neighborhoods, marriages, schools, or businesses. The state then decides whether to protect, ignore, regulate, or suppress the practice.

Legal conflict is therefore sometimes a lagging indicator of social change.

The 1850s school gives us the practice before the later prohibition.

It also shows why education and race became so tightly connected in Reconstruction.

After emancipation, formerly enslaved people pursued schooling with extraordinary intensity. They established schools, supported teachers, built institutions, and demanded public education because literacy and formal learning were connected to autonomy, labor contracts, political participation, family life, religious leadership, and protection against fraud.

The stereotype that education was bestowed on freedpeople by northern benevolence collapses under the historical record. Black communities were active educational agents.

Berea's earlier interracial project belongs adjacent to that history, not above it.

The school was founded by a white abolitionist, but its meaning depended on Black students choosing to attend, families accepting risk, teachers sustaining instruction, and communities contesting what equality would look like.

No integration story should be narrated as if the institution generously allowed passive beneficiaries inside.

Admission is one act. Participation makes the school.

This will become especially important in the chapter on Little Rock Central High School. The constitutional crisis of 1957 is often told through Eisenhower, Governor Orval Faubus, federal troops, and the Supreme Court. Those actors matter. But the school was changed by nine Black students who had to enter it.

Policy becomes history through people willing to inhabit it.

Berea's students inhabited an idea before national law could secure it.

The decade around them was moving toward war.

The Compromise of 1850 failed to settle sectional conflict. “Bleeding Kansas” exposed how territorial democracy could become armed struggle. The Republican Party emerged. Dred Scott narrowed Black citizenship. John Brown's raid at Harpers Ferry in 1859 intensified fear and polarization. By 1861 the Union would fracture.

Against that chronology, a school can look peripheral.

It is not.

The war was about political power and slavery, not a dispute over classroom seating. But the classroom seating revealed the social order underneath the political conflict.

What would freedom mean if slavery ended?

Would Black Americans be citizens? Voters? property owners? professionals? teachers? students? classmates?

Abolition could destroy the legal institution of slavery without answering any of those questions.

Berea answered one of them in advance.

Classmates.

That answer helps explain why Reconstruction would become an educational revolution as well as a constitutional one.

The end of slavery created millions of people whom the old order had systematically denied education. Schools became among the first institutions through which freedom was made practical.

Learning to read a contract changes labor. Learning law changes the ability to claim rights. Training teachers makes the next school possible. A college can produce ministers, lawyers, physicians, educators, and political leaders.

Education multiplies emancipation.

White supremacists understood this too.

That is why schools, teachers, and educational institutions became targets of intimidation and political control.

The struggle was not only over what Black people knew. It was over what roles knowledge would allow Black people to occupy.

An integrated school sharpened the threat by refusing the premise that those roles should be racially separate.

Berea's story also exposes the limits of relying on local tolerance for fundamental rights.

Localism is one of the most persistent American educational values. Communities want control over their schools because schools embody local culture and children are deeply important to families.

But local control can defend injustice.

If a local majority opposes interracial education, should it be able to prohibit an institution from practicing it? If a state passes a segregation law, does constitutional liberty protect the school?

The early twentieth-century Supreme Court would answer Berea's challenge in a way that allowed Kentucky's segregation law to stand.

Half a century later, the Court would hold state-mandated public-school segregation unconstitutional in Brown v. Board of Education.

The distance between those outcomes illustrates how constitutional meaning can change while institutions live through the interval.

A school can be morally ahead of the law and legally defeated by it.

That is not a comforting fact for people who assume legal progress moves in one direction.

Institutions need more than virtue. They need political protection.

The one-room school of 1855 therefore contains a larger lesson about reform.

It is not enough to imagine a just arrangement.

Someone has to build it, enroll in it, finance it, defend it, and preserve enough legal space for it to continue.

Berea survived, but not without interruption and not without compromise forced by the state.

Its endurance matters because it preserved memory of a different possibility.

Segregationists could claim separation was natural or inevitable.

Berea's own history said otherwise.

There had been a school where Black and white students learned together before the Civil War.

The law later made that harder, not history.

This is why the school of the 1850s is not a battlefield, political convention, or famous university.

It is a one-room experiment in Kentucky that refused to organize intelligence by race.

The country would soon go to war over slavery.

Berea’s founding in the 1850s is most revealing when its interracial and coeducational mission is placed against the region around it rather than treated as an isolated statement of principle. A school does not become historically significant merely by announcing inclusion. The significance comes from attempting to operate an inclusive institution where law, custom, violence, economics, and political power make that inclusion costly.

That is why the independent scholarly history matters alongside Berea’s own institutional memory. Founders’ stories naturally emphasize intention. A broader history asks what happened to the intention once it met the social order. Who could attend? Who stayed? How did outside pressure shape the institution? How did regional conflict alter what was possible? Those questions move the chapter from commemoration to institutional history.

Berea also complicates the assumption that educational inclusion always spreads outward from the nation’s largest cities or wealthiest colleges. Educational experiments often emerge at margins where local conditions make the contradiction especially sharp. Kentucky sat at a border—geographic, political, and social—between different regimes of slavery, emancipation, migration, and postwar development. An interracial school there made a national conflict tangible in a local institution.

The school’s coeducational character adds another dimension. Race and gender exclusions do not operate separately in the lives of students, even when institutions discuss them in separate policies. A school that changes both boundaries has to negotiate multiple inherited hierarchies at once. That can widen possibility while also producing new compromises about curriculum, labor, residence, respectability, and public presentation.

The important point is not to turn Berea into a flawless moral exception. Institutions that pursue egalitarian missions still live inside unequal economies and political systems. They can retreat, adapt, or be constrained. Their students can experience both opportunity and discrimination. Their rhetoric can exceed their practice. The serious historical question is how much institutional capacity an egalitarian idea can build before outside power reshapes it.

That question would become acute after the Civil War, when emancipation made Black education a national political issue rather than a regional reform project. Berea’s earlier experiment provides a bridge into that later world. It showed before Appomattox that integrated education could be organized, and therefore that segregation was not simply a natural consequence of separate social development. It was a political order that had alternatives.

Educational institutions matter in such moments because they generate evidence. A functioning interracial classroom disproves claims that racial separation is required for learning simply by existing. The existence does not abolish prejudice, but it changes the argumentative terrain. Opponents can no longer say only that another arrangement is unimaginable; they have to oppose something that has been tried.

That is a recurring form of school-based political change. The institution becomes a demonstration project for a different social rule. Sometimes the demonstration spreads. Sometimes law crushes it. Sometimes it survives in compromised form. In every case, the school turns an abstract dispute over equality into a practical dispute over who may sit next to whom, study the same material, receive the same credential, and claim the same future.

The school’s later encounters with segregation law also underscore why the 1850s founding cannot be read backward from whatever institutional form survived later. An egalitarian educational project can be forced to change by external law even when its internal mission remains broader. That difference—between what a school chooses and what the state permits—becomes crucial in later chapters on Little Rock and Boston. Berea shows an earlier version of the same contest: educational practice can expose the political nature of rules presented as social inevitabilities.

That helps explain why institutional endurance matters. A reform school that lasts becomes an archive of conflict. Its admissions records, catalogs, buildings, alumni, and policy changes preserve evidence that alternative arrangements existed and that people fought over them. The institution’s importance is therefore not only in whom it educated in a particular year, but in the historical memory it makes available to later generations arguing over what was possible.

Berea had already begun asking what freedom would require after the war ended.
