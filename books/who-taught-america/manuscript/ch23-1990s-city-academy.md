# Chapter 23 — 1990s: City Academy — The First Charter School Opens a Door

In September 1992, a small school in St. Paul, Minnesota, opened under a new legal arrangement.

City Academy served students who had struggled in conventional schools, including young people who had dropped out or were at risk of doing so. Its scale was modest. Its place in educational history was not.

Minnesota had passed the nation's first charter-school law in 1991. City Academy became the first operating charter school in the country the following year.

The school of the 1990s matters because it created a new answer to a question Americans had been asking for more than a century.

Who gets to run a public school?

The ordinary answer had been a school district.

Districts were public institutions governed through elected or appointed boards, administrative hierarchies, collective bargaining, state law, regulation, and local taxation. Their bureaucratic form had grown because mass schooling required coordination at scale.

Chartering proposed a different bargain.

A school could remain public—tuition-free, publicly funded, and accountable to public law—while operating with greater autonomy from some district rules in exchange for a formal agreement about mission and results.

The charter was both permission and contract.

That distinction gave the movement its name and its political flexibility.

Different people could see very different things in it.

Teachers could see autonomy from central-office bureaucracy.

Parents could see another option when the assigned school was not working.

Civil-rights advocates could see a way to build schools around underserved communities.

Market-oriented reformers could see competition introduced into a public monopoly.

District leaders could see laboratories for innovation.

Unions could see a threat to collective bargaining and professional protections.

Entrepreneurs could see an opening for new school-management organizations.

Conservatives and progressives could both imagine the charter as their reform.

The coalition was powerful because the term was broad enough to hold incompatible theories.

City Academy's own origin was much closer to the small, teacher- and community-oriented alternative-school vision than to the large charter networks that later came to define parts of the movement.

That gap between origin and expansion is the chapter.

Educational innovations rarely remain owned by their inventors.

Once the legal form exists, other people use it for other purposes.

The Minnesota law authorized a new species of public institution.

That species evolved.

Some charter schools became small community schools. Some became highly structured college-preparatory academies. Some emphasized arts, language, classical curricula, technology, or vocational themes. Some were run by nonprofit networks. Some states permitted for-profit management relationships. Some schools achieved strong academic outcomes. Others performed poorly or closed. Some served diverse student populations. Others contributed to racial or socioeconomic isolation.

The category became too broad to carry one judgment.

That is one of the most important lessons of the charter era.

A governance structure is not a pedagogy.

Calling a school a charter tells you something about its legal relationship to public authority.

It tells you much less about what happens in a classroom.

The same is true of “district school,” “private school,” “magnet school,” “religious school,” or “homeschool.”

Institutional categories matter, but adults often attach educational quality to the category before examining the practice.

This is politically convenient because categories support coalitions.

Evidence is messier.

City Academy is therefore a useful place to begin because the first charter was not a national ideological brand yet.

It was a local solution to a practical problem.

Some students had not succeeded in the conventional system.

A small group wanted enough autonomy to build something different without leaving public education entirely.

That is the strongest intellectual case for chartering.

A public system should have mechanisms for trying alternatives when standard arrangements repeatedly fail particular students.

Innovation requires variance.

A system that requires every school to operate identically protects consistency at the cost of experimentation.

The question is how much variance public legitimacy can tolerate.

Autonomy sounds attractive until a school fails.

Then someone has to be responsible.

Who hired the teachers?

Who reviewed the finances?

Who ensured students received required services?

Who decided the curriculum was adequate?

Who closes the school?

The charter model answers with the institution of the authorizer.

An approved public or nonprofit body grants the charter, monitors the school, and can revoke or decline to renew authorization if conditions are not met. Minnesota's modern charter system still uses authorizers as a central accountability mechanism.

This creates a different governance chain from the traditional district.

A district school belongs to a larger bureaucracy that can reassign leaders, move staff, change programs, or close the campus.

A charter school has more organizational independence and therefore needs a separate mechanism for public oversight.

The quality of that oversight becomes decisive.

Autonomy without accountability can become license.

Accountability without autonomy recreates the district bureaucracy under another name.

The charter bargain lives between the two.

That balance is difficult because educational outcomes are slow and multidimensional.

A school can have weak test scores because it serves students who arrive far behind and still be doing valuable work.

Another can post strong scores partly because it attracts families with more information or screens indirectly through discipline, transportation, enrollment timing, or cultural expectations.

A simple outcome measure can punish schools serving harder populations and reward schools benefiting from selection.

This is not an argument against measurement.

It is an argument for causal humility.

School quality is not identical to student outcomes because student outcomes reflect both the school and the students' prior circumstances.

Policymakers know this in theory and repeatedly forget it in rhetoric.

Charter schools intensified the issue because comparison was built into the reform.

If families can choose between schools, results become market information.

But education is an unusual market.

The consumer is often a child. The purchaser is partly the state. The decision-maker is usually a parent. The benefits unfold over years. Quality is difficult to observe before enrollment. Switching schools has social and logistical costs. Transportation limits realistic options. Popular schools may have more applicants than seats. Families differ in time, language, confidence, and access to information.

Choice exists inside unequal capacity to choose.

That does not make choice meaningless.

It makes the design more important.

A wealthy family already possesses substantial school choice through housing and private tuition.

A poor family assigned to a weak neighborhood school may possess very little.

Charter advocates correctly identified the inequality inside the phrase “neighborhood school.”

If an address determines access and good-school addresses are expensive, the housing market has already created a choice system for people with money.

The charter asks whether public policy should create additional exits.

The counterargument is that exits can weaken the common system.

If engaged families leave, political pressure to improve the district school may decline. Enrollment loss can reduce funding faster than fixed costs fall. A city can end up maintaining multiple parallel infrastructures. Competition may encourage improvement or fragmentation depending on context.

The charter debate therefore sits between two theories of public improvement.

Voice and exit.

Voice says citizens improve common institutions by participating in them, voting, organizing, and demanding reform.

Exit says institutions improve when dissatisfied people can leave and alternatives can grow.

Healthy systems often need both.

Too much voice without exit can trap people inside institutions resistant to reform.

Too much exit can hollow out shared institutions and allow people with greater resources to abandon the consequences.

City Academy created a new public exit that did not require leaving the public sector.

That was the innovation.

The 1990s were fertile ground for it because confidence in large bureaucratic institutions had weakened.

Across politics, leaders promoted decentralization, competition, performance management, and entrepreneurial approaches to government. The Cold War had ended. Technology firms were becoming cultural models of innovation. “Reinventing government” entered public-administration language. Democrats as well as Republicans embraced forms of market-oriented reform.

Education policy absorbed the mood.

The district began to look like a monopoly.

The entrepreneur began to look like a reformer.

This was a substantial cultural change from the Progressive Era, when expert bureaucracy had itself been the reform against corruption, patronage, and local inconsistency.

Institutions age into the problem they were built to solve.

Bureaucracy once represented professionalism.

By the late twentieth century, bureaucracy often represented rigidity.

The charter school promised an organizational reset.

Its strongest advocates said teachers and school leaders could design coherent institutions without layers of central rules.

This claim deserves serious attention because coherence matters enormously in education.

A school where hiring, schedule, curriculum, discipline, professional development, and mission point in the same direction can be more effective than one where each function is controlled by a different office with different incentives.

Autonomy can create coherence.

It can also create idiosyncrasy.

A charismatic founder can build a strong culture and weak succession plan. A board can lack expertise. Financial controls can fail. A school can become too dependent on one philosophy or leader.

District bureaucracy is slow partly because it contains redundancy.

Redundancy is inefficient until something goes wrong.

Then it looks like protection.

Chartering therefore raises the same systems question that appears throughout this book.

How much freedom should an institution have before variation becomes unacceptable risk?

The answer depends partly on what is at stake.

A failed restaurant closes and customers eat elsewhere.

A failed school may consume years of a child's education that cannot be fully returned.

This makes educational experimentation ethically different from ordinary entrepreneurship.

Students are not venture capital.

A system needs room to innovate and mechanisms to detect failure quickly enough to protect children.

Authorizing was supposed to provide those mechanisms.

The history of chartering shows how hard the job is.

Some authorizers became rigorous. Others were permissive. Political pressure could encourage expansion before oversight capacity existed. States designed different funding, enrollment, closure, and governance rules, making “charter school” a different institutional creature from one jurisdiction to another.

The original Minnesota model cannot be treated as the single national model.

Legal replication created variation.

This is another reason City Academy is historically interesting.

The first instance was small enough that nobody could yet know what the category would become.

The founders were opening a school.

They were also opening a legal door.

Thousands of institutions would later walk through it.

The 1990s charter movement also changed the role of parents in school governance.

Traditional public education gives parents collective political power through school boards and individual relational power through teachers and principals.

Choice reforms add consumer power.

If you do not like the school, select another.

Consumer power can be immediate and concrete.

Political power is slower and shared.

The two forms shape behavior differently.

A school competing for enrollment has incentives to respond to families.

It may also have incentives to market itself, avoid expensive students, or emphasize visible features that influence choice more than deeper educational quality.

Rules against discrimination and selective admission become essential because schools cannot be allowed to choose only the easiest students while claiming superior results.

Minnesota's current charter framework describes charter schools as tuition-free and open to all students, reflecting the public obligation inside the autonomy model.

The open-door principle is central.

Without it, chartering risks becoming public subsidy for private selection.

But formal openness does not guarantee equal access.

A school without transportation may be practically inaccessible to some families. A complicated application can deter others. Strict discipline can create attrition. Specialized themes can attract particular populations. Location matters.

Access has logistical architecture.

The same lesson appeared with disability education, college admissions, and neighborhood schools.

A right on paper requires an operating path.

Charter schools also became entangled with teachers' unions.

The original charter concept had roots that included educators interested in giving groups of teachers room to create schools with professional autonomy. As the movement grew, chartering often became associated with operating outside district collective-bargaining structures.

A reform that could be described as teacher empowerment became, in some places, a challenge to teacher organizations.

The shift demonstrates how institutional forms can change political meaning through implementation.

Autonomy from bureaucracy can mean autonomy for teachers.

It can also mean autonomy for management from teachers.

Who holds the autonomy?

That question should accompany every decentralization reform.

Moving power away from a central office does not make power disappear.

It moves somewhere.

To a principal. A nonprofit board. A management organization. Parents. Teachers. A vendor. An authorizer.

The design should be judged by where the power goes and what checks follow it.

Charter rhetoric often celebrated “freedom” without specifying the holder.

The first City Academy model makes the original localism easier to see.

A small institution serving students the conventional system had not served well could use organizational flexibility to create a different environment.

That is the humane core of the movement.

The danger is turning a tool into an ideology.

Once charter schools became national political symbols, evidence about individual schools could be recruited into preexisting camps. A successful charter became proof the sector worked. A failed charter became proof the sector failed.

The category replaced analysis.

This is one of the recurring pathologies of educational politics.

Adults want models to be either solutions or threats.

Schools are too particular for the demand.

Leadership matters. Curriculum matters. culture matters. enrollment matters. funding matters. governance matters. neighborhood matters. state law matters.

Two schools with the same legal category can be educational opposites.

City Academy's historical significance therefore should not be used as an endorsement of every charter school that followed.

Nor should later sector problems erase the innovation represented by the first school.

A public system had created a mechanism for a new school to exist without waiting for a district to build it in the conventional way.

That mechanism changed American education permanently.

It also anticipated the broader unbundling of institutions that the internet would accelerate.

A charter separates the public function of schooling from one traditional provider.

MIT OpenCourseWare, in the next chapter, will separate course content from admission and credential.

Online education will separate learning from place.

Artificial intelligence will separate some forms of instruction and feedback from human teachers.

Each unbundling asks which parts of the old institution were essential and which were merely bundled together by history.

Chartering asked first at organizational scale.

Do public funds, public purpose, district employment, district rules, district buildings, and district governance all have to come as one package?

Minnesota said no.

That answer created possibility and complexity in equal measure.

The 1990s school is therefore not important because it proved a new model superior.

It proved a new model possible.

There is a difference.

Possibility is enough to alter politics.

After City Academy opened, opponents of conventional district arrangements could point to an operating alternative. Legislatures in other states could copy the legal form. Parents could demand charter laws. Educators could build new schools. Investors and philanthropists could organize networks. Researchers could compare outcomes.

The experiment escaped the building.

That is how institutional innovation spreads.

The first school does not need to become large.

The legal idea does.

City Academy opened in St. Paul with a small group of students.

The important thing was not the size of the class.

City Academy’s opening matters because a charter school is defined as much by its legal architecture as by its classroom. Minnesota’s 1991 law created a new way for a public school to exist: publicly funded and tuition-free, but operating with a degree of autonomy under an authorizing relationship rather than as a conventional district school. City Academy made that statutory possibility operational in 1992.

The authorizer is central to the bargain. Autonomy was not supposed to mean absence of accountability. The charter structure exchanged some traditional input controls for a contract-like arrangement in which an outside body could approve, oversee, renew, or close a school. That design embodied a theory of reform: give educators room to build different institutions, then judge whether the institution earns the right to continue.

The appeal is easy to see. Large districts can be slow to change, and a new school can test a model without redesigning an entire system. Families may gain another public option. Teachers and community organizations may be able to create schools around missions that would be difficult to establish through ordinary district procedures.

The risks are equally structural. Once many schools operate under different authorizers, organizations, and management arrangements, oversight can fragment. Quality can diverge. The same autonomy that enables innovation can enable weak governance. A sector designed to escape bureaucracy can develop a new bureaucracy of authorizers, contracts, compliance systems, and operators.

That later divergence should not be projected backward onto City Academy as though the first school contained every future outcome. The historical significance of the opening is that it created a new organizational species inside public education. What the species later became depended on laws, authorizers, markets, communities, and political choices that varied dramatically across states.

Charter schooling also changed the meaning of “public.” A conventional definition ties public education to district operation. The charter model emphasizes public funding, open access within legal rules, public authorization, and accountability while allowing non-district organizations to operate schools. That distinction produced decades of argument because it separates public purpose from one inherited organizational form.

The 1990s were full of institutions being unbundled by markets, networks, and new management ideas. Charter schools brought that logic into K–12 education before the internet would unbundle access to course content. They asked whether the public could preserve a guarantee—education available without tuition—while permitting more variation in who built and ran the institution.

City Academy therefore matters less as proof that charter schools work or fail than as proof that the organizational boundary could move. After 1992, the argument was no longer whether such a public school could legally exist somewhere in the United States. It was what rules should govern a growing sector once it did.

The first school also illustrates a general rule of institutional innovation: once the legal category exists, political attention moves from permission to performance. Before the law, advocates argue that the model should be allowed. After opening, families, authorizers, legislators, and critics can ask whether particular schools are serving students well. That shift from hypothetical debate to observable institutions is what turns reform into a sector.

It was the fact that Minnesota had changed who was allowed to create one.
