# Chapter 26 — 2020s: Harvard — The Gate Changes Again

On June 29, 2023, the Supreme Court decided *Students for Fair Admissions v. President and Fellows of Harvard College*.

The case concerned admissions at one of the most selective universities in the world, but the decision reached far beyond Cambridge.

For decades, American colleges had been permitted under Supreme Court precedent to consider an applicant's race as one factor within a broader admissions process under constitutional and statutory limits. Harvard defended its version of that practice through years of litigation. The Court rejected the system.

The gate changed.

Harvard is the school of the 2020s not because the decade can already be summarized by one institution. It cannot. The decade is unfinished. A pandemic emptied classrooms. Generative artificial intelligence altered what it means to produce schoolwork. College costs and public confidence remain under pressure. Student protest has again placed universities at the center of national politics. Enrollment patterns are shifting. States and institutions are experimenting with new pathways, credentials, tests, and technologies.

Harvard belongs because one legal dispute at its admissions office captures the oldest question in this book in its most modern form.

Who gets in?

The question has followed American education from the beginning.

William & Mary trained a narrow governing class.

Public universities widened the state's role without immediately widening the public very far.

Troy Female Seminary challenged gender limits on curriculum.

Oberlin expanded collegiate admission across race and sex.

Howard built a historically Black institution because the broader system did not provide equal access.

Little Rock forced a public high school to honor a constitutional right against racial exclusion.

Harvard in the 2020s asks something different.

What may an institution consider when the legal gate is formally open but the applicants have arrived through profoundly unequal histories?

That question is harder than explicit exclusion.

When a school says “you may not apply because of your race,” the injustice is direct.

When every applicant may formally apply but preparation differs through family wealth, neighborhood schools, housing, discrimination, immigration history, health, language, extracurricular access, legacy networks, test preparation, and accumulated opportunity, the institution has to decide what fairness means inside unequal inputs.

Merit does not arrive raw.

It arrives documented.

A transcript. A test score. An essay. A recommendation. A list of activities. An award. A job. A family responsibility. A talent. A hardship. An institutional priority.

Admissions offices turn these fragments into a class.

That act is necessarily judgment.

The fantasy of perfectly objective admissions survives because both defenders and critics of selectivity want a neutral machine that does not exist.

A university has fewer seats than qualified applicants.

Selection therefore requires values.

Which achievements matter more?

How should opportunity be considered?

Does unusual accomplishment under difficult circumstances demonstrate more than the same accomplishment under abundant support?

Should geographic diversity matter?

First-generation status?

Athletic ability?

Alumni connection?

Military service?

Intellectual distinction?

Potential contribution to a class?

No formula can answer these questions without embedding a theory of what the institution is for.

Harvard's legal dispute became explosive because race has a unique constitutional and historical status in the United States.

Supporters of race-conscious admissions argued that institutions should be able to consider race within individualized review because racial inequality affects opportunity and because diverse educational environments can produce important benefits.

Opponents argued that treating applicants differently because of race violates equal-treatment principles, unfairly burdens some applicants, and permits institutions to classify individuals through categories the Constitution should constrain.

The Supreme Court's majority largely accepted the latter constitutional critique of the programs before it.

The dissents emphasized the country's history of racial exclusion and argued that the Constitution did not require universities to ignore race while inequality remained racially structured.

Both positions claim equality.

They disagree about what equality requires.

That is why the case cannot be understood as one side supporting fairness and the other opposing it.

The conflict is between two theories of fairness.

One emphasizes individual nonclassification: the state and institutions governed by relevant law should not allocate opportunity by race.

The other emphasizes historical and social context: formally identical treatment can preserve inequalities produced through past and present racial structures.

American civil-rights law has repeatedly moved between these perspectives.

Schools make the tension unusually concrete because admission is a scarce opportunity allocated one person at a time.

A policy can be justified statistically and experienced individually.

That is where politics becomes intimate.

An applicant receives one answer.

Yes or no.

The institution sees a class.

The applicant sees a life.

Selective education therefore creates moral intensity beyond the number of seats involved.

Harvard enrolls a tiny fraction of American students. Most young people will never apply there. Yet Harvard admissions becomes national news because elite schools function as symbols of meritocratic legitimacy.

The argument is not really about one campus.

It is about whether the country's most prestigious gates distribute opportunity in a way Americans can regard as fair.

That symbolic burden is partly deserved and partly absurd.

Harvard does not control the entire opportunity structure of the United States.

Its graduates nevertheless occupy influential positions in government, law, business, research, media, and culture. The institution's admissions practices therefore look upstream from power.

If access to an elite school affects access to elite networks, the gate attracts political scrutiny.

Prestige turns admissions into public policy even at a private university.

This is one reason Harvard returns us to the founding chapters.

The republic began by training a governing class through narrow institutions.

Two and a half centuries later, Americans are still arguing about how governing and professional classes reproduce themselves.

The vocabulary changed from birth and race exclusion to merit, diversity, legacy, context, and holistic review.

The underlying issue did not disappear.

Who gets prepared for power?

The 2023 decision changed what Harvard could consider directly in answering the question.

The Court did not require applicants to erase their life histories from essays. The majority opinion acknowledged that universities could consider an applicant's discussion of how race affected the person's life, so long as admissions did not simply use an essay as a mechanism for the racial preferences the Court rejected.

That distinction creates a new interpretive problem.

The institution may consider experience.

It may not consider race as an abstract plus factor.

But experience and race can be related.

Admissions officers now operate inside a legal boundary that requires them to distinguish the individual's story from the category itself.

That is an intensely American solution.

Treat the person as an individual, but do not pretend the individual's life occurred outside history.

How institutions apply the distinction will take years to understand.

Harvard's first post-decision class data showed immediate shifts and continuities rather than one simple national pattern. For the Class of 2028, Harvard reported a decline in the share of students identifying as Black from the prior class while the Asian American share was unchanged and the Hispanic or Latino share rose. Harvard itself cautioned that one class provides only an early view of the decision's effects.

That caution matters.

A major legal change should not be evaluated through one admissions cycle as if institutions and applicants do not adapt.

High schools may change counseling. Students may change essay strategies. Universities may place greater emphasis on socioeconomic background, first-generation status, geography, recruitment, transfer pathways, or financial aid. Litigation may clarify boundaries. States may adopt their own rules.

Policy effects are dynamic.

The first year measures the shock.

Later years measure adaptation.

This is another reason the 2020s chapter has to end without pretending the decade has reached a verdict.

The gate is still moving.

Harvard's current admissions data illustrate how selective the gate remains regardless of the legal criterion. For the Class of 2029, the university reports nearly forty-eight thousand applicants and roughly two thousand admissions.

The scarcity is extreme.

That should change how we interpret every debate about admission.

When many applicants are academically capable of succeeding, rejection does not necessarily mean an applicant lacked merit.

It means the institution could not or did not choose the applicant within a process of assembling a class.

This distinction is psychologically important because selective universities often allow their scarcity to masquerade as a precise hierarchy of human worth.

The ranking culture reinforces the mistake.

Students are told to reach for the best school.

Schools compete for the best students.

Admissions rates become prestige signals.

Rejection can feel like objective judgment because the institution's status is high.

But extreme selectivity creates stochastic edges.

Tiny differences in institutional priority can decide among people who would all perform well.

Merit becomes overspecified after the fact.

The admitted student appears obviously deserving because admission happened.

The rejected student becomes evidence that something was missing.

History should make us skeptical.

Gates often rationalize themselves.

The most important educational question may therefore be less how perfectly elite schools select and more why access to a small group of elite institutions carries so much social consequence.

If opportunity were distributed broadly across strong colleges, universities, apprenticeships, and professional pathways, the exact composition of one Harvard class would matter less.

The intensity of the admissions fight is partly evidence of scarcity elsewhere.

Americans compete furiously for elite credentials because elite credentials are trusted shortcuts to opportunity.

That returns us to the credential problem.

MIT OpenCourseWare had already demonstrated that access to knowledge could become abundant while access to institutional status remained scarce.

Harvard admissions makes the scarcity explicit.

You can read books by Harvard professors without being a Harvard student.

You can watch lectures, use open educational resources, and learn much of the same subject matter elsewhere.

The credential still carries a social signal that content alone does not.

The question for the 2020s is whether technology will weaken that signal.

Generative artificial intelligence intensifies the challenge.

By the middle of the decade, universities were no longer debating whether AI-generated text existed. They were designing course policies around how students could or could not use it. Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences requires instructors to communicate policies governing generative AI use, and the university provides models ranging from restrictive to permissive approaches.

This variation is historically appropriate.

Different courses teach different things.

An introductory writing seminar may need students to struggle through sentences without outsourcing them because the struggle is the learning.

A computer-science course may reasonably teach students to use AI-assisted coding while evaluating output critically.

A statistics course may permit a tool for explanation but prohibit it during an exam.

The meaningful unit of policy is the learning objective.

AI makes that phrase unavoidable.

For years, schools could confuse the assignment with the learning.

Write an essay.

If the student submits a competent essay, we infer that the student researched, reasoned, structured, drafted, revised, and learned.

Generative AI breaks the inference.

A competent product can now be produced without the learner doing all of the cognitive work the product previously implied.

This is not merely a cheating problem.

It is an assessment-design problem.

What evidence demonstrates learning when the artifact can be generated cheaply?

The answer may bring education back toward practices seen earlier in this book.

Oral defense.

Public explanation.

In-class performance.

Projects with process records.

Teacher knowledge of the student.

Portfolio assessment.

The future may look strangely like Central Park East with better tools.

AI could also widen access in ways earlier reformers would have recognized immediately.

A student can receive explanations at any hour. A multilingual learner can translate. A person embarrassed to ask a basic question can ask repeatedly. A programmer can receive debugging help. A learner can generate practice exercises adapted to a specific misunderstanding.

Instructional abundance grows.

The same technology can bypass the cognitive work education is meant to create.

Again, the question is not whether the tool works.

Work toward what?

Carlisle taught us that effectiveness is morally meaningless without a defensible purpose.

AI brings the principle into a different domain.

If the goal is to produce an essay, AI can be extraordinarily effective.

If the goal is to produce a person capable of writing and reasoning, the evaluation changes.

Schools have to decide which product they care about.

This may be the most important educational question of the 2020s.

The Harvard gate case and the AI question appear unrelated.

They are connected through evidence.

Admissions asks what evidence should count when deciding who has earned entry.

AI asks what evidence should count when deciding whether a student has learned.

Both destabilize traditional signals.

A polished essay used to reveal something about the applicant or student.

Now institutions have to ask how the essay was produced.

A high test score used to seem more objective than an essay.

Now test-optional policies, unequal preparation, and debates over predictive validity complicate the signal.

A transcript reflects performance and the opportunities a school offered.

A recommendation reflects achievement and the recommender's own context.

Every signal is noisy.

Education in the 2020s is becoming an argument over authentication.

What was done by this person?

What does the credential certify?

What does the score measure?

What does admission mean?

What does authorship mean when a tool can generate prose?

What does expertise mean when an AI system can produce a plausible answer faster than an expert can type one?

These questions are not technical side effects.

They go to the social function of the school.

A school has always been an institution that says, after some period of observation, “We know something about what this person can do.”

If the observation becomes unreliable, the credential weakens.

The future of education may therefore depend less on delivering content and more on establishing trustworthy evidence of human capability.

That is a surprising place for 250 years of school history to land.

The first chapter began with a law professor helping prepare men to govern a republic whose institutions were unfinished.

The final chapter ends at a university trying to decide what counts as fair evidence for admission and honest evidence for learning in a society whose institutional rules are changing again.

The symmetry should not be made too perfect.

America's educational history is not a circle.

More people possess formal access to schooling than in 1776 by an almost unimaginable margin. Women dominate many areas of higher-education enrollment. Black students attend institutions that once excluded them by law. Students with disabilities possess legal rights earlier generations did not recognize. Public schools serve a vastly broader definition of the public. Digital knowledge crosses borders instantly.

The gains are real.

So are the old questions in new forms.

Who gets the best preparation?

Who defines merit?

Who controls curriculum?

What does the public owe each child?

What kinds of difference should schools preserve?

Which forms of expertise deserve authority?

When does a credential signal knowledge and when does it mainly signal access to an institution?

What should students still have to learn for themselves when a machine can perform parts of the work?

The 2020s do not answer them.

That is why Harvard is an ending rather than a conclusion.

The Supreme Court changed one admissions rule.

The wider gatekeeping system remained.

AI changed the cost of producing many kinds of intellectual work.

The need to learn remained.

A pandemic proved that instruction could move online almost overnight.

It also reminded families how much school is not instruction: childcare, meals, friendship, routine, social development, safety, identity, and a place where adults notice when a child is missing.

The institution survived the disruption because the institution was more than the lesson.

That may be the deepest lesson of the entire book.

Americans repeatedly redesign schools because they are never only places where information is transferred.

They are places where society allocates attention.

Attention to a language.

A child.

A profession.

A constitutional right.

A scientific question.

A movement.

A future.

The school decides what deserves time, and time is one of the few resources no technology can make infinite.

Harvard's admissions office allocates an especially scarce form of institutional attention.

Its classrooms now have to decide how much cognitive work can be delegated to machines without delegating the education itself.

These are different gates.

They ask the same underlying question.

What must a person do, experience, or demonstrate before an institution is willing to say: you belong here, and you learned something here?

America is 250 years old in 2026.

Harvard’s place in the 2020s is necessarily provisional. The Supreme Court’s 2023 decision changed the legal framework governing race-conscious admissions, but the long-run institutional effects cannot yet be known. Early cohort statistics are evidence of what happened under the first years of a changed regime; they are not a final verdict on what selective admissions will look like a decade from now.

That uncertainty is part of the chapter’s argument. Admissions is a gate whose design responds to law, institutional priorities, applicant behavior, demographic change, and public legitimacy. When one legally available mechanism is removed or constrained, institutions adapt through the remaining parts of the process. The gate changes rather than disappears.

Harvard is also a useful symbol because selective admissions concentrates a much larger American anxiety about merit. Standardized measures, grades, extracurricular achievement, essays, recommendations, geography, family circumstance, athletic recruitment, and institutional priorities all represent competing theories of what opportunity should reward. There is no admissions system without judgment; the dispute is over which judgments are legitimate.

The arrival of generative AI adds a second gate problem inside the institution. Harvard’s guidance requiring instructors to communicate course policies recognizes that students now encounter tools capable of producing text, code, explanation, and analysis on demand. The question shifts from who may enter the university to what kinds of work inside the university still demonstrate learning.

Those two changes belong together because both force institutions to articulate criteria they once could leave partly implicit. Admissions offices must explain what selection means under new legal constraints. Instructors must explain when AI assistance is permitted, prohibited, or pedagogically useful. The 2020s are turning institutional judgment into explicit policy.

That makes Harvard an unfinished final chapter rather than a conclusion. The school is useful not because its choices settle the future of American education, but because its gates make the unsettled questions unusually visible.

The answer is still being graded.
