# Chapter 1 — 1770s: William & Mary — The Republic Needs Lawyers

In December 1779, three years after Americans had declared that governments derived their just powers from the consent of the governed, the College of William & Mary created a new job.

The title was Professor of Law and Police. “Police” still carried an older meaning connected to public administration rather than the modern police department. The man appointed to the chair was George Wythe, a Virginia lawyer, judge, signer of the Declaration of Independence, and longtime teacher of law through apprenticeship. Thomas Jefferson had studied with him. So had other young men who expected law to be learned in the customary way: by reading, observing, copying, arguing, and working under a practicing attorney until one could plausibly join the profession.

William & Mary did something different. It put law inside a college.

The distinction sounds small after two and a half centuries of law schools, business schools, public-policy schools, and professional programs. In 1779 it carried a larger question. The revolution had created institutions faster than it had created people trained to operate them. A republic could denounce hereditary rule, but it still needed legislatures, courts, constitutions, property rules, contracts, public finance, administrative habits, and people who knew how to argue about all of them. Independence did not remove the need for expertise. It changed the political problem of who expertise should serve.

William & Mary is the school of the 1770s because the decade was not only about throwing off authority. It was about discovering how much authority a self-governing society still required.

The new chair of law was part of a broader reorganization of the college encouraged by Jefferson and undertaken while the war for independence was not yet over. William & Mary had been founded under a royal charter in 1693. The revolution therefore presented it with a problem familiar to many colonial institutions: what does a school created under one political order become when that order collapses?

One answer was simply to keep teaching the old subjects under a new flag. Another was to redesign education around the needs of republican government.

The college chose at least part of the second path. Wythe's appointment is widely described by William & Mary as the first collegiate professorship of law in the United States. More important than the first is the reason. The legal curriculum was not conceived merely as technical preparation for private practice. Wythe's students staged mock trials and mock legislative exercises. The point was to train what the college later came to call “citizen lawyers”: people who could move between legal reasoning and public responsibility.

That phrase contains a recurring American hope. Education would not merely produce workers. It would produce citizens capable of governing themselves.

The difficulty was that the new republic's definition of citizen was narrow.

William & Mary in the late eighteenth century was not an institution of democratic access. Its students were male. The Virginia society around it was organized through slavery, property, family status, and racial hierarchy. The men preparing to speak the language of liberty often lived in a political economy built on the denial of liberty to others. Jefferson, whose educational ambitions were immense, enslaved human beings. So did many members of the Virginia elite whose political theories treated independence as a natural right.

A school can teach us something precisely because of such contradictions. William & Mary's law chair did not resolve the gap between republican ideals and the republic's exclusions. It helped build the professional machinery through which those ideals and exclusions would both be administered.

That is a less comforting story than a tale in which legal education simply advances liberty. It is also more useful.

The Revolution produced an extraordinary burst of constitution making. Before Americans could settle the meaning of their national union, states were already writing governing documents, restructuring legislatures, redefining relationships among church, property, officeholding, courts, and executive power. Lawyers became translators between political philosophy and institutional form. Someone had to turn phrases such as consent, rights, sovereignty, and representation into rules that could survive contact with disputes over land, debt, inheritance, taxation, commerce, crime, and power.

The generation associated with the founding is often remembered for writing. The Declaration. The Federalist papers. State constitutions. The Constitution. The Bill of Rights. But the durable achievement of the period depended just as much on interpretation. A written constitution has to be read, argued over, applied to facts, enforced by institutions, and revised through political practice. The nation would soon discover that the text itself did not answer every question.

Lawyers were unusually well positioned to live inside that ambiguity.

Their craft was already built around inherited authorities and new disputes. English common law came with precedents, treatises, procedures, and habits formed under monarchy. Americans could not simply delete the legal past in 1776. Nor could they retain all of it unchanged. Every inherited rule now sat beneath a new political claim: the people, not the Crown, were sovereign.

A law professor in 1779 therefore taught more than doctrine. Whether he intended it in those terms or not, he taught students how to inhabit a transition between regimes.

Wythe's own career made the transition unusually visible. He had practiced law under colonial government and participated in revolutionary politics. He signed the Declaration. He served as a judge. He taught men who would occupy major roles in the new republic, including John Marshall, later chief justice of the United States. Marshall's long tenure on the Supreme Court would help define federal judicial power. It would be too neat to draw a straight causal line from Wythe's classroom to every Marshall opinion. Education rarely works like that. Students carry away fragments, habits, examples, disagreements, and networks rather than a teacher's complete intellectual program.

Still, the classroom mattered because the new country was beginning to institutionalize the preparation of people who would interpret its institutions.

That sentence can sound circular. It is also how states become durable.

Revolutions are dramatic because they destroy or discredit an old legitimacy. Governing is slower. It requires repetition. The legislature meets again. The court opens again. A contract is enforced. A clerk records a deed. A tax is collected. A dispute is appealed. A new officeholder takes an oath. The extraordinary claim of independence has to become ordinary administration.

Schools help make that conversion because they produce continuity without requiring hereditary office.

Under monarchy, continuity can be represented by bloodline. Under republican government, offices are theoretically transferable. One president leaves; another arrives. One judge dies; another takes the bench. One legislator loses an election; another takes the seat. If public authority is not supposed to belong permanently to a family, a class, or a courtly caste, the society needs other ways to reproduce competence.

Education becomes one of them.

That did not make the early American college democratic. It made schooling more politically important. A republic that justified itself through the capacity of citizens had reason to care about how capacity was formed.

This concern appeared throughout the founding era. Americans worried about civic virtue, ignorance, faction, corruption, luxury, and whether republican institutions could survive among people unprepared for self-government. Education was repeatedly offered as part of the answer. Yet there was never agreement about what kind. Classical learning? Religious instruction? Practical science? Local common schools? Elite colleges? Apprenticeship? Military discipline? Legal study?

William & Mary's 1779 reform offers one answer: teach the people most likely to govern how law works.

The limits of that answer are obvious now and were visible even then. Training a governing class can improve government while also consolidating the governing class. Professional expertise can serve the public while creating barriers around who gets to speak with authority. Credentials can replace inherited rank and then become a new form of rank.

American education will spend the next 250 years moving back and forth across that problem.

Open the gate and institutions become more representative, but perhaps harder to distinguish by selectivity. Raise professional standards and quality may improve, but entry becomes more expensive. Make expertise more technical and public decisions may become better informed, but ordinary citizens can feel that their judgment is being displaced. Make education a prerequisite for power and schooling becomes a route to mobility, but people born nearer to good schools inherit an advantage that can look suspiciously like the privilege the merit system was supposed to replace.

The first law professor is therefore not only an origin story for legal education. He is an early figure in the American attempt to reconcile expertise with equality.

That reconciliation was never completed.

There is another reason William & Mary belongs in the 1770s. The school itself had to be converted from an institution of empire into an institution of the republic.

Colonial colleges were not neutral containers that happened to survive independence. Their charters, funding, religious relationships, governance, curricula, and social roles were connected to the old order. When politics changed, schools had to decide what continuity meant. A college could preserve knowledge that predated the revolution while renouncing the sovereignty under which it had first been organized.

This is one of education's quiet powers. Schools can make radical political change feel continuous by keeping people in the same buildings, reading some of the same books, using familiar rituals, while changing the purpose assigned to them.

The 1779 reorganization at William & Mary did not erase the colonial college. It repurposed it.

That act would become a recurring American pattern. Existing institutions would be asked to absorb political transformations larger than themselves. Colleges founded for clergy would become research universities. Normal schools would become state universities. segregated schools would be ordered to integrate. Military campuses would become coeducational. Religious colleges would broaden. Public schools built around neighborhood assignment would be told that neighborhood boundaries could not justify state-enforced racial separation. Universities designed around physical libraries and lecture halls would put materials online. Institutions rarely start over. They accumulate revolutions.

William & Mary accumulated one early.

The temptation is to romanticize the classroom in Williamsburg: the first law professor, future statesmen listening, republican ideals passing from one generation to the next. The documentary record supports some of the institutional importance, but history becomes misleading when it turns instruction into prophecy. John Marshall did not sit in class knowing the future shape of judicial review. The United States did not yet possess the political stability that later textbooks project backward onto the founding. The war continued. The Articles of Confederation would prove inadequate. The Constitution had not been written. The Bill of Rights was years away.

The students were not studying a finished system. They were preparing to work inside one that did not yet exist.

That may be the most modern thing about the scene.

Schools are often accused of preparing students for the past because institutions change more slowly than the world around them. Sometimes the opposite happens. The school is asked to prepare students for a political, technical, or social order still under construction. It cannot know exactly what the future job will require because the job has not stabilized.

Wythe could teach legal reasoning, public law, and habits of argument. He could simulate courts and legislatures. He could expose students to inherited legal authorities. He could not provide a settled manual for constitutional government in the United States because no such manual existed.

The school therefore trained judgment under uncertainty.

This is different from memorizing rules. Rules matter, but founding moments create situations in which the rules themselves are contested. A person educated only to apply settled doctrine is poorly prepared when doctrine must be reconstructed.

The same problem will return in later chapters in different forms. West Point will train officers who become engineers for a country building physical infrastructure at continental scale. Normal schools will train teachers for a mass public system still taking shape. Black colleges will prepare students for citizenship in a nation violently resisting the implications of emancipation. Progressive schools will design curricula for an industrial society whose work and family structures are changing. Berkeley students will argue that a modern university cannot separate learning from political speech. MIT will place course materials on a network whose effect on higher education remains unsettled decades later.

Schools matter most when they are not only transmitting a settled world but negotiating the arrival of a new one.

The new republic also forced another question that would never go away: who pays for the preparation of public capacity?

William & Mary was not yet an example of broad taxpayer-funded higher education. That story develops more clearly in the next two chapters, when Georgia charters a state-supported university and North Carolina turns the idea into an operating campus. But the political logic is already present. If the public needs competent governors, lawyers, engineers, teachers, physicians, and administrators, their education cannot be treated only as a private luxury. The benefits spill beyond the student.

The problem is deciding how far that logic extends.

Should a state support elite professional training because institutions need skilled leaders? Should it provide basic schooling to every child because citizenship itself requires literacy and numeracy? Should higher education be subsidized because research and skilled labor benefit the economy? Should public money follow students to privately operated schools? Should people without children in public schools still pay taxes for them? Should the federal government intervene when state systems deny equal access?

The country will answer all of these questions differently at different moments. The arguments begin early because the underlying tension begins early. Education produces private advantage and public capacity at the same time.

A law degree can increase an individual's status and income. A functioning legal profession can also make contracts more reliable, rights more enforceable, institutions more predictable, and government more competent. The first effect belongs partly to the student. The second belongs to everyone who has to live under the system.

That dual character is why education attracts both public subsidy and intense competition.

People want schools to serve the republic. They also want schools to serve their children.

Those desires overlap until they do not.

William & Mary in 1779 was far from the universal schoolhouse. Its importance lies almost at the opposite end of the system: the preparation of an elite for public life. Yet beginning there clarifies what later educational expansion is expanding toward. The early republic did not discover that education mattered when common schools spread in the nineteenth century. Education already mattered because power required preparation. The democratic struggle was over whether that preparation would remain concentrated.

The answer would change slowly, unevenly, and never completely.

There is a final reason to choose this school over a more obvious symbol of revolutionary education. The 1770s are usually remembered as a decade in which ideas broke institutions apart. William & Mary's law chair shows ideas building an institution instead.

A declaration can say that people have rights. A college has to decide what someone should study on Tuesday morning.

That movement from principle to routine is where republics either become durable or remain slogans.

In 1779, William & Mary placed law on the timetable.

The war was unfinished. The Constitution was unwritten. The shape of federal power was unresolved. The boundaries of citizenship were unjustly narrow. The country's most celebrated claims contradicted much of its social order.

And still, someone had to learn how to govern the thing that was being made.

The 1779 statute is useful because it makes the institutional change less mystical. William & Mary did not merely happen to have an eminent lawyer teaching nearby. Its governing board created an office, assigned law a place in the college’s structure, and attached public preparation to an institution that could outlast any one tutor. That distinction matters. Apprenticeship transmits a craft through a personal relationship. A professorship turns the craft into a reproducible institutional function. The republic would need both, but the second form scales differently. It can be budgeted, inherited by successors, placed alongside other fields, and made part of a curriculum whose purpose is debated by a governing body.

That is one reason the founding-era story should not be reduced to a parade of famous students. The durable innovation was not that Wythe knew Jefferson or Marshall. It was that legal preparation could become part of a college’s standing machinery. Once a society places professional formation inside institutions, it also creates new questions about curriculum, standards, access, and authority. Who decides what a lawyer should know? How much of that education should be about private practice and how much about public duty? Does institutional training broaden entry by making instruction more systematic, or narrow it by making credentials more important? Those questions are already latent in the move from the lawyer’s office to the college classroom.

The title “Law and Police” also reminds us that eighteenth-century categories do not map cleanly onto modern departments. Public administration, political economy, legal doctrine, and civic responsibility overlapped more visibly than they often do in a contemporary professional school. The new chair therefore belongs to a moment before legal education had settled into the specialized form later generations would recognize. It was professionalization in motion, not a finished profession reproducing itself.

That makes William & Mary a revealing first chapter for a history told through schools. A political revolution can happen faster than institutional competence. The Declaration could be printed in days; a culture of legal judgment had to be taught across years and then renewed across generations. The republic’s promise depended on offices that changed hands without collapsing. Education offered one way to make that handoff possible.

But institutional continuity has a moral edge. The same machinery that can preserve constitutional practice can preserve exclusion. A college can reproduce public competence and reproduce a governing class at the same time. The 1779 reform therefore deserves neither a simple celebratory reading nor a cynical dismissal. It shows the central American bargain in miniature: use education to make authority less hereditary, then struggle over whether the educational route to authority is itself genuinely open.

America's first school of the decade is therefore not a school because it taught the revolution. It is the school because it confronted the morning after.
