# Chapter 10 — 1860s: Howard University — Reconstruction Builds a University

On March 2, 1867, the United States government chartered Howard University in Washington, D.C.

The date matters because the Civil War had ended less than two years earlier.

Slavery had been abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment. The Fourteenth Amendment was moving through ratification. The country was trying to decide whether emancipation meant only the end of legal bondage or the creation of institutions through which formerly enslaved people could exercise freedom.

A university was one answer.

Howard is the school of the 1860s because Reconstruction was not only about rewriting constitutions. It was about building the human institutions that freedom required.

The familiar political chronology is monumental. Emancipation Proclamation. Appomattox. Thirteenth Amendment. Civil Rights Act of 1866. Fourteenth Amendment. Reconstruction Acts. Fifteenth Amendment soon after. Each transformed the legal position of millions of Americans.

But a person does not become free in daily life merely because a constitutional amendment has changed the law.

Freedom has administrative requirements.

A person needs the ability to read a labor contract, understand a ballot, correspond with family, keep accounts, study law, enter a profession, teach children, challenge fraud, interpret a statute, and organize institutions. Literacy does not guarantee freedom. Its denial makes freedom easier to steal.

Formerly enslaved people understood this with extraordinary clarity.

Across the South, Black communities pursued education with urgency. They organized schools, hired teachers, raised money, used churches as classrooms, demanded public provision, and sent children and adults to learn. The story cannot be told as benevolent outsiders discovering that freedpeople needed schooling. Freedpeople were among the strongest agents of the educational revolution.

Howard belongs in that larger landscape.

The university was established with connections to the Freedmen's Bureau and named for General Oliver Otis Howard, who led the bureau. Religious organizations and white reformers participated in its creation. Yet the institution's historical significance would come from the Black students, faculty, professionals, scholars, and communities who turned it into far more than a federal Reconstruction project.

Founders can start a school.

Users decide what the school becomes.

Howard opened educational routes in law, medicine, theology, liberal arts, and other fields at a moment when most established institutions sharply restricted Black access.

That mattered because emancipation created a professional problem as well as a literacy problem.

A free society needs Black teachers if Black children are to be educated at scale in a segregated or hostile system. It needs Black doctors when hospitals exclude patients and medical schools exclude students. It needs Black lawyers when newly recognized rights are contested in courts. It needs ministers, editors, businesspeople, civil servants, and political leaders.

The professions are where rights become practice.

A constitutional amendment can say equal protection. A lawyer has to bring the case.

A law can create a school system. Teachers have to staff it.

A public-health need can be obvious. Physicians and nurses have to serve the community.

Howard was therefore not simply a place where Black students could receive higher education. It was part of an institutional ecosystem for Black self-government within a country that was already organizing resistance to Black citizenship.

The resistance came quickly.

Reconstruction is sometimes described as a brief period when the federal government tried racial equality before the nation lost interest. That summary understates the force used to destroy the project. White supremacist violence, political terror, labor coercion, legal restriction, and organized resistance targeted Black political and economic gains. The collapse of Reconstruction governments was not a peaceful policy correction.

Education sat directly in the conflict.

Schools represented taxes, public authority, literacy, and the possibility that the children of formerly enslaved people would enter a different social order than their parents had been forced to inhabit.

That possibility made education a target and a priority at the same time.

Howard's location in Washington gave the university unusual proximity to federal power. Students and faculty could live near the institutions responsible for writing and interpreting national policy. That proximity would later make Howard a center of legal strategy and public leadership, most famously through the work of figures connected to the campaign against segregation.

Those later achievements should not be loaded back into 1867 as destiny.

The young university did not open knowing it would educate Thurgood Marshall or become associated with Charles Hamilton Houston's legal strategy against Jim Crow. The institution had to survive before it could accumulate that history.

Still, its founding purpose made such futures possible.

A school creates optionality.

That word is more common in finance and technology than in education, but it fits. Education expands the number of future roles a person can plausibly enter. A university expands the number of collective roles a community can fill from within.

This is especially important after a system of oppression has deliberately restricted education.

Slavery did not merely steal labor. It restricted movement, family autonomy, property, political rights, and in many places literacy. The resulting inequality after emancipation could then be misread as evidence of incapacity rather than the predictable effect of enforced deprivation.

Education interrupts that fraud.

It gives people opportunities to develop and demonstrate capabilities that the old system denied them the chance to acquire.

This is why post-emancipation education cannot be understood as charity.

It was part of restitution, state building, and freedom.

The distinction matters politically. Charity depends on the giver's continued goodwill. A right or institution can make a stronger claim.

Howard's federal charter placed Black higher education closer to national public responsibility than private benevolence alone could provide.

The federal connection also introduces ambiguity.

The same federal government that supported institutions for freedpeople had enormous power to define what education should accomplish. Government support can enable opportunity and impose priorities. Freedmen's Bureau schools, missionary schools, and historically Black institutions operated within complex relationships among Black communities, northern philanthropists, churches, federal officials, and local politics.

Control was never a trivial matter.

Who chooses the curriculum?

Who appoints teachers?

Who decides whether education should emphasize classical study, industrial training, religion, citizenship, or immediate economic skills?

These questions become especially contentious when the funder and student come from different social worlds.

Education offered to an oppressed group can become paternalistic when outsiders define the group's needs.

Howard's development would push against that tendency as Black scholars and professionals increasingly shaped the institution's intellectual life.

The university became important not merely because Black students entered, but because Black authority could accumulate.

This is a different form of integration from Oberlin.

Oberlin challenged exclusion by admitting Black students into a shared institution. Howard created a historically Black institution in which Black intellectual and professional life could become central rather than exceptional.

Both models widen opportunity. They do different social work.

An integrated institution can break racial boundaries by creating common membership. A historically Black institution can protect space for leadership, community, scholarship, and networks that majority institutions may marginalize.

American education often treats these models as if one must prove the other obsolete.

History suggests otherwise.

There are situations in which shared institutions are essential to equal citizenship and situations in which minority-serving institutions provide forms of belonging and leadership unavailable elsewhere.

The question is whether separation is chosen or imposed, whether resources are equitable, and whether institutions expand or restrict opportunity.

Howard existed in a country moving toward imposed segregation.

Its strength would therefore become partly defensive.

When white institutions excluded Black students, historically Black colleges and universities created professional pipelines the country desperately needed and often refused to provide elsewhere.

That is an achievement born from injustice.

The institution should be celebrated without romanticizing the exclusion that made it necessary.

The same paradox appears elsewhere in marginalized communities. Specialized schools can create extraordinary culture because the mainstream failed to include the people who built them. Success inside the alternative institution can then be used perversely to argue that exclusion caused no harm.

It did.

A person should not have to build a parallel world simply to obtain rights promised in the common one.

But parallel institutions can become sources of power in their own right.

Howard did.

The university's professional schools are central to the story because professions translate education into public authority.

Consider law.

After the Civil War, constitutional amendments transformed the formal rights landscape. Their enforcement depended on courts, legislatures, administrative action, political organization, and lawyers capable of interpreting the new constitutional order.

A Black lawyer could do more than enter a respected occupation. The lawyer could challenge discriminatory laws, defend clients, draft documents, advise organizations, and participate in the development of civil-rights doctrine.

The profession creates leverage.

Medicine offered another form. A Black physician could serve patients denied equal treatment and participate in public health and scientific institutions. A teacher could multiply educational access. A minister could combine spiritual leadership with political organization. A journalist could create a public record outside white-controlled newspapers.

Higher education therefore changes the institutional density of a community.

A community with its own trained professionals is harder to govern entirely from outside.

This is one reason education and self-determination are so closely linked.

The point should not be overstated into a bootstrap myth.

No amount of schooling can by itself overcome law, violence, discriminatory credit markets, employment exclusion, segregated housing, stolen property, or political disenfranchisement. Education is frequently asked to solve inequalities produced elsewhere because schooling is more politically comfortable than redistributing power.

A society can tell an excluded person to study harder instead of removing the rule that excludes them.

That is the meritocratic escape hatch.

Howard's history should not be used to say that individual achievement proves structural discrimination can be overcome through effort.

The opposite lesson is stronger.

Black achievement occurred despite structures deliberately designed to restrict it, and institutions such as Howard helped create collective capacity to challenge those structures.

Education mattered because politics mattered too.

Reconstruction was a brief period in which federal power was used more aggressively to protect Black citizenship in the South. Howard's 1867 charter belongs inside that moment of possibility.

The possibility would narrow after federal withdrawal and the rise of Jim Crow.

That retreat demonstrates another theme of this book: schools can outlive political coalitions.

An institution created during a reform era can preserve capabilities after the reforming government loses power.

This is one of the reasons durable institutions matter more than temporary programs.

A program ends with an appropriation. A university, if it survives, can continue teaching under a different administration, through different political periods, and across generations.

It stores reform.

The phrase is not perfect because institutions also store prejudice, hierarchy, and outdated practice. Longevity preserves whatever has been built, good and bad.

Howard stored a commitment to Black higher education through decades in which national policy retreated from Reconstruction's egalitarian promise.

That continuity mattered enormously.

By the twentieth century, Howard would become associated with scholars, artists, physicians, lawyers, and public officials whose influence extended far beyond the campus. The law school in particular became a center for legal strategy against segregation. Charles Hamilton Houston, dean of the law school, helped train a generation of civil-rights lawyers, including Thurgood Marshall.

The Brown v. Board of Education decision of 1954 is often remembered as a Supreme Court event.

It was also an educational event before it reached the Court.

Lawyers had to be trained. Cases had to be selected. Facts had to be developed. Arguments had to be refined. Institutions had to support people through years of work whose outcome was uncertain.

The legal victory was downstream from schools.

That may be the most appropriate long echo of the 1867 founding.

A university created during Reconstruction later helped provide intellectual infrastructure for a second Reconstruction in the civil-rights era.

This does not make history cyclical in a neat way. It shows institutional memory carrying unfinished work.

Howard also complicates the phrase “elite education.”

Elite can describe selectivity, social status, resources, or the preparation of people likely to enter powerful roles. Historically Black institutions were sometimes elite in intellectual and professional function while operating with far fewer resources than prestigious white universities.

Resource inequality matters because excellence is expensive.

Libraries, laboratories, faculty salaries, hospitals, scholarships, facilities, and research all depend on capital. American higher education has often judged institutional performance without accounting adequately for accumulated wealth.

The endowment is history earning interest.

Universities founded early with wealthy constituencies can compound financial advantage for centuries. Institutions serving populations denied wealth accumulation begin from a different base.

This makes comparisons of institutional resources partly comparisons of historical opportunity.

Howard's endurance amid that inequality is therefore an institutional achievement, but endurance should not become an excuse for underfunding.

The school also belongs to Washington because proximity to national power creates distinctive educational possibilities.

Students can observe politics not as textbook abstraction but as an operating system around them. Federal agencies, courts, Congress, advocacy organizations, media, embassies, and professional networks create opportunities unavailable in more isolated places.

Location is curriculum.

West Point used geography for military training. Black Mountain College would use relative isolation to create an experimental artistic community. Los Alamos would be chosen partly because isolation served secrecy. Howard's location placed Black higher education near the center of a government whose relationship to Black citizenship was continually contested.

That geography gave the institution symbolic force.

A historically Black university in the nation's capital is a statement even before anyone speaks.

The statement in 1867 was that emancipation required intellectual institutions capable of producing free citizens and professionals.

The country did not fulfill that promise uniformly.

Howard helped preserve it.

This is why the school of the 1860s is not simply one of many colleges founded after the Civil War.

The decade remade the Constitution. Howard helped build the people who would live inside the remade Constitution, defend it, criticize its failures, and demand that its language become real.

A legal revolution without educational infrastructure is fragile.

Rights written on paper need readers.

Courts need lawyers.

Schools need teachers.

Communities need institutions they can lead.

In 1867, Reconstruction built a university.

The nation would retreat from Reconstruction.

Howard’s 1867 incorporation makes sense only inside the institutional emergency of Reconstruction. Emancipation changed the legal status of millions of people, but freedom could not be sustained by a legal proclamation alone. Families sought schools. Communities organized teachers and classrooms. The Freedmen’s Bureau became involved in educational work alongside a much larger network of Black churches, mutual-aid efforts, northern aid societies, local organizers, and students themselves. Howard belongs to that landscape as an attempt to build advanced institutional capacity where slavery had deliberately restricted it.

The federal charter is important because it gives the founding a legal form, but the surrounding educational movement prevents the charter from becoming a top-down creation story. Black education after the Civil War was not a gift bestowed on passive recipients. Formerly enslaved people had already demonstrated extraordinary demand for literacy and schooling, often building institutions under dangerous conditions. A university founded in Washington existed because the political possibilities of Reconstruction intersected with that demand.

Higher education mattered because freedom required more than basic literacy. A society emerging from slavery needed teachers, ministers, physicians, lawyers, administrators, writers, scholars, and professionals capable of building institutions within Black communities and contesting exclusion in the larger nation. Howard’s importance therefore lies partly in vertical range. It stood for the proposition that Black educational aspiration did not stop at the elementary school door.

The location in the national capital amplified that proposition. Washington was both a local city and the seat of federal power. A university there placed Black higher education close to Congress, federal agencies, courts, and national political networks. That proximity did not guarantee protection or equality, but it made the institution part of the infrastructure through which Reconstruction-era citizenship could be argued and practiced.

Howard also demonstrates why education and civil rights repeatedly become inseparable. If a group is newly recognized as possessing legal rights but is denied access to the institutions that prepare people for influential professions, formal equality can coexist with durable inequality. Schools therefore become mediating institutions between rights on paper and capacity in practice. They help determine who can actually enter occupations that interpret law, provide medical care, teach children, administer organizations, and shape public debate.

That does not mean professional education alone can overcome structural discrimination. Credentials can open doors only where employers, courts, governments, and markets allow them to matter. Black graduates would continue to confront violence, segregation, disenfranchisement, and exclusion. Yet that is precisely why an institution such as Howard could become more than a collection of classrooms. It could sustain networks, archives, professions, and traditions of argument that survive hostile political cycles.

The founding moment therefore belongs to Reconstruction’s broader contest over whether emancipation would be institutionalized. One side of that struggle sought to make freedom durable through schools, voting, law, land, family autonomy, and public institutions. Another sought to restore racial domination through violence, restrictive law, and political reversal. A university could not decide that conflict by itself. It could, however, make one vision of the postwar nation concrete.

Howard’s charter is thus less interesting as a ceremonial birthday than as an act of state building under democratic pressure. It shows the federal government participating in the creation of an institution whose students and graduates would later demand that the government honor promises it repeatedly failed to keep. The school was created inside Reconstruction, but its historical importance comes from outliving Reconstruction’s collapse and carrying forward claims the era left unfinished.

The university form also gave the project a particular kind of durability. Temporary relief programs can disappear when appropriations change or political coalitions collapse. A university seeks permanence: trustees, faculties, departments, buildings, students, degrees, alumni, and eventually an endowment and institutional memory. That permanence can make a political opening harder to erase completely. Reconstruction would be violently narrowed, but an institution created during the opening could continue to educate people under later, less favorable regimes.

This is one reason universities can become repositories of unfinished political projects. Their founding coalitions may vanish, yet the institution continues to carry assumptions about who deserves advanced study and what kinds of public leadership should be possible. Howard’s existence after Reconstruction became evidence that the period had created more than temporary legislation. It had built organizations capable of remembering and renewing aspirations the nation itself repeatedly abandoned.

The university would remain.
