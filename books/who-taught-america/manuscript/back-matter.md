# Back Matter — The Schools I Left Out

A book that chooses one school for every decade should end by admitting the obvious.

There is another list.

Actually, there are thousands of them.

Change the definition of *important* and the entire book rearranges itself.

If importance means the school that educated the most consequential political leaders, Harvard appears repeatedly. If it means the largest influence on mass public schooling, elementary schools and normal schools occupy more chapters. If it means scientific impact, research universities and technical institutes dominate the twentieth century. If it means social mobility, community colleges deserve much more space. If it means the history of exclusion, different schools become unavoidable. If it means sheer number of American lives touched, the book should probably contain fewer famous institutions and more ordinary public high schools.

That is why I have resisted pretending this list is a measurement.

The selections are arguments.

Each chapter asks which single institution best opens a decade onto the larger American story. The winning school is the one that made me understand something about the country I could not see as clearly before.

A strong rival sometimes lost because it told a story another chapter already covered. Sometimes a smaller institution produced a sharper national aperture than a more influential one. Sometimes the losing school was historically “better” and the winning school exposed a contradiction the book needed.

Below is the shadow list: one plausible alternate school for every decade and the reason it nearly took the chapter.

## 1770s — Transylvania's predecessor schools and the frontier academies

William & Mary won because the 1779 law professorship makes the shift from revolution to governance unusually visible. A frontier academy would tell another important story: education moving inland with settlement before the new nation had stable public systems. The alternative book might begin not with the governing class but with geography.

## 1780s — Franklin College and other post-Revolution academies

The University of Georgia won because its 1785 charter embodies public higher education as a state promise before the institution could operate. A school that actually taught students during the decade would avoid the oddity of selecting a university that existed largely on paper. I kept the oddity because the gap between legal promise and institutional reality becomes one of the book's recurring themes.

## 1790s — Phillips Academy or another elite academy

The University of North Carolina won because opening a public university made the state commitment real. An elite academy could have shown the private preparation pipeline that fed early American colleges and leadership. That story remains important because public higher education did not erase private advantage; it grew beside it.

## 1800s — Jefferson's educational experiments in Virginia

West Point won because federal technical training links education directly to state capacity. A Jefferson-centered school story could have emphasized the founding generation's obsession with republican education and the long road toward the University of Virginia. West Point offered the stronger institutional surprise: the young republic needed engineers badly enough to educate them itself.

## 1810s — Female academies and emerging schools for girls

The American School for the Deaf won because it changes the question from who deserves more schooling to how a school recognizes intelligence when its ordinary interface fails. A female academy would have accelerated the gender story that arrives in the next decade. I preferred to give disability, language, and institutional accessibility a chapter of their own.

## 1820s — Rensselaer School

The institution that became Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute was founded in 1824 and offered a compelling alternative story about practical science, engineering, and laboratory-oriented technical education. Troy Female Seminary won because the curriculum crossing a gender boundary exposes the political meaning of what subjects a school believes a person is capable of studying.

## 1830s — McGuffey's classroom America

A chapter centered on the McGuffey Readers could have represented the growth of common educational culture more broadly than Oberlin. The problem is that the book asks for a school, not a textbook, and Oberlin makes admission itself into a national argument about race and sex. The McGuffey story remains one of the great examples of curriculum becoming cultural infrastructure.

## 1840s — Horace Mann's common schools

Any history of American education that reaches the 1840s can reasonably give the decade to Horace Mann and the Massachusetts common-school movement. Bridgewater Normal School won because it lets the chapter ask a less familiar question: once society promises mass education, who trains the adults who must deliver it? The third normal school tells the story of an experiment becoming a profession.

## 1850s — Antioch College

Antioch, with Horace Mann as its first president and an early commitment to coeducation, offered a powerful chapter about broad liberal learning and reform. Berea won because interracial education in slaveholding Kentucky places the classroom directly against the country's central moral and political contradiction on the eve of civil war.

## 1860s — Fisk University

Fisk is an exceptionally strong rival. Founded in 1866, it embodies Black higher education during Reconstruction and later produced the Jubilee Singers, whose tours carried the institution's story internationally. Howard won because its federal charter, professional schools, Washington location, and later role in civil-rights legal strategy allow one institution to connect Reconstruction to the long institutional work of making rights enforceable. A different author could choose Fisk and write an equally serious chapter.

## 1870s — Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute

Hampton belongs near the center of any account of postwar Black and Native education, industrial training, philanthropy, and the politics of assimilation. Carlisle won because importance in this book includes harm. The federal boarding-school system requires a chapter in which education itself becomes the instrument of coercion rather than the automatic solution to injustice.

## 1880s — Spelman Seminary

Spelman, founded in 1881, could have carried a remarkable story about Black women's education, religion, philanthropy, professional formation, and institution building. Tuskegee won because the argument over “useful education” lets the chapter examine work, liberal learning, race, philanthropy, and political strategy all at once. Spelman is one of the omissions I feel most strongly.

## 1890s — Johns Hopkins University

Johns Hopkins has the stronger claim to be the pioneering American research university. It opened in 1876 and deeply influenced graduate education. The University of Chicago won the 1890s because it shows the model arriving with extraordinary capital, speed, urban ambition, and institutional scale. Hopkins invented more of the grammar; Chicago demonstrates how fast America learned to speak it.

## 1900s — The University of Wisconsin

The “Wisconsin Idea,” in which university expertise was connected explicitly to public service and state policy, could easily carry the decade. Emerson School in Gary won because the industrial city redesigned the school day itself. Gary makes time, space, facilities, and system efficiency visible as educational choices rather than natural facts.

## 1910s — Montessori schools in the United States

The spread of Montessori education offered a compelling story about child development, classroom environment, and an international educational idea entering American practice. The Lincoln School won because the laboratory-school model makes curriculum revision itself the institution's purpose and exposes both the promise and danger of educational experimentation.

## 1920s — Hampton Institute or a great urban high school

Rhea County Central High School won for a reason that has almost nothing to do with the school's educational reputation. One teacher's classroom became the legal hook for a national argument over science, religion, democracy, and curricular authority. A more representative school would tell us more about what students actually experienced in the 1920s. Rhea County tells us more about who Americans thought should control the experience.

## 1930s — Highlander Folk School

Highlander, founded in Tennessee in 1932, may be the shadow-list school I was closest to choosing. It became an extraordinary site of adult education, labor organizing, civil-rights leadership, and movement learning. Black Mountain College won because its influence on art and interdisciplinary experimentation shows how a tiny institution can matter far beyond enrollment. Highlander would produce a more explicitly political chapter and would fit beautifully beside Berkeley and Parkland.

## 1940s — Tuskegee Institute and the Tuskegee Airmen training ecosystem

The wartime story of Black military aviation, technical training, segregation, and citizenship would make an extraordinary chapter. Los Alamos Ranch School won because the disappearance of one school into the Manhattan Project makes the entire American research-and-education system visible as an instrument of national power. It is difficult to find another campus whose purpose changed so completely in so little time.

## 1950s — Brown's Monroe Elementary School in Topeka

Monroe Elementary, associated with the Brown case, has the cleanest claim to represent the constitutional turning point in school segregation. Little Rock Central won because a judicial principle becomes real only when somebody has to enforce it and students have to live inside it. Brown wrote the rule. Little Rock tested whether the rule could survive a school morning.

## 1960s — Tougaloo College

Tougaloo offered space and institutional support for civil-rights organizing under extraordinary pressure in Mississippi. It would produce a chapter connecting higher education to Black freedom struggle without making Berkeley the default national symbol of student politics. Berkeley won because the Free Speech Movement changed the public image of the American campus and helped establish a political repertoire that later student movements would use.

## 1970s — University of California, Davis medical school admissions

The Bakke litigation arising from UC Davis's medical-school admissions program produced the Supreme Court decision that became foundational to later affirmative-action doctrine. It would make a powerful bridge from desegregation to the Harvard chapter. South Boston High won because the school bus reveals something broader: formal desegregation law colliding with a metropolitan geography shaped by housing, class, race, and local control.

## 1980s — A Nation at Risk's ordinary comprehensive high school

The most representative institution of the decade may be no famous school at all. It may be the large comprehensive public high school responding to new graduation requirements, standardized tests, economic anxiety, and the rhetoric of national decline. Central Park East Secondary School won precisely because it was a counterexample. It refused to accept the era's panic as the only route to intellectual seriousness.

## 1990s — Edison Schools

For-profit school management and the broader movement toward market mechanisms in public education could have supplied a sharper story about privatization and business reform. City Academy won because the first charter school captures the moment before the political category hardened. The small St. Paul school makes it possible to see the legal innovation separately from everything the charter sector later became.

## 2000s — University of Phoenix

A book emphasizing scale, adult students, online learning, debt, and the commercialization of higher education might give this decade to the University of Phoenix. MIT won because OpenCourseWare reveals a more fundamental transformation: instructional content becoming abundant while institutional membership and credentials remain scarce. The contrast between the two institutions could sustain a book by itself.

## 2010s — Sandy Hook Elementary School

Sandy Hook is the most painful omission. The 2012 killing of twenty children and six adult staff members profoundly altered school safety, public grief, and the national gun debate. I chose Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School because the students' rapid transformation into a national youth movement changes the role of the student in the story. That choice should never be read as a claim that one tragedy mattered more than another.

## 2020s — A public school on Zoom

The most representative school of the early 2020s may be no single institution. It may be the ordinary American school that closed its building during the COVID-19 pandemic and tried to recreate itself through laptops, video calls, learning-management systems, meal pickup, improvised childcare, and exhausted families. Harvard won because the decade's arguments about admissions and generative AI place two kinds of evidence under pressure at once: what counts as fair evidence for entry and what counts as trustworthy evidence of learning.

If this book is revised after 2029, I may choose differently.

The decade has not finished arguing.

## What the shadow list changes

The alternate schools reveal several biases in the main list.

Universities remain overrepresented because universities preserve archives, generate scholarship about themselves, and sit near formal power. Their histories are easier to research than the histories of ordinary schools whose records may be scattered across districts, local libraries, yearbooks, newspapers, and private collections.

Famous institutions also create narrative gravity. When a school is already associated with a national event, the chapter can open quickly into the country's history. This is useful for storytelling and dangerous for representativeness.

Most Americans did not attend Harvard, Berkeley, Howard, Tuskegee, MIT, or Black Mountain College.

Most experienced schools more ordinary than any institution in this book.

That does not make the famous schools irrelevant. It makes them windows rather than samples.

A window shows something clearly precisely because it frames out almost everything else.

The book also favors moments when schools became sites of visible conflict. Carlisle, Rhea County, Little Rock, South Boston, Berkeley, Parkland, and Harvard all generated public controversy. Quiet educational success receives less attention because it leaves fewer dramatic records.

This may distort the history toward crisis.

Most education is not crisis.

Most education is repetition.

A teacher explains a difficult idea again.

A child reads a page more fluently than last month.

Someone learns to divide fractions, identify a plant, argue from evidence, speak a new language, repair an engine, write a sentence, conduct an experiment, perform a song, or work beside a person they would not otherwise know.

The ordinary accumulation is the institution's real power.

History notices the days when the schoolhouse door is blocked.

A life may be changed on a day when nothing newsworthy happens at all.

## The missing school

There is one institution I could not put on the list because it has no single address.

The American family is the country's oldest school.

Before a child enters a classroom, someone has already taught language, trust, fear, manners, stories, songs, rules, expectations, and a theory of what the world is like. Families differ enormously in time, money, education, stability, language, books, health, housing, and social networks. Schools inherit those differences and then get blamed for not erasing them quickly enough.

The blame is often unfair.

So is the opposite conclusion that schools cannot change much because family background matters.

A good school can alter a life precisely because initial conditions are unequal.

It can give a child an adult who notices something nobody else noticed.

It can supply a language the household does not speak.

It can offer a laboratory, orchestra, workshop, library, team, club, or friend unavailable at home.

It can create distance from a family's limitations without requiring rejection of the family.

It can also injure a child, underestimate a child, track a child into a narrow future, punish a language, or reproduce the prejudices outside the building.

The school receives a person already being formed and participates in the formation for a while.

It never owns the outcome.

That is a useful humility after 250 years of educational ambition.

Americans have repeatedly asked schools to save the republic, assimilate immigrants, eliminate poverty, produce workers, integrate society, win wars, create scientists, prevent crime, improve health, prepare citizens, defeat foreign rivals, close achievement gaps, adapt to technology, and now teach young people how to think in a world where machines can produce language on demand.

Schools matter enough that some of these requests are reasonable.

They do not matter enough to satisfy all of them.

Education is one institution among families, neighborhoods, markets, governments, religions, media systems, workplaces, and technologies. It can amplify or counter forces created elsewhere. It cannot substitute for the whole society.

The persistent American mistake is to treat education as both miracle and scapegoat.

When the country wants equality without redistributing anything else, it says schools will create opportunity.

When inequality remains, it blames schools for failing.

When the country wants economic growth, it tells schools to produce skills.

When the economy changes faster than curricula, it blames schools for being outdated.

When political culture becomes poisonous, it asks civics teachers to repair democracy.

When children encounter technologies adults barely understand, it asks schools to teach responsible use before adults have established the norms.

The demands are testimony to the institution's importance.

They are also a way of avoiding responsibility elsewhere.

The history in this book suggests a different standard.

Ask the school to do what only a school can do well.

Concentrate attention.

Create relationships among people who would not otherwise meet.

Preserve knowledge long enough for a new generation to encounter it.

Give learners structured chances to practice difficult things.

Expose claims to evidence.

Certify capability cautiously.

Create room for curiosity that the market does not immediately reward.

Give children and adults access to futures their starting circumstances did not make obvious.

Do those things without making one culture, class, race, body, language, ideology, or technology the definition of human worth.

That is already difficult enough.

## The final argument

If I had to compress the 250 years into one observation, it would not be that American education steadily improved.

The record is not that clean.

It would be that the definition of the student kept expanding.

The early republic's college student was a narrow social figure.

Then institutions appeared for people the standard system had excluded or underestimated.

Women entered more rigorous curricula.

Black students entered and built colleges.

Deaf students entered institutions organized around their ability to learn.

Public systems expanded. Teachers professionalized. High schools became mass institutions. Disabled students eventually acquired stronger legal rights. Adult students, working students, online students, and people outside formal admission gained new routes to knowledge.

Every expansion created a new argument about standards, cost, authority, or belonging.

That is not evidence that expansion failed.

It is what happens when institutions admit people whose needs and experiences expose assumptions built around the previous population.

The school changes when the student changes.

America has been changing the student for 250 years.

The next expansion may be stranger.

A learner can now enter a conversation with an artificial system that has no classroom, no office hours, no childhood, no citizenship, and no stake in whether the learner actually understands.

The system can explain, imitate, generate, and respond.

It can make some forms of instructional help abundant.

It cannot decide what kind of person America wants an education to help create.

That remains a human argument.

It belongs to families, teachers, students, institutions, communities, and citizens.

The most important school of the next decade may not be the school with the best technology.

It may be the institution that remembers why the technology is there.

That is the one school I would like to put on the next list.
