# Research brief — Who Taught America?

## Core question

For every named calendar decade touched by the United States between 1776 and 2026, which single school best reveals what America was trying to teach, whom it was willing to teach, and what it believed education was for?

The book is not a ranking of academic quality. “Most important” means most useful for explaining a consequential national change or conflict. A school can qualify because it created a model, concentrated a conflict, trained a class of people the nation newly needed, widened access, narrowed access, became a symbol, or exposed a contradiction between American ideals and practice.

## Selection tests

Each finalist is tested against five questions:

1. **National aperture.** Does the school open onto a national question rather than remain a local anecdote?
2. **Consequence.** Did the institution, its model, or the conflict around it matter beyond the campus?
3. **Narrative density.** Is there a real story with people, decisions, pressures, reversals, and consequences?
4. **Counter-canonical value.** Does the choice add something that a standard presidents-and-elite-universities history would miss?
5. **Falsifiability.** Can the central claims be checked against primary sources, institutional archives, court records, public agencies, serious scholarship, and contemporaneous reporting?

## Working selections

| Decade | School | Why this one |
|---|---|---|
| 1770s | College of William & Mary | The 1779 law chair made legal education part of building a republican governing class. |
| 1780s | University of Georgia | Its 1785 charter placed public higher education inside the new state’s constitutional imagination before the campus was ready. |
| 1790s | University of North Carolina | It opened in 1795 and made the state university an operating institution rather than only a charter. |
| 1800s | United States Military Academy at West Point | Founded in 1802, it linked officer training, engineering, federal capacity, and continental infrastructure. |
| 1810s | American School for the Deaf | Opened in 1817 and became foundational to American deaf education and public support for special education. |
| 1820s | Troy Female Seminary | Emma Willard’s 1821 school made an expansive academic curriculum for young women into an institution rather than a petition. |
| 1830s | Oberlin Collegiate Institute | Its policies on Black enrollment and coeducation made access itself part of the college’s public meaning. |
| 1840s | Bridgewater Normal School | Teacher preparation became a public institution and a replicable model for professionalizing mass education. |
| 1850s | Berea College | An interracial, coeducational school founded in slaveholding Kentucky tested whether abolitionist equality could be institutionalized. |
| 1860s | Howard University | Founded during Reconstruction, it tied federal power, Black higher education, professional training, and the unfinished meaning of emancipation. |
| 1870s | Carlisle Indian Industrial School | Its 1879 opening helped institutionalize a federal assimilation policy whose violence belongs inside any serious history of American schooling. |
| 1880s | Tuskegee Normal and Industrial Institute | Founded in 1881, it made the relationship among Black education, labor, self-help, philanthropy, accommodation, and political power a national argument. |
| 1890s | University of Chicago | Incorporated in 1890 and opened in 1892, it accelerated the American research-university model and the professionalization of knowledge. |
| 1900s | Emerson School, Gary, Indiana | Opened in 1909 as a showcase for William Wirt’s work-study-play system and the effort to redesign public schooling for the industrial city. |
| 1910s | Lincoln School of Teachers College | Founded in 1917 as a laboratory school, it embodied progressive faith in curriculum design, experimentation, and the school as a research site. |
| 1920s | Rhea County Central High School | John Scopes’s teaching became the factual hook for the 1925 trial that turned curriculum, evolution, religion, and local authority into national theater. |
| 1930s | Black Mountain College | Founded in 1933, it joined progressive education, European exile, modern art, and small-institution experimentation with an influence far beyond its size. |
| 1940s | Los Alamos Ranch School | The federal government acquired and closed the school so the site could become the Manhattan Project’s weapons laboratory. The school literally disappeared into the atomic state. |
| 1950s | Little Rock Central High School | The 1957 desegregation crisis tested whether federal constitutional authority would be enforced at an actual schoolhouse door. |
| 1960s | University of California, Berkeley | The 1964 Free Speech Movement made the campus a national model for student political power and the conflicts around university governance and speech. |
| 1970s | South Boston High School | Court-ordered desegregation and busing made one urban high school a symbol of the collision among race, neighborhood identity, local control, and federal courts. |
| 1980s | Central Park East Secondary School | Founded in 1985, it offered a small, teacher-driven, portfolio-based answer to the era’s standards-and-accountability panic. |
| 1990s | City Academy, St. Paul | Opened in 1992 as the first charter school in the United States, turning an alternative program for at-risk students into a new legal form for public schooling. |
| 2000s | Massachusetts Institute of Technology | MIT’s 2001 OpenCourseWare decision separated access to course materials from admission, tuition, place, and credential. |
| 2010s | Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School | The 2018 shooting and the student-led movement that followed made a school community a national political actor. |
| 2020s | Harvard University | The 2023 Supreme Court decision on Harvard’s admissions system marked a constitutional break in race-conscious admissions and reopened the question of what elite gates may consider. |

## What this list deliberately does not claim

The list does not claim that each chosen school had the largest enrollment, the greatest educational quality, the widest direct causal influence, or the most admirable legacy in its decade. Those would require different metrics and would often produce different schools.

The manuscript also avoids treating institutional self-descriptions as neutral. School websites are useful for dates, archival pointers, and how institutions narrate themselves, but claims about consequence and controversy require triangulation with public records, independent scholarship, court decisions, archives, and contemporaneous accounts.

## Narrative standard

Each chapter should begin with a concrete institutional moment and widen from there. The prose should distinguish what participants knew at the time from what later history makes obvious. No invented dialogue, sensory detail, motives, or quotations. No heroic montage. Counter-evidence belongs in the chapter rather than in a disclaimer at the end.

## Source practice

The source ledger records at least one anchor source per chapter and should grow during revision. The strongest factual claims should, where possible, rest on primary or official records: statutes, charters, court opinions, archival documents, National Park Service material, university archives, and contemporaneous records. Institutional histories may establish dates but should not carry contested interpretations by themselves.

## Research questions for revision

- What rival school is the strongest challenger for each decade, and what does choosing one over the other reveal about the thesis?
- Where do institutional origin stories simplify race, land, labor, or financing?
- Which schools exported a model directly, and which became important mainly as symbols?
- How much of each school’s later fame is being projected backward onto the selected decade?
- Which chapters over-credit a founder and under-credit students, families, teachers, donors, legislators, courts, communities, or opponents?
- Where did a reform solve one problem while creating another?
- Which decade would most historians dispute, and can the chapter make the selection argument strong enough to be worth the disagreement?
