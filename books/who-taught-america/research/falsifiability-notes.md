# Falsifiability notes — Who Taught America?

This file records the claims most likely to be overstated by a narrative built around one school per decade. It is a revision checklist, not a defense brief.

## The master claim

**Claim:** A single school can serve as the most revealing educational institution for each decade of American history.

**What would weaken it:** If a selected school mattered mainly because of events that happened in another decade; if its influence was narrower than a rival's; if the chapter depends on later fame projected backward; or if the institution does not actually illuminate a national question.

**Revision response:** Name the strongest rival inside the chapter when useful. Explain why the chosen school earns the slot rather than pretending the selection is self-evident.

## Decade-by-decade vulnerabilities

### 1770s — William & Mary

The 1779 law chair is unusually well documented, but the chapter must not imply that professional legal training alone created the American governing class. Apprenticeship, classical education, political networks, wealth, race, gender, and family status remained decisive.

### 1780s — University of Georgia

UGA's 1785 charter is historically important, but the university did not actually open until 1801. The chapter must distinguish a state committing itself to public higher education from an operating campus. A rival choice such as an academy or proprietary professional school could claim more immediate educational activity.

### 1790s — University of North Carolina

Claims of “first public university” are contested depending on whether the metric is charter date, opening date, or other institutional definitions. Use the narrower verified formulation: UNC was the first public university in the United States to open its doors and admitted its first class in 1795, according to UNC system records.

### 1800s — West Point

West Point's engineering role was large, but the chapter must not imply that the academy single-handedly engineered American expansion. Civilian engineers, enslaved labor, immigrant labor, private companies, state governments, Indigenous dispossession, and federal land policy also shaped continental infrastructure.

### 1810s — American School for the Deaf

Avoid a hearing-centered story in which Thomas Gallaudet simply “gave language” to deaf Americans. Laurent Clerc, French sign traditions, deaf students, and emerging American Deaf culture require agency in the chapter.

### 1820s — Troy Female Seminary

Do not describe the school as producing gender equality. It widened academic possibility for a particular class of young women while operating inside strong social limits. Rival institutions for female education existed before and alongside it.

### 1830s — Oberlin

Institutional “first” claims around Black admission and coeducation need precise wording. Policies, individual admissions, degree conferral, and continuous practice are different metrics.

### 1840s — Bridgewater Normal School

Bridgewater was the third Massachusetts state normal school, not the first. Its selection rests on the spread and professionalization of teacher training rather than a simple first.

### 1850s — Berea

Berea's interracial mission was radical, but its institutional development extended across the Civil War and Reconstruction. The 1855 one-room school should not be retroactively described as the mature college it later became.

### 1860s — Howard

Howard was only one of several foundational Reconstruction-era Black institutions. The chapter should not erase Fisk, Hampton, Atlanta University, Morehouse's predecessor, or the wider network of schools created by freedpeople, churches, aid societies, and the Freedmen's Bureau.

### 1870s — Carlisle

The chapter must avoid centering Richard Henry Pratt so strongly that Native children become props in his institutional story. Test claims against National Park Service records, student accounts, tribal histories, federal reports, and scholarship on boarding schools. “Importance” here describes consequence, including harm, not merit.

### 1880s — Tuskegee

Do not flatten Booker T. Washington into either hero or accommodationist caricature. Industrial education, liberal education, Black political strategy, white philanthropy, southern violence, and the later Washington–Du Bois debate need to be separated historically rather than forced into one binary.

### 1890s — University of Chicago

The research university model had German and American precedents and powerful rivals including Johns Hopkins. The selection should rest on Chicago's scale, ambition, urban setting, disciplinary influence, and institutional design rather than a claim that it invented research higher education.

### 1900s — Emerson School, Gary

The Gary Plan influenced national reform debates, but the chapter needs stronger archival sourcing before making quantitative claims about adoption. Avoid treating a factory-city metaphor as evidence that the school simply copied industrial management; the work-study-play model also aimed to broaden use of facilities and curriculum.

### 1910s — Lincoln School

Lincoln was well funded and socially selective. Its experimental curriculum cannot be treated as a typical American student's experience. That tension is part of the chapter: elite resources helped build reforms later discussed as public models.

### 1920s — Rhea County Central High School

The Scopes Trial was partly staged as a test case and a publicity event. Do not imply that Scopes was a lone teacher spontaneously persecuted for a lesson. The constructed nature of the case is central to its historical meaning.

### 1930s — Black Mountain College

Its cultural influence is easier to demonstrate than its national educational influence. The chapter should distinguish influence on art, design, literature, and experimental pedagogy from mass adoption in public schools.

### 1940s — Los Alamos Ranch School

The conceptual hook is strong but vulnerable to overstatement: the Ranch School did not “become” the atomic bomb in a literal institutional sense. The federal government acquired its campus, closed the school, and transformed the site into Project Y. Preserve that distinction.

### 1950s — Little Rock Central

The Little Rock Nine and their families must remain central. Do not turn the chapter into a contest between Eisenhower and Faubus that reduces students to symbols. Also distinguish school desegregation from full educational equality.

### 1960s — Berkeley

The Free Speech Movement was not the beginning of American student activism. The chapter's claim is that Berkeley became a nationally visible model and accelerator, not an absolute origin.

### 1970s — South Boston High

Boston's desegregation conflict cannot be reduced to “busing,” nor can white resistance be treated as the only community story. Housing segregation, school committee policy, federal court findings, Black parent activism, neighborhood politics, class, and media framing all require space.

### 1980s — Central Park East Secondary School

Claims about college-going and outcomes need contemporaneous documentation and context about selection, district structure, school size, and student population. The school is a counterexample to the era's standardization impulse, not proof that all small-school reforms work.

### 1990s — City Academy

City Academy was the first charter school to open, but the later charter sector diverged substantially from the original teacher-led alternative-school model. The chapter should make that divergence a central historical development rather than imply continuity.

### 2000s — MIT

OpenCourseWare provided materials, not an MIT credential or full substitute for campus learning. The distinction between access to content and access to institutional membership is the chapter's central tension.

### 2010s — Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School

The shooting must not be used as a dramatic device detached from the people killed and injured. Student activism grew from tragedy but did not represent every survivor or every affected family. Claims about movement scale should use sourced estimates.

### 2020s — Harvard

The decade is incomplete. The 2023 admissions decision is unquestionably consequential, but its long-run effects are still developing. Avoid forecasting a settled post-affirmative-action regime. The chapter should end with uncertainty and with the broader transformation of schooling through pandemic disruption, AI, cost pressure, and changing credential markets.
