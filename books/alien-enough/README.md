## Thesis

Astrobiology has an instrument problem disguised as a philosophical problem: every search for unfamiliar life begins by deciding which properties of familiar life are allowed to count. The proposed terrestrial “shadow biosphere” has never been demonstrated, and the arsenic-life episode is a warning against lowering evidentiary standards for exciting chemistry. But those failures do not erase the deeper measurement problem. If genuinely alien biochemistry existed beside us, many standard biological assays could classify it as contamination, mineral chemistry, failed amplification, or nothing at all. The book asks how to search for life without first requiring the answer to resemble the searcher.

## Reader promise

This is not a book claiming hidden alien organisms live on Earth. It is a book about the harder and more useful question: what experiment would deserve to discover them if they did?

## Defeat condition

The governing thesis weakens substantially if broad, chemistry-agnostic surveys show that plausible alternative biochemical systems would reliably trigger existing detection pipelines without requiring familiar nucleic acids, proteins, chirality, membrane chemistry, or metabolism. If standard methods are already demonstrably agnostic across that design space, the claimed detection blind spot is mostly rhetorical.
