# SCANATE

Before remote viewing became a military acronym, it belonged to Menlo Park.

Stanford Research Institute had the right kind of institutional ambiguity for the early 1970s. It was serious enough to have laboratories, government contracts and engineers who knew what a shielded room was. It was independent enough from Stanford University to take work that might have been awkward inside a conventional psychology department. And the period itself was unusually hospitable to technical heresy. The Cold War was spending heavily on things that could be dismissed as fantasy until they worked.

Harold Puthoff, a physicist and electrical engineer, joined SRI in 1971. Russell Targ, a laser physicist, followed. Their names would become inseparable from the government's parapsychology history, though the mythology that formed around them later makes it easy to forget how the work first appeared to outsiders: not as a cult but as an engineering claim.

Could information cross a boundary without an ordinary channel?

That framing mattered. Parapsychology had carried decades of baggage from card-guessing experiments, séance culture and claims that often dissolved under replication. Puthoff and Targ brought a different visual vocabulary: targets, shielding, coordinates, outbound experimenters, judges, transcripts. The language did not make the phenomenon real. It made it testable in a form intelligence agencies could imagine using.

The Central Intelligence Agency's interest emerged in 1972 amid reports of Soviet research into parapsychology and psychotronics. The American concern was not uniquely credulous. Intelligence services routinely collect rumors about adversary science precisely because rumors can be cheap and surprises expensive. The difficulty is that the collection process can magnify the rumor. Once analysts begin asking how much the other side is spending on an exotic capability, the existence of the estimate can become evidence that the capability deserves domestic investigation.

This feedback loop will return later. At SRI it helped produce a small research effort sometimes associated with the label SCANATE, a contraction of “scan by coordinate.”

The basic experiment was elegant enough to survive ridicule.

A person remained at SRI. An outbound team traveled to a randomly chosen local location. The viewer, separated from the target, was asked to describe where the team had gone. The resulting verbal material and drawings were later compared with candidate target sites. In the strongest version of the design, the judge did not know which transcript belonged to which location and had to rank correspondences blindly.

The method had an intelligence-shaped advantage over card guessing. A remote viewer could produce a map-like description rather than select among five symbols. If the effect was real, it might carry richer information. The same freedom created the central methodological danger: richer outputs offer more ways to match after the fact.

In October 1974, *Nature* published Targ and Puthoff's “Information transmission under conditions of sensory shielding.” The paper remains the canonical public starting point. It described experiments with several subjects and concluded that their results suggested one or more perceptual modalities through which people could obtain information not presented to any known sense. The paper was not merely a curiosity column. It appeared in one of the world's most prominent scientific journals.

That fact has been used ever since as a kind of reputational escrow: *Nature* published it, therefore the work must have been extraordinary.

The real story is better.

Publication meant the claim entered an adversarial system. It gave critics enough methodological detail to attack. David Marks and Richard Kammann later attempted related work and reported that their results did not verify the SRI conclusions. Marks argued that sensory cues embedded in transcripts and judging procedures could account for apparent success in some remote-viewing experiments. Targ, Puthoff and Charles Tart disputed the criticism. The argument continued through *Nature* correspondence into the 1980s.

This sequence is more scientifically valuable than a ceremonial citation to the 1974 paper.

The early experiments contain nearly the entire remote-viewing problem in miniature. There is a strange positive result. There is a plausible ordinary artifact. There is a fight over whether protocol changes remove the artifact. There is a move toward more formal judging and statistics. And after each improvement, the underlying interpretation becomes harder rather than easier.

That last part deserves attention.

In ordinary stories of science, better controls make the truth converge. Sometimes they do. In marginal sciences, better controls can create a narrowing corridor in which the effect grows smaller, more statistical and less dramatic while remaining stubbornly nonzero. Supporters say the residue is what survives the removal of error. Critics say the residue is what remains after known error has been reduced but unknown error has not. Both statements can be coherent at the same time.

The SRI program also introduced the personalities who would dominate later accounts. Ingo Swann, an artist from New York with longstanding interest in psychic phenomena, became central to the development of remote-viewing methods and later of “coordinate remote viewing.” Pat Price, a former police commissioner and city councilman from Burbank, California, produced several sessions that supporters considered exceptional. Uri Geller appeared in the early SRI work, bringing celebrity and additional controversy.

Their presence made the research easier to tell as a story and harder to evaluate as a system.

Exceptional subjects create an engineering problem. If a capability depends on a few unusual people, selection becomes part of the mechanism. The military cannot treat the phenomenon as a general human sense. It needs to know how to find performers, how stable performance is, whether it can be trained, and how to distinguish a temporarily poor session from a permanently poor viewer.

This sounds mundane. Mundanity is where capabilities live.

Consider the difference between discovering one person who can, under some conditions, produce descriptions that judges match above chance and establishing an intelligence discipline. The second task requires personnel screening, training standards, tasking rules, feedback controls, performance metrics, security procedures, documentation, analyst integration and an exit criterion for bad performance. The psychological romance of the first achievement can actively obscure the bureaucracy required for the second.

SRI spent years trying to cross that gap.

The early CIA relationship did not last continuously. According to later CIA histories, the Agency reduced and eventually ended its direct involvement in the 1970s, while the work migrated through other parts of the defense and intelligence establishment. This institutional handoff is sometimes presented as evidence that the program went “deeper” or became more secret because it worked too well. The documented explanation is less cinematic: sponsors changed, priorities changed, personalities changed and political risk mattered.

Remote viewing was a reputational hazard even inside intelligence.

That fact cuts two ways.

Skeptics can reasonably argue that embarrassment may have delayed proper scrutiny or encouraged the work to live in organizational corners where enthusiastic sponsors mattered too much. Proponents can reasonably argue that stigma may have discouraged replication and made negative institutional judgments partly social. Neither inference settles the data. Stigma is not evidence for a phenomenon, and ridicule is not a control condition.

The 1974 paper also points toward a distinction that will haunt the entire program: sensory shielding is not the same as informational shielding.

A participant can be physically isolated yet still learn something through protocol structure, experimenter behavior, transcript handling, target selection or later judging. The scientific challenge is not merely to close the door. It is to account for every path by which target-specific information could enter the result.

This is why remote viewing is unusually educational. The output is semantic rather than binary. A drawing of a bridge can be uncannily specific. A phrase like “tall vertical structure” can be almost meaningless. Between those poles lies human judgment, and human judgment is very good at finding pattern after the answer is known.

The best remote-viewing protocols tried to make that judgment blind and quantitative. They did not always succeed. They also created their own puzzle: if the statistical procedure says the set of transcripts is unlikely under chance, but no one can use an individual transcript reliably enough to make a decision, what exactly has been discovered?

SCANATE began as an attempt to see at a distance.

It ended by forcing a more difficult problem into view.
