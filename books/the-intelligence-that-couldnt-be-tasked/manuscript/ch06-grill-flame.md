# GRILL FLAME

By the time the Army inherited remote viewing, the question had changed from “is this interesting?” to “can soldiers do it?”

That sounds like a small shift. It is the difference between a research curiosity and an attempted doctrine.

Army Intelligence and Security Command began exploring remote viewing in the late 1970s. The effort became associated with GRILL FLAME, one of several names that now float through popular accounts as though they were aliases for a single secret project. The bureaucracy was messier. Funding and sponsorship moved. The Defense Intelligence Agency had its own research interests. SRI continued contract work. Army personnel at Fort Meade were selected and trained. Eventually pieces of the effort would be reorganized under CENTER LANE, SUN STREAK and STAR GATE.

Names make history look cleaner than it was.

The Army faced a practical problem SRI could postpone: talent acquisition.

If a civilian researcher believes a few exceptional people display remote-viewing ability, the researcher can keep testing those people. A military program needs replacements. People transfer, retire, become unavailable, lose clearances, perform inconsistently or simply stop wanting to participate. A capability based on irreplaceable individuals is fragile before an adversary ever touches it.

The Fort Meade effort therefore tried to identify viewers among military and civilian personnel and to train them.

This is where remote viewing began to acquire procedural grammar.

The viewer should report basic sensory impressions before naming the target. Shape before object. Texture before interpretation. Temperature, color, dimension, movement. The term “analytic overlay” described the moment the ordinary mind leapt from sensation to story: “cold, hard, cylindrical” became “missile,” and the label then contaminated everything that followed.

There is something almost meditative about the discipline. It resembles techniques used in other domains to separate observation from inference. A good field scientist learns to write down what is present before deciding what caused it. A radiologist distinguishes a visible opacity from a diagnosis. An intelligence analyst is supposed to separate source reporting from analytic judgment.

Remote-viewing training made that separation part of the alleged perceptual act itself.

The problem was that the military ultimately wanted the diagnosis.

A page filled with “vertical,” “metallic,” “cool,” and “enclosed” might be a clean phenomenological record and a poor intelligence product. The Army's adoption therefore sharpened the contradiction already present at SRI. The protocol tried to protect signal by suppressing interpretation, while the user needed interpretation to act.

GRILL FLAME records from the period are useful because they are not written like later memoirs. Session reports often state the purpose, describe the protocol, include drawings and transcript material, and explicitly label the output as raw intelligence. Some concern training targets. Some respond to real tasking.

They also preserve failure.

A 1978 training session in the declassified collection states that the viewer's target images showed “absolutely no target correlation.” A 1982 training report says a viewer showed “very little correlation” and directs attention to another session on the same target. These lines are almost comically incompatible with the mythology of a black program producing effortless psychic surveillance.

They are compatible with a program trying to train a difficult or nonexistent skill.

That ambiguity is the point.

If the effect was an artifact, training could still appear to work. Repeated exposure teaches participants what kinds of descriptors judges reward. Interviewers learn how to elicit cleaner material. Viewers learn to avoid over-specific guesses that create obvious misses. Everyone becomes better at the protocol without becoming better at anomalous perception.

If the effect was real, the same training process might genuinely improve signal-to-noise ratio.

Behavior alone cannot distinguish those explanations without careful controls.

The military context added another complication: security.

Remote-viewing proponents sometimes emphasize that viewers could be given minimal target information, perhaps a coordinate or opaque identifier, reducing ordinary cueing. Operational security benefited from that arrangement. Scientific control did too, at least in principle. Yet operational tasking inevitably involved people who knew the question. Someone selected the target. Someone prepared the cue. Someone interviewed or monitored the session. Someone interpreted the result.

A classified environment can reduce leaks to the outside world while making independent replication harder.

That tradeoff matters whenever anomalous claims appear in secret programs. Secrecy is often treated as though it protects truth. It protects information. Those are not the same thing.

A classified laboratory can hide a capability from an adversary. It can also hide methodological weakness from critics. It can restrict the pool of independent evaluators. It can create a small epistemic community in which the same people train viewers, run sessions, interpret results and brief sponsors. Compartmentation, useful for security, can be hostile to scientific correction.

GRILL FLAME lived inside that tension.

The Army and DIA also investigated foreign psychoenergetics, countermeasures, search problems and methods for evaluation. The 1984 DIA report summarizing three years of work made ambitious claims. It said remote viewing was real, not degraded by distance or shielding, improvable through training, and potentially useful for intelligence. It also contained a qualification that should have received more attention: descriptive content was more reliable than analytic content such as function or complex technical data.

That is almost the entire operational problem compressed into one sentence.

Descriptions are easier to match than functions.

“Large structure” can be accurate in many places. “Gaseous diffusion plant” is useful because it narrows. A sketch resembling a crane can be visually arresting. A report that the crane is assembling a particular submarine class at a particular stage of construction is a different order of claim.

The program wanted the second kind while often being strongest, by its own account, at the first.

This discrepancy would later appear in the 1995 operational review, where users could rate output as containing accurate elements while still judging it of little value.

GRILL FLAME also reveals how experimental programs become communities.

Viewers develop reputations. Certain sessions circulate. Sponsors visit. Briefings create institutional memory. An officer who sees one extraordinary-looking result may tolerate many mediocre sessions because the imagined upside is enormous. This is not uniquely irrational. Venture capital works similarly. So does early-stage scientific funding. A few rare successes can justify a portfolio of failures if the successes are real and valuable enough.

The portfolio logic becomes dangerous when successes are difficult to authenticate.

A missile test either flies or it does not. A sensor either detects the calibrated target or misses it. Remote-viewing success can be gradated, semantic and retrospective. The sponsor who already finds the phenomenon plausible may score the same transcript differently from the skeptic.

The program needed an evaluation culture strong enough to withstand its own best stories.

It never fully got one.

This is not because nobody tried. Researchers developed formal scoring methods. Operations were documented. External skeptics were sometimes involved. The later AIR review exists because the government eventually demanded a more independent reckoning.

But during the period when the capability was being built, the line between research and use remained porous. Operational sessions could be cited as evidence the method worked. Laboratory findings could justify continued operations. Foreign-threat assessments could justify both. Training successes could support claims of controllability. Each stream had weaknesses that the other streams appeared to compensate for.

Together they produced persistence.

Persistence is not proof.

It is an institutional outcome.

That is why GRILL FLAME matters more than its name. It was the stage when remote viewing stopped being merely an experiment conducted by researchers and became an attempted organizational capability. The Army had to confront questions that a journal article could leave unresolved: who does this, how are they trained, what do we ask them, what do we do with the answer, and how do we know when not to believe it?

A military program can survive a mysterious mechanism.

It cannot survive indefinitely without a reliable answer to the last question.
