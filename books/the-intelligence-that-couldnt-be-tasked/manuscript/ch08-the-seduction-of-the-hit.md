# The Seduction of the Hit

Every anomaly field has a photograph.

Remote viewing has sketches.

A line on paper resembles a gantry. A cluster of forms resembles storage tanks. A shape looks like a submarine. The pairing can be eerie enough that explanation feels almost impolite.

That feeling deserves respect and distrust in equal measure.

People who have never read a remote-viewing paper often encounter the field through its best examples. A television documentary shows the target and the drawing side by side. A former participant recalls the day a viewer described an installation that was supposedly unknown to anyone in the room. A book retells a case in which a new Soviet submarine or hidden facility was seen before conventional intelligence confirmed it.

The story has a natural camera angle: target on the left, response on the right.

The denominator is off screen.

This does not make the example fake. It means the example has been selected for survival.

Intelligence organizations understand selection bias because they live with it. Analysts who warn of a surprise attack are remembered differently depending on whether the attack occurs. Sources who provide one dramatic warning can acquire reputations out of proportion to their full reporting history. After an event, old fragmentary reports are reread with new eyes. Phrases that once seemed vague become prophetic.

Remote viewing amplifies the effect because its outputs are flexible.

The famous cases are therefore worth reading twice. The first time, let the correspondence hit. The second time, ask what had to happen for this particular pairing to reach you.

Was the target assigned before the session? Was it blind? Was the complete response preserved? How many viewers attempted the task? How many sessions were run? Was the celebrated image chosen from several drawings? Were misses reported with the same energy? Did the person describing the case know the target when selecting quotations? Did the operational user rate the information as useful at the time, or did the story become impressive only after later confirmation?

A case can survive some of those questions and remain interesting.

The best examples should.

One recurrent story concerns remote viewing of Soviet technical targets. The U.S. intelligence community had obvious interest in shipyards, weapons development and denied facilities. Remote-viewing proponents later described sessions that, in their telling, anticipated unusual structures or features subsequently confirmed by imagery. Some accounts involve Pat Price; others Ingo Swann or military viewers. Details vary across retellings, and that variation is itself evidence to handle carefully.

The correct posture is not to flatten all such stories into “debunked” or “proven.”

It is to reconstruct them.

A reconstruction begins with the earliest contemporaneous document, not the best later anecdote. It separates what the viewer produced from what the interviewer said. It preserves the full session rather than extracting the image that looks best in hindsight. It records what target information was available to participants. It identifies when conventional intelligence obtained corroboration. It asks whether the claimed prediction was actually specific enough to be unlikely under ordinary matching.

This is forensic work, not belief work.

The reason is memory.

Human memory does not preserve evidentiary chains. It preserves meaning.

A participant who spent years inside the program may sincerely remember the successful session as the moment everything changed. Over decades, the messy context can compress. The exact date blurs. A later satellite image and an earlier sketch become mentally adjacent. A supervisor's excitement gets folded into the event. The viewer's original uncertainty is forgotten. None of this requires deceit. Memory is a narrative system.

Declassified records provide resistance.

They are not perfect resistance. Documents can be incomplete, redacted or wrongly dated. But contemporaneous paperwork has one advantage over recollection: it cannot know which details will become famous later.

This is why the dull session reports matter so much. They establish a population around the anecdotes.

If a program generated thousands of pages over two decades, some astonishing correspondences are expected by chance alone, especially when outputs are complex and targets are rich. The correct expected number depends on how sessions were scored and selected, which is precisely what popular retellings rarely provide.

A simple mental experiment helps.

Ask one hundred people to draw what will appear on the front page of a newspaper next week. Most drawings will be useless. A few will contain circles, crowds, aircraft, buildings, flags, faces, maps, smoke, numbers. When the front page appears, some drawing may look uncannily related. If you publish only that one, the result feels impossible. If the drawing contains an unusually specific correct detail under genuinely blind, preregistered conditions, it becomes more interesting. The difference between those outcomes is not intuition. It is design.

Remote-viewing proponents can reasonably object that controlled studies attempted exactly that design. They are right. The field did not consist only of uncontrolled anecdotes. Laboratory experiments used random targets, blind judging and formal statistics. The operational anecdotes should therefore not be used as substitutes for the experimental literature.

Nor should the experimental literature be used to launder the anecdotes.

A statistically significant laboratory database does not authenticate every famous operational story. Each claim needs its own chain of evidence.

This is where the four evidence classes become useful.

A declassified session transcript is class one: documented. A retired officer's firsthand account that the transcript influenced a real task may be class two: credible but incomplete if corroboration is missing. A claim that the session located a secret submarine before any conventional source knew of it may be class three if the chronology cannot be independently established. The idea that this demonstrates a nonlocal property of consciousness belongs in class four until mechanism and replication support it.

The categories are not insults. They prevent evidence from changing type as it moves through a story.

A classified stamp does not promote testimony to proof. A sincere witness does not promote memory to contemporaneous documentation. An unexplained correspondence does not promote anomaly to mechanism.

The hit seduces because it collapses these distinctions emotionally.

There is also a reverse seduction available to skeptics: the bad miss.

One ludicrous transcript can make the whole enterprise look foolish. A viewer describes mountains while the target is a parking lot. An operational prediction fails. The reader feels the same closure in the opposite direction.

That is equally dangerous.

If the claim is statistical, individual failures are expected even under a real effect. Showing a miss does not refute an aggregate result. The correct skeptical move is to ask whether the aggregate survives proper controls, independent replication and bias correction.

Remote viewing demands a kind of intellectual bilingualism.

At the anecdote level, we must resist being hypnotized by matches. At the statistical level, we must resist pretending that small effects cannot exist. At the operational level, we must ask whether either form of evidence can support decisions.

These levels produce different kinds of surprise.

The single spectacular hit says: how could this happen?

The meta-analytic effect says: why does this keep happening slightly more often than expected?

The operational audit says: why, after all that, could users still not depend on it?

That third question is the one this book cares about most.

It is possible to imagine a world in which all three observations are true. A weak anomalous effect exists. Occasionally it produces a remarkable session. Most sessions remain too noisy for intelligence use. The rare hits cannot be identified prospectively. The mechanism remains unknown. Skeptics can reproduce some artifacts but not explain every dataset. Proponents can demonstrate statistical anomalies but not an operationally calibrated service.

That world would frustrate everyone.

It would also look a great deal like the public record we inherited.

The alternative world is more prosaic. There is no anomalous channel. Methodological flaws, flexible scoring, selection effects and memory generate the apparent residue. The best anecdotes are survivors from a huge search space. Better controls reduce the effect toward zero, though never perfectly because research itself is noisy.

The book does not need to decide between those worlds by force.

It needs to identify the observations that would separate them.

A hit becomes scientifically powerful when it was specified before feedback, scored under a rule set in advance, generated under genuine information isolation, replicated across independent teams, and embedded in a complete denominator of attempts. It becomes operationally powerful when users can recognize its value before confirmation and make better decisions because of it.

That is a much harsher standard than resemblance.

It is supposed to be.

The strange thing about the STAR GATE story is not that people were impressed by hits. Anyone would be.

The strange thing is how long the government kept trying to discover whether the next one could be known in advance.
