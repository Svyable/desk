# The Judge Problem

The remote viewer is the glamorous part of a remote-viewing experiment. The judge may be more important.

A free-response experiment produces mess.

The target might be a pedestrian bridge. The viewer might say there is “a horizontal line,” “open space,” “movement below,” “hard gray material,” “a sense of crossing,” and then spend five minutes describing a rounded red object that does not exist. Another viewer might draw two vertical lines connected by a third and say “industrial.” A third might say almost nothing useful except “water.”

What happened?

If the answer is “the viewer saw the bridge,” somebody has already done interpretive work.

This problem predates SRI. Any experiment in which subjects produce ambiguous material and judges later compare it with targets must specify how matching happens. The more semantic freedom the response contains, the more dangerous hindsight becomes. Human beings are superb at fitting descriptions to known outcomes. We do it in medicine, finance, intelligence, astrology, criminal investigation and ordinary memory. Once a target is revealed, a phrase that looked vague five minutes earlier can suddenly feel specific.

The remote-viewing researchers knew this. Blind judging was not an optional decoration; it was an attempt to prevent the evaluator from knowing the answer while scoring the response.

But blinding a judge is harder than covering the target label.

David Marks and Richard Kammann's criticisms of the early SRI work focused in part on cues that could remain in transcripts. If transcripts contained references to the order of sessions, comments about previous targets, dates, or other information that allowed a judge to infer which response went with which target, then successful matching did not require anomalous perception. Later exchanges disputed the extent and significance of these flaws, and procedures were changed. The point is not that one criticism automatically invalidated every subsequent remote-viewing study. The point is that the judge became part of the signal path.

Once you see this, the entire field changes shape.

A remote-viewing experiment is not simply viewer versus target. It is target selection, randomization, cueing, viewer, interviewer, transcript, editing, target pool, judge, scoring rule, statistical analysis and feedback. Each stage can create, destroy or leak information.

This is why “sensory shielding” is a dangerously narrow phrase.

You can put a participant in a Faraday cage and still run a bad information experiment.

The important boundary is not physical isolation. It is information flow.

Consider a simple ranking design. The viewer produces one transcript for one target. Later, a judge receives that transcript along with five candidate targets and ranks them from best to worst match. If the true target is ranked first more often than chance, the experiment may suggest information transfer. But the validity of that inference depends on details that sound pedantic until they change the result.

Who selected the five candidate targets? Were they equally distinct? Did the transcript contain any clue to when it was produced? Did the judge see the transcript in its original form? Were irrelevant remarks removed, and if so, by someone who knew the target? Was there one judge or several? Was the scoring rule chosen before the data were seen? Were unsuccessful sessions excluded because the viewer reported feeling “off”? Were multiple analyses attempted and only one reported?

Every question is a possible door.

Good experiments close doors before anyone knows where the target is.

This is one reason preregistration became important in other contested fields decades later. The danger is not limited to fraud. Researchers can make reasonable decisions one at a time and still create a path through the data that favors the hoped-for result. Flexible analysis converts uncertainty into hidden researcher degrees of freedom.

Remote viewing adds another layer because the raw material is linguistic.

Language is compressible after the fact. A long transcript can be remembered by its best line. A miss can be reinterpreted metaphorically. “Water” near a cooling tower becomes a cooling system. “Mountain” near a pyramid becomes shape. “Underground” near a secure installation becomes secrecy. None of these mappings is necessarily dishonest. That is precisely why they are dangerous.

A good scoring method tries to stop meaning from expanding after revelation.

The famous early experiments sometimes used blind judges who visited or reviewed target sites and matched transcripts. Later laboratory work developed other scoring methods. The 1995 AIR evaluation spent substantial effort on whether the statistical procedures supported an anomalous effect. Jessica Utts and Ray Hyman disagreed sharply about what could be inferred from the data, but their disagreement was not a cartoon of statistician versus skeptic. Both were looking at the same deeper problem: how much confidence should be assigned to results produced by a research system with a long methodological history and imperfect independent replication?

The judge problem becomes even worse in intelligence operations.

A laboratory judge has a closed set of targets and a scoring rule. An operational analyst has the world.

Suppose a military unit asks where a missing person is being held. The viewer describes “brick,” “stairs,” “a large old building,” “radios,” and “boards over windows.” There may be hundreds of buildings fitting pieces of that description. If investigators later find the person in a red-brick building with boarded windows, the transcript will look impressive. If the person is found elsewhere, individual descriptors may still match something around the site. The search space is not five candidate photographs. It is geography.

This difference is so large that “laboratory success” and “operational success” should almost be treated as different phenomena.

In the lab, the judge can ask which of five known targets best fits the response. In operations, the user asks what in the world the response refers to.

The direction of inference reverses.

Closed-set recognition is easier than open-set identification in almost every domain. A witness who can pick a face from five photographs may still be unable to describe the person well enough for police to find them in a city. A classifier that distinguishes cats from dogs may fail when shown a fox, a toy, or a pattern deliberately designed to confuse it. An intelligence source who provides one accurate detail among many vague ones may be valuable if analysts can independently corroborate it. Without corroboration, the same detail can send resources in the wrong direction.

Remote viewing was therefore trapped by its own most interesting feature: it produced rich, unconstrained material.

Richness allowed astonishing matches. It also created combinatorial freedom.

The program tried to manage this with structured protocols. Interviewers were instructed to avoid leading. Viewers were taught to distinguish raw sensory impressions from what some methods called “analytic overlay,” the mind's tendency to name or interpret too quickly. A viewer might report “cool, smooth, vertical” rather than “a missile.” This was psychologically sophisticated. It recognized that cognition itself can contaminate perception.

But it created a new translation burden for the user.

An intelligence officer does not want “cool, smooth, vertical.” The officer wants to know whether the thing is a missile.

Here is the operational paradox in one sentence: the more carefully remote viewing avoided premature interpretation, the more interpretive work it handed to the analyst.

That analyst was not blind.

The analyst knew the intelligence question. If the target was a suspected missile facility, “cylindrical” suddenly mattered. If the target was a submarine, “metal,” “water” and “long” became charged words. The very context required to make the data useful also created the conditions for confirmation bias.

This does not mean operational users were fools. Analysts work with ambiguous sources constantly. Human intelligence reports are fragmentary. Signals intercepts are incomplete. Imagery can be obscured. Sources lie. Intelligence tradecraft exists partly to combine weak streams without overtrusting any one of them.

Remote viewing could, in principle, have been another weak stream.

That possibility makes evaluation harder, not easier.

A source does not need to be perfect to be useful. It needs to improve decisions relative to its cost and alternatives. A source with a low hit rate might still matter if it occasionally produces unique, verifiable information at low cost. But proving that requires tracking what was known before the source reported, what was newly added, what actions followed, and whether those actions outperformed what would have happened without the source.

Anecdotes rarely preserve that structure.

They preserve the moment the match became visible.

That is why the judge problem is not a footnote to the remote-viewing story. It is the story's epistemic engine. Every claim of success contains a hidden question: who decided it was a success, when did they decide, what alternatives were available, and how much did they know?

The viewer may have been the person trying to see at a distance.

The judge was the person deciding whether anything had been seen at all.
