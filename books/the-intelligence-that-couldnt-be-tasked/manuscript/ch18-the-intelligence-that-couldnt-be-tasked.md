# The Intelligence That Couldn't Be Tasked

In 1972, the proposition was small enough to fit inside a room: can one person describe a place they cannot see?

By 1995, the room had filled with everything that proposition had failed to settle.

There were laboratory statistics, Army transcripts, Soviet threat estimates, training protocols, classification markings, special-access compartments, famous drawings, embarrassing misses, operational users, skeptical critiques, methodological repairs, internal believers, external reviewers and thousands of pages of declassified material that would later be mistaken for a verdict simply because there was so much of it.

The volume is real.

The verdict is not.

The public record supports several conclusions with very different levels of confidence. The United States government genuinely investigated remote viewing for years. The effort was not one continuous CIA program, and its changing names should not be read as stages of secret scientific confirmation. Some controlled experimental series produced results that sympathetic and technically competent reviewers considered difficult to explain by chance. Serious critics disputed whether those results established anomalous information acquisition. Operational remote viewing generated occasional reports users or participants regarded as striking, while the overall capability remained too inconsistent and poorly calibrated for CIA to restore after the 1995 review.

Those statements can stand together.

The urge to collapse them is what has kept the subject confused.

If one begins from the assumption that psychic functioning is impossible, the archive looks like a museum of bureaucratic credulity. The misses are decisive; the positive statistics are artifacts not yet fully diagnosed; the famous hits are selection effects. This account has explanatory power. It fits known human tendencies toward retrospective matching, publication bias and motivated interpretation. It deserves to remain the default competing explanation until stronger prospective evidence dislodges it.

If one begins from the experimental anomaly, another picture appears. Perhaps a weak form of anomalous cognition survives good controls. Perhaps it is variable across people, low-bandwidth, difficult to introspect and mixed with ordinary imagery. In that world, STAR GATE did not discover nothing. It discovered something harder to commercialize than to measure.

The historical record does not let us promote that possibility to established fact.

It does let us notice how much of the argument has been aimed at the wrong threshold.

The intelligence community did not need a philosophical answer to consciousness. It needed a source it could task.

Tasking means more than asking a question. A taskable source has predictable interfaces. The collector knows what sort of request the source can answer, how long it will take, what form the response will take, what kinds of error to expect and how much confidence to assign. The user knows what to do with the product. The source's history provides a basis for trust or distrust before the answer is confirmed.

Remote viewing never acquired that kind of ordinary reliability.

Its descriptions could be evocative. Its best experimental results could be statistically interesting. Its famous sessions could be astonishing. The program still struggled with the simplest operational sentence: how much should I believe this report now?

That sentence contains the whole failure.

A source that is occasionally brilliant but cannot signal when it is brilliant is difficult to use. A source that becomes impressive after analysts know the target is difficult to score. A source that produces accurate general description without enough specific discrimination is difficult to task. A source that requires conventional intelligence to decode its meaning may still add value, but that value has to be measured against what the conventional intelligence already supplied.

None of these problems requires the phenomenon to be fake.

That is why STAR GATE remains intellectually useful.

Most arguments about extraordinary claims are built as courtroom dramas. Evidence is admitted for one side or the other until the reader is expected to declare belief. Intelligence engineering asks a less satisfying question: even if I grant you this evidence, what can I do with it?

That question is merciless in productive ways.

Suppose Utts was right that the later laboratory database contained a genuine anomaly. We still need calibration.

Suppose Hyman was right that methodological and replication concerns prevent paranormal attribution. We still need to explain why some controlled results looked anomalous enough to sustain serious technical disagreement.

Suppose a famous operational hit survives archival reconstruction. We still need the denominator.

Suppose Soviet researchers pursued similar ideas. We still need evidence of capability rather than interest.

Suppose a classified stamp appears on the page. We still need to know whether the classified thing was knowledge, uncertainty, method, target or embarrassment.

Epistemology is not what remains after the story. It is the story.

The program also offers a warning about secrecy that cuts in both directions.

Classification can protect a fragile capability from adversaries. It can also protect a fragile belief from correction. A small compartment may preserve unconventional research long enough to mature. It may also narrow the circle of people capable of finding ordinary mistakes. Declassification later exposes the paperwork without restoring the social context in which decisions were made, inviting outsiders to use real secrecy as evidence for whichever theory they brought with them.

The solution is not radical transparency in every intelligence program. That would be unserious.

The solution is internal adversarial evaluation strong enough that secrecy does not become an excuse for epistemic softness.

STAR GATE eventually received a version of that evaluation. It came late, after the research lineage had accumulated decades of heterogeneous material. Yet the final split remains unusually instructive: statistical interest did not rescue operational weakness.

That principle travels.

A fraud model can show genuine predictive signal and still be unusable because investigators drown in false positives. A biomarker can correlate with disease and fail as a screening test. An AI system can outperform a benchmark and remain unfit for deployment because its errors arrive in the wrong places. A sensor can detect an exotic phenomenon in aggregate and still fail to identify the next event when anyone needs it.

We keep relearning the difference between signal and capability because signal is easier to celebrate.

Capability is bookkeeping.

It is calibration tables, denominators, ugly nulls, error budgets, user studies, replication failures, maintenance, conditions where performance degrades, and the humiliation of discovering that a thing which exists cannot yet be trusted.

Remote viewing never became boring enough.

Perhaps that is because there was nothing paranormal underneath it. Perhaps a small anomaly survived but never escaped the laboratory's statistical shelter. The available public evidence does not justify pretending to know which explanation will ultimately win.

What it does justify is a harder standard for the next extraordinary claim.

Ask what was observed.

Ask how the information could have entered.

Ask whether the evaluation rule existed before the answer.

Ask for the complete denominator.

Ask whether independent groups can reproduce the result without inheriting the same hidden choices.

Then, if the effect survives, ask the question believers often treat as an insult and skeptics often never reach:

What is it good for?

The answer may be nothing yet.

That is not the same as nothing.

A final page from the old program could still carry the warning used on so many session reports. The impressions are raw. They have not yet been subjected to enough analysis, evaluation or collation. Interpretation remains the responsibility of the requestor.

For once, the bureaucracy had the philosophy exactly right.
