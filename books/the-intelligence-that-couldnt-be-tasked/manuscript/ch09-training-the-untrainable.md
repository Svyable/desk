# Training the Untrainable

A military capability that cannot be taught is a talent show.

The Army needed something else.

Ingo Swann became central to the attempt to turn remote viewing into a method rather than a gift. The system that emerged, usually called coordinate remote viewing or controlled remote viewing in later usage, divided the session into stages and taught viewers to move from coarse impressions toward more complex information while policing the mind's tendency to jump to conclusions.

The procedure has acquired a second life outside government. Training courses, manuals, competitions and online communities now treat its vocabulary as a craft tradition. That afterlife can obscure the original institutional question: could a small group of soldiers be trained to generate better intelligence than they could before?

The answer is surprisingly hard to extract from the program's own history.

A 1984 DIA summary described training as promising and stated that remote-viewing ability could be improved by appropriate procedures. Five years later, an SRI review of work from 1973 through 1988 was more cautious. It reported no quantitative evidence supporting a training hypothesis, even while arguing that experienced viewers performed better than the general population. Those two statements can coexist if training and selection are being confused.

That distinction matters everywhere expertise is studied.

Suppose the best viewers improve over time. Perhaps the training works. Perhaps poor performers leave. Perhaps researchers spend more time with people who already show promise. Perhaps familiarity with the scoring system improves output without changing any anomalous ability. Perhaps feedback teaches subjects what kinds of descriptors transfer well across target pools. Perhaps confidence alone improves compliance with the protocol. A longitudinal improvement curve cannot tell these stories apart by itself.

The program needed randomized training evidence.

It rarely had enough of it.

This is one of the more mundane reasons extraordinary claims remain unresolved. The experiment you actually need is often expensive, slow and socially awkward. Researchers who believe they have discovered rare talent do not want to assign promising subjects to a no-training control for two years. Military sponsors want usable results, not elegant causal inference. Subjects talk to one another. Trainers innovate during the study. Small sample sizes make everything unstable.

The result is a program that can accumulate craft without establishing pedagogy.

The craft itself is interesting.

Remote-viewing training took seriously a problem familiar to every observer: naming too early can blind you.

A viewer reports “sharp, metallic, cool.” The ordinary mind says knife. Once “knife” enters consciousness, the next impressions bend around it. The protocol calls this analytic overlay and encourages the viewer to mark it, set it aside and return to lower-level sensation. The idea has a psychological plausibility independent of psi. Perception is not a camera. Expectations alter what we notice. Experts in ordinary domains routinely learn to delay interpretation.

A birder may notice wing shape before species. A mechanic hears a frequency before naming a failing bearing. A radiologist may describe margins and density before committing to a diagnosis.

The remote-viewing method built an entire discipline around that restraint.

Here the story becomes epistemically awkward. A technique can improve reasoning while the phenomenon it is designed to access remains unproven.

Astrologers can become skilled interviewers. Dowsers can learn landscape cues. Traders using a bad theory can still develop good risk discipline. A ritual can contain useful cognitive practices without validating its cosmology.

Coordinate remote viewing may belong partly in this category even if every paranormal claim eventually fails.

That possibility does not explain positive blinded experiments. It explains why participants might experience training as genuinely transformative. They learn to attend differently. They produce more structured reports. They become less impulsive in naming. Their drawings may improve because they practice drawing spatial relationships. The subjective sense of acquiring a new perceptual skill can be real.

Whether the information comes from the target is another question.

The training problem also exposes a hidden dependency: feedback.

Skills improve through error correction. A marksman sees where the bullet hit. A language learner hears the correct pronunciation. A remote viewer receives target feedback after the session. If feedback is detailed and emotionally salient, the viewer can learn which kinds of responses seem successful. This is necessary for training and dangerous for evaluation.

The system needs feedback to improve and blindness to prove that improvement is anomalous.

Those goals must be separated in time and design.

A rigorous training study would define performance metrics before training, randomize comparable participants, use target pools and judging procedures protected from leakage, keep evaluators blind, and test on novel targets under conditions different from practice. It would track every participant, including those who drop out. It would distinguish improvement in session discipline from improvement in target-specific information. It would preregister the analysis and use independent teams for replication.

The historical program rarely operated with all of those modern expectations.

That is not a cheap criticism. Most psychology of the period did not either. Research norms evolve. The correct historical question is what conclusions the design at the time could support, not whether researchers violated standards that barely existed.

The 1989 SRI review is valuable precisely because it complicates the myth. Its authors were sympathetic to the existence of psychoenergetic effects, yet they did not claim quantitative support for training. This is not what a simple propaganda record looks like.

It is what a difficult research program looks like when one hoped-for result refuses to become robust.

Training also intersected with individual differences. Program lore emphasized “star” viewers. If a small fraction of people have unusually high ability, as some internal reports suggested, selection may dominate training. That creates an operational strategy: search for talent rather than manufacture it.

But talent selection needs a stable test.

If the selection task itself is noisy, regression to the mean becomes a trap. Choose people after an unusually good run and many will perform worse later even if nothing changed. The program may interpret the decline as fatigue, poor target selection, emotional interference, interviewer mismatch or loss of focus. All of those may be possible. Regression to the mean is certain.

A serious talent program has to beat it.

The military context adds another practical limit. A tiny cadre of unusually gifted people can be useful if the capability is extraordinary enough. Codebreakers, linguists and specialized pilots are not interchangeable either. But their skills can be demonstrated against objective tasks. If a viewer's output requires a sympathetic expert to recognize a hit, the talent cannot be scaled safely.

This is where the terminology “viewer” may have quietly harmed the field.

It suggests a sensory analogy. We know what vision does. We can measure acuity, field of view, contrast sensitivity and color discrimination. A person can usually tell when the lights are off. Remote viewing, by contrast, was described by practitioners as weak, fragmentary and mixed with internally generated material. The viewer often could not distinguish signal from imagination at the moment it appeared.

That is a terrible property for intelligence and an interesting property for consciousness research.

The two ambitions kept colliding.

A consciousness researcher can spend twenty years studying a fragile anomaly because fragility itself may reveal something. An intelligence service wants fragility reduced. It wants a procedure that turns uncertain humans into dependable collection assets.

The training enterprise was the attempt to perform that conversion.

Its partial success may have been real in the ordinary sense: better discipline, cleaner sessions, a shared vocabulary, improved documentation. Its paranormal success remained contested. Its operational success remained bounded by the same old problem.

A trained viewer could produce a more elegant page.

The user still had to decide which marks on that page belonged to the world.
