# Hyman

Ray Hyman had spent years studying the places where extraordinary evidence can acquire ordinary explanations.

He was therefore easy to cast as the designated skeptic in the 1995 review. That label is accurate and inadequate. Hyman's useful role was methodological: he asked whether the experimental record justified the causal interpretation that proponents wanted to place on it.

The distinction is subtle enough to disappear in argument.

A dataset can contain statistically unusual results while the explanation remains unsettled. Hyman did not need to prove that every positive session was produced by cueing or every experiment contained the same flaw. He needed to show that the inferential chain from statistical departure to paranormal information transfer was not yet secure.

His concerns included the familiar problems of parapsychology: methodological heterogeneity, the history of flaws in earlier work, possible unrecognized dependencies, the difficulty of treating work from a closely connected research lineage as fully independent replication, and the absence of an accepted mechanism that could generate useful boundary predictions.

The last concern is sometimes caricatured.

A phenomenon does not need a known mechanism to be real. Continental drift was argued before plate tectonics supplied the mechanism. Physicians used anesthetics before understanding every molecular detail. Demanding complete theory before accepting observation would freeze science.

But a missing mechanism still has costs.

Mechanisms tell researchers where effects should stop. They create failure conditions. They help distinguish a surprising signal from a protocol artifact. If remote viewing works regardless of distance, ordinary shielding and perhaps time order, then the usual physical variables provide little leverage for testing it. That may be a property of nature. It also means the experimentalist has fewer independent constraints.

Hyman's skepticism therefore belongs beside Utts's statistics rather than underneath them.

The two reviewers disagreed about whether the evidence already justified the conclusion of anomalous cognition. They were closer on another point: the research database and the operational program were different questions.

This matters because public retellings often turn their reports into a boxing match. Utts says yes. Hyman says no. Choose your corner.

The actual lesson is more productive.

Utts asks whether the residual statistical structure is too stable to call chance. Hyman asks whether ordinary methodological explanations have been excluded strongly enough to assign that residual to psi. Those are sequential questions. A serious experiment should be designed to make both reviewers uncomfortable for the same reason: the result survives the controls they each insisted upon.

That is what independent replication is for.

Independent does not merely mean a different person presses the button. The new team should not inherit hidden analytic choices from the old team. Target generation, randomization, transcript handling, judging, exclusion rules, stopping rules and statistical models should be specified so another laboratory can reproduce the logic without reproducing the social ecology.

Remote-viewing research had replications in the broad sense. It also had decades of dispute over whether they were independent and comparable enough to close the question.

Hyman's position points toward a deeper problem in anomalous science: methodological repair can leave historical evidence permanently mixed.

Suppose an early experiment contains a cue. Researchers fix the cue. A later experiment remains positive. Critics find another concern. Researchers fix that too. Another positive result appears. At no point does one flaw explain the entire literature, but neither does the literature become equivalent to one pristine preregistered experiment. Evidence accumulates with scars.

How should it be weighted?

The answer cannot be “all studies count equally.” It also cannot be “one historical flaw poisons everything forever.”

A better approach is generational. Separate experiments by protocol quality. Identify which ordinary explanations each generation excludes. Give the cleanest prospective studies the greatest evidentiary weight. Require independent teams to reproduce the effect under the strongest version of the design. Treat meta-analysis as a summary of a defined literature, not as an alchemical process that turns heterogeneous studies into certainty.

This is especially important in a field with publication incentives.

Positive anomalous results are interesting. Null results are dull. A small field can accumulate a distorted public record even without anyone behaving badly. Selective publication, selective analysis and selective continuation of promising paradigms can shift an aggregate effect upward. Modern positive syntheses of remote viewing have argued that publication bias does not explain their results; modern negative syntheses in adjacent psi paradigms have found failures of replication. The existence of the argument is itself reason to value registered reports and complete denominators.

Hyman also understood something operational analysts know instinctively: interpretation must be disciplined before feedback.

Once the target is known, a response can be made to fit. This is why blind judging was central to the laboratory protocol and why operational reports remained harder to assess. The open world supplies unlimited decoys after the fact.

A skeptical methodology is therefore not a social attitude toward strange claims.

It is an architecture for preventing the answer from leaking backward into the evidence.

That principle applies whether the claim concerns telepathy, a cancer biomarker, a trading strategy or a machine-learning benchmark. Lock the rules. Protect the holdout. Count the failures. Publish the denominator. Make the person deciding whether a result matches the target ignorant of which target is correct.

Remote viewing became controversial enough that these ordinary protections acquired philosophical weight.

They should not have.

If the phenomenon is real, strong controls are its ally.

A genuine anomalous channel should benefit from the removal of cueing, retrospective matching and analytic flexibility because every ordinary explanation eliminated increases the value of the residual. If an effect disappears when the protocol becomes more adversarial, that disappearance is information too.

Hyman's skepticism is therefore best read as a design brief.

Do not ask critics to explain a result after the experiment. Invite them to help build the experiment before it begins.

Do not ask proponents to defend a historical anecdote. Ask them to state in advance what should happen.

Do not let a null result be rescued by a new scoring rule invented afterward. Do not let a positive result become proof of a mechanism the experiment never tested.

These are not anti-psi rules.

They are the conditions under which psi, if present, becomes difficult to deny.

The 1995 review did not provide that final experiment. It inherited the archive it was given.

Hyman's contribution was to resist treating the archive's unresolved residue as a completed explanation.

Utts made it hard to say nothing unusual had happened.

Hyman made it hard to say we knew what had.
