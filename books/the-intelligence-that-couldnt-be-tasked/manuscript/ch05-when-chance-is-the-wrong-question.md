# When Chance Is the Wrong Question

“Better than chance” sounds like a verdict.

It is not.

It is a comparison.

If a remote-viewing experiment produces results that would be unlikely under a specified random model, something has happened that the model did not expect. That something might be anomalous information transfer. It might be a flaw in randomization, a judging artifact, data dependence, optional stopping, selective reporting, a hidden cue, a statistical mistake, or a combination nobody has identified yet. Statistical significance does not name the cause of its own existence.

Parapsychology has spent a century colliding with this fact.

The field's positive evidence often lives in aggregates. Effects are small. Trials accumulate. Meta-analyses combine studies. The signal, if signal is the right word, may become clearer only when many observations are stacked. That is perfectly respectable in science. Plenty of real effects are small and probabilistic.

Intelligence operations have a different geometry.

The analyst does not get an ensemble of alternate hostage crises. There is one.

This is the point at which debates over remote viewing become needlessly binary. A supporter may show a statistically significant experimental series and treat operational skepticism as denial of data. A skeptic may show that an operational session was vague or wrong and treat laboratory statistics as irrelevant. Both can be describing their own level correctly.

The question is how the levels connect.

Suppose a laboratory protocol yields an effect size modestly above chance across hundreds of trials. The result might be scientifically important if the design is clean and independently replicable. Now suppose the operational use case requires locating a person among thousands of plausible locations. A small advantage in a constrained ranking task may contribute almost nothing to the open-world problem.

This is not special pleading against psi. It is the ordinary mathematics of base rates and decision thresholds.

A medical test can have high sensitivity and specificity and still produce mostly false alarms when screening for a rare condition. A fraud detector can outperform chance and still swamp investigators if it flags too many legitimate transactions. A missile warning system can be statistically impressive and strategically catastrophic if its false-positive behavior is poorly understood.

The relevant metric depends on the decision.

Remote viewing rarely had one clean metric across research and operations. Laboratory researchers could score ranks or binary choices. Operational users often received narrative descriptions. Those descriptions might later be rated for accuracy and value, but the rating itself depended on the user's knowledge and task.

The distinction between accuracy and value is crucial.

A report can be accurate and useless. “There is a large building” may be true at a target and add nothing. “The person is in the northeast room on the second floor” might be valuable if correct and harmful if wrong. Information theory, in the colloquial sense rather than the formal one, tempts us to treat any correspondence as useful. Intelligence does not have that luxury.

Useful information must reduce the uncertainty that matters.

Imagine a viewer describing a naval facility. The transcript contains twenty impressions: cold, gray, metal, water, movement, vertical structure, open area, mechanical noise, circular form, underground space, heat, people, fencing, a road, pressure, a tower, darkness, a long object, wind and a sense of isolation. If the target is a submarine base, the reader can find matches. If the target is an oil terminal, the reader can find matches. If the target is a hydroelectric plant, the reader can find matches.

The transcript may still contain signal. The question is how much it narrows.

This is why free-response psi research produces unusual statistical arguments. The experimenter needs to convert semantic resemblance into something countable. Ranking against a fixed target pool is one answer. Independent judges are another. Formal similarity scoring can help. Each method buys quantification by imposing structure after a response that was originally unstructured.

Operational intelligence cannot always impose that structure.

A commander cannot ask whether the viewer's transcript matches photograph A, B, C or D if the whole point is that nobody knows where the target is.

This gap is not merely theoretical. The 1995 American Institutes for Research review made operational utility a separate component from laboratory research. That separation was one of the most intelligent decisions in the entire history of the program. It prevented a statistical argument from settling an engineering question.

Jessica Utts would later argue that the laboratory evidence was strong enough to conclude that psychic functioning had been demonstrated. Ray Hyman disagreed with that interpretation and emphasized methodological concerns and the need for independent replication. Yet even a reader who grants the strongest version of Utts's statistical case still has to cross another bridge before reaching intelligence value.

How large is the effect for the task at hand?

How variable is it across viewers?

How variable is it within the same viewer?

Can the viewer say when a session is good?

Can an analyst identify the accurate parts before feedback?

Does tasking change performance?

Can the output beat or complement existing collection methods?

What does an error cost?

These questions sound pedestrian next to “Can consciousness transcend space?” Pedestrian questions decide whether someone gets a budget line.

The intelligence world is full of sources that are not perfectly reliable. Human assets misremember. Defectors exaggerate. Radar returns contain clutter. Imagery analysts disagree. Signals are intercepted without context. The existence of noise does not disqualify a source.

But every mature intelligence discipline develops an error culture.

A source gets reliability ratings. A sensor has known failure modes. A collection platform has geometry and weather constraints. Analysts know what kind of deception an adversary can attempt. Confidence language has institutional meaning. Competing sources can corroborate or contradict one another.

Remote viewing struggled to acquire the equivalent.

Some proponents tried. Viewers used confidence judgments. Training methods attempted to distinguish “analytic overlay” from raw impressions. Researchers looked for predictors of successful sessions. Statistical work investigated target features and individual differences. The 1989 SRI review claimed, among other things, that experienced viewers outperformed general populations and that natural scenes worked better than symbols.

But the central calibration problem remained.

If a viewer is right twenty percent more often than expected in an experimental scoring system, what does “twenty percent” mean when the user receives a page of prose about an unknown facility?

There is no natural conversion.

This is why operational anecdotes carry so much weight in remote-viewing culture. They perform the conversion emotionally. A story about a viewer sketching a crane at a secret Soviet facility, describing a submarine, locating a missing aircraft or identifying a hostage site lets the reader feel what intelligence value would look like. The story supplies the missing bridge from aggregate statistics to concrete use.

The trouble is that stories are sampled differently from failures.

Nobody gives a conference talk titled “the fourteen sessions that produced nothing useful.”

This does not make the famous cases false. It changes the burden of proof. For each celebrated success, the reader needs the contemporaneous tasking, the information available to the viewer and interviewer, the complete unedited response, the target facts known at the time, the set of possible interpretations, the feedback process, and the operational action that followed. Without those pieces, the case may remain intriguing but cannot carry a capability claim.

There is a deeper scientific issue here as well.

A small true effect can be harder to establish than a large false one.

Large claims attract attempts at replication, but they also attract methodological scrutiny. As controls improve, an artifact often collapses. A genuine weak effect may persist but become entangled with publication bias, measurement error and researcher choices. Remote viewing, by the 1990s, existed in exactly this uncomfortable territory: enough positive experimental material for serious statisticians to argue over, not enough consensus for mainstream science to accept a mechanism, and operational records that did not yield a dependable intelligence service.

The temptation is to demand one word: real or unreal.

The archive gives us a more useful vocabulary.

Documented. Suggestive. Disputed. Operationally insufficient. Unexplained. Poorly calibrated. Statistically anomalous. Methodologically vulnerable. Occasionally striking. Often vague. Not independently established.

Those phrases are less satisfying than belief.

They are also closer to what the evidence can bear.

Chance is an important opponent in a laboratory.

In intelligence, chance is only one of the things that can kill you.
