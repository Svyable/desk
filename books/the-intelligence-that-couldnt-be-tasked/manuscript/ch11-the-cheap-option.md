# The Cheap Option

The strangest thing about remote viewing may not be that the government funded it.

It may be that the government did not have to believe very strongly to keep funding it.

Compared with satellites, aircraft, submarines, signals stations and weapons programs, a small human-source experiment is cheap. A handful of personnel, some contractors, secure rooms and research grants can survive in the rounding error of an intelligence budget. That matters because low cost changes the logic of uncertainty.

If a program costs ten billion dollars, ambiguity becomes expensive quickly. If it costs a few million spread across years, a sponsor can rationally say: I am not sure this works, but I am not sure enough to stop either.

This is the option-value argument for strange research.

It deserves more respect than it usually gets.

Organizations facing technological surprise need ways to investigate low-probability possibilities without requiring consensus first. Most speculative bets will fail. The portfolio works only if failure is affordable and learning is real. Intelligence agencies, advanced-research organizations and military laboratories all use versions of this logic.

Remote viewing was a natural beneficiary.

The possible upside was enormous. If a person could acquire information from a denied target without physical access, the collection advantages were obvious. Distance would be cheap. Borders would not matter. Conventional counterintelligence might fail. A hostile state could be observed without risking an aircraft or agent.

Even a weak capability might be worth exploring.

The low cost also created a trap: continuation could be easier than resolution.

A program that is cheap enough never has to win a budget fight on its central merits. It can persist through champions, small appropriations, reorganizations and renewed foreign-threat concerns. Each continuation is then misread later as another institutional endorsement.

Twenty years of funding becomes “the government studied this for twenty years, so they must have found something.”

Maybe they did find something. Duration does not tell us what.

The same fallacy appears in reverse. Critics point to the small scale and say the program could not have been important. But a low-cost research effort can be strategically important precisely because it is exploratory. Size measures commitment, not truth.

To understand STAR GATE's longevity, we need to separate three reasons to continue.

The first is scientific evidence. Researchers believed some controlled experiments produced results difficult to explain by chance. Internal summaries sometimes stated this very strongly. The 1989 review of fifteen years of SRI work reported tens of thousands of trials and astronomical odds against simple statistical fluctuation under its analysis. This gave proponents a quantitative case that could not be dismissed merely by pointing at a silly anecdote.

The second is operational anecdote. Certain sessions impressed users and sponsors. Some viewers acquired reputations for startling correspondences. These stories kept the possibility of practical value alive even when aggregate operational evaluation was weak.

The third is strategic uncertainty. Foreign programs existed. Soviet and Chinese interest was repeatedly cited. If adversaries were studying similar phenomena, stopping could feel like unilateral blindness.

Each reason could support continuation while remaining insufficient alone.

Together they made a resilient political object.

There were also people.

Programs are never only propositions. They are careers, friendships, rivalries, offices, chains of command and patrons who decide that a question deserves one more year. Major General Albert Stubblebine, commander of Army INSCOM in the early 1980s, is often associated with interest in unconventional human capabilities. Other officials in DIA, Congress and contracting organizations played roles in preserving or reshaping the work. The archival record shows debates over transfer, funding categories and whether psychoenergetic collection should remain in particular commands.

It is easy to turn those people into caricatures.

The believer becomes the gullible general. The skeptic becomes the closed-minded bureaucrat. Neither archetype helps.

A senior officer may sponsor an unlikely capability because the cost of missing it seems unacceptable. A budget official may oppose it because opportunity cost still exists even when a program is small. A scientist may defend the work because a statistical anomaly looks genuine. Another scientist may reject the inference because the same database does not satisfy independent replication standards.

All can be acting rationally from different loss functions.

That phrase—loss function—is useful because it strips the romance away.

What error are you most afraid of?

The remote-viewing proponent fears a false negative: a real human capability exists and bureaucracy kills it because the subject sounds embarrassing.

The skeptic fears a false positive: ambiguous statistics and selective anecdotes consume attention, acquire security protection, and become self-sealing because the program is too secret to challenge.

The intelligence sponsor fears adversary surprise.

The operator fears bad leads.

The researcher fears terminating a phenomenon before it is understood.

Those errors are not symmetrical.

A good program design should make them visible rather than letting the loudest sponsor choose implicitly.

The cheapness of remote viewing should therefore have made experimental discipline easier. Because the financial stakes were modest, the government could have required harsh prospective tests. Predefined targets. Independent judges. External replication. Operational scoring agreed before feedback. A standing red team. A termination criterion if calibration failed.

Some of these elements appeared at different times. They did not form a durable governance system.

Instead, evaluation remained episodic.

That pattern is common in small special programs. The very features that protect exploratory work from premature bureaucracy can also protect it from mature accountability. A project starts because normal processes are too conservative. It survives through special handling. Years later, nobody can agree on what evidence would count as failure because the project was never built around a measurable operational contract.

Special-access status can intensify the problem.

CENTER LANE became a Special Access Program within Army INSCOM. Later transfer documents contemplated DIA control and classified handling. Special access can be perfectly legitimate: names, methods, targets and vulnerabilities may require protection. Yet compartmentation narrows the set of people who can see the whole evidentiary picture.

Secrecy creates epistemic debt.

The debt comes due when an external reviewer asks a simple question and discovers that no single dataset was designed to answer it.

Did the program work?

Which program?

In the laboratory or in operations?

For which viewers?

Against which targets?

By which scoring method?

At what time?

Useful for what decision?

The longer a low-cost exploratory effort survives, the more important these definitions become.

Otherwise, persistence itself becomes the result.

This is where remote viewing intersects a broader class of frontier programs, including some that have nothing to do with paranormal claims. Governments investigate unconventional propulsion, unusual materials, biological effects, foreign technical rumors and emerging weapons concepts under uncertainty. The public later sees a declassified project and assumes the government knew more than it said.

Sometimes it did.

Sometimes it was paying to find out.

The documents do not glow differently.

A contract can record ignorance as easily as knowledge.

STAR GATE is useful because the story eventually contains an explicit attempt to cash out the option. In the mid-1990s, as responsibility returned to CIA for review, the agency conditioned continuation on an independent evaluation. The question was no longer whether another interesting session could occur. After more than twenty years, what had the portfolio bought?

This is the right endpoint for exploratory research.

An option is valuable because it preserves the right to act later, not because it should remain open forever.

Remote viewing's low cost helped it survive long enough to face that reckoning.

The next question was whether survival had produced a capability—or only a larger archive.
