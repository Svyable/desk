# Raw Intelligence

The most honest phrase in the STAR GATE archive may be the least glamorous one: raw intelligence.

It appears in session reports as a warning. The viewer's impressions have not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation or collation. Interpretation and use are the responsibility of the requestor.

The phrasing sounds procedural because it is borrowed from a real intelligence distinction. Collection is not analysis. A source says something. A sensor records something. An intercept captures something. Before the material becomes an analytic judgment, somebody has to evaluate reliability, compare it with other information, resolve contradictions and decide what it means.

Remote viewing fit naturally into that vocabulary.

Too naturally.

Calling the transcript raw intelligence gave the output a home inside the system before its source reliability had been solved.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that. Intelligence services handle uncertain reporting constantly. A walk-in source with unknown motives does not need to be ignored; the report needs to be graded, corroborated and bounded. The remote-viewing unit could have been treated the same way: one collection source with unusual provenance and low initial confidence.

The difficulty was that ordinary source evaluation depends on histories that remote viewing could not cleanly supply.

A human source can be rated after repeated reporting. Did previous claims prove accurate? Did the source have access? What incentives exist to lie? A radar has test specifications. A satellite camera has resolution limits. A signals collection system has frequencies and geometry. The failure modes may be complicated, but they are legible.

Remote viewing's failure mode was “noise.”

Noise is a useful word until it explains everything.

A vague description? Noise. A wrong analytic label? Analytic overlay. A failed session? Poor conditions. A spectacular hit? Signal. A talented viewer suddenly performing badly? Variability. None of these explanations is necessarily false, but a capability becomes unfalsifiable if every outcome is absorbed by post hoc categories.

The program needed prospective source grading.

Can the viewer assign confidence to an impression? Does high-confidence material prove more accurate than low-confidence material? Are some target features reliably easier? Does the interviewer know when a session is going badly? Can a monitor stop a bad session without also suppressing good ones? Do particular viewers have stable performance signatures?

These are calibration questions, and they are more important operationally than the grand mechanism.

An intelligence officer can use a mysterious source if the source is calibrated. A perfectly understood source with terrible calibration is still dangerous.

The CENTER LANE mission documents show that the Army understood part of this requirement. One 1984 record described the mission as establishing training programs, intelligence collection techniques, and a mechanism for responding to collection requirements so that remote-viewing data could be used quickly and efficiently. The stated philosophy was to see whether responsible Army personnel could replicate results reported by SRI and other laboratories.

That is a more sober mission than the popular phrase “psychic spies” suggests.

The unit was trying to build a collection workflow.

Workflow is where uncertainty gets operationalized.

A request arrives. Someone decides whether remote viewing is appropriate. The target is encoded or cued. One or more viewers conduct sessions. Reports are produced. The reports are passed to the requestor. The requestor compares them with known information or later feedback. Ideally, the results are evaluated and returned so performance can improve.

Every arrow in that workflow can distort evaluation.

If the requestor supplies too much context, cueing risk grows. If the viewer receives too little, the output may be too generic to use. If feedback is delayed, learning suffers. If feedback is rich, later sessions may be contaminated by target-family knowledge. If analysts know which viewer is considered “good,” they may interpret that person's ambiguities more generously. If viewers know the broad class of targets—military facilities, missing persons, technical systems—their base-rate guesses improve without anomalous information.

This is not fraud. It is ecology.

The program existed inside an ecology of classified targets.

That fact makes retrospective analysis particularly hard. The public archive is not a laboratory notebook arranged for our convenience. Some target identities remain redacted or difficult to reconstruct. Some feedback records are missing. Some operational users never provided clear evaluations. Some famous stories come from later accounts that may preserve the essence of an event while losing the audit trail.

An intelligence service can function with such mess. A scientific adjudication cannot.

The “raw intelligence” framing therefore did two things at once. It was appropriately modest about what the viewer produced, and it deferred the hardest validation step downstream.

The transcript did not have to be right in itself. It had to become useful after analysis.

But once analysis enters the chain, the remote-viewing phenomenon can no longer be evaluated by looking only at the transcript.

Suppose a viewer says “large open area, concrete, rail-like lines, vertical metallic object.” An analyst working a missile target interprets this as a launch site. Another analyst working a shipyard sees a gantry. A third sees an airfield. If one interpretation later proves correct, where did the information reside? In the viewer's descriptors? In the analyst's domain knowledge? In the target cue? In chance?

The answer can be “some of each.” That answer is operationally tolerable and scientifically frustrating.

Modern machine-learning systems create a useful analogy, though the mechanisms are entirely different. A model can produce a score or embedding that improves downstream classification without any one internal feature mapping cleanly onto the final decision. Evaluation is then end-to-end: does the system improve performance on held-out cases? Remote viewing needed the same kind of end-to-end test.

Not “did the viewer say true things?”

Did the remote-viewing pipeline cause analysts to make better decisions than they would have made without it?

That experiment is harder because intelligence decisions are not easily replayed. Analysts learn. Targets differ. Ground truth may arrive much later. Ethical and security constraints prevent controlled operational trials. Still, the program could track narrower outcomes: whether remote-viewing reports generated unique leads, how many leads were checked, how many proved correct, how much collection was saved or wasted, and whether users would request the service again if costs were explicit.

By the 1990s, the government finally tried to gather some of this evidence systematically.

The attempt arrived late.

That delay is understandable. Programs often measure what is easiest first. Remote viewing's laboratory metrics were easier than its operational value. A rank against a target set produces a number. “Did this help the intelligence community?” produces interviews, politics and arguments about counterfactuals.

Yet the counterfactual is the only thing that matters for utility.

If analysts would have reached the same conclusion anyway, the remote-viewing report added no value. If it caused them to investigate a correct hypothesis sooner, it did. If it sent them down a false path, it imposed a cost even if parts of the transcript were accurate.

Raw intelligence is therefore not a compliment.

It is a promise that more work must happen before belief.

STAR GATE spent years producing raw material while the standards for that next step remained unsettled.

The eventual external review would discover that users could sometimes see accuracy in the reports without seeing value.

The archive had warned them from the beginning.
