# Utts

Jessica Utts was given an unenviable assignment: look at a field saturated with argument and decide what the statistics could actually support.

By 1995, remote viewing no longer suffered from a lack of data. It suffered from too much history. There were early SRI experiments, later work conducted under changing protocols, operational reports, internal reviews, critiques, replies to critiques and newer experiments at Science Applications International Corporation. Some of the older work had known methodological vulnerabilities. Some later work had been designed with those criticisms in mind. The entire record was difficult to treat as one clean experiment because it was not one clean experiment.

Utts's role in the AIR review was therefore narrower than the folklore that grew around it.

She was not asked to certify psychic spies.

She was asked to assess experimental evidence.

Her conclusion was strongly positive. Utts argued that the statistical evidence in the later research was convincing enough that chance alone was not a satisfactory explanation for the observed results. She regarded anomalous cognition as having been demonstrated to the standard she thought appropriate for experimental science. She also argued that the research had progressed beyond the point where simply repeating the same basic demonstration was the most useful scientific task.

That conclusion is important because it came from a professional statistician working inside an evaluation commissioned to decide the future of a government program.

It is also frequently asked to carry more weight than it can bear.

Statistical evidence can establish that a dataset is inconsistent with a specified null model. It cannot, by itself, guarantee that the null model captured every ordinary source of structure in the experiment. That is why experimental design and statistics are inseparable. A perfectly computed p-value from a subtly compromised protocol remains perfectly computed.

Utts understood this.

Her argument depended heavily on the claim that the later experiments, especially the SAIC series, had incorporated lessons from earlier criticisms. Randomization and blinding were improved. Targets and judging procedures were better controlled. The later work was meant to reduce precisely the cueing and handling problems that had dogged the first SRI era.

The strongest version of her position deserves to be stated without sarcasm.

If properly blinded experiments repeatedly produce above-chance target correspondence, if the effect appears across multiple experiments and cannot be reduced to one obvious procedural flaw, then “people are imagining patterns” is not yet an explanation. A skeptic has to identify a mechanism capable of generating the observed structure or show that the evidence disappears under genuinely independent, better-controlled replication.

That is a fair demand.

It is also not the same as saying remote viewing was ready for intelligence use.

This distinction gets blurred in retellings because the phrase “psychic functioning has been demonstrated” sounds like the end of the case. Operationally it may be the beginning.

Imagine that the Utts interpretation is correct in the strongest scientifically interesting sense. Under certain controlled conditions, people extract a small amount of information about targets through a process not explained by known sensory channels. The effect is repeatable enough to produce statistical significance across experiments. No agreed physical mechanism exists.

What follows for intelligence?

Surprisingly little, unless more is known.

The effect must have enough bandwidth to distinguish useful target features. It must persist under operational tasking. Viewers must be selectable or trainable. Their performance must be calibrated. Sessions must be scorable before feedback. The useful portions of a response must be identifiable before analysts know the answer. The process must produce information not already available through conventional collection. Its errors must be bounded. Its cost, including false leads, must compare favorably with alternatives.

A statistically anomalous channel could fail every one of those tests.

This is why Utts's position is more interesting when preserved rather than exaggerated. If she was right about the laboratory evidence, the later operational rejection becomes harder to explain away as mere disbelief. The government could accept that something statistically unusual was present and still decide that the phenomenon was not a collection capability.

The CIA's later public description of the review captures this split in unusually plain terms. It says the independent report found enough accurate remote-viewing experiences to defy randomness, while the phenomenon remained too unreliable, inconsistent and sporadic to be useful for intelligence. That summary is not a mechanism claim. It is an operational judgment layered on top of an anomaly claim.

The pairing unsettles both easy camps.

A strict skeptic may want the laboratory anomaly to disappear completely. A committed proponent may want statistical evidence to compel operational acceptance. STAR GATE offers a third possibility: weak evidence of an effect and strong evidence of unusability can coexist.

This possibility matters beyond remote viewing.

Science is full of effects that exist but do not scale into applications. A material can exhibit an exotic property only at temperatures that make devices impractical. A drug can produce a statistically detectable average benefit too small or variable to justify routine use. A forecasting model can beat a baseline across a large dataset while remaining too poorly calibrated for high-stakes individual decisions.

The fact of an effect does not contain its engineering specification.

Utts also forces a question about what counts as replication.

A research lineage can improve itself while remaining socially and methodologically concentrated. SRI and later SAIC work involved recurring investigators, target methods and conceptual assumptions. Proponents can reasonably say continuity allows cumulative expertise and correction. Critics can reasonably say genuinely independent laboratories are needed because unnoticed artifacts can travel with the method.

Both positions are ordinary scientific concerns.

The more controversial the effect, the more valuable independent replication becomes—not because extraordinary claims require a mystical extra burden, but because the space of unnoticed ordinary explanations is large.

The best test of Utts's conclusion would therefore not be another historical argument over the same files. It would be a modern prospective experiment designed jointly by proponents, skeptics, statisticians and information-security specialists, with protocols locked before data collection and the entire denominator published.

That experiment can be specified. We will return to it.

For now, the important point is that Utts did not make the operational problem disappear. She made it sharper.

If there was no anomaly at all, STAR GATE becomes a long lesson in experimental artifact and institutional persistence.

If there was an anomaly, the program becomes stranger: the government may have spent decades standing next to a real weak signal it could not turn into dependable intelligence.

That is not a smaller mystery.
