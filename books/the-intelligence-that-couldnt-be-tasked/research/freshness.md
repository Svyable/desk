## Selected thesis

**The Intelligence That Couldn't Be Tasked: STAR GATE, Remote Viewing, and the Difference Between a Signal and an Intelligence Capability**

The underwritten tension is not “did the CIA prove psychic powers?” It is that three evidence streams can collide without yielding one verdict:

- controlled experiments produced a statistical record serious enough for a professional statistician in the 1995 AIR review to argue that anomalous functioning had been demonstrated;
- methodological critics continued to dispute whether ordinary explanations had been excluded strongly enough to justify a paranormal mechanism;
- the operational record remained too inconsistent and poorly calibrated for CIA to retain remote viewing as an intelligence capability.

The book asks what has to happen between a statistical anomaly and a taskable source. The empirical core is experimental design, blind judging, cue leakage, target-set geometry, military training, open-world search, source calibration, special-access governance, foreign-threat estimates, operational user ratings and the final AIR audit.

## Distinctness test

The closest recent Desk book in institutional form is *Clog the Sky*, because both involve intelligence organizations confronting uncertain anomalous reports. The mechanism and evidentiary core are substantially different. *Clog the Sky* is about mass reporting, air-defense overload, sensor/perception ambiguity and the security value of noise. This book is about controlled anomalous-cognition experiments, statistical inference, human-source calibration and the conversion of a putative weak signal into an operational collection discipline.

The closest recent book in human sensing is *The Compass Under the Skin*. That manuscript concerns established animal behavior with unresolved receptor biology. This one concerns disputed anomalous information acquisition and the much harder standard of prospective operational utility. The empirical bases do not overlap.

## Working reader promise

By the end, the reader should be able to separate five statements that public discussions routinely collapse:

1. a government program existed;
2. a controlled dataset departed from a chance model;
3. an ordinary artifact has or has not been excluded;
4. an individual report contained target-correlated information;
5. the reporting system improved a real decision before feedback.

The book succeeds only if those distinctions make the historical record more, rather than less, interesting.
