# 12 — Repeaters and Apparent Nonrepeaters

The word nonrepeater is a confession disguised as a category.

What it literally means in most catalogs is: this source has not repeated above our threshold, in our band, during the exposure we have accumulated, under the pipeline that was running, while we were looking.

That is a mouthful. Taxonomy prefers a noun.

CHIME changed the field by supplying a large, comparatively uniform sample. Its first catalog reported 536 bursts detected during the initial survey interval, including multiple bursts from known repeating sources. Statistical comparisons suggested differences in temporal width and spectral bandwidth between bursts from repeaters and apparent nonrepeaters.

Those differences might point to distinct physical populations. They might also be partly sculpted by exposure and detection.

Imagine two identical sources. One happens to emit frequently during the telescope’s observing window. It is called a repeater. The second emits rarely or at frequencies outside the band during the same interval. It becomes a one-off. The taxonomy has converted a sampling outcome into an apparent physical distinction.

This does not mean the distinction is meaningless. With enough exposure, injection-recovery simulations, and population modeling, observing bias can itself become measurable. The point is to keep the grammar honest while that work proceeds.

There may indeed be multiple FRB populations. Some events may be produced by engines that repeat for years; others may accompany catastrophic transformations. Even repeating magnetars might have burst-energy distributions and active lifetimes that make most sources appear singular at cosmological distance.

The right question is not whether repeaters and nonrepeaters are “really the same.” It is what observations would force the answer one way or the other.

Host demographics can help. Burst morphology can help. Polarization, rotation measure, repetition-rate distributions, energetics, environment, and multiwavelength counterparts can help. But every discriminator has a selection function.

The telescope is not outside the taxonomy.

It is one of the things making it.
