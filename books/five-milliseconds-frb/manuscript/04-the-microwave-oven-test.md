# 4 — The Microwave Oven Test

The culprit was close enough to heat lunch.

In 2015, Emily Petroff and collaborators reported that Parkes perytons could be generated when a microwave oven door was opened prematurely while the magnetron was shutting down, provided the geometry between oven and telescope was favorable. Out-of-band monitoring around 2.3 to 2.5 gigahertz helped point toward the source. Then came the experimentally satisfying part: they reproduced the phenomenon.

There are few moments in modern astronomy better designed to puncture vanity. A radio telescope capable of hearing cosmological events had also been hearing impatient people open microwave ovens.

The cheap version of this story is comic. The useful version is methodological.

The team did not stop at identifying an appliance. They compared the artificial signal with the FRB population and, crucially, with FRB 010724. The microwave-oven result explained the perytons’ local behavior and timing. It did not reproduce every defining property of the Lorimer burst. The site ovens, under the relevant conditions, could not simply be substituted as the source of the 2001 event.

That distinction is exactly why the discovery strengthened the case for FRBs rather than destroying it.

Before the oven test, “maybe it is local interference” was broad. Afterward, at least one especially troublesome interference class had a mechanism, spectrum, geometry, temporal behavior, and reproduction protocol. A vague alternative had become a specific alternative. Specific alternatives can lose.

This is one of the places where anomalous fields often go wrong. The exotic camp accumulates peculiarities. The skeptical camp accumulates labels: misidentification, artifact, hoax, interference. Neither side forces itself to generate quantitative signatures.

The microwave oven was better science than either posture.

It also demonstrated something uncomfortable about sophisticated instruments: they do not automatically confer sophistication on the data. A billion-dollar detector can be fooled by a cheap emitter if the filtering assumptions are wrong. Better hardware does not abolish context. It creates new contexts worth misunderstanding.

Modern transient astronomy therefore relies on layers of veto and comparison: multiple beams, multiple sites, radio-frequency-interference monitoring, dispersion-law fitting, coincidence searches, polarization, localization, and eventually independent telescopes. No single test is sacred. The strength comes from tests that fail differently.

The peryton episode did not prove a philosophy of science. It did something better. It made one philosophical point concrete enough to plug into a wall socket.

Reality is what remains after the microwave is accounted for.
