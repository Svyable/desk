# 6 — Distance Without a Ruler

Dispersion measure is one of those quantities that becomes dangerous when translated too fluently.

In a radio paper, DM is an integral of free-electron density along the line of sight. In a headline, it becomes distance.

The shortcut often works statistically because a longer extragalactic path generally encounters more ionized matter. But an individual burst’s DM can be partitioned among several reservoirs: the Milky Way disk, the Galactic halo, the intergalactic medium, the host galaxy, and plasma local to the source. Those terms are not equally known.

Early FRBs were therefore distant partly by subtraction. Estimate the Milky Way contribution. Observe a much larger total. Assign the excess to matter beyond the Galaxy. Use cosmological electron-density expectations to infer a rough redshift scale.

This was an excellent argument. It was not a redshift measurement.

Direct host localization changed the epistemic quality of the distance. Once an FRB could be pinned to a galaxy and the galaxy’s redshift measured spectroscopically, DM could be turned around. Instead of using electrons to guess distance, astronomers could use known distance to study the electrons.

That reversal is one of the great conceptual moves in the FRB story.

A mystery observable became a probe.

The same burst that complicated source physics could inventory otherwise difficult-to-see ionized matter. In 2020, Macquart and collaborators used localized FRBs to constrain the cosmic baryon content along their sight lines. The result did not require the microscopic emission process to be settled. It required the pulse to be bright, brief, extragalactic, localized, and dispersed in a measurable way.

There is a lesson here about what “understanding” means in science. We often imagine understanding as a pyramid with mechanism at the top. Until the source mechanism is known, everything below feels provisional. FRBs violate that intuition. Propagation physics can be mature while source physics remains open. Cosmological use can proceed while the engine debate continues.

Nature does not require our explanatory layers to mature in order.

The book will return to that fact at the end. For now it is enough to keep one sentence clean: dispersion measure is not distance.

It is electron column density.

Distance is what we can sometimes wring from it, once other assumptions enter the room.
