# 2 — Five Milliseconds

Five milliseconds is too short to narrate. Human reaction time is a geological era by comparison. A radio telescope does not experience such an event as a flash in the ordinary sense. It experiences voltages, channels, clocks, beams, and a shape distributed over frequency.

That shape was the first argument for the sky.

Cold plasma makes a pulse late in a particular way. Lower radio frequencies travel with a slightly lower group velocity than higher ones, producing a delay proportional to the integrated column of free electrons—the dispersion measure—and approximately to frequency to the minus second power. Pulsar astronomers know this signature intimately. It is not merely a slanted line in a waterfall plot. The curvature carries physics.

FRB 010724 looked like a pulse that had crossed a great deal of ionized material. The excess dispersion over the modeled Galactic contribution made an extragalactic path attractive. But the inference had several layers that popular accounts flatten into one. The telescope measured arrival times. The cold-plasma relation described the sweep. A Galactic electron model estimated how much dispersion the Milky Way should contribute. The remainder was then available to be assigned to halo, intergalactic medium, host galaxy, and local source environment.

Distance entered through a model stack.

That does not make the distance inference weak. It makes it conditional. Science becomes brittle when a conditional inference is retold as a directly observed property.

Brightness created a second problem. A millisecond source cannot be arbitrarily large without smearing its own variability; light-crossing arguments force compactness unless relativistic geometry changes the accounting. Put a very bright radio flash at a cosmological distance and the implied brightness temperature becomes absurd by ordinary incoherent-emission standards. Something coherent is required. Charged particles must, in some effective sense, radiate with phase organization rather than independently.

This is where the source problem becomes genuinely difficult. Compact astrophysical objects can generate brutal magnetic fields, relativistic plasma, shocks, reconnection, pair cascades, and maser-like processes. They can produce conditions that terrestrial intuition is not built for. But “neutron star” is not a mechanism any more than “engine” explains combustion.

The beam pattern mattered too. Parkes used a multibeam receiver. A distant point source and a nearby interference source illuminate those beams differently. The Lorimer event’s appearance in the receiver became one piece of the case against ordinary local interference. Later, perytons would make this geometry central.

Five milliseconds therefore contained several questions at once: where did the pulse originate; what path did it cross; what emitted it; what made the emission coherent; what was the intrinsic duration; and what did the instrument itself do to the recorded shape?

The temptation was to bundle them. If the dispersion looked astrophysical, then the source must be distant; if distant, the energy must be enormous; if enormous, the source must be catastrophic; if catastrophic, it should not repeat.

Each arrow was plausible. None was logically compulsory.

FRB science would advance by cutting those arrows apart.

One of the pleasures of the field is that the variables have different allegiances. Dispersion mostly tells you about electrons along a path. Rotation measure tells you about magnetized plasma. Scattering tells you about inhomogeneities. Burst morphology tells you something about emission and propagation entangled together. Localization tells you where to look. Host spectroscopy tells you a redshift. Repetition tells you the source survived—or that at least the location can produce another event.

The event is only milliseconds long. The evidence is not.

That asymmetry is the book’s real subject. A vanishingly brief signal can accumulate a long afterlife of tests. If it survives them, its reality no longer depends on its original strangeness.
