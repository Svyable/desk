# 8 — Pinning a Millisecond to a Galaxy

Single-dish radio telescopes are magnificent listeners and blunt pointers.

An FRB can be detected with enormous signal-to-noise and still be localized to a patch of sky containing many optical sources. Without a host, distance remains indirect. Without a precise environment, source models remain promiscuous.

FRB 121102 changed that because it repeated often enough to be caught interferometrically. In 2017, Chatterjee and collaborators reported subarcsecond localization using the Very Large Array. The bursts were tied to a faint persistent radio source and a faint optical counterpart. The location was inconsistent with a Galactic source and opened the way to a host-galaxy redshift.

The philosophical fog thinned abruptly.

An extragalactic interpretation no longer rested mainly on excess dispersion. There was a galaxy at the address.

But localization solves less than people think. A host galaxy is not a culprit lineup with one suspect. Galaxies contain star-forming regions, old populations, compact objects, binaries, black holes, nebulae, clusters, and line-of-sight coincidences. Even a subarcsecond radio position can correspond to a physically complicated environment.

Still, the move from statistical distance to an identified host changed everything downstream: energy estimates, host demographics, local environment, star formation, metallicity, persistent radio emission, and the possibility of comparing FRB sites across cosmic history.

This is how transient astronomy becomes forensic astronomy. First detect the gunshot. Then find the room.

The room is not the shooter.
