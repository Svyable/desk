# 7 — The Repeater Breaks the Story

FRB 121102 did something rude to elegant theories. It came back.

In 2016, Laura Spitler and collaborators reported multiple bursts from the same sky location. The existence of a repeating fast radio burst immediately ruled out any universal explanation in which every burst requires an event that destroys the emitter.

Not every theory died. A catastrophic event might still produce some FRBs. The crucial word was universal.

This is why one good counterexample can be worth more than a hundred compatible observations. Before repetition, a merger or terminal collapse could be made to fit a bright, brief, extragalactic pulse. After repetition, those mechanisms could no longer explain the whole class unless the apparent class itself contained multiple populations.

FRB 121102 also refused the tidy clockwork that the word repeater suggests. Bursts clustered. Spectra varied. Arrival times did not reveal a simple pulse train. The source was active, but not obligingly periodic at the timescales first examined.

Repetition opened a practical door as well. A source that speaks again can be scheduled against. Telescopes can wait. Interferometers can correlate voltages during a burst. Optical facilities can examine a much smaller patch of sky. The same physical property that broke models made localization easier.

There is a broader scientific pattern here. An anomaly becomes tractable when it repeats without becoming boring.

If every event were identical, one might suspect an instrument artifact. If every event were unique, population inference would remain weak. FRB 121102 gave astronomy a persistent address with variable messages.

That address would matter more than almost any individual burst property.
