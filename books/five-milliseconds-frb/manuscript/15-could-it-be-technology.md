# 15 — Could It Be Technology?

Any sufficiently strange radio signal eventually acquires an engineer.

Fast radio bursts have inspired artificial-source proposals: propulsion beams, beacons, directed energy, communication schemes. The attraction is obvious. The signals are narrow in time, cosmological in reach, extraordinarily luminous in radio, and in some cases repeating. If one begins with the question “what would an advanced civilization look like from across a galaxy?” a millisecond flash is irresistible material.

The problem is not that the idea is forbidden. The problem is evidentiary asymmetry.

Astrophysics already contains compact objects with stupendous magnetic fields, relativistic particles, enormous energy reservoirs, and observed bursting behavior. In 2020 a Galactic magnetar produced an FRB-like radio event. Any artificial hypothesis now has to beat a natural source class that is not merely conceivable but observed doing something relevant.

That does not make technology impossible. It raises the price of admission.

What would an engineered FRB predict that a magnetar model does not? Information-rich modulation that survives propagation? Frequency behavior optimized for receiver detectability rather than plasma physics? Repetition schedules with low algorithmic complexity and no plausible rotational/orbital/activity-window explanation? Spatial association with environments where technological life is independently plausible? Energetics or beaming geometry that become more economical under engineering than under compact-object physics?

Even those signals would require caution. Pulsars looked engineered enough to earn “LGM” jokes before neutron-star rotation supplied the mechanism. Nature produces clocks, chirps, narrowband masers, coherent emission, and structures that look designed when viewed through an impoverished model.

The correct standard is not “we cannot imagine a natural source.” That is an autobiography of the imaginer.

An artificial-source hypothesis becomes scientifically interesting when it carries risky predictions that natural models do not cheaply absorb.

FRBs have not yet forced that move.

This chapter belongs in the book because exotic possibilities should not be protected by taboo or inflated by romance. They should be made expensive in exactly the same currency as every other hypothesis: discriminating observation.
