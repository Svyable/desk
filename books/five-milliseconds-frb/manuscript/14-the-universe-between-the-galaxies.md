# 14 — The Universe Between the Galaxies

By the time a fast radio burst reaches Earth, the universe has written on it.

The pulse begins at a compact source and then crosses whatever lies between: local plasma, host interstellar medium, circumgalactic gas, the intergalactic medium, other halos, our own halo, our own Galaxy, and finally the Earth’s radio environment. Each stage can leave structure.

Dispersion is the simplest signature to describe. Free electrons delay lower radio frequencies more than higher ones. If the source redshift is independently known, the excess electron column becomes a probe of diffuse ionized matter that is otherwise frustratingly hard to inventory.

In 2020, Macquart and collaborators used a sample of localized FRBs to measure a relation between dispersion and redshift consistent with the expected cosmic baryon inventory. The result was celebrated as locating the universe’s “missing” normal matter—not missing in the cosmological equations, but difficult to observe directly because much of it resides in diffuse ionized gas.

This is a remarkable inversion.

The same dispersion excess that originally made FRBs suspiciously distant became, once distance was measured another way, a ruler for invisible matter.

The method is not magic. Host contributions must be modeled. Galactic halo estimates matter. Sight lines fluctuate because matter is clumpy. A small sample can be dominated by cosmic variance. But those are statistical problems, not a collapse of the method.

This is where the FRB story escapes the genre of mystery entirely. The bursts no longer need to be interesting because their engines are unknown. They are useful because their propagation is informative.

A phenomenon has crossed a threshold when researchers can exploit one part of it while remaining uncertain about another.

We know less about how the flash begins than about what the flash encounters on the way here.

That is not a contradiction. It is division of labor between physical layers.
