# 3 — Perytons

The creature was named before anyone knew what it was.

A peryton, in Borges, is a fabulous animal: deer in front, bird in shadow. Radio astronomers adopted the name for signals that carried the right silhouette and the wrong body. They were transient. They were swept in frequency. They could be made to look disturbingly like a pulse dispersed by plasma. And they were terrestrial.

This was not a side story. It was the kind of embarrassment that can kill a young phenomenon.

After the Lorimer burst, the field needed additional events. Instead it encountered signals that looked close enough to contaminate confidence. Some perytons clustered around dispersion measures not wildly different from the first burst. Their occurrence patterns were suspicious. They were detected over a wide telescope field rather than behaving like clean far-field point sources. They appeared disproportionately during office hours and weekdays.

The universe, famously, does not keep office hours.

But that joke is only useful after the pattern is established. Before then the researcher has a harder problem. Interference can be intermittent. Observatories are complicated radio environments. A terrestrial source can acquire frequency structure through electronics, propagation, chirped transmission, or instrumental response. “It looks dispersed” is not enough.

The presence of perytons created two bad intellectual options. One was to protect the exciting interpretation: FRBs are cosmic, perytons are something else, move along. The other was to collapse the categories: here are terrestrial signals that imitate dispersion, therefore the first burst was probably an artifact too.

The correct response was slower. Characterize the impostor.

That phrase deserves more respect than it gets. In anomaly research, skeptics are often imagined as people who explain things away. But a useful mundane explanation has to do technical work. It must generate the signal under relevant conditions. It should explain timing, spectrum, beam illumination, frequency sweep, recurrence pattern, site dependence, and whatever else made the event anomalous in the first place.

A bad debunking story is structurally identical to a bad exotic story. Both name a cause without earning the mechanism.

Perytons became valuable because they forced radio astronomers to stop treating “terrestrial interference” as a trash bin. If local contamination could counterfeit some of the signature used to argue for cosmic distance, then interference itself had to become an experimental object.

There is a larger lesson here, but it should not be made mystical. The lesson is not that skepticism always strengthens anomalies. Plenty of anomalies die when better controls arrive. The lesson is that a surviving signal becomes more credible when the near-miss is understood in enough detail to distinguish the two.

This is the part of the FRB history that I find more persuasive than any rhetoric about scientific open-mindedness. Nobody needed to vote on whether to believe the bursts. They needed measurements that made the alternatives separate.

The perytons were not merely noise.

They were the control group that wandered onto the telescope by accident.
