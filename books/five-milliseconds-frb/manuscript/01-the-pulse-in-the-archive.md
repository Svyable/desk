# 1 — The Pulse in the Archive

The universe did not announce the fast radio burst with a new telescope, a press conference, or an alarm in a control room. It hid the thing in old data.

That is already a useful insult to the way discovery stories are usually told. We like the clean instant: a person looks through an eyepiece, a trace jumps on a screen, history divides into before and after. FRB 010724 did not behave so politely. The Parkes multibeam receiver had recorded the pulse on July 24, 2001. Years later, David Narkevic was searching archival survey data for short radio transients under Duncan Lorimer and colleagues when the event acquired a second life.

It was violently brief. Less than five milliseconds in the discovery paper. Bright—about thirty janskys at its peak. And dispersed: lower radio frequencies arrived later than higher ones in the pattern expected when a pulse crosses free electrons. The measured dispersion exceeded the contribution expected from the Milky Way along that line of sight under the electron-density model then in use. The obvious inference was distance. If the excess electrons were mostly between galaxies, the event could be extragalactic.

Obvious is not the same as proved.

A radio telescope is an exquisite machine for detecting the universe and an equally exquisite machine for detecting civilization. Radar, satellites, electronics, vehicles, electrical faults, switching power supplies, microwave links: the terrestrial electromagnetic environment is not background scenery. It is an adversary. The more improbable the astronomical signal, the more earnestly one must entertain the possibility that the telescope has discovered the observatory.

The Lorimer burst also arrived before there was a mature category waiting for it. There was no established population called fast radio bursts against which its properties could be compared. One event can support a physical interpretation. It cannot, by itself, tell you the distribution from which it came.

The 2007 paper handled that uncertainty better than later retellings sometimes do. Its authors argued that the burst properties favored a source beyond the Galaxy or Small Magellanic Cloud. They estimated a distance scale using then-current models and noted that, if such events were common, pulsar surveys might be sitting on a new transient population. But follow-up found no additional bursts from the same direction. That pushed thinking toward singular events—supernova-like or merger-like possibilities—in part because the source had apparently not spoken again.

This is where hindsight becomes dangerous. A field that now has hundreds and then thousands of detections can look backward and turn the first observers into people who merely lacked the answer. They lacked something more important: the denominator.

Was the event common or rare? Was the source repeating on a timescale longer than the follow-up? Did every event have the same dispersion curve? Would another telescope see one? Would a second event arrive in a different beam? Did this instrument have a peculiar failure mode? Was the Milky Way electron model wrong along this line of sight? How much of the dispersion lived near the source rather than in intergalactic space?

A class is a machine for asking those questions. Before the class exists, the first event has to carry all the epistemic weight itself.

I keep coming back to one feature of the story: the data were old. That means the physical event and the scientific event were separated by years. The universe had already done whatever it did. Nothing about the sky changed when the paper appeared. What changed was the status of a trace.

That distinction matters for anomalous research in general. A signal is not born when someone notices it. But a phenomenon, in the scientific sense, often is. It becomes a phenomenon when there are procedures for finding another one, variables worth measuring, rival explanations that can lose, and instruments capable of disagreeing with one another.

The Lorimer burst was not yet there. It was a bright scratch on a hard drive with a persuasive physical story attached.

That was enough to begin.
