# 5 — A Population Appears

One event invites stories. Four more events invite statistics.

In 2013, Dan Thornton and collaborators reported four millisecond radio bursts found in a high-time-resolution survey. They arrived far from the Galactic plane. Their dispersion measures substantially exceeded modeled Milky Way contributions. Taken together, the events supported the existence of a cosmological population.

This changed the intellectual texture of the problem.

The Lorimer burst had been a specimen without a species. With additional detections, astronomers could begin asking about event rates, sky distribution, fluence, dispersion distributions, telescope exposure, and selection effects. Models now had to explain not just one dramatic pulse but a population that might occur thousands of times across the sky per day above relevant thresholds.

Population is a dangerous word because it sounds more direct than it is. What telescopes give us is a detected population. The actual population has been filtered by beam sensitivity, observing frequency, sky coverage, time resolution, scattering, search algorithms, thresholds, RFI cuts, and human choices about what constitutes a candidate.

The field would spend the next decade learning that an FRB catalog is partly a catalog of the universe and partly a portrait of the instrument that made it.

Still, 2013 mattered because independent events began to close the space in which FRB 010724 could be dismissed as a singular Parkes pathology. The signals had the same broad grammar: bright, brief radio emission and large dispersion. The exact bursts differed. That was useful. A genuine astrophysical class should not look like a perfectly cloned software error.

Rates also made the source problem harder. If the sky was producing these events frequently, then models based on extremely rare catastrophes faced arithmetic constraints. If every burst destroyed a neutron star, for instance, the birth and death rates of candidate systems had to keep up. If sources repeated, the bookkeeping changed.

At this stage repetition was not yet the organizing fact it would become. The population could still be imagined largely as one-off violence.

The important shift was more basic: the question moved from “what was that?” to “what kind of universe makes these?”

That is the moment an anomaly begins becoming a field.
