# The Wreckage Economy

*Captured Technology, Foreign Materiel, and the Crash-Retrieval Question*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a lazy argument on both sides of the crash-retrieval question.

One side says governments cannot keep something that large secret. The other says governments kept the Manhattan Project secret, therefore they can hide anything.

Both claims are mostly gestures. They spare us the trouble of looking at how secrecy actually behaves around hardware.

The useful comparison is not a slogan about human nature. It is the history of states obtaining machines they were never supposed to possess.

The United States has secretly acquired Soviet fighters, radars, missile hardware, manuals and other foreign systems. It has rebuilt them, flown them, measured them, fought against them in training, and changed doctrine because of what it learned. The CIA built an entire ship around an attempt to raise part of a Soviet ballistic-missile submarine from the Pacific floor. Some of these operations were hidden for years. Some involved cover stories elaborate enough to become industries of their own. Some produced only partial technical success. Some changed how American pilots survived wars.

That record is useful because it gives us something the modern UAP debate almost never has: a control group.

A real secret exploitation program has anatomy. Material has to come from somewhere. Somebody has to move it. Somebody has to sign for it, even if the signature is disguised. Engineers need instruments. Test pilots need runways. Metallurgists need samples prepared. Security officers need rules. Contractors need money. Broken equipment creates maintenance records. Dangerous equipment creates accidents. Useful findings migrate into tactics and procurement. Useless findings generate their own residue: reports saying the thing could not be made to work.

The object can be hidden. The work around the object is harder.

That does not settle the non-human question. It sharpens it.

David Grusch testified to Congress in July 2023 that he had learned of a multi-decade crash-retrieval and reverse-engineering program and had interviewed people with direct knowledge. Other former officials and witnesses have made related claims. Those statements are evidence that serious allegations exist and that some people with relevant government backgrounds believe them. They are not physical proof of non-human vehicles. That distinction is not an insult to witnesses. It is the basic grammar of evidence.

The Department of Defense's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office reached the opposite public conclusion in its 2024 historical review: it said it found no verifiable evidence that the U.S. government or private industry had possessed or reverse-engineered extraterrestrial technology, and it argued that some alleged program names resolved into misidentified real national-security activities, proposals that never became operational programs, or circular reporting. That report is evidence too. It is not omniscience. An office can fail to find a hidden program that exists; a witness can sincerely report a hidden program that does not.

So this book will not ask which tribe sounds more confident.

It will ask what the wreckage itself would force an institution to become.

The method is simple enough to state and difficult enough to execute. First, reconstruct documented foreign-materiel exploitation at the level of custody, personnel, facilities, testing, contracts, doctrine and eventual documentary residue. Second, identify which signatures are contingent on human technology and which follow from exploitation work in general. Third, compare those signatures with public crash-retrieval claims without promoting analogy into proof. Fourth, state what evidence would materially change the conclusion.

Four classes recur throughout:

**Documented fact** means a claim supported by declassified records, official histories, primary technical reports, archival material or comparable evidence.

**Credible but incomplete reporting or testimony** means an attributable statement or report that matters but does not independently establish the underlying extraordinary claim.

**Disputed allegation** means a claim for which public independent confirmation is absent, contradictory, provenance-poor or actively contested.

**Speculative synthesis** means an inference I am making by connecting evidence streams. It may be useful. It may also be wrong.

The point is not to make mystery respectable by dressing it in bureaucracy. It is to make bureaucracy do evidentiary work.

If the United States recovered a MiG, we know what happened next. If it recovered part of K-129, we know a remarkable amount about what happened next. If a government recovered something built by no human civilization, the question is not merely whether somebody could lock it in a hangar.

The question is what would have to grow around it.
