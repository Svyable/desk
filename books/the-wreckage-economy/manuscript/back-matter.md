# Back Matter

This manuscript uses documented foreign-materiel exploitation as a control group for evaluating modern crash-retrieval claims. That comparison has limits. Soviet aircraft, radars and submarines were built inside human scientific and industrial systems. A genuinely non-human artifact, if one exists, may impose very different exploitation burdens and could remain technically sterile for reasons with no historical analogue.

The comparison is therefore institutional rather than technological. Matter has to be recovered, moved, stored, measured, protected and interpreted. Programs have lifecycles. Contractors have interfaces. Useful knowledge migrates. Failure leaves records. These regularities generate testable expectations without assuming the origin of the object.

No claim in this manuscript that a witness testified to, reported, or believed an extraordinary proposition should be read as independent confirmation of that proposition. In particular, the public testimony of David Grusch, Luis Elizondo and other current or former officials is treated as attributable evidence that claims were made by identifiable people with relevant backgrounds. It is not converted into proof that non-human craft or biologics are in government or contractor custody.

Likewise, the 2024 AARO historical report is treated as an official investigative finding with meaningful evidentiary weight, not as logically infallible proof of nonexistence. Its specific program resolutions, access claims and circular-reporting analysis should be tested against underlying records as those records become available.

The manuscript's strongest current conclusion is deliberately bounded: documented history makes secret retrieval and exploitation of foreign technology institutionally plausible, but it also shows that consequential exploitation programs generate secondary evidence in proportion to their duration, physical workload and technical success. The public non-human legacy-program claim has not yet produced a comparably coupled evidence chain.

That conclusion should change if the evidence changes.

## What would change the book

The book would move substantially toward the legacy-program hypothesis if any of the following became public and independently verifiable: authenticated contemporaneous program records showing custody of anomalous material; multiple independent direct participants whose roles and details converge without common sourcing; material with a secure recovery-to-laboratory chain and reproducible properties strongly inconsistent with terrestrial manufacture or natural origin; inspector-general or congressional findings that relevant programs or records were improperly withheld; or a technically traceable derivative system whose provenance can be connected to recovered material.

The book would move substantially away from the legacy-program hypothesis if source genealogy showed that major witness clusters reduce to a small number of shared stories; named programs continue to resolve into ordinary classified work, proposals or inactive efforts; alleged materials repeatedly fail provenance or terrestrial-alternative tests; and claims of successful reverse engineering remain unaccompanied by authentic technical, industrial or operational traces as older records mature into releasable history.

## Note on lawful sources

All research for this manuscript is based on public, lawfully available material: declassified records, official histories, congressional material, government reports, public testimony, museum histories, archival collections and public reporting. No access to non-public classified material is claimed or implied.

The relevant working files are in `research/`, including the source log, evidentiary matrix, chronology, contradiction audit, thesis-freshness note and publication gate.
