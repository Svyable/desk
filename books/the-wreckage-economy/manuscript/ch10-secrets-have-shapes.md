# Secrets Have Shapes

A secret program is not a point. It has geometry.

Some secrets are narrow and deep. A few people know almost everything. Others are broad and shallow. Thousands know small pieces. Some have a hard center and a fuzzy edge: the core mission is tightly held while support personnel see enough oddities to generate rumors. Some are temporary, protected fiercely for a few years and then declassified. Others become institutions and acquire the sediment of generations.

This geometry matters because “how many people would have to know?” is usually the wrong question.

The better question is: how many people would have to know *what*?

In HAVE DOUGHNUT, a pilot flying the MiG needed detailed knowledge of the aircraft. A person fueling it needed less. A commander receiving tactical lessons could know the conclusions without the acquisition source. A procurement officer elsewhere might receive no knowledge at all. The whole program could therefore involve many participants without giving most of them the secret people later cared about.

Compartmentation is powerful because it separates task knowledge from contextual knowledge.

An alleged crash-retrieval program could do the same. A machinist is asked to cut a sample. A physicist measures emission spectra. A biologist examines tissue under a coded specimen number. A security team moves a container. A contractor builds a chamber to specifications. Very few people need to be told, “this came from a non-human vehicle recovered at a named location.”

This is one reason head-count arguments against secrecy are weak.

But compartmentation has costs.

People make worse decisions when they cannot see dependencies. Teams duplicate work. Engineers miss relevant expertise. Safety risks increase because one group does not know what another group learned. Program managers become bottlenecks. The same security architecture that reduces leakage can reduce progress.

For a reverse-engineering effort, this trade is severe.

Science benefits from criticism, replication and cross-disciplinary contact. Secrecy restricts all three. If the artifact is genuinely unprecedented, the program needs a wider range of expertise than ordinary compartmentation wants to permit. Materials scientists may need condensed-matter physicists. Physicists may need electrical engineers. Engineers may need manufacturing specialists. If biological material is involved, the program expands again.

The organization faces a dilemma: protect origin or increase comprehension.

The more tightly it protects the secret, the more likely it is to produce decades of confused, duplicated, partial work.

This could explain alleged long-term failure without requiring incompetence. It would also generate a characteristic signature: many small technical efforts with poor integration, repeated rediscovery, contradictory internal beliefs, and periodic attempts to reorganize or “read in” new talent.

That is a prediction.

If credible insiders ever produce records, we should expect the internal history of a genuinely compartmented extraordinary program to look less like a monolithic cabal and more like a badly integrated research federation.

We should also expect disputes over access.

Who needs to know enough to solve the problem? Who becomes a security risk? Which outside laboratory can be trusted? Can a Nobel-caliber scientist be recruited without revealing provenance? Can peer review occur inside a secure enclave? How are negative findings adjudicated when nobody outside the compartment can reproduce them?

Secrets have epistemic shapes as well as administrative ones.

A small, stable program may preserve provenance well but lack expertise. A large distributed program may gain expertise while increasing leakage and interpretive drift. A contractor network may provide capability but complicate oversight. A waived special-access program may reduce ordinary reporting but increase dependence on a small oversight chain.

These trade-offs are not optional. They follow from organization design.

This is why the claim of a perfect secret deserves suspicion even when the existence of a secret does not.

Real secrecy is lumpy.

It leaks in some directions and holds in others. The Soviet Union knew some things about American programs that the American public did not. Journalists learned fragments. Local communities noticed strange aircraft. Congress sometimes knew less than executive-branch insiders; sometimes select committees knew more than line organizations. Foreign allies could be essential acquisition partners while most domestic personnel remained ignorant.

A modern UAP legacy program, if real, would probably be similarly asymmetric.

The public might know rumors before most officials knew facts. A handful of lawmakers might receive allegations without proof. Contractors might know technical fragments. Security personnel might know names without mission. Foreign partners might control parts of the acquisition history. The idea that there must be either total secrecy or total exposure misunderstands how compartments age.

This is also why apparent contradictions among witnesses do not automatically disprove a program.

Two people can occupy different positions in the same secret geometry.

One is told no such program exists. Another works on an unusual materials effort. A third sees a code name in a briefing. A fourth is told the material is foreign adversary technology. A fifth hears privately that this cover story is itself false. Their accounts can conflict even if all are sincere.

But sincerity plus compartmentation cannot be allowed to explain every contradiction after the fact.

That move makes the hypothesis unfalsifiable.

At some point, claims about the same object, facility, program or event should converge on externally checkable details: dates, personnel, organizational homes, transfer points, technical methods, budgets, documents. If every inconsistency is explained as “they were in different compartments,” compartmentation becomes a theology.

A good investigation therefore maps claims spatially.

Who says they had direct access? Who says they were briefed? Who heard from a colleague? Which names repeat? Which facilities repeat? Which claims appeared before they circulated publicly? Which witnesses could plausibly have shared an information source? Which alleged program names correspond to real entities, proposed entities or no authenticated record?

The resulting graph may look more informative than the stories.

Clusters can reveal independent corroboration or circular reporting. A large number of witnesses may reduce to one original source. Conversely, two witnesses with no plausible contact who independently provide the same obscure technical detail may deserve greater attention than twenty people repeating a famous code name.

The shape of secrecy is therefore also the shape of evidence.

This is where the foreign-materiel control group gives us a warning. Programs like HAVE DOUGHNUT and CONSTANT PEG were real, compartmented and consequential. Their eventual record contains identifiable organizational bones.

A decades-long non-human program should have bones too.

They may be buried.

But bones are not the same as atmosphere.
