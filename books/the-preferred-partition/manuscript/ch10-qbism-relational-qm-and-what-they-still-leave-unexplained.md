# QBism, Relational QM, and What They Still Leave Unexplained

Two modern interpretations of quantum mechanics attempt to eliminate the measurement problem by denying that measurements reveal pre-existing facts. QBism—Quantum Bayesianism—treats quantum states as personal degrees of belief. Relational quantum mechanics treats quantum states as descriptions relative to an observer. Both interpretations make the quantum state observer-dependent. Neither requires wavefunction collapse. Neither postulates many worlds. Both claim to dissolve the measurement problem by rejecting the assumption that physical systems have objective states independent of observation.

The interpretations are radical. They challenge the idea that science describes a mind-independent reality. They embrace subjectivity as fundamental. They argue that the quantum formalism is a tool for updating beliefs or describing correlations, not a description of how the world is. These positions are philosophically coherent. They are also incomplete in specific ways that matter for understanding the partition.

QBism, developed primarily by Carlton Caves, Christopher Fuchs, and Rüdiger Schack, treats the quantum state as a personalized state of knowledge. When Alice assigns a quantum state to a system, she is not describing an objective property of the system. She is expressing her own beliefs about what she will experience if she performs a measurement. The beliefs are subjective, but they are constrained by the quantum formalism. The formalism provides rules for how beliefs should be updated when new information is acquired. Measurement is not a physical collapse. It is an update of belief. Alice learns the outcome. She updates her quantum state assignment to reflect her new knowledge. The update is personal. Bob may assign a different state to the same system if Bob has different information. Both assignments are valid. They reflect different states of knowledge, not different objective facts.

This subjectivity avoids the measurement problem because there is no objective wavefunction to collapse. Alice's state assignment changes when Alice learns something. Bob's state assignment does not change until Bob learns something. The two assignments can disagree without contradiction because they are personal, not objective. There is no fact of the matter about what state the system is in. There are only facts about what each observer believes and what each observer will experience. The experiences are objective—Alice really does see the outcome she sees—but the quantum state is not. The state is a tool for predicting experience, not a description of reality.

The interpretation is logically consistent. It is also difficult to apply in practice. If quantum states are personal, what grounds the agreement among observers? Alice and Bob perform the same experiment and obtain consistent results. QBism says the consistency is explained by the fact that Alice and Bob share common experiences and use a common formalism. The formalism is normative—it tells you how to update beliefs rationally—but it does not describe an external reality. The agreement is pragmatic rather than ontological. Alice and Bob agree because they follow the same rules, not because they are both measuring the same objective state.

But the rules are not arbitrary. They are the rules of quantum mechanics. The rules are extraordinarily successful. They make accurate predictions across every tested domain. Why do these rules work? QBism says the rules are the correct way to manage uncertainty in the presence of quantum correlations. The correctness is pragmatic. The rules work because using them leads to reliable expectations. But reliable expectations about what? QBism must answer: reliable expectations about personal experiences. The experiences are grounded in something. QBism is vague about what that something is. It cannot be an objective quantum state because QBism denies that objective quantum states exist. It cannot be purely subjective because multiple observers agree. The grounding is left unexplained. The interpretation dissolves the measurement problem by making states subjective, but it does not explain why the subjective states obey the quantum rules rather than some other rules.

The rules include the Born rule. The Born rule gives the probabilities for measurement outcomes. QBism treats the Born rule as a normative principle: it tells you how to assign probabilities to future experiences. But why squared amplitudes? QBism offers no derivation. The rule is a postulate. The postulate is empirically validated. Validation is sufficient for pragmatic purposes. It is not sufficient for explanatory purposes. The Born rule is not explained. It is assumed. The assumption is more palatable in QBism than in realist interpretations because QBism does not claim to describe reality. The rule is just a rule, useful for prediction. But the usefulness is not explained. It is a brute fact about the formalism. Brute facts are acceptable in a pragmatic framework. They are unsatisfying in an explanatory framework. QBism straddles the two frameworks uncomfortably.

Relational quantum mechanics, developed primarily by Carlo Rovelli, takes a different approach. It treats quantum states as relational rather than personal. A system has a state relative to another system. The state is not absolute. It is not personal. It is a description of correlations between systems. Alice assigns a state to a photon relative to Alice. Bob assigns a state to the same photon relative to Bob. The two states are different if Alice and Bob have interacted with the photon differently. The difference is not subjective. It is relational. The states describe different physical relations: the Alice-photon relation and the Bob-photon relation. Both relations are real. They are just different relations. There is no absolute state that both Alice and Bob are trying to describe. There are only relational states. Relationality replaces objectivity.

This move avoids the measurement problem because there is no objective collapse. Alice's measurement establishes a correlation between Alice and the photon. Relative to Alice, the photon has a definite state. Relative to Bob, who has not interacted with the photon, the photon is still in superposition. The two descriptions are compatible because they are relative to different observers. They are not descriptions of the same thing. They are descriptions of different relations. The photon does not have a state in itself. It only has states relative to other systems. Every quantum state is relational. Measurement is the establishment of a correlation. Correlation is physical and objective. The correlation is between two systems. One system is the observer. The observer is a physical system. The observer has no special status. The observer is just another system that can enter into correlations.

The interpretation is elegant. It treats all systems symmetrically. It eliminates the observer-observed distinction. It makes measurement a physical process without requiring collapse. It is also radically relational. It denies that systems have intrinsic states. Every property is relational. This is consistent with the formalism. The formalism describes correlations. It does not require that systems have states independent of their relations to other systems. But the relational view has consequences. If states are always relative, then facts are relative. A photon can be spin-up relative to Alice and in superposition relative to Bob. Both facts are true. They are not contradictory because they are facts about different relations. But they are incompatible in the usual sense of facts. A realist would say the photon is either spin-up or in superposition, and one of the observers is wrong. Relational quantum mechanics says both observers are right. The difference is not in their knowledge. It is in what they are describing. They are describing different relations.

This relativity of facts is philosophically bold. It is also operationally constrained. The facts become absolute when the observers communicate. Alice tells Bob the result. Bob now knows what Alice measured. Relative to Bob, the photon is now in a definite state, the same state Alice measured. The communication establishes a correlation between Bob and Alice. The correlation makes the facts consistent. Before communication, the facts were relative. After communication, they are shared. The sharing is physical. It involves interaction. Interaction is what establishes correlations. Correlations are the only facts. Facts are not absolute. They are correlations. The relationality is preserved. The consistency is explained by interaction.

But interaction is itself relational. Alice and Bob interact relative to some other observer, Carol. Relative to Carol, Alice and Bob may still be in superposition. Carol has not interacted with Alice or Bob. Relative to Carol, there is no fact about what Alice measured. There are only correlations between Alice, Bob, and the photon. The correlations are entangled. Carol describes the entire system with a quantum state. The state is relative to Carol. It is different from the states Alice and Bob assign. All three states are correct relative to their respective observers. None is privileged. The relationality is infinite. It terminates only when every system is included. But then there is no external observer relative to whom the state is defined. The relationality is global. The global state is not defined. There is no view from nowhere. Every description is from somewhere. The somewhere is a physical system. The system is part of the world being described. The description is circular.

The circularity is not vicious. It is a feature. Relational quantum mechanics says the world is a network of interactions. There is no external perspective from which the whole is viewed. Every perspective is internal. Every description is relative. Absolute descriptions do not exist. This is consistent with the quantum formalism. The formalism describes correlations. Correlations are relative. The formalism does not require an absolute perspective. But the formalism is used by physicists. Physicists are systems inside the world. They describe other systems. The descriptions are relative to the physicists. The physicists are not privileged. They are just systems. But they are systems that use the formalism. The formalism is a set of rules. The rules are followed by physicists. The physicists are physical. The rules are implemented in brains. Brains are quantum systems. The rules describe brains. The description is circular again. The circle is not explained. It is accepted. Relational quantum mechanics does not explain why the formalism works. It shows that the formalism is consistent with relationality. Consistency is not explanation.

Both QBism and relational quantum mechanics avoid the measurement problem by denying objective states. The denial is philosophically defensible. It is also empirically untestable. No experiment can distinguish a world where states are subjective or relational from a world where states are objective but unknowable. The interpretations are empirically equivalent to other interpretations. They differ in metaphysics, not physics. The choice among them is a choice of philosophical framework. The framework affects how the theory is understood, but it does not affect the predictions. The predictions come from the formalism. The formalism is neutral on the reality of states.

The neutrality is maintained by the partition. The partition determines what is system and what is environment. It determines what is measured and what is traced over. It determines what is accessible and what is hidden. The partition is explicit in the calculation. It is not explained by the interpretation. QBism says the partition is personal. Alice chooses which variables to measure and which to ignore. The choice is subjective. Relational quantum mechanics says the partition is relational. The system-environment split depends on which observer is describing the system. The dependence is relational. Both interpretations make the partition observer-dependent. Neither explains why the partition is where it is. They explain the consequences of the partition—why states are subjective or relational, why measurements update states, why observers disagree—but they do not explain the partition itself. The partition is a prerequisite for applying the formalism. The interpretations apply the formalism. They do not derive the partition. The partition is chosen. The choice is constrained by the experiment. The experiment is designed by the physicist. The physicist is an observer. The observer is not eliminated by QBism or relational quantum mechanics. The observer is central. The observer makes the partition. The partition determines the description. The description is the quantum state. The state is subjective or relational. But the partition is not. The partition is physical. It is implemented by measurement apparatus, isolation, control, and dissipation. The implementation is objective. It does not depend on whether the physicist is a Bayesian or a relationalist. It depends on the hardware, the Hamiltonian, and the thermodynamic resources available.

QBism and relational quantum mechanics are valuable interpretations. They clarify that quantum states do not need to be objective to be useful. They show that the formalism can be understood without reference to collapse or many worlds. They highlight the role of the observer in defining what is measured. But they do not eliminate the observer. They make the observer fundamental. The observer chooses or defines the partition. The partition is where the physics happens. The partition is not explained by subjectivity or relationality. It is explained by thermodynamics, information theory, and the structure of the measurement process. Those explanations are interpretation-independent. They apply whether the state is subjective, relational, objective, or epistemic. The partition is where the interpretations converge. The partition is the common structure beneath the different metaphysical stories. The partition is physical. It is maintained by observers. Observers do work. The work is measurable. The measurement is interpretation-independent. The partition is where the interpretational disputes end and the physics begins.
