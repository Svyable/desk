# Many-Worlds as Bookkeeping vs Ontology

The many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics claims that all possible measurement outcomes actually occur. The wavefunction never collapses. It continuously branches. Each branch is as real as any other. Observers experience one branch because they are part of the wavefunction. An observer in a given branch cannot access other branches because decoherence has suppressed interference between them. The branches coexist but do not interact.

This interpretation eliminates wavefunction collapse. It preserves unitarity. It treats all observers symmetrically. It requires no modification to the Schrödinger equation and no additional postulates beyond unitary evolution. The interpretation is deterministic—the wavefunction's evolution is completely determined by the Hamiltonian—but individual observer experiences are probabilistic because each observer exists in only one branch and cannot predict which branch that will be.

The interpretation is logically consistent. It is also ontologically extravagant. It asserts that the universe duplicates at every quantum event. A spin measurement creates two universes, one for each outcome. A photon passing through a beam splitter creates two universes. A radioactive decay creates two universes. The duplication is continuous, occurring at every interaction that creates entanglement. The number of branches grows exponentially with time. After $n$ binary measurements, there are $2^n$ branches. After a macroscopic amount of time, the number of branches is unimaginably large. Each branch contains a version of every observer. Most versions of any given observer are in typical branches. Atypical branches exist but are rare in a measure-theoretic sense determined by the branch amplitudes.

The ontology is hard to accept for many physicists. It requires believing in an enormous number of unobservable entities—all the other branches—based solely on the commitment to unitary evolution and the refusal to add a collapse postulate. The trade-off is a matter of taste. Many-worlds advocates argue that removing collapse is worth the cost. Critics argue that postulating a collapse rule is more parsimonious than postulating infinitely many branches.

The debate is often framed ontologically: are the branches real? But there is another way to interpret many-worlds. Treat the branches as bookkeeping. The wavefunction is a computational tool for tracking correlations and calculating probabilities. The branches are the terms in the wavefunction. They are mathematical objects, not physical objects. Unitary evolution is a rule for updating the wavefunction. The rule works because it conserves probability and respects locality. The branches do not need to be real in any sense beyond being terms in an equation. The interpretation is epistemic, not ontic. The wavefunction represents information, not reality.

This epistemic reading is compatible with the formalism. It is also compatible with decoherence. Decoherence explains why branches cease to interfere. It does so without requiring the branches to be real. The branches are computational artifacts. They are useful because they organize the calculation in a way that makes predictions easy to extract. The organization is not arbitrary. It reflects the physical fact that decoherence selects a preferred basis. But the organization is not ontological. It is methodological. The branches are how we describe the system, not what the system is.

The distinction between bookkeeping and ontology is sharpest when considering what counts as a branch. A branch is often defined as a component of the wavefunction corresponding to a definite measurement outcome. But "definite" relative to whom? A measurement outcome is definite from the perspective of the observer who performed the measurement. It is not definite from the perspective of an external observer who has not yet interacted with the measurement apparatus. The external observer treats the entire system—measured system plus apparatus plus first observer—as being in a superposition. The first observer treats their outcome as definite. Both descriptions are valid within the formalism. They correspond to different choices of partition.

The partition determines the branching structure. If the partition includes the first observer in the system, the branching occurs when an external observer later interacts with the system. If the partition places the first observer outside the system, the branching occurs at the first measurement. The two descriptions give the same predictions for all measurable quantities. They are empirically equivalent. They differ only in when and where the branching is said to occur. The "when and where" is partition-dependent. It is not a fact about the world. It is a fact about the description.

This partition dependence is clearest in relative-state formulations of many-worlds. Hugh Everett's original 1957 formulation treated states as relative. A system has a state relative to an observer. The state is not absolute. Different observers can assign different states to the same system. The assignments are consistent in the sense that they are derived from the same total wavefunction by tracing over different degrees of freedom. But they are different assignments. The difference reflects the different partitions used by different observers. The partition determines the relative state. The relative state determines the branching structure. The branching structure is observer-dependent.

Observer dependence is acceptable if the interpretation is epistemic. Different observers can have different information and therefore different descriptions. The descriptions are compatible as long as they make the same predictions when the observers communicate. Communication involves interaction. Interaction causes the observers to become entangled. After entanglement, the observers' states are correlated. The correlation ensures consistency. The consistency is maintained by the formalism. The formalism does not require the branches to be ontologically real. It requires only that they be computationally useful and empirically adequate.

Empirical adequacy is the test. An interpretation is adequate if it reproduces the predictions of quantum mechanics. Many-worlds reproduces the predictions. So do other interpretations. The choice among them cannot be made on empirical grounds. It is a choice of explanatory framework. The framework determines what is treated as real and what is treated as artifact. Many-worlds treats branches as real. Epistemic interpretations treat branches as artifacts. Both frameworks give the same answers to experimental questions. The answers are determined by the quantum formalism, not by the interpretation.

The formalism is neutral on ontology. It does not say whether branches are real or merely calculational. It does not say whether the wavefunction is real or epistemic. It does not say whether observers are special or merely physical systems. The formalism provides a recipe for calculating probabilities. The recipe works. The interpretation explains why the recipe works. Different interpretations give different explanations. The explanations are not empirically testable. They are metaphysical.

Metaphysics is not physics. Physics makes testable predictions. Metaphysics provides the conceptual framework within which predictions are understood. The framework matters for intuition and guidance. It does not matter for observation. Observation is what separates physics from metaphysics. The separation is not absolute. It is contextual. What is metaphysical today might become physical tomorrow if someone finds a way to test it. Testing requires making the question empirical. Empirical means measurable. Many-worlds has not been made fully empirical. The branches are not observable. The lack of observability is by design. The design is to avoid collapse. Avoiding collapse preserves unitarity. Preserving unitarity is the motivation. The motivation is strong. It is not testable.

Testability requires observable differences. Observable differences are what experiments measure. Experiments do not distinguish many-worlds from other interpretations. Non-distinction is the problem. The problem is interpretational underdetermination. Underdetermination means the evidence is insufficient to choose among competing hypotheses. Insufficient evidence is the state of quantum foundations. The state persists because the hypotheses make the same predictions. Same predictions mean empirical equivalence. Empirical equivalence is the outcome for many-worlds. The outcome is unsatisfying for those who want a unique answer. Uniqueness is not available. Availability is determined by the structure of the theory. The theory is quantum mechanics. Quantum mechanics is empirically adequate. Adequacy is all that is required. Requirements beyond adequacy are philosophical. Philosophy is valuable. It is not the same as physics. Physics is what this book is about. The book is about the partition. The partition is where many-worlds meets physics. The meeting is the branching. Branching is decoherence. Decoherence is physical. It is measurable. It is testable. Testing confirms that decoherence occurs. Testing does not confirm that the branches are real. Reality is interpretation. Interpretation is choice. Choice is many-worlds or something else. Something else is the subject of the next chapter. The next chapter examines alternative interpretations that take observers seriously. Taking observers seriously means treating them as part of the physics rather than external to it. External means outside the formalism. Inside the formalism means observers are physical systems. Physical systems obey physical laws. Laws include thermodynamics. Thermodynamics includes dissipation. Dissipation is the cost of observation. Cost is measurable. Measurability is testability. Testability is what makes observer-based interpretations potentially scientific rather than merely philosophical. Potentially scientific means they could be tested if the right experiments are done. The right experiments are those that measure the thermodynamic cost of maintaining partitions. Partitions are what observers maintain. Maintaining partitions is observation. Observation is physics.

Metaphysics is not irrelevant. It provides the intuition that guides physical reasoning. But it is not physics. Physics is the part that makes predictions. The predictions come from the formalism. The formalism can be used without committing to an interpretation. That is how most physicists use quantum mechanics. They apply the rules, calculate the results, compare to experiment, and move on. The interpretation is left implicit. The implicit interpretation is usually Copenhagen-ish: the wavefunction represents knowledge, measurement updates knowledge, and the update corresponds to a physical process that makes one outcome definite. This interpretation is operationally adequate. It is not philosophically satisfying. It is vague about what constitutes a measurement and when the update occurs. But it is pragmatically successful.

Many-worlds is an attempt to make the interpretation philosophically satisfying by eliminating vagueness. It succeeds in eliminating the vagueness around collapse—there is no collapse, so no rule is needed for when it occurs. But it introduces vagueness around the Born rule and the nature of probability. If all branches are real, why should an observer expect to find themselves in a branch with high amplitude? Expectation is a probabilistic concept. It makes sense if only one branch is real and the observer does not know which one. It is less clear if all branches are real and the observer exists in all of them. The many-worlds advocate must explain probability in a deterministic branching universe. Several explanations have been proposed: decision-theoretic, measure-theoretic, self-locating uncertainty. None has achieved consensus. The vagueness has been relocated, not eliminated.

The decision-theoretic approach argues that rational agents in a branching universe should behave as if the Born rule holds. The argument relies on decision theory. Decision theory is the mathematical framework for choosing actions under uncertainty. In the branching universe, there is no uncertainty about what happens—all branches occur. But there is uncertainty about self-location—which branch am I in? The uncertainty is epistemic rather than ontological. Before a measurement, the observer knows they will split into multiple copies. Each copy will find a different outcome. The observer does not know which copy they will be. The ignorance is structural. It is built into the branching process. Given this ignorance, how should the observer assign credences to outcomes? The decision-theoretic answer is that rational credences must satisfy certain consistency conditions. The conditions include additivity, non-negativity, and normalization. Imposing these conditions plus a symmetry assumption—that all observers in branches with equal amplitude should have equal credence—leads to the Born rule. The derivation is not universally accepted. Critics argue that the symmetry assumption is not justified. Why should branches with equal amplitude be treated symmetrically? The amplitude is a mathematical property of the wavefunction. It is not obvious that it should correspond to observer credence. Defenders respond that amplitude is the only relevant property available in the formalism. If credences do not track amplitude, what should they track? The debate continues. The debate is philosophical. It is not settled by experiment. Experiment tests the Born rule. Experiment does not test why the Born rule holds. The why is interpretation. The interpretation is many-worlds. Many-worlds says the Born rule holds because of rational constraints on self-locating beliefs in a branching universe. The rationality is normative. It is not descriptive. It does not explain why observers in fact follow the Born rule. It explains why they should. Should is not the same as do. The gap is unbridged.

The measure-theoretic approach avoids decision theory. It argues that the Born rule follows from the natural measure on branches. A measure assigns weights to branches. The weights determine what counts as typical. Typical branches are those with high measure. Atypical branches are those with low measure. The measure is derived from the amplitudes. The square of the amplitude is the measure. This is the Born rule. The derivation is mathematical. It does not appeal to observers, credences, or rationality. It appeals only to the structure of Hilbert space and the requirement that the measure be invariant under unitary evolution. Invariance is a natural condition. It ensures that the measure does not depend on the choice of time or basis. The condition is sufficient to derive the Born measure. The derivation is elegant. It is also controversial. Critics argue that mathematical elegance does not imply physical correctness. The measure is a property of the wavefunction. It is not clear why observers should care about it. The defender responds that observers are part of the wavefunction. Their experiences are determined by the branch they inhabit. Typical experiences correspond to high-measure branches. Atypical experiences correspond to low-measure branches. Observers should expect typical experiences because most copies of themselves are in typical branches. The expectation is probabilistic. Probability is the measure. The measure is the squared amplitude. The squared amplitude is the Born rule. The loop is closed. The closure is formal. It is not empirical. Empirically, the Born rule is tested by experiment. Formally, it is derived from the measure. The derivation assumes that observers should expect typical experiences. The assumption is plausible. It is not proven. Proving it requires connecting the measure to something observable. The connection is the self-locating uncertainty. The uncertainty links the observer to the branch. The link is the interpretation. The interpretation is many-worlds.

The relocation is not a failure. It is progress. It clarifies what needs explaining. The Born rule needs explaining. Decoherence shows why branches separate. It does not show why the Born rule holds. Many-worlds must derive the Born rule from the branching structure plus some auxiliary assumptions. The assumptions are the subject of ongoing research. The research is conceptual rather than experimental. It is not clear that any experiment could distinguish many-worlds from other interpretations. The distinction is in the metaphysics, not the physics.

That separation is the key point. The branches in many-worlds are either ontological entities or bookkeeping devices. If they are ontological, they are unobservable, and the interpretation requires belief in an enormous unseen reality. If they are bookkeeping, they are useful fictions, and the interpretation is a convenient way to organize calculations. The distinction does not affect the calculations. It affects the story told about the calculations. The story is important for understanding, but it is not part of the predictive content. The predictive content is the quantum formalism. The formalism is interpretation-independent.

That independence is liberating. It allows physicists to choose an interpretation based on explanatory goals rather than empirical fit. For foundational research, many-worlds is attractive because it takes the formalism seriously and follows it to its logical conclusion. For practical work, many-worlds is less attractive because it introduces concepts—branches, measures, self-locating uncertainty—that do not appear in the apparatus or the data. The practical physicist uses whatever interpretation makes the calculation easiest. Often, that is no interpretation at all. The physicist writes down the Hamiltonian, evolves the state, applies the Born rule, and compares to experiment. The intermediate steps do not reference branches or collapse or observers. They reference operators, eigenstates, and probabilities. The language is mathematical. The mathematics is neutral.

The neutrality is possible because the partition is explicit in the calculation. The system is defined. The environment is traced over. The reduced density matrix is computed. The eigenvalues are the probabilities. The procedure does not require knowing whether the branches are real. It requires only specifying the partition. The partition is chosen based on the experiment. The experiment defines what is measured and what is ignored. The measured part is the system. The ignored part is the environment. The partition is the boundary. The boundary is drawn by the experimentalist. The drawing is a physical act. The act determines the calculation. The calculation determines the prediction. The prediction is compared to the measurement. The comparison tests the theory. The theory is the quantum formalism, not the interpretation. The interpretation explains the formalism. The explanation is useful but not necessary. The formalism suffices for prediction.

That sufficiency is why many-worlds can be treated as bookkeeping. The branches are terms in the wavefunction. The wavefunction is a sum over branches. The sum is weighted by amplitudes. The amplitudes determine probabilities via the Born rule. The Born rule is a postulate. The postulate is empirically validated. The validation is interpretation-independent. Many-worlds offers a particular way to think about the postulate. The thinking is helpful for some questions and unhelpful for others. It is not mandatory. The formalism works without it.

The formalism also works with it. That flexibility is the hallmark of an interpretational question. Interpretations are underdetermined by evidence. They are chosen for explanatory or aesthetic reasons. Many-worlds is explanatorily powerful in some contexts—it eliminates collapse and preserves unitarity—and explanatorily weak in others—it multiplies entities and leaves probability unexplained. The strengths and weaknesses are matters of judgment. Reasonable physicists disagree. The disagreement is not about the predictions. The predictions are fixed by the formalism. The disagreement is about what the formalism means. Meaning is interpretational. Interpretation is optional. The partition is not.

The partition is where the physics lives. The partition determines what is system and what is environment. It determines what is accessible and what is hidden. It determines what decoheres and what remains coherent. It determines what is measured and what is traced over. The partition is not bookkeeping. It is not ontology. It is structure. The structure is physical. It is implemented by isolation, control, measurement, and dissipation. It costs energy. It generates entropy. It is maintained by observers. Observers are not outside the formalism. They are part of it. They are physical systems that perform measurements, store information, and dissipate entropy. The dissipation is the thermodynamic signature of the partition. The signature is measurable. It is the part of the many-worlds story that is empirically accessible. The branches are not accessible. The dissipation is. The dissipation is where the partition becomes physics.
