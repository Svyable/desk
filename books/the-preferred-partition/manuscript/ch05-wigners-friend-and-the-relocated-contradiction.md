# Wigner's Friend and the Relocated Contradiction

In 1961, Eugene Wigner proposed a thought experiment that has become one of the sharpest tests of how quantum mechanics treats observers. The setup is simple. A friend of Wigner performs a quantum measurement inside a sealed laboratory. The friend measures the spin of an electron and records the result. Outside the laboratory, Wigner does not observe the result. From Wigner's perspective, the friend, the apparatus, and the electron remain in a superposition until Wigner opens the laboratory and looks.

The contradiction is immediate. The friend experiences a definite outcome. The spin is up or down. The result is recorded. The friend has knowledge. From the friend's perspective, the wavefunction collapsed at the moment of measurement. But from Wigner's perspective, no collapse has occurred. The entire laboratory—including the friend—is in a superposition. Both the friend's claim of a definite result and Wigner's claim of a superposition are valid within their respective frameworks. The frameworks contradict each other.

Decoherence was supposed to eliminate this contradiction. The modern response to Wigner is that the friend becomes entangled with the electron. The friend's brain state correlates with the electron's spin. Decoherence spreads the correlation into environmental degrees of freedom. The friend's brain is macroscopic, warm, and in constant interaction with its surroundings. Decoherence is effectively instantaneous. The friend's reduced density matrix becomes diagonal. The friend's experience is definite. The collapse is apparent, not real. The total state remains in superposition, but the friend cannot access the other branches. Wigner can, in principle, perform an interference measurement on the entire laboratory, but the decoherence time is so short that such a measurement is impossible in practice.

This response is correct about the physics. It does not resolve the contradiction. It relocates it.

The friend experiences a definite outcome immediately. Decoherence explains why the friend cannot observe the superposition. It does not explain why the friend observes one outcome rather than remaining in a superposed state of having observed spin-up and spin-down. The friend's consciousness appears to select a branch. Decoherence suppresses interference between branches. It does not explain why one branch is experienced. The collapse is subjective from Wigner's perspective but objective from the friend's perspective. Decoherence makes the subjective-objective distinction unmeasurable but does not eliminate it.

The distinction is cleanest in the many-worlds interpretation. The friend's measurement causes the wavefunction to branch. One branch contains a friend who observed spin-up. Another branch contains a friend who observed spin-down. Both branches are real. Both friends are real. They cannot communicate because decoherence has suppressed the interference terms. From Wigner's perspective, both branches exist. From each friend's perspective, only one branch is experienced. The branching is real and objective. The experience of a single branch is also real and subjective. There is no contradiction because the friends in different branches are different entities. They share a common past but not a common future.

This resolution is logically consistent. It requires accepting that the friend is duplicated at every measurement, that all branches are equally real, and that subjective experience selects a branch in a sense that is probabilistic but not determined by the wavefunction. The many-worlds advocate says there is no selection—all outcomes occur, and each copy of the friend experiences one outcome. But from the friend's perspective before the measurement, the outcome is uncertain. After the measurement, the outcome is definite. The before-after asymmetry is unexplained. The branching is explained. The experience of one branch rather than a superposition of branches is not.

Copenhagen-style interpretations take the opposite approach. The friend's measurement causes a real collapse. The wavefunction is not merely epistemically updated; it is physically reduced to a single eigenstate. The collapse is objective and irreversible. Decoherence explains why the collapse appears irreversible to Wigner. The environmental entanglement makes the other branches inaccessible. From Wigner's perspective, the collapse looks like decoherence. From the friend's perspective, the collapse is a real event. The two perspectives are reconciled by treating Wigner's description as incomplete—Wigner does not have access to the friend's outcome, so Wigner uses a superposition. The superposition is a description of ignorance, not of reality. Once Wigner learns the outcome, Wigner's description collapses too.

This resolution is also consistent. It treats collapse as a physical process that occurs when a measurement is made, independent of Wigner's knowledge. The collapse happens when the friend measures, not when Wigner looks. Decoherence is the mechanism by which the collapse becomes irreversible and propagates outward. The friend's subjective experience of a definite outcome corresponds to an objective physical event. Wigner's superposition is a mathematical artifact of incomplete information. The contradiction is dissolved by asserting that only one of the descriptions—the friend's—reflects reality.

Both resolutions have costs. Many-worlds multiplies entities without empirical distinction. Copenhagen multiplies descriptions without explaining when they apply. Neither interpretation is forced by decoherence. Decoherence explains why interference vanishes. It does not explain whether the branches are real, whether collapse is real, or how subjective experience relates to physical state.

The problem becomes sharper when Wigner's friend is made operational. In 2019, several groups performed laboratory versions of the thought experiment using photons and beam splitters to simulate the friend's measurement and Wigner's observation. The experiments confirmed that the friend's outcome and Wigner's superposition can coexist without logical contradiction within the formalism, but they do not observe the contradiction because the "friend" is a quantum system, not a conscious observer. The friend is a photon. The photon's state is controlled and measured. The experiment demonstrates that a quantum system can record a measurement result, that the system can be placed in a superposition of having recorded different results, and that an external observer can verify the superposition through interference. The experiment does not demonstrate that a conscious observer can be placed in such a superposition, because no conscious observer is used.

The substitution of a photon for a conscious observer is not a limitation of the experiment. It is a feature. The experiment tests the quantum formalism. The formalism applies to photons. Whether it applies to conscious observers is an interpretational question, not an experimental one. The Wigner's friend thought experiment is about interpretation. It asks what happens when the formalism is applied consistently to an observer. The laboratory experiments show that the formalism can be applied consistently to quantum systems that record information. They do not show that the formalism can be applied consistently to conscious experience.

The gap between information and experience is where the contradiction lives. Decoherence theory handles information. It explains how information spreads from a system into an environment, how correlations are established, how interference is suppressed. It does not explain how information becomes experience. It does not explain why the friend experiences one outcome rather than experiencing a superposition, or experiencing nothing, or experiencing both outcomes in some ineffable subjective sense. The formalism does not contain experience. It contains states, amplitudes, probabilities. The connection between probability and experience is not derived. It is assumed.

The assumption is the Born rule. The rule says that the probability of an outcome is the squared amplitude of the corresponding branch. The friend uses this rule to predict that spin-up and spin-down each have fifty percent probability before the measurement. After the measurement, the friend updates the probability to one hundred percent for the observed outcome. The update is called collapse, or state reduction, or Bayesian updating, depending on the interpretation. The update is not derived from the Schrödinger equation. It is added as a separate postulate. Decoherence explains why the update appears irreversible. It does not explain why the update happens or why it happens to produce a single outcome.

Many-worlds denies that the update happens. All outcomes occur. The apparent update is a selection effect. Each copy of the friend observes one outcome. The copy cannot observe the other outcomes because decoherence has suppressed interference. The Born rule is reinterpreted as a measure on branches. The measure determines how much "existence" or "reality" each branch has. High-amplitude branches are more real than low-amplitude branches in some sense that remains controversial. The friend in a high-amplitude branch should expect to have been in a high-amplitude branch, because most branches with the friend's history are high-amplitude branches. This self-locating uncertainty is supposed to recover the Born rule without invoking collapse. The derivation has been disputed. It requires assumptions about typicality, rationality, and probability that are themselves interpretational. The Born rule is reproduced, but not derived from the formalism alone.

Copenhagen-style interpretations accept that the update happens but deny that it requires explanation beyond the postulate. The Born rule is a fundamental law. It connects the mathematical formalism to empirical observation. The connection is irreducible. Decoherence explains why the rule works in practice—why macroscopic measurements yield definite outcomes that obey the probabilities. It does not explain why the rule is squared amplitude rather than amplitude or some other function. The rule is validated by experiment. That is sufficient.

Both positions are defensible. Neither is forced by decoherence. The Wigner's friend experiment does not resolve the dispute. It clarifies it. The dispute is not about the mechanics of decoherence. It is about what decoherence leaves unexplained: the status of the unobserved branches, the nature of probability, the connection between formalism and experience.

The relocation of the contradiction is clearest when multiple friends are involved. Suppose Wigner has two friends, Alice and Bob, each performing independent measurements in separate sealed laboratories. Alice measures the spin of electron A. Bob measures the spin of electron B. The electrons are entangled. Alice observes spin-up for A. Bob observes spin-down for B. The outcomes are correlated due to the entanglement. From Alice's perspective, her measurement caused the correlation. Her observation of A's spin instantaneously determined B's spin. From Bob's perspective, his measurement caused the correlation. From Wigner's perspective, neither measurement has happened—the entire system of Alice, Bob, and the electrons is in a superposition of correlated outcomes. Wigner can, in principle, perform an interference measurement on the four-way system and verify the superposition. The three descriptions are incompatible as descriptions of a single objective reality. They are compatible as descriptions relative to different observers.

This relativity is handled differently by different interpretations. Many-worlds says all outcomes occur. Alice sees spin-up and Bob sees spin-down in one branch. Alice sees spin-down and Bob sees spin-up in another branch. Wigner sees a superposition of both branches until Wigner measures. When Wigner measures, Wigner branches too. Every measurement creates branching. The branches are observer-independent in the sense that they are determined by the Hamiltonian. They are observer-dependent in the sense that different observers have access to different branches. The relativity is built into the structure of branching.

Relational quantum mechanics takes relativity further. States are relative to observers. Alice has a state relative to the electron she measured. Bob has a state relative to the electron he measured. Wigner has a state relative to the entire laboratory. There is no absolute state. Each observer has their own state assignment. The assignments are consistent in the sense that they obey the quantum formalism. They are inconsistent in the sense that they assign different definite values to the same system. The inconsistency is resolved by denying that systems have absolute values. Values are relational. A spin is up relative to Alice and in superposition relative to Wigner. Both statements are true. The truth is relative.

QBism takes subjectivity further still. States are personal beliefs. Alice's state for the electron is her own state assignment, reflecting her own information. Bob's state is Bob's own assignment. Wigner's state is Wigner's own assignment. The assignments are subjective. They cannot be right or wrong in an objective sense. They can only be updated consistently with experience. The formalism governs the updates. It does not describe an external reality. This position dissolves the contradiction by denying that there is an objective state to disagree about. The disagreement is a disagreement of beliefs, not of facts.

These interpretations are not empirically distinguishable. They make the same predictions for all measurable quantities. The Wigner's friend experiment cannot decide among them because the experiment involves a conscious observer only as a thought experiment. When implemented in the laboratory, the "friend" is a quantum system. The experiment demonstrates that quantum systems can record information, be placed in superposition, and later be measured to verify the superposition. That is consistent with the formalism. It does not tell us what happens when the system is replaced by a person.

The question is whether consciousness matters. Decoherence-based responses to Wigner typically say it does not. A person is a physical system. The person's brain is a quantum system, albeit a large and complex one. The brain obeys the Schrödinger equation. Decoherence applies to the brain just as it applies to a photon or an electron. The brain's reduced density matrix becomes diagonal. The person experiences a definite outcome. Consciousness is correlated with brain state. Superpositions of brain states correspond to superpositions of conscious states, which decohere so rapidly that they are never experienced. The person feels a single outcome because the person's brain is in a single decohered state, even though the total wavefunction of the brain plus environment is in superposition.

This response is materialist and reductionist. It treats consciousness as an emergent feature of brain dynamics. It requires no additional physics beyond quantum mechanics. It is compatible with decoherence. It does not explain why consciousness corresponds to decohered brain states rather than to superposed brain states. It does not explain why a decohered density matrix corresponds to a single experienced outcome rather than to a statistical mixture of outcomes that are not experienced individually. The correspondence between physical state and subjective experience is assumed, not derived.

The assumption is not arbitrary. It is grounded in the fact that brains operate at room temperature, interact constantly with their environment, and have decoherence times far shorter than neural processing times. Quantum coherence is irrelevant to brain function at the scales where cognition occurs. The brain is effectively classical. Measurements performed by the brain are effectively irreversible. The brain's state is effectively definite. The word "effectively" does all the work. It means the superposition is still there in the total wavefunction but is inaccessible to the brain or to any measurement the brain could perform. The brain cannot distinguish a decohered superposition from a true mixture. For all practical purposes, the superposition has collapsed.

For all practical purposes is not the same as in fact. The distinction matters to Wigner. Wigner can, in principle, measure the superposition. Wigner's friend cannot. The difference is not in the physics—the Hamiltonian is the same for both—but in the partition. Wigner's partition treats the friend as part of the system. The friend's partition treats the friend as the observer. The two partitions are incompatible. The contradiction is relocated from the measurement event to the choice of partition.

The relocation is progress. It clarifies that the contradiction is not about what happens physically—decoherence happens, interference is suppressed, the environment entangles—but about how the partition is chosen. The partition determines whether an outcome is definite or superposed. The choice is observer-dependent. Wigner and the friend choose different partitions and obtain different conclusions. Both conclusions are correct within their respective partitions. The contradiction arises when the partitions are claimed to describe the same objective reality. If reality is partition-independent, the claims are incompatible. If reality is partition-dependent, the claims are compatible but relativistic.

Decoherence does not eliminate the contradiction. It explains why the contradiction is unmeasurable. Wigner's interference experiment is impossible in practice. The friend's perception of a definite outcome is inevitable in practice. The two perspectives cannot be brought into conflict empirically. The conflict remains at the level of interpretation. Decoherence has moved the conflict from the physics to the metaphysics. That is progress. It is not resolution.

The relocated contradiction remains. It is sharper than before. Decoherence has eliminated the possibility of explaining it away through ignorance or imprecision. The friend's definite outcome and Wigner's superposition are both consequences of applying the quantum formalism consistently. The formalism permits both. It does not choose between them. The choice is made by the interpretation. The interpretation is made by the physicist. The physicist is part of the system that needs explaining.
