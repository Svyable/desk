# Failed Dissolutions and Category Errors

The history of quantum mechanics is full of claims that the measurement problem has been dissolved. Each claim rests on showing that what appeared to be a physical question is actually a conceptual confusion, a category error, or a misunderstanding of what the formalism says. The claims are often sophisticated. They are rarely successful. The failures are instructive. They reveal that the measurement problem is not one problem. It is a family of problems. Dissolving one member of the family does not dissolve the others.

The oldest dissolution attempt is Copenhagen. The argument is that quantum mechanics applies only to microscopic systems. Macroscopic apparatus is classical by definition. Measurement is the transition from quantum to classical. The transition is not described by quantum mechanics because quantum mechanics does not apply to the apparatus. The apparatus is a boundary condition. It defines the experimental setup. The setup determines what is measured. The measurement produces an outcome. The outcome is described classically because the apparatus is classical. There is no measurement problem because there is no attempt to describe the apparatus quantum mechanically.

The dissolution works operationally. Physicists use Copenhagen-style reasoning routinely. They treat apparatus as classical, apply quantum mechanics to the microscopic system, and predict measurement outcomes. The predictions are accurate. But the dissolution is circular. It assumes that apparatus is classical. It does not explain why apparatus is classical. Saying "apparatus is macroscopic" is not an explanation. Macroscopic systems are made of microscopic quantum constituents. The constituents obey quantum mechanics. The apparatus obeys quantum mechanics. The Schrödinger equation does not stop applying at a certain size. Decoherence theory shows that macroscopic superpositions are fragile, but fragility is not the same as non-existence. A macroscopic object in a superposition decoheres quickly. But before it decoheres, it is in a superposition. The superposition is described by quantum mechanics. Treating it as classical before decoherence is not justified by the formalism. Copenhagen asserts the classical-quantum divide without deriving it. The assertion works in practice. It does not work in principle. The divide is put in by hand. It is not explained. That is a category error: treating a distinction that must be explained as a distinction that can be assumed.

The next major dissolution attempt is Everett's many-worlds. The argument is that there is no measurement problem because there is no collapse. The wavefunction evolves unitarily. All branches are real. Observers are part of the wavefunction. Each observer experiences one branch. The experience is subjective, but the branching is objective. The measurement problem is dissolved because it asked the wrong question. It asked why only one outcome occurs. The answer is that all outcomes occur. The uniqueness of experience is explained by the structure of branching. Each copy of the observer sees one outcome. The copies cannot communicate. They live in separate branches. The separation is enforced by decoherence. Decoherence suppresses interference. Without interference, the branches are independent. The independence makes the experience of one outcome consistent with the reality of all outcomes.

The dissolution is logically consistent. It also introduces new problems. The Born rule is not explained. The rule gives probabilities. Probabilities are frequencies of outcomes. But in many-worlds, all outcomes occur. There are no frequencies. There are only branches. Each branch is equally real. Why should an observer expect to find themselves in a high-amplitude branch? The expectation makes sense if only one branch is real and the observer does not know which one. It makes less sense if all branches are real and the observer exists in all of them. Several responses have been proposed. They invoke decision theory, self-locating uncertainty, or measures on branch structures. None has achieved consensus. The Born rule remains unexplained or explained by assumptions that are themselves contestable. That is a category error: treating a prediction rule as if it were derivable from branching when it must be added as an independent postulate. The branching does not explain probabilities. It removes outcomes and replaces them with branches. Probabilities are still needed to connect branches to experience. The connection is not derived. It is assumed. The dissolution replaces one mystery with another.

A more recent dissolution attempt is QBism. The argument is that quantum states are not objective. They are personal degrees of belief. Measurement is not a physical process that changes the system. It is an update of belief that changes the agent's expectations. The measurement problem asks why a physical state collapses. QBism says there is no physical state to collapse. The state is epistemic. It encodes what the agent expects to experience. Experience changes when measurement provides new information. The change is Bayesian updating. It is not physical. It is rational. The problem is dissolved because it misidentified the quantum state as a physical object rather than a tool for managing uncertainty.

The dissolution is philosophically bold. It is also practically evasive. If states are personal, why do different agents agree? QBism says agents share experiences and use a common formalism. The formalism is normative. It tells agents how to update beliefs consistently. But the formalism is not arbitrary. It is the quantum formalism. The quantum formalism works because it matches the world. The matching requires explanation. QBism provides none. It says the formalism is useful. Usefulness is not explanation. The formalism makes predictions. The predictions are accurate. Accuracy requires that the formalism captures something about the structure of the world. That something is not explained by calling states personal. Calling states personal dissolves the question of why they collapse. It does not dissolve the question of why they obey the quantum rules. That is a category error: treating an explanatory question—why does the formalism work?—as if it were answered by a re-categorization—states are beliefs, not physical properties. Re-categorization changes the language. It does not provide new physics.

Another dissolution attempt is relational quantum mechanics. The argument is that quantum states are not absolute. They are relational. A system has a state relative to another system. Measurement establishes a correlation. The correlation is real. The state is not. The measurement problem asks why a state collapses to a unique outcome. Relational quantum mechanics says states are correlations, not properties. Correlations do not collapse. They are established through interaction. The interaction is physical. The correlation is objective. But the state is relative. Different observers can assign different states to the same system if they have interacted with it differently. The differences are not contradictions. They are facts about different relations. The problem is dissolved because it assumed states were absolute. They are not. They are relational. Relationality explains why observers can disagree without being wrong. Each observer is right about the relation they participate in. There is no global fact about the state. There are only local relational facts.

The dissolution is elegant. It is also incomplete. If states are relational, why does the world appear objective? Most observers agree on most facts most of the time. Agreement is not universal, but it is common enough to support science. Science assumes that measurements reveal facts about the world, not merely facts about observer-system relations. Relational quantum mechanics says the appearance of objectivity arises because observers interact and correlate their states. Once correlated, they agree. The agreement is physical. But the agreement is local. It applies to observers who have interacted. Observers who have not interacted can disagree. The disagreement is real. It is not resolved until the observers interact. Interaction is required for objectivity. But interaction is itself relational. It is defined relative to some third observer. The regress continues. There is no global objectivity. There are only local agreements that expand through interaction. This is consistent. It is also unsatisfying. It dissolves the measurement problem by relativizing reality. Relativizing reality solves some puzzles. It creates others. The new puzzles are about the nature of relationality, the role of interaction, and the emergence of agreement. These are not trivial. They are the measurement problem in different language. That is a category error: treating a reformulation as a solution. Reformulation can clarify. It does not eliminate. The problem remains. It is relocated to the structure of relationality.

A different kind of dissolution attempt is decoherence without interpretation. The argument is that decoherence explains the emergence of classicality. No further interpretation is needed. Classicality is what we observe. Decoherence explains why we observe it. The measurement problem asks for more than this. It asks for collapse, or branching, or relationality, or some ontological story about what is real. But these stories are not needed for predictions. Predictions come from the formalism. The formalism includes decoherence. Decoherence plus the Born rule plus unitary evolution is sufficient for all predictions. Interpretation adds nothing. The problem is dissolved because it was a pseudo-problem. It asked for an ontology when only an algorithm is needed. Science provides algorithms. Ontology is metaphysics. Metaphysics is not science.

The dissolution is pragmatic. It is also incomplete. Decoherence explains why coherence is lost. It does not explain why one outcome is observed. The Born rule explains the probabilities of outcomes. It does not explain why outcomes occur rather than the system remaining in a superposition. Unitary evolution explains how states propagate. It does not explain when to apply the Born rule or what constitutes a measurement. These gaps are not minor. They are the core of the measurement problem. Saying "interpretation adds nothing to predictions" is true. It is also irrelevant. The measurement problem is not about making new predictions. It is about understanding the structure of the theory. Understanding requires explaining why the algorithm works. An algorithm is a procedure. A procedure can be followed mechanically. Following it does not require understanding it. Understanding requires knowing why the procedure is correct. The "why" is interpretational. Interpretation is not metaphysics. It is the conceptual structure that makes the formalism intelligible. Intelligibility is not optional. It is what allows physicists to extend the theory, apply it to new domains, and recognize when it might break down. Dismissing interpretation as metaphysics is a category error: treating explanatory questions as if they were irrelevant to science when they are central to scientific understanding. Predictions are not enough. Science also explains.

The pattern across these dissolution attempts is consistent. Each attempt identifies a real insight. Copenhagen identifies that macroscopic apparatus behaves classically. Many-worlds identifies that unitary evolution is sufficient for branching. QBism identifies that quantum states can be understood epistemically. Relational quantum mechanics identifies that states can be relational. Decoherence without interpretation identifies that predictions do not require ontology. These insights are valuable. They clarify aspects of the measurement problem. But none of them eliminates the problem. Each relocates it. Copenhagen relocates it to the quantum-classical boundary. Many-worlds relocates it to the Born rule. QBism relocates it to the success of the formalism. Relational quantum mechanics relocates it to the nature of relationality. Decoherence without interpretation relocates it to the question of why the algorithm works. The relocation is progress. It narrows the problem. It does not solve it.

The failure to dissolve the measurement problem is not a failure of these approaches. It is evidence that the problem is real. A real problem cannot be dissolved by redefinition. It can only be solved by new physics or deeper understanding of existing physics. The new physics might be a modification of quantum mechanics: objective collapse theories, hidden variables, pilot waves. The deeper understanding might be recognizing that the partition is physical: that the work of maintaining system-environment boundaries is part of the theory, not external to it. Both paths are active research programs. Neither has achieved consensus. The lack of consensus is not a defect. It is the current state of the field. The field is open. The problem is unsolved. The dissolution attempts are useful. They clarify what the problem is not. They do not show that the problem does not exist. Existence is confirmed by the fact that every dissolution leaves something unexplained. The unexplained remainder is the problem. The problem is the partition. The partition is where all the dissolution attempts converge. They all require specifying what is system and what is environment. They all require specifying when a measurement occurs. They all require specifying which observable is measured. These specifications are the partition. The partition is not dissolved by any interpretation. It is assumed by every interpretation. The assumption is the common structure. The structure is physical. It is not bookkeeping. It is not metaphysics. It is the part of quantum mechanics that still needs explaining. That explanation is the subject of the next chapter.
