# Preferred Bases That Are Not Free

Quantum mechanics allows a state to be expressed in any basis. An electron can be described as a superposition of spin-up and spin-down along the z-axis, or along the x-axis, or along any other direction. The choice of basis is a matter of convenience. The physics does not care. This basis freedom is one of the theory's elegant features. It is also a source of the preferred basis problem.

Decoherence selects a basis.

When a quantum system interacts with an environment, the reduced density matrix becomes diagonal in a particular basis—the pointer basis, or the preferred basis. Interference terms vanish in this basis. The system appears classical. But the basis is not chosen by the analyst. It is determined by the system-environment interaction. The environment "measures" the system in a specific basis, and decoherence occurs in that basis.

The preferred basis is not arbitrary. It depends on the form of the interaction Hamiltonian. For a particle scattering photons, the interaction couples the particle's position to the photon states. Decoherence occurs in the position basis. The particle's momentum superposition is destroyed, but its position distribution remains. For a qubit coupled to a fluctuating magnetic field, the interaction couples the qubit's energy to the field states. Decoherence occurs in the energy basis. The qubit's energy is well-defined, but phase coherence between energy eigenstates is lost.

The selection of the preferred basis is one of decoherence theory's major successes. It explains why we observe certain classical variables and not others. Position is robust because most environmental interactions couple to position. Energy is robust in isolated systems because the interaction Hamiltonian commutes with the system Hamiltonian. Phase coherence is fragile because environmental coupling typically breaks the symmetry that protects it.

This success also reveals a problem. The preferred basis is an output of the calculation, not an input. But the calculation requires specifying the interaction Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian is not given by the universe. It is written by the physicist. The physicist chooses which interactions to include, which to approximate, and which to ignore. That choice determines the preferred basis. The basis is not free—it is not chosen arbitrarily—but it is not automatic either. It depends on which degrees of freedom are treated as part of the environment and how those degrees of freedom are modeled.

Consider a spin in a magnetic field. If the field is classical and perfectly uniform, the spin precesses but does not decohere. There is no environment. If the field has quantum fluctuations, the spin becomes entangled with the field modes. Decoherence occurs in the basis that diagonalizes the interaction. If the interaction is proportional to the spin along the z-axis, the preferred basis is the z-eigenbasis. If the interaction is proportional to the spin along the x-axis, the preferred basis is the x-eigenbasis. The orientation of the preferred basis is determined by the form of the coupling.

But the form of the coupling is not a fundamental fact. It depends on what is included in the environment. If the fluctuating field comes from nearby nuclear spins, the interaction Hamiltonian is the hyperfine coupling. If the fluctuating field comes from magnetic impurities in the substrate, the interaction is dipolar coupling. If the field comes from external sources like power lines or the Earth's field, the interaction is Zeeman coupling to a stochastic classical field. Each model produces a different interaction Hamiltonian and a different preferred basis.

The models are not equally good. Experiment constrains which model is appropriate. A nitrogen-vacancy center in diamond decoheres primarily due to coupling with the surrounding carbon-13 nuclear spins. The interaction Hamiltonian is known. The preferred basis is the NV spin eigenstate basis along the symmetry axis. This is measurable, reproducible, and consistent across experiments. The preferred basis is not arbitrary.

But it is contingent. It depends on the material. In a different host crystal with fewer nuclear spins, the same NV center would decohere more slowly and possibly in a different basis if the dominant interaction changed. In an isotopically purified diamond with no carbon-13, the NV decoheres even more slowly, and the next-most-important interaction—electric field noise, perhaps—becomes the dominant source of decoherence. The preferred basis is stable only to the extent that the dominant interaction is stable. Change the environment and the preferred basis can change.

That contingency is even clearer in engineered systems. A superconducting qubit can be designed to have a preferred basis in the energy eigenstate basis by minimizing charge noise and maximizing dephasing relative to relaxation. Alternatively, it can be designed to have a preferred basis in the charge eigenstate basis by coupling it strongly to charge fluctuations. The design determines the interaction Hamiltonian, which determines the preferred basis. The basis is an engineering choice, constrained by the physics of the materials and the geometry, but not dictated by fundamental principles.

The contingency becomes a problem in foundational discussions because the preferred basis is often invoked to explain why we observe definite classical properties. The argument goes: the environment selects the pointer basis, and the pointer basis corresponds to the classical variables we measure. Position is classical because environmental scattering selects the position basis. Energy is classical in isolated systems because the Hamiltonian selects the energy basis. This argument is correct as far as it goes. But it is circular if the environment is chosen to produce the desired basis.

The circularity is subtle. In a laboratory, the experimenter controls the environment, or at least the part of the environment that dominates decoherence. The apparatus is designed to couple strongly to the variable of interest and weakly to everything else. A position measurement apparatus couples to position. A momentum measurement apparatus couples to momentum. The apparatus enforces the preferred basis. The environment does not independently select the basis; the experimenter designs the environment to select the basis that corresponds to the measurement being performed.

In this picture, the preferred basis is not a fact about quantum mechanics. It is a fact about experimental design. Decoherence occurs in the basis the apparatus is built to measure. This is not a failure of decoherence theory. It is a recognition that the theory describes controlled systems where the interactions are engineered rather than accidental.

But not all systems are controlled. Astrophysical systems, biological systems, and geological systems interact with environments that are not designed by experimenters. In these settings, the preferred basis is determined by the dominant naturally occurring interactions. A photon traveling through interstellar space scatters off dust grains and the cosmic microwave background. The scattering couples to the photon's position and wavelength. Decoherence occurs in the position-momentum basis. The photon retains its spatial localization but loses coherence between different momentum components. This is not engineered. It is imposed by the environment.

Yet even here, the preferred basis is not automatic. The analyst must decide which environmental interactions are important. Dust scattering dominates in dense regions. CMB scattering dominates in empty regions. Gravitational interactions become relevant at cosmological scales. Each source of decoherence contributes a term to the interaction Hamiltonian. The terms can compete. The preferred basis can be a complicated mixture of eigenstates from different interactions. The dominant term wins, but "dominant" is a quantitative judgment that depends on coupling strengths, environmental state densities, and interaction timescales. These are calculated, not assumed.

The calculation requires a model. The model requires assumptions: which degrees of freedom are in the system, which are in the environment, which interactions are strong enough to matter, which can be neglected. These assumptions are justified by experiment and prior knowledge, but they are assumptions nonetheless. The preferred basis is an output of the model. Change the model and the preferred basis can change.

This dependency is not a defect. It reflects the fact that decoherence is a relative process. A system decoheres relative to an environment. The preferred basis is the basis in which the system-environment entanglement is maximized. Different environments produce different entanglement patterns and thus different preferred bases. The basis is preferred relative to a specific system-environment partition and a specific interaction.

The partition is where the problem lives. The partition determines which degrees of freedom are in the system and which are in the environment. The interaction Hamiltonian is written across the partition. The preferred basis is the basis that diagonalizes that interaction. All three ingredients—partition, interaction, preferred basis—depend on each other. None is independently defined.

In practice, the partition is often obvious. A single atom in a gas of other atoms: the atom is the system, the gas is the environment. A macroscopic object moving through space: the object is the system, the radiation field and surrounding medium are the environment. A qubit in a quantum computer: the qubit is the system, the substrate, control lines, and electromagnetic environment are the environment. These divisions are natural because the system is spatially localized, energetically separated, or functionally distinct from the environment.

But there are cases where the partition is ambiguous. Two atoms that are entangled with each other and decohering due to a shared environment: each atom can be treated as the system with the other atom included in the environment, or both atoms can be treated as a joint system with the surrounding gas as the environment. The two descriptions give different predictions for the decoherence rate and the preferred basis. Both are valid. Neither is privileged.

A molecule with many internal vibrational modes coupled to a solvent: some modes can be treated as part of the system and others as part of the environment. The choice affects the decoherence dynamics and the preferred basis. If all the vibrational modes are included in the system, the preferred basis is the basis that diagonalizes the coupling between electronic states and the solvent. If some vibrational modes are traced over, they contribute to the effective environment, and the preferred basis includes both electronic and vibrational character. The partition is a modeling choice, constrained by what is measurable and tractable.

Quantum chemistry makes this partition explicit through the active-space approximation. Certain orbitals are treated quantum mechanically. Others are frozen. Others are approximated. The frozen and approximated orbitals constitute an effective environment for the active-space electrons. Decoherence within the active space occurs due to coupling to the frozen and virtual orbitals. The preferred basis within the active space depends on the form of that coupling. The coupling depends on which orbitals were frozen. The choice of active space determines the decoherence structure and the preferred basis.

The choice is not arbitrary, but it is not unique either. Different active spaces are appropriate for different phenomena. For ground-state properties, a small active space around the Fermi level may suffice. For excited-state dynamics, a larger active space including high-energy orbitals is needed. For charge-transfer processes, orbitals on both donor and acceptor sites must be included. Each active space defines a different system-environment partition, a different interaction Hamiltonian, and a different preferred basis.

The fact that different partitions are valid for different purposes suggests that the preferred basis is not a fact about the molecule. It is a fact about the question being asked. The basis is preferred for a specific observable, a specific measurement, a specific level of description. Change the question and the preferred basis can change.

That variability is clearest in interpretational debates. Proponents of the Everett interpretation argue that the preferred basis is observer-dependent. Different observers, or the same observer at different times, can use different bases to describe the same system. The environment selects a basis locally, but there is no global preferred basis for the entire universe. Each partition has its own locally preferred basis. The bases are consistent with each other in the sense that decoherence suppresses interference, but they are not uniquely defined.

Proponents of objective collapse theories argue the opposite. The preferred basis is selected by a physical collapse mechanism that does not depend on observers. The collapse occurs in a basis determined by the mass distribution, the gravitational field, or some other objective property. The environment contributes to decoherence, but the ultimate selection of the basis is fixed by the collapse mechanism. In these theories, the preferred basis is a fundamental feature of the world, not a feature of the model.

Decoherence theory alone cannot adjudicate between these positions. It can describe how quickly different bases decohere given an interaction Hamiltonian. It cannot say whether the basis exists independently of the interaction or is created by it. It cannot say whether all bases are equally real or whether one is ontologically privileged. These are interpretational questions. Decoherence provides the mechanism. Interpretation provides the ontology.

The preferred basis problem is therefore not solved by decoherence. It is clarified. Decoherence explains why certain bases are more stable than others. It explains why classical variables correspond to robust bases. It explains why macroscopic objects appear to have definite positions and energies. It does not explain why those bases exist, whether they are observer-independent, or what happens to the other bases that are not selected.

The problem is sharpest when the preferred basis is not obvious. In some systems, the interaction Hamiltonian does not have a simple diagonal form. The environment couples to multiple system observables simultaneously. The resulting preferred basis is a complicated mixture of eigenstates that does not correspond to any simple classical variable. This is not a pathology. It is common in condensed matter systems, where lattice vibrations couple to electronic, magnetic, and orbital degrees of freedom simultaneously. The preferred basis is not position, not energy, not spin, but a hybrid.

In these cases, the preferred basis is defined operationally: it is the basis in which the off-diagonal terms of the reduced density matrix decay fastest. That definition is precise and calculable. It is also unsatisfying. It defines the preferred basis as the basis that is preferred, which is circular. The basis is preferred because the environment suppresses interference in that basis. But the environment was chosen to model the system's surroundings. The surroundings were not chosen to produce a particular basis. The basis emerges from the surroundings. Yet the surroundings were included in the model based on physical reasoning about what interactions are important. That reasoning presupposes some understanding of what the system is and what should be measured. The reasoning is not independent of the question.

The circularity is not vicious. It is iterative. The physicist makes an initial guess about the system-environment partition, calculates the preferred basis, checks whether the prediction matches experiment, and refines the model. This is standard scientific practice. The problem is that the textbook presentation of the preferred basis often omits the iteration. It presents the preferred basis as if it were an automatic output of the quantum formalism, when in fact it is the output of a formalism plus a partition plus an interaction model, all of which involve choices.

The iteration is observable in actual research. Consider the development of superconducting qubits. Early designs suffered from charge noise, which caused decoherence in the charge basis. Experimentalists redesigned the circuits to operate at the charge degeneracy point, where charge fluctuations have minimal effect. This moved the preferred basis toward the flux basis. Later designs introduced the transmon qubit, which is even less sensitive to charge noise and more sensitive to flux noise. The preferred basis shifted again. Each design was motivated by measurements showing what kind of noise dominated. The noise determined the environment. The environment determined the preferred basis. The basis determined which quantum states were long-lived.

Long-lived states are what quantum computing requires. The requirement drove the engineering. The engineering changed the environment. Changing the environment changed the preferred basis. The preferred basis is therefore not an immutable property of the qubit. It is a consequence of the qubit plus its engineered surroundings. The surroundings are designed by the experimentalist. The design embodies choices about what to couple and what to decouple. The choices determine the partition. The partition determines the preferred basis. The basis is the output of the design. Design is physics applied with intent. Intent is not external to physics. It is how physics is done.

Physics is not a collection of facts about a world that exists independently of observers. Physics is a practice. The practice involves asking questions, designing experiments, analyzing data, and building models. Each step involves choices. The choices shape what is discovered. What is discovered is physics. The physics includes the preferred basis. The preferred basis is discovered by measurement. Measurement is shaped by the apparatus. The apparatus is shaped by intent. Intent is the observer's role. The observer is therefore not external to the physics. The observer is part of it. Part means the observer affects what is measured and what is discovered. The effect is physical. It is not merely epistemic. Epistemic means about knowledge. Physical means about the world. The distinction is real. But the preferred basis sits at the boundary. It is partly epistemic—it reflects the observer's choice of apparatus and measurement—and partly physical—it is determined by the interaction Hamiltonian. The mixing is unavoidable. It is the consequence of observers being embedded in the world they study. Embedding means the observer cannot stand outside the system. The observer is inside. Inside means subject to the same physical laws as the system. Laws include thermodynamics. Thermodynamics constrains what partitions are viable. Viable partitions are those that can be maintained at finite cost. Cost is energy. Energy is dissipated. Dissipation is heat. Heat is measurable. Measurement confirms that partitions are physical. Physical partitions have preferred bases. Preferred bases are discovered by engineering. Engineering is physics. Physics is observer-dependent. Observer-dependence is not subjectivity. It is contextuality. Contextuality is quantum mechanics. Quantum mechanics is the foundation. The foundation includes observers.

The choices are constrained. Not every partition is physically reasonable. Not every interaction model is consistent with known physics. Not every preferred basis matches experiment. The constraints are strong enough that the preferred basis is often unambiguous in practice. But unambiguous in practice is not the same as unique in principle. The distinction matters in foundational physics, where the question is not whether decoherence works—it does—but whether it solves the measurement problem—it does not, because the preferred basis is part of the problem, not part of the solution.

The preferred basis is preferred relative to an environment. The environment is defined relative to a partition. The partition is chosen relative to a measurement. The measurement is performed relative to an observer. The observer is not eliminated by decoherence. The observer is relocated to the beginning of the chain: the entity that decides which partition to use, which environment to model, and which basis to calculate. That entity is not metaphysical. It is the physicist, the engineer, the experimentalist, the theorist—the person or institution that asks the question and builds the apparatus to answer it.

The preferred basis is not free. It is constrained by the Hamiltonian. But the Hamiltonian is not free either. It is chosen by the analyst. The choice is not arbitrary. It is guided by experiment, theory, and prior knowledge. But it is a choice. The preferred basis reflects that choice. It is part of the partition, not an independent fact that justifies the partition.
