# Living With an Unfinished Physics

Quantum mechanics is unfinished. This is not a failure. It is a fact. The theory is extraordinarily successful. It has been tested more rigorously than any other physical theory. It has not failed a single experimental test in a century of use. It predicts phenomena across energy scales from atomic to cosmological. It is the foundation of chemistry, solid-state physics, nuclear physics, and particle physics. It enables technologies from transistors to lasers to MRI scanners. It is, by any reasonable standard, one of the great intellectual achievements of human history.

And it is unfinished.

The incompleteness is not in the predictions. The predictions are accurate, sometimes to fifteen significant figures. The incompleteness is in the explanation. The theory does not explain what it is describing. It provides a formalism: a mathematical recipe for calculating probabilities of measurement outcomes. The recipe works. But why does it work? What are quantum states? What is measurement? When does it occur? What is the status of unmeasured systems? How do classical outcomes arise from quantum superpositions? These questions do not have agreed-upon answers. They have competing interpretations. The interpretations are not empirically distinguishable. They agree on all predictions. They disagree on what those predictions mean. The disagreement is not a detail. It is a sign that something fundamental is not understood.

The measurement problem is the clearest sign. The problem is that the quantum formalism contains two evolution rules. The first is the Schrödinger equation, which describes continuous, deterministic, unitary evolution. The second is the measurement postulate, which describes discontinuous, probabilistic, non-unitary collapse. The two rules are incompatible. Unitary evolution preserves superpositions. Collapse destroys them. The formalism does not say when to use which rule. It does not say what constitutes a measurement. It does not say where the boundary sits between the quantum system and the classical apparatus. These are left to the physicist's judgment. The judgment is informed by experiment and experience. It is not derived from the theory. The theory is silent.

Decoherence was supposed to complete the theory. It explains why superpositions become practically unobservable when a system interacts with a large environment. It explains the emergence of classical behavior from quantum dynamics. It explains why macroscopic objects appear definite rather than superposed. These are significant accomplishments. But decoherence does not eliminate the measurement postulate. It explains why collapse appears to happen. It does not explain whether collapse actually happens, or whether all branches coexist, or whether the wavefunction is merely a description of knowledge. These explanatory questions remain open. They are the domain of interpretation. Interpretation matters. It is how physicists understand what they are calculating. Different interpretations guide intuition differently. They suggest different extensions of the theory. They frame different research questions. They are not mere metaphysics. They are part of the scientific process.

The preferred partition is part of what remains unexplained. The partition between system and environment is essential for applying quantum mechanics. It determines which degrees of freedom are kept in the description and which are traced over. It determines what is measured and what is ignored. It determines what is accessible and what is hidden. The partition is not given by the formalism. It is chosen by the physicist. The choice is constrained by the physical situation, the available instruments, and the questions being asked. But it is not uniquely determined. Different choices give different reduced density matrices, different decoherence rates, different preferred bases. The choices are not wrong. They are different levels of description. But the formalism does not specify which level is appropriate for which situation. That specification is external to the theory. It is part of the art of using the theory. The art is teachable, but it is not formalizable. It depends on judgment. Judgment is informed by experience. Experience is accumulated over lifetimes. The accumulation is personal and cultural. It is transmitted through training, mentorship, and tacit knowledge. It is not written in textbooks. The textbooks present the formalism. They do not fully explain how to use it. The gap between formalism and use is where the partition lives. The partition is the interface between theory and experiment. The interface is not arbitrary. It is constrained by thermodynamics, information theory, and the resources available to the observer. But it is not automatic. It requires work. The work is done by the observer. The observer is part of the world the theory describes. That self-reference is at the root of the measurement problem. It is also at the root of the incompleteness.

The incompleteness is not eliminable by interpretation alone. Interpretation provides a framework for thinking about the theory. It does not provide new predictive content. New predictive content would require modifying the formalism or adding structure that is currently missing. Several candidates have been proposed. Objective collapse theories modify the Schrödinger equation to include non-unitary terms that cause spontaneous collapse. Hidden variable theories add variables that are not in the standard formalism but determine measurement outcomes. Pilot wave theories treat the wavefunction as a physical field that guides particles. Each of these modifications aims to complete the theory by making it deterministic or by explaining collapse. None has achieved consensus. None has made a clear testable prediction that distinguishes it from standard quantum mechanics. The absence of clear tests does not mean the theories are wrong. It means they are not yet mature. They are research programs. Research programs take time to develop. Some succeed. Most fail. The ones that succeed produce new physics. The ones that fail still produce understanding. Understanding is how science progresses even when theories are wrong. Failed theories reveal what does not work. They narrow the space of possibilities. They guide future research. They are part of the process.

The process is ongoing. Quantum mechanics is ninety-nine years old as of 2026. It has been the subject of intense study by thousands of physicists. The foundations are still being explored. The exploration is not academic in the pejorative sense. It is connected to practical challenges in quantum computing, quantum cryptography, quantum sensing, and other technologies. These technologies push quantum mechanics into regimes where the formalism's ambiguities become engineering constraints. Engineering constraints force clarification. Clarification is progress. Progress accumulates. The accumulation is not linear. It is punctuated. Long periods of incremental work are interrupted by sudden insights. The insights often come from unexpected directions. Thermodynamics was not expected to contribute to quantum foundations. It has become central. Information theory was developed for communication. It has become essential for understanding quantum measurement. Complexity theory was developed for computation. It is now relevant to understanding decoherence and entanglement. The cross-fertilization is productive. It is also unpredictable. Unpredictability is why the foundations cannot be abandoned. The next insight may come from thinking carefully about what measurement is, or what an observer is, or what the partition is. These questions are not metaphysical. They are physical. They are about the structure of the theory and the role of observers within it.

Observers are physical systems. This is not a new claim. It is an old claim that has been insufficiently developed. Physics treats observers as external. They are the users of the theory, not the subjects of it. But observers are made of atoms. Atoms obey quantum mechanics. Observers obey quantum mechanics. This is Wigner's insight. It is also the source of the Wigner's friend paradox. The paradox arises because treating the observer as a quantum system leads to predictions that conflict with treating the observer as external. The conflict is not a contradiction in the formalism. It is a conflict between two applications of the formalism that use different partitions. The partitions are both valid. They are incompatible. Resolving the incompatibility requires understanding what determines the partition. Understanding the partition requires understanding observers. Understanding observers requires integrating them into the physics. Integration is unfinished. Finishing it may require new physics. The new physics may involve thermodynamics. Thermodynamics is the science of irreversibility. Irreversibility is the signature of measurement. Measurement establishes partitions. Partitions are maintained by dissipation. Dissipation is entropy increase. Entropy increase is the arrow of time. The arrow of time is connected to the measurement problem. The connection is not fully understood. Understanding it may complete the theory. Or it may reveal that the theory is incomplete in ways not currently anticipated. Either outcome is progress. Progress is the goal. The goal is understanding. Understanding is why the foundations matter.

Living with an unfinished physics is not a temporary condition. It is the normal state of science. No theory is final. Every theory is provisional. Provisional does not mean unreliable. It means improvable. Quantum mechanics is reliable. It is also improvable. The improvements may come from extending the theory to new domains: quantum gravity, quantum cosmology, quantum biology. They may come from deepening the theory in existing domains: better understanding of measurement, better understanding of decoherence, better understanding of entanglement. They may come from recognizing that the theory is already complete in a sense not currently appreciated. Completeness in this sense would mean that the formalism plus the partition plus the thermodynamic constraints plus the observer's role is sufficient, and that no further modification is needed. This is possible. It is not yet demonstrated.

Demonstrating it would require showing that all the interpretational puzzles dissolve once the partition is treated as physical structure rather than descriptive convenience. That demonstration is the task of the preferred-partition thesis. The thesis is that the partition does physical work, that the work is measurable, and that understanding the work completes the picture without requiring new fundamental physics. The thesis may be wrong. Testing it is how physics proceeds. Testing requires experiments, calculations, and conceptual clarity. Conceptual clarity requires thinking carefully about what the partition is, what observers do, and what it costs to maintain the boundary between system and environment. Those costs are physical. They are the signature that the partition is not arbitrary. If the signature holds up under scrutiny, the preferred-partition thesis survives. If it does not, the thesis is revised or replaced. Revision is progress. Replacement is also progress. Either way, understanding improves. Improvement is accumulation of knowledge. Accumulation is science. Science continues.

The continuation is not inevitable. Science is a human activity. It requires resources, institutions, and cultural support. Resources are finite. Institutions can fail. Cultural support can erode. The fragility is real. It is not often acknowledged. Scientists treat science as permanent. It is not. It is contingent. It depends on decisions made by people who are not scientists: politicians, funders, administrators, publics. The decisions are not always informed. They are not always rational. They are shaped by priorities that change. Priorities shift with economics, politics, and crises. Crises redirect resources. Redirection can starve fields. Fields can collapse.

Collapse is not hypothetical. It has happened. Ancient civilizations developed sophisticated astronomy, mathematics, and engineering. The knowledge was lost. Rediscovering it took centuries. Modern science is more robust. It is distributed across many institutions, many countries, many generations. But distribution is not immunity. Global challenges—pandemics, climate change, economic disruption—can strain the system. Strain can become fragility. Fragility can become failure. Failure is the end of accumulation. Accumulation is what makes science work. Science builds on previous results. Each generation stands on the shoulders of the last. The metaphor is apt. But it assumes the shoulders remain. If they crumble, the next generation falls.

Preventing crumble requires maintenance. Maintenance is work. Work costs energy. Energy must be supplied continuously. The supply is not automatic. It is negotiated. Negotiation is social. Social processes are outside the formalism. They are also outside the control of individual scientists. Scientists can advocate. They can explain. They can demonstrate utility. But they cannot command. Command belongs to the larger society. Society decides whether to support science. The decision is not permanent. It is renewed or withdrawn with each funding cycle, each election, each crisis. The renewal is the condition of continuity. Continuity is the condition of cumulative progress. Progress is the point. Without progress, science stagnates. Stagnation is invisible decline. Decline becomes visible only in retrospect. Retrospect is too late. Prevention requires foresight. Foresight requires understanding the contingency.

Understanding the contingency is part of understanding science. Science is not self-sustaining. It is sustained by effort. The effort is collective. The collective is fragile. Fragility is physical. It is thermodynamic. It is social. All three are real. The fragility is measurable. It appears as funding volatility, citation half-lives, and the rate of field extinction. Fields become extinct when practitioners retire faster than new ones are trained. Extinction is common. Most subfields that existed a century ago are gone. Their questions were answered, their methods were superseded, or their problems were abandoned.

Abandonment is not failure. It is reallocation. Resources are finite. Finite resources must be prioritized. Prioritization means some questions are pursued and others are not. The unpursued questions are not answered. They become lost knowledge. Lost knowledge is not permanently lost. It can be recovered if someone asks the question again and has the resources to pursue it. But recovery is expensive. It requires rediscovering methods, rebuilding equipment, and retraining people. The expense is why lost knowledge often stays lost. The permanence is contingent. Contingency is the condition of historical existence. Science exists in history. History is contingent. Contingent means subject to events. Events are unpredictable. Unpredictability is irreducible. Irreducibility is the condition of openness. Openness is the space where the future is made.
