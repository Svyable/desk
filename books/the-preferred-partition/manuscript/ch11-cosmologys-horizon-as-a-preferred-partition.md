# Cosmology's Horizon as a Preferred Partition

The universe has edges we cannot see past. The cosmic horizon is the boundary beyond which light has not had time to reach us since the Big Bang. Anything beyond that boundary is causally disconnected. It cannot affect us. We cannot affect it. It exists—assuming the universe extends beyond the observable patch—but it is inaccessible.

This inaccessibility is not technological. It is geometric. The universe is expanding. Distant regions are receding faster than light can travel. The recession is not a violation of relativity. Space itself is expanding. Objects embedded in space are carried apart. Light traveling through expanding space is redshifted. At sufficient distance, the recession velocity exceeds the speed of light. Photons emitted from those regions will never reach us. The regions are beyond the horizon. They are permanently inaccessible.

The horizon is a partition. It divides spacetime into observable and unobservable regions. The division is observer-dependent. Different observers have different horizons. An observer in a distant galaxy has a different observable region. The observable regions overlap but do not coincide. There is no global observable universe. There are only local observable regions, each bounded by a horizon. The horizon is relational. It is defined relative to an observer's worldline. It is not an absolute feature of spacetime. It is a feature of the causal structure given an observer.

The partition created by the horizon has physical consequences. The unobservable region is environment. It cannot be measured. It is traced over. The observable region is system. It can be measured, at least in principle. The reduced density matrix of the observable region is obtained by tracing over the unobservable region. The tracing is not merely formal. It corresponds to the physical fact that correlations between observable and unobservable regions cannot be accessed by any measurement confined to the observable region. The correlations exist in the total state of the universe. They are hidden by the horizon. The hiding is absolute. No improvement in technology can overcome it. The horizon is a fundamental limit on information access.

The limit has thermodynamic consequences. The horizon has entropy. The entropy is proportional to the area of the horizon. The proportionality is not accidental. It is required by the second law of thermodynamics applied to observers confined within the horizon. An observer inside the horizon can perform measurements. Measurements increase the observer's information. Increasing information decreases the observer's entropy. But the total entropy of the universe must increase. The increase is accounted for by the horizon. The horizon's entropy increases as information is acquired by the observer. The increase is not local. It is distributed over the horizon area. The distribution is not understood microscopically. It is described macroscopically by the Bekenstein-Hawking formula: the entropy is proportional to the area divided by the Planck length squared. The formula is semi-classical. It combines general relativity and quantum mechanics without a full theory of quantum gravity. But it is believed to be exact. It has been validated in black hole thermodynamics. It is conjectured to apply to cosmological horizons. The conjecture is the holographic principle.

The holographic principle says that the information content of a region of space is bounded by the area of its boundary, not its volume. The bound is saturated by horizons. A horizon encodes all the information about the region it bounds. The encoding is not understood in detail. But the principle is robust. It appears in string theory, loop quantum gravity, and other approaches to quantum gravity. The principle implies that the degrees of freedom of the universe are not uniformly distributed through space. They are concentrated on boundaries. The boundaries are horizons. Horizons are partitions. The partition between observable and unobservable is not merely epistemic. It is fundamental. The information about the unobservable region is encoded on the horizon. The encoding is in degrees of freedom that are not accessible from inside. The inaccessibility is built into the geometry.

The geometry is dynamical. The universe is expanding. The horizon is growing. As the universe expands, previously unobservable regions become observable. New information becomes accessible. The information was always there, encoded on the horizon. It becomes accessible as the horizon moves outward. The movement is not under the observer's control. It is determined by the cosmological dynamics. The dynamics are governed by general relativity and the matter content of the universe. The observer cannot speed up or slow down the horizon's expansion. The observer can only wait. The waiting is passive. The horizon expansion is active. It is a physical process. The process increases the observable region. The increase brings new degrees of freedom into the system. The degrees of freedom were previously environment. They become system. The transition is irreversible. Once a region is observable, it remains observable. The horizon does not contract. It expands. The expansion is monotonic. It is the cosmological arrow of time.

The arrow is tied to the partition. The partition between observable and unobservable is time-dependent. The dependence is objective. It does not depend on the observer's state of knowledge. It depends on the observer's worldline and the geometry of spacetime. The geometry is objective. It is determined by the Einstein field equations. The equations are deterministic. They predict the future evolution of the horizon given the current state of the universe. The prediction is independent of interpretation. It is a consequence of general relativity. The consequence is that the partition evolves. The evolution is physical. It is not a change in description. It is a change in what is accessible. The change is the signature of the partition's physical reality. The partition is not bookkeeping. It is structure.

The structure is sharpest in de Sitter space. De Sitter space is the spacetime geometry of a universe dominated by a positive cosmological constant. The geometry is maximally symmetric. It has a cosmological horizon at a fixed distance from any observer. The horizon is static. It does not expand or contract. Regions beyond the horizon are permanently inaccessible. The observer can never receive information from them. The observer can never send information to them. The horizon is an absolute partition. The partition is maintained by the cosmological constant. The constant is equivalent to vacuum energy. Vacuum energy generates a repulsive gravitational effect. The repulsion drives the expansion. The expansion keeps distant regions beyond the horizon. The horizon is a stable feature of the geometry. It does not depend on the observer's motion or state. It is built into the spacetime.

The stability makes de Sitter space a useful model for quantum mechanics in curved spacetime. The horizon is treated as a boundary. The boundary separates the observable region from the rest of the universe. The observable region is a causal diamond: the set of spacetime points that can both send and receive signals from a given observer's worldline. The causal diamond is finite. It has a boundary. The boundary is the horizon. The horizon is a null surface: light emitted tangent to the horizon stays on the horizon forever. The surface is generated by null geodesics. The geodesics are the paths of light rays. The light rays define the edge of accessibility. The edge is sharp. It is not fuzzy. It is not approximate. It is exact. It is determined by the causal structure. The causal structure is determined by the metric. The metric is determined by the field equations. The field equations are exact. The partition is exact.

The exactness is problematic for quantum mechanics. Quantum mechanics assumes a global Hilbert space. The Hilbert space factors into system and environment. The factorization is part of the formalism. But in a universe with a horizon, the factorization is imposed by the geometry. The geometry determines the partition. The partition is observer-dependent. Different observers have different horizons. They factor the Hilbert space differently. The different factorizations are incompatible as global descriptions. They are compatible as local descriptions. Each observer uses their own factorization. The factorizations agree on overlapping regions. They disagree on non-overlapping regions. The disagreement is not a contradiction. It is a feature of observer-dependent horizons. The horizons are relational. The factorizations are relational. The quantum states are relational. The relationality is imposed by the geometry, not by the interpretation. It is a consequence of general relativity plus quantum mechanics. It is predicted by the formalism. The testing is not feasible with current technology. Cosmological observers are separated by enormous distances. Communication is limited by the speed of light. The light travel time exceeds the age of the universe for sufficiently distant observers. The observers cannot compare notes. They cannot check whether their factorizations agree. The compatibility is a theoretical claim, not an experimental result. The claim is supported by consistency arguments. The arguments show that local measurements made by different observers must be compatible if the observers later communicate. The compatibility is enforced by locality and causality. Locality says that measurements at spacelike-separated points commute. Causality says that information cannot travel faster than light. Together, these principles ensure that different observers' descriptions are consistent even if they use different factorizations. The consistency is not automatic.
