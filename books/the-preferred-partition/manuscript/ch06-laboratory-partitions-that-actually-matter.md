# Laboratory Partitions That Actually Matter

Thought experiments about Wigner's friend and Schrödinger's cat are useful for clarifying conceptual problems. They are not how quantum mechanics is practiced. Real quantum mechanics happens in laboratories with specific apparatus, measurable outcomes, and engineering constraints. The partition between system and environment is not a philosophical puzzle in this context. It is a design parameter that determines whether an experiment works.

Consider a which-path experiment. A photon passes through a double slit. If no information exists about which slit the photon passed through, interference appears on the screen. If information exists—anywhere in the universe, stored in any degree of freedom—the interference vanishes. The standard explanation invokes decoherence. The environment entangles with the photon's path. The path information leaks into the environment. The interference is suppressed. The explanation is correct. It is also incomplete. The suppression depends on where the partition is drawn.

The which-path information can be recorded in several ways. A detector can be placed at one slit. A marker photon can be emitted when the photon passes through the slit. The photon's state can become correlated with an atomic state. The photon can scatter off a gas molecule. Each method creates a correlation between the photon's path and some other degree of freedom. That degree of freedom is part of the environment if it is traced over. It is part of the system if it is included in the quantum description. The choice determines whether interference is predicted.

Place the partition between the photon and the detector. The detector is environment. Trace it over. The photon's reduced density matrix is diagonal in the path basis. Interference is gone. This is the standard decoherence story. Place the partition to include the detector in the system. Now the photon and detector are in an entangled state. The interference is not gone. It is encoded in correlations between photon and detector. If the detector is later measured, the interference is destroyed at that stage. If the detector is not measured but instead interfered with itself—by combining its two possible states at another beam splitter—the photon's interference can be recovered. This is the quantum eraser.

The quantum eraser is not a loophole. It is a demonstration that the partition matters. Interference is absent relative to one partition and present relative to another. Both descriptions are correct. They correspond to different experimental setups: one where the detector is read out, and one where the detector is treated as part of the quantum system and manipulated coherently. The physics is partition-dependent. The partition is determined by the apparatus.

The apparatus does not merely reveal a pre-existing partition. It creates the partition. The choice of which degrees of freedom to measure, which to control, and which to ignore determines where the cut sits. That choice is engineering. It is also physics. The two are not separable.

The separation is clearest in delayed-choice experiments. A delayed-choice quantum eraser allows the experimentalist to decide, after the photon has passed through the slits, whether to measure the which-path information or to erase it. If the information is measured, no interference appears. If the information is erased, interference is recovered. The photon's behavior at the screen depends on a choice made later. The dependence is not causal in the usual sense—no signal travels backward in time. The dependence is conditional. The interference is always present in the total wavefunction. Whether it appears in a particular subset of the data depends on how the data is partitioned after the fact.

The partition is made by the experimentalist when analyzing the data. The data contains correlations between photon arrivals and detector outcomes. If the data is partitioned by detector outcome—keeping only photons correlated with a specific detector state—interference appears in each partition. If the data is analyzed without partitioning—averaging over all detector outcomes—interference disappears. The physics is the same in both cases. The correlations are the same. The partition is different. The partition determines what is observable.

This partition dependence is not unique to quantum erasers. It is generic in quantum optics. A two-photon interference experiment can show interference in the joint detection probability even when neither photon individually shows interference. The interference is a feature of correlations. It is observable only if the correlations are measured. Measuring correlations requires partitioning the data into coincidence events and non-coincidence events. The partition determines whether the interference is seen. The interference is not a property of the photons alone. It is a property of the photons relative to a partition defined by the detection scheme.

Weak measurement makes the partition dependence even more explicit. A weak measurement is an interaction between a system and a probe that creates a small correlation but does not collapse the system's state. The probe is measured. The measurement result gives partial information about the system. The system remains in a superposition, but the superposition is now correlated with the probe. Repeat the weak measurement many times with many copies of the system. Average the probe results. The average gives an estimate of a quantity called the weak value, which can be outside the range of eigenvalues of the system observable. The weak value is not a property of the system. It is a property of the system conditioned on a particular pre-selection and post-selection.

The pre-selection and post-selection define a partition. The pre-selection prepares the system in a known state. The post-selection keeps only systems that are later found in a particular state. The weak value is the expectation value of the observable within the subset of systems that pass both selection criteria. The subset is a partition of the full ensemble. The weak value depends on the partition. Change the post-selection and the weak value changes. The system is the same. The partition is different. The observable quantity is partition-dependent.

Weak measurement has practical applications. It can amplify small effects, measure fragile states without disturbing them significantly, and extract information about quantum trajectories. It can also produce apparent paradoxes. A weak measurement can give a result suggesting that a particle was in two places at once, or that a property had a value inconsistent with other properties. These results are not paradoxes. They are artifacts of the partition. The weak value is meaningful only relative to the pre-selection and post-selection. It is not a value the system had before measurement. It is a value that characterizes a subset of an ensemble conditioned on future information. The future information defines the partition. The partition determines the weak value.

The dependence on future information sounds acausal. It is not. The weak value is calculated from data that includes both past and future boundary conditions. The future condition—the post-selection—is imposed by the experimentalist after the weak measurement. The post-selection partitions the data. The partition determines what is calculated. The calculation is retrodictive rather than predictive. It describes what must have been true given that the post-selection succeeded. The "must have been true" is relative to the partition. Change the partition and the retrodiction changes.

This retrodictive structure is not limited to weak measurement. It is implicit in any quantum experiment that uses post-selection. A heralded single-photon source produces a photon pair. One photon is detected, heralding the presence of the other. Only the heralded photons are used in the experiment. The heralding partitions the ensemble into detected and undetected pairs. The experiment measures properties of the heralded subset. The properties are not properties of all photon pairs. They are properties of the pairs conditioned on the heralding event. The heralding event is a partition. The partition is defined by the apparatus. The apparatus determines what is measured.

The apparatus is not neutral. It is designed to answer a specific question. The design determines which variables are controlled, which are measured, and which are ignored. The ignored variables are traced over. They become part of the environment. The environment is defined by the apparatus design. The design is a choice. The choice is constrained by physics—not all designs are feasible—but the constraints do not determine a unique design. Different designs partition the Hilbert space differently. Different partitions lead to different predictions. The predictions are not contradictory. They correspond to different experiments.

This correspondence between partition and experiment is exact in quantum information protocols. Quantum teleportation transfers a qubit state from Alice to Bob using entanglement and classical communication. The protocol works by partitioning the three-qubit system—Alice's original qubit, Alice's half of the entangled pair, and Bob's half—into Alice's two qubits and Bob's one qubit. Alice performs a joint measurement on her two qubits. The measurement result is sent to Bob. Bob applies a unitary operation conditioned on the result. The final state of Bob's qubit is the original state of Alice's qubit. The state has been teleported.

The protocol succeeds because Alice's measurement partitions the Hilbert space into four orthogonal subspaces, each corresponding to a different measurement outcome. Within each subspace, Bob's qubit is in a state related to Alice's original state by a known unitary. The unitary is determined by the measurement outcome. Bob applies the inverse unitary. The state is recovered. The recovery works because the partition is designed to make it work. The measurement basis is chosen to create subspaces with the correct structure. The partition is not arbitrary. It is optimized for the protocol.

Different protocols use different partitions. Quantum dense coding sends two classical bits using one qubit. The protocol works by partitioning the two-qubit entangled state into four subspaces corresponding to the four possible messages. The sender applies a unitary to their qubit to select a subspace. The receiver performs a joint measurement on both qubits to identify the subspace. The message is recovered. The partition is designed to make the message extractable. The design is not unique—other partitions could work—but the partition must have specific properties. The properties are determined by the protocol requirements. The requirements are set by the experimentalist.

Quantum error correction is an even clearer example of engineered partitioning. A logical qubit is encoded in multiple physical qubits. Errors affect the physical qubits. The errors are detected by measuring syndromes. The syndromes are properties of the multi-qubit state that reveal the presence of errors without revealing the logical state. The syndrome measurement partitions the Hilbert space into error subspaces. Each subspace corresponds to a specific error or set of errors. The partition is designed so that errors are detectable and correctable. The design is nontrivial. It requires that the code space and the error subspaces are orthogonal, that the syndrome measurements are fast compared to the error rate, and that the corrections do not introduce more errors than they fix. These requirements constrain the partition. The partition is chosen to satisfy the requirements. The choice is engineering.

The engineering is not merely applied physics. It is part of the physics. The error correction code defines which states are protected and which are expendable. The protected states are the logical qubits. The expendable states are the ancilla qubits used for syndrome measurement. The ancillas are measured and reset. The measurement is a partition. The reset is a trace. The ancillas are part of the environment after reset. They were part of the system before reset. The transition from system to environment is controlled by the protocol. The protocol is designed by the engineer. The engineer determines the partition.

That determination is explicit in fault-tolerant quantum computing. A fault-tolerant protocol is a sequence of operations that maintains the logical qubit error rate below a threshold despite errors in the physical operations. The protocol partitions the Hilbert space into logical and physical, protected and unprotected, reversible and irreversible. The partitioning is dynamic. It changes at each step of the protocol. The changes are designed to prevent errors from propagating into the protected logical space. The design is algorithmic. The algorithm specifies which operations are performed, which qubits are measured, which are reset, and which are left alone. The specification is a partition schedule. The schedule is chosen to achieve fault tolerance. The choice is optimized computationally. The optimization uses classical algorithms to search over possible partition schedules and select ones that minimize logical error rates.

The optimization process treats the partition as a variable. Different partitions have different error rates. The best partition is the one that maximizes the coherence time of the logical qubit given the error rates of the physical qubits. The best partition depends on the physical error model. Change the error model and the best partition changes. The error model is derived from the hardware. The hardware is built by engineers. The engineers choose materials, geometries, operating conditions. The choices determine the error model. The error model determines the optimal partition. The partition is not given by the Schrödinger equation. It is determined by the full stack from materials to algorithm.

The stack includes calibration. Calibration is the process of characterizing the hardware: measuring qubit frequencies, gate fidelities, coherence times, crosstalk. The measurements are experiments. The experiments produce data. The data is fit to models. The models are parameterized descriptions of the hardware. The parameters are the error rates, coupling strengths, and detunings. The parameters determine the effective Hamiltonian. The effective Hamiltonian determines the dynamics. The dynamics determine what partitions are viable.

Viable partitions are those that can be maintained with the available resources. Resources include cooling power, control bandwidth, measurement fidelity, and computational power for classical feedback. Each resource is finite. Finiteness constrains the partition. The constraints are quantitative. They can be calculated. The calculations predict which logical encodings will work and which will not. The predictions are tested. Testing is done by running error correction protocols and measuring logical error rates. The measured rates are compared to predicted rates. Agreement validates the model. Disagreement reveals that the model is incomplete.

Incompleteness is corrected by adding parameters or changing the partition. The correction is iterative. It is the process of refining the description until predictions and measurements align. Alignment is the goal. The goal is achieved when the partition is chosen correctly. Correctness is empirical. It is determined by whether the partition leads to accurate predictions. Accuracy is tested by experiment. Experiment is the ultimate arbiter. The arbiter is physics. The physics is the validation of the partition.

Validation is not uniqueness. Multiple partitions can be empirically adequate. Adequacy is sufficient. Uniqueness is not required. The partition is chosen from the adequate set based on convenience, computational cost, or physical insight. Insight is what makes one partition preferable to another even when both are adequate. Preference is the judgment of the physicist. The physicist is part of the experiment. The experiment determines the partition. Determining the partition is not external to physics. It is part of physics. Physics is the systematic study of the natural world through observation and experiment. Observation requires choosing what to observe. Experiment requires choosing what to measure. Choosing is the partition. The partition is therefore unavoidable. It is built into the structure of physics as a practice.

Practice is how physics is done. How physics is done determines what is discovered. What is discovered is physics. The circle is not vicious. It is productive. Productivity is what makes science work. Science works by testing predictions. Predictions come from models. Models embody partitions. Partitions are tested by experiment. Experiments use apparatus. Apparatus embodies choices. Choices determine partitions. Partitions determine predictions. Predictions are tested. Testing is experiment. Experiment uses apparatus. The circle closes. The closing is not a problem. It is the structure of empirical science. Empirical science requires observation. Observation requires observers. Observers require partitions. Partitions require choices. Choices require observers. The circularity is inescapable. It is not a defect. It is a feature. The feature is that observers are part of physics. Being part of physics means observers are physical systems. Physical systems obey physical laws. Laws include thermodynamics. Thermodynamics includes the second law. The second law requires entropy increase. Entropy increase is dissipation. Dissipation is the cost. The cost is the thermodynamic signature of partition maintenance. Partition maintenance is observation. Observation is physics. Physics is what this book is about.

The circularity becomes productive when it is recognized as feedback. Feedback means the output of one stage becomes the input to the next. The output is experimental results. The input is theoretical models. Models predict results. Results test models. Testing refines models. Refinement improves predictions. Improvement is measured by agreement between prediction and experiment. Agreement validates the partition. Validation is not proof. It is confirmation that the partition is adequate for the present purpose. Adequacy is contingent. It depends on the precision required, the regime explored, and the questions asked. Questions change. New questions require new partitions. New partitions are discovered by experimentation. Experimentation is systematic variation of parameters, couplings, and observables. Systematic variation reveals which partitions are viable and which are not. Viable partitions are those that give stable, reproducible, predictive results. Stability is essential. Unstable partitions lead to irreproducible results. Irreproducible results are not science. They are noise. Noise is what partitions are designed to suppress. Suppressing noise is the function of the partition. The function is performed by isolation, cooling, shielding, and error correction. These are engineering operations. Engineering operations have costs. Costs are thermodynamic. Thermodynamics is physics. Physics includes observers. Observers perform engineering. Engineering maintains partitions. Partitions are physical. Physicality is measurable. Measurement is science.

This stack dependence is not a failure of quantum mechanics. It is a recognition that quantum mechanics is applied within a context. The context includes the hardware, the control system, the error model, the protocol, and the goals of the computation. The context determines the partition. The partition determines what is preserved and what is traced over. The preservation and tracing are not external to the physics. They are implemented by physical operations: measurements, resets, gates, cooling, isolation. The operations are controlled by the engineer. The engineer is part of the experimental setup. The setup is part of the physics.

The inclusion of the engineer in the physics is not anthropocentric. The engineer is a physical system. The engineer's decisions are physical processes. The decisions affect the apparatus. The apparatus affects the system. The system evolves according to the Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian includes interactions introduced by the apparatus. The apparatus is designed by the engineer. The engineer's design choices are boundary conditions on the Hamiltonian. Boundary conditions are part of the specification of a physical problem. They are chosen based on the question being asked. The question is chosen by the person asking it. The person is a physical system embedded in the world the question is about.

That embedding is not paradoxical. It is the normal state of affairs in experimental physics. The experimentalist is in the lab. The lab is part of the world. The experimentalist builds apparatus, runs experiments, records data, interprets results. These activities are physical. They involve energy, entropy, information. They are described by the same physical laws that govern the system under study. The experimentalist is not outside the laws. The experimentalist is inside them, using them to study other systems.

The study requires a partition. The partition separates the experimentalist from the system, or the apparatus from the environment, or the logical qubit from the physical substrate. The separation is practical. It is also physical. It is maintained by isolation, cooling, control, measurement, error correction. The maintenance is active. It requires resources. It requires work. The work is done by the experimentalist and the apparatus. The work is part of the physics of the partition.
