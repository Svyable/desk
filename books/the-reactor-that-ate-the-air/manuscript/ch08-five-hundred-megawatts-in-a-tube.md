# Chapter 8 — Five Hundred Megawatts in a Tube

A power reactor spreads itself out. A missile engine tries to disappear.

That contrast explains much of Pluto's technical violence. Civilian reactors can afford pressure vessels, coolant loops, shielding, containment, redundant pumps and thick concrete because their mission is to sit still and produce energy for years. A cruise missile cannot carry a building.

Tory II-C's thermal output was of the order of hundreds of megawatts. Livermore later summarized the successful 1964 test at roughly 500 megawatts. The engine had to produce that power inside a compact cylinder while allowing a huge airflow through the core.

Power density becomes the governing fact. Heat is useful only if it can move into the air quickly enough. The hotter the fuel surface and the greater the available transfer area, the more energy can enter the stream. But thermal gradients punish materials. Small channels increase surface area but also increase pressure drop and manufacturing complexity. More solid material helps structural strength but blocks flow. More void helps breathing but changes neutronics.

This is engineering by mutually hostile requirements.

The phrase 'nuclear-powered missile' can encourage a cartoon in which a reactor generates electricity and an electric motor somehow produces thrust. Pluto was much more direct. Reactor heat became jet exhaust without a turbine-generator intermediary. That removed conversion stages and their mass. It also removed the comforting distance between reactor and atmosphere.

The absence of a chemical fuel load promised extraordinary range. Fission energy density is so far beyond chemical combustion that endurance stops looking like a fuel-volume problem. But range is not freedom. A long-lived missile still needs actuators, electronics, guidance references, structural fatigue margin, lubricants, control authority and a reason to remain airborne.

The proposed SLAM vehicle was often described as capable of very long flights. Such endurance created strategic possibilities that chemical cruise missiles could not match, but it also created strange operational questions. Where would the missile loiter, if it loitered? How would it be recalled? What happens if guidance fails? What happens if a mission is aborted after the reactor has been operating? How does one test a weapon whose representative flight itself creates radiological and diplomatic consequences?

These are not objections external to engineering. They are system requirements.

The half-gigawatt figure also clarifies why shielding was such a brutal problem. Shielding a reactor against gamma rays and neutrons costs mass. In a crewed nuclear aircraft, shielding exists to keep people alive. An unmanned missile has no onboard humans to protect. That makes radical shielding reductions possible, but it transfers radiation tolerance requirements into electronics and creates consequences for people outside the vehicle.

It is easy to call this monstrous and stop. The more useful analysis asks what the design actually required. Components near the core had to tolerate intense radiation. Control mechanisms had to function in that environment. Guidance and avionics placement had to respect both radiation and aerodynamic packaging. Maintenance before reactor operation was one thing; service after a powered run was another.

Pluto's compactness was therefore not simply a triumph of miniaturization. It was a redistribution of burdens. The reactor became smaller because many functions normally surrounding a reactor were reduced, displaced or accepted as mission externalities.

This is a recurring pattern in military technology. A weapon can look radically efficient because it is allowed to spend resources that civilian machines must conserve: component life, noise, emissions, safety margin, recoverability, even the machine itself.

Five hundred megawatts in a tube is impressive.

The bill is everything the tube did not carry.
