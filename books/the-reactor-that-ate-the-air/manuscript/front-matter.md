# The Reactor That Ate the Air

## Project Pluto, the Nuclear Ramjet, and the Difference Between Impossible and Unwanted

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is a photograph from the Nevada Test Site that looks less like a weapon than a municipal utility dragged into the desert by mistake. A cylindrical machine sits on a rail carriage. Pipes, valves, pressure vessels and service structures crowd around it. Nothing in the picture advertises the central fact: this thing was meant to become the engine of a missile whose fuel supply, for practical purposes, was the atmosphere.

Project Pluto is frequently retold as one more grotesque Cold War fantasy, usually with a stack of lurid claims attached: a missile flying at Mach 3, skimming low over terrain, dropping nuclear weapons, irradiating everything beneath it, perhaps continuing to circle after its payload was gone. Some of those claims have documentary roots. Others have been simplified, inflated, or detached from design variants and operational assumptions. The strangest fact needs no inflation at all.

The reactor worked.

Lawrence Livermore designed and tested two nuclear ramjet reactors. Tory II-A ran in 1961. Tory II-C, a flight-engine prototype, reached roughly half a gigawatt of thermal power in 1964. Livermore's own historical account says all six tests of the two Tory reactors were conducted without failure. Project Pluto was then canceled.

That sequence is the book.

It is easy to mistake cancellation for scientific failure because history is usually written backward from deployment. Machines that enter service acquire pedigrees. Machines that do not become anecdotes. Pluto deserves the opposite treatment. It is valuable precisely because it forces a clean separation among four things that popular technological history tends to collapse: physical possibility, engineering success, military usefulness, and political acceptability.

A machine can clear the first two and still die.

This book follows the actual artifact rather than the myth. It begins with ramjet thermodynamics, reactor geometry, ceramic fuel elements, test-air infrastructure, remote handling and the Tory ground runs. Then it widens to the proposed Supersonic Low Altitude Missile around the reactor: guidance, structure, low-altitude flight, warhead carriage, basing, command and control, test planning, contamination, and the strategic competition from ballistic missiles and submarines. The point is not to rehabilitate Pluto. The point is to understand it well enough that ridicule is no longer doing analytical work.

The evidence posture is explicit. Declassified and official records establish Project Pluto, the Tory reactors, their successful ground tests, the development of a nuclear ramjet, and the cancellation of the program in 1964. Period technical reports establish much of the reactor engineering. Secondary reconstructions by participants and historians can fill gaps but are not silently promoted to primary evidence. Claims about exact proposed flight profiles, radiological effects, intended post-strike behavior, and colorful internal quotations are treated according to their source quality and design context.

This is also a book about secrecy without romance. Pluto was a classified strategic program. Classification did not make its engineers supernatural. They still needed ceramic that would survive heat, a test stand that could deliver the right mass flow, actuators that would work near a reactor, instrumentation that would return useful numbers, budgets, committees, and a military requirement. Secret technology still has to obey heat transfer.

That may be the most useful sentence in the whole story.
