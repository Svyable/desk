# Chapter 7 — Tory II-C

On May 20, 1964, the atmosphere at Jackass Flats was manufactured.

Tory II-C was fixed to its test vehicle in Nevada, so the test stand had to bring flight to the reactor. The full-power run was meant to reproduce the inlet conditions of roughly Mach 2.8 at sea level on a 100-degree day. Compressed air climbed to about 1,663 pounds per second. Inside the core, temperatures reached roughly 2,268 degrees Fahrenheit. Reactor power rose to about 461 megawatts and stayed there for five minutes before the operators deliberately shut it down.

Five minutes was not an endurance record. It was the amount of full-power operation the air system could furnish, and it was long enough to make Project Pluto much harder to dismiss as an extravagant sketch.

The reactor was not a paper engine and not merely a small physics experiment. Tory II-A had already shown that a nuclear ramjet core could operate. II-C forced the idea toward flight scale. Its design had to reconcile a reactor with an engine: a compact neutron economy, enormous airflow, extreme heat, pressure loss, vibration, controls, and a structure drilled through with passages because the atmosphere itself was the working fluid.

That is why the numbers matter more than the nickname. At full power, II-C was not briefly flashing an exotic laboratory effect. It was moving hundreds of kilograms of air through a working nuclear heat source every second under deliberately flight-like conditions. When the five-minute run ended, it ended with a manual scram rather than a structural failure. The subsequent inspection reported no blocked passages, obvious corrosion, or damage requiring the success to be explained away.

The word *prototype* still needs discipline. II-C was not an operational SLAM engine waiting to be bolted into a finished missile. A flying weapon still needed an inlet and airframe that survived sustained low-altitude supersonic flight, launch and acceleration to ramjet takeover, guidance, controls, nuclear-hard electronics, a credible test program, maintenance and storage procedures, and command arrangements for a vehicle whose propulsion system became intensely radioactive by doing its job. The reactor had crossed a demonstration threshold. The weapon had not.

That distinction is more interesting than saying Pluto either worked or failed. Hardware programs do not mature as single propositions. A successful subsystem retires particular doubts while leaving others untouched, and sometimes exposes new ones. Tory II-C made the question *can the reactor heat enough air without destroying itself?* considerably less useful. It did almost nothing to answer whether the United States should build fleets of nuclear ramjet cruise missiles, how to flight-test them without creating a radiological problem of its own, or whether the strategic advantages still justified the system by the time the engineering caught up with the idea.

There is a strong counterargument to reading the remaining integration work as fatal. Difficult airframes, guidance systems, launchers, electronics, and maintenance regimes are exactly what large aerospace programs are built to solve. A ground test cannot be faulted for failing to prove a flight program it was never designed to complete. If military demand had remained strong enough, II-C's success was evidence for continuing, not stopping.

That is what makes the timing so revealing. Project Pluto was not canceled because the desert test exposed a reactor that could not work. The more awkward possibility was arriving: the reactor could work well enough that the country now had to decide whether it still wanted the machine around it.

A failed engine would have made the decision easy.

Tory II-C did not grant that convenience.
