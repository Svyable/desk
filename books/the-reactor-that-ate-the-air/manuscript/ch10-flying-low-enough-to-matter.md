# Chapter 10 — Flying Low Enough to Matter

Altitude was not a detail. It was the weapon's argument.

The attraction of a supersonic low-altitude missile lay in the geometry of radar and interception. A vehicle close to terrain can hide below distant radar horizons and reduce warning time. Terrain clutter complicates detection and tracking. At very high speed, whatever warning remains must be converted into a firing solution almost immediately.

That is the offensive logic. The engineering bill arrives with the same terrain.

Air density is much higher near the surface than at strategic-bomber altitudes. Dynamic pressure scales with density and the square of velocity. At Mach numbers around three, even a small change in density, gust, bank angle or terrain-following command can generate substantial loads. The vehicle's structure, control surfaces and actuators have to keep up.

The phrase '500 feet' appears frequently in popular accounts of SLAM, but exact altitude claims should be tied to particular design documents and mission segments. What matters for the book is not one sacred number. The program's concept depended on sustained very-low-altitude supersonic penetration, and that requirement was inherently harsher than high-altitude cruise.

At low level, the guidance system sees the world arrive fast. A hill is not an object in the future for long. Terrain-following requires reliable sensing, an accurate reference of what the terrain should look like, and control laws that do not drive the missile into the ground while attempting to remain beneath defenses.

The proposed solution belonged to a broader family of terrain-contour navigation methods then under development. A stored map of terrain elevation could be compared with radar-altimeter measurements along the route, allowing the vehicle to estimate and correct its position. The concept later matured in other cruise-missile systems under names such as TERCOM. For Pluto, the challenge was doing enough of that work with early-1960s electronics at extreme speed and over enormous distances.

This is a useful place to resist technological hindsight. Modern readers carry satellite navigation in a pocket. The engineers did not. Inertial systems drift. Celestial fixes are difficult at low altitude and high speed. Radio navigation can be jammed or unavailable over hostile territory. Terrain offers a reference that the enemy cannot easily move, but the missile must measure and recognize it.

Now add a reactor.

Electronics near a high-power fission core face neutron and gamma environments that ordinary avionics do not. Shielding helps but costs mass. Distance helps but costs volume and airframe arrangement. Component choice, redundancy and physical placement become part of nuclear engineering even when the component is 'only' a guidance computer.

The strategic requirement therefore tied together radar evasion, aerodynamics, computing and radiation hardness. None of those problems was individually mystical. Their simultaneous satisfaction was the project.

Low flight also shaped the mythology of Pluto's blast and shock effects. A large supersonic vehicle passing near the surface would produce intense acoustic and pressure signatures. Popular retellings sometimes describe the shock wave itself as a weapon capable of killing people along the route. That claim should be treated quantitatively rather than repeated. Sonic booms can be severe; whether a specific overpressure at a specific altitude and vehicle geometry is lethal requires calculation.

Again, the truth does not need embellishment. The machine was already a strategic nuclear missile powered by an operating reactor and intended to penetrate at supersonic speed near terrain.

The exact overpressure can wait for a source.
