# Chapter 9 — The Missile Around the Reactor

By the time the reactor worked, the reactor was no longer the hardest question.

The proposed Supersonic Low Altitude Missile—SLAM—had to turn Tory from a stationary heat source into a strategic delivery system. That meant building an aircraft around an engine whose preferred operating condition was fast, low and hot.

The missile would not take off from rest under ramjet power. Ramjets require forward speed before they become effective, so the launch concept needed a separate acceleration stage. A booster would drive the vehicle to the regime where the inlet could compress enough air for the nuclear ramjet to take over.

That handoff is a classic integration seam. Boost phase and cruise phase impose different loads and control requirements. The inlet that behaves correctly at cruise may behave badly during transients. The reactor must move from launch condition to sustained high-power operation without introducing a failure mode that no ground test fully reproduces.

Then comes the airframe.

Low-altitude supersonic flight is punishing. The atmosphere is dense, which is useful if your ramjet eats air, but density multiplies aerodynamic forces. Gusts and terrain-induced disturbances arrive quickly. Skin heating and structural loads rise. Small guidance errors at high speed become large geographic errors in seconds.

The intended vehicle therefore had to be more than a carrier for a reactor and nuclear payload. It was a continuously self-correcting structure surviving an environment chosen partly because it made interception difficult.

This is where the nuclear engine's endurance becomes almost perverse. A chemical missile's limited range bounds the time over which vibration, actuator wear, navigation drift and component fatigue can accumulate. Pluto's reactor promised to remove that natural bound. Every other subsystem then had to become worthy of the engine's endurance.

The payload concept changed over time and should not be frozen into one canonical cartoon. Public descriptions commonly portray SLAM as a multiwarhead system able to traverse target areas and release several nuclear weapons. Some accounts add claims that after dropping its payload it could continue flying as a radiological or kinetic nuisance. Those claims require variant-by-variant sourcing. The strategic core is simpler: long endurance plus low-altitude penetration could, in principle, allow one vehicle to approach and attack multiple targets on a complicated route.

But complexity is never free. Multiple targets mean more navigation. More navigation means more dependence on terrain reference, maps, sensors and computing. Longer missions mean more exposure to failure. A vehicle designed to be hard for enemy defenses to intercept may also be hard for its owner to manage once launched.

The reactor solved the energy problem so thoroughly that command became visible as the scarce resource.

This inversion is one reason Pluto remains relevant. Frontier technology often removes a familiar constraint and then discovers that the removed constraint had been hiding several others. Cheap launch exposes traffic management. Abundant computation exposes verification. Extreme endurance exposes control.

The missile around the reactor was therefore not packaging. It was the real strategic system.

Tory could heat air.

SLAM had to know where to go.
