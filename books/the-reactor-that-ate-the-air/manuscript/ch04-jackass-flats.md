# Chapter 4 — Jackass Flats

A secret engine still needs plumbing.

Project Pluto's test site at Jackass Flats is one of the best physical demonstrations of that rule. The proposed missile was elegant in the abstract because it would carry no conventional propulsion fuel. The ground test was the opposite of elegant. To simulate flight while the reactor remained stationary, Livermore had to build an artificial atmosphere under pressure.

The Nevada Test Site already hosted nuclear work with infrastructure, security and empty space difficult to reproduce elsewhere. Pluto added its own peculiar industrial landscape. High-pressure air storage, heaters, piping, controls, a test vehicle, remote observation, handling facilities and radiological provisions existed because a ramjet cannot be meaningfully tested by letting it idle on a concrete pad.

The air had to arrive with the right pressure, temperature and mass flow. Livermore's historical account says the Tory II-C experiment required hundreds of tons of heated air to sustain a run of only minutes. That number is more revealing than the missile's imagined range. It shows how much hidden infrastructure is required to make a self-contained flight concept stand still.

This is a standard trick of aerospace development. A wind tunnel is a machine that creates flight conditions around an object that is not flying. A rocket test stand restrains an engine whose natural behavior is to leave. Pluto's air system turned the desert into the missing forward velocity.

But the reactor changed what happened after the test. A conventional engine can be walked up to after shutdown once temperatures and propellant hazards are managed. A fission engine accumulates radioactive inventory during operation. The most interesting hardware becomes the least approachable at the moment engineers most want to inspect it.

Remote handling was therefore not an optional safety flourish. It was part of the development loop. If a component failed, somebody had to learn why without casually dismantling a hot reactor by hand. Shielding, distance, manipulators and decay time entered the schedule.

This is one reason exotic propulsion programs leave such large institutional footprints. A PowerPoint miracle engine can be tiny. A real nuclear engine creates buildings.

Project Pluto needed a place where radioactive machinery could be assembled, tested, moved and eventually disassembled. Decades later, cleanup records for the Nevada site would preserve another kind of program history: contaminated facilities, fuel-handling areas, demolition decisions and corrective-action documentation. The weapon never flew, but the ground it occupied had an afterlife.

That afterlife matters to the epistemology of secret programs. People sometimes imagine classification as a cloak draped over an object. In reality, complex classified systems produce dependencies outside the central secret. Concrete gets poured. Roads get built. specialized air systems are procured. Waste is characterized. Buildings are transferred to new uses. Environmental restoration programs inherit old decisions long after the original mission has become irrelevant.

This does not mean every classified program becomes easy to discover. It means physical programs have metabolism.

Jackass Flats also makes the scale of the test problem legible. To prove a nuclear ramjet on the ground, the engineers constructed a temporary thermodynamic world around it. The compressed-air farm substituted for Mach-number flight. The heater substituted for inlet conditions. The rail car substituted for an airframe. Remote handling substituted for maintainability.

Each substitute retired uncertainty in one domain while leaving another domain untouched.

This is why the successful Tory tests could be both impressive and insufficient. The test stand could demonstrate that the reactor produced the intended thermal performance under controlled flow. It could not prove that a full missile would navigate across continents at low altitude, survive aerodynamic loads for the mission duration, manage reactor control through maneuvers, satisfy command-and-control requirements, or be tested politically in a representative end-to-end flight.

The machine at Jackass Flats was not the missile.

It was the part of the missile the engineers could force the Nevada desert to impersonate.
