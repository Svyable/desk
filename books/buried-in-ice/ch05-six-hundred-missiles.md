# Six Hundred Missiles

Six hundred is the number that makes Iceworm feel real and unreal at the same time.

It is large enough to imply a system, not an experiment. It is also so large that the first responsible question is not where the missiles would go but what infrastructure each missile quietly drags behind it. Weapons histories often count the glamorous object and let the support architecture disappear. Iceworm becomes clearer when the counting is reversed.

A missile that is supposed to move beneath an ice sheet requires a route. A route requires excavation, structural support, inspection and repair. Movement requires power and rolling stock. Crews require shelter, food, heat, water, sanitation and medical support. Nuclear warheads require security and handling procedures. Launch sites require communications, surveying and command links. A dispersed network requires spare parts in the right place when weather and distance make the wrong place functionally unreachable.

The strategic appeal grew from dispersion. If Soviet planners could not know where a given missile was located, they would have to attack many possible positions. But each false target still had to be believable enough to complicate attack planning, and each real route had to remain operable. Dispersion therefore converts survivability into maintenance geography.

The proposal's enormous footprint matters for another reason. Greenland was not American territory. The legal foundation for U.S. military presence came from agreements with Denmark, and Danish nuclear policy made hidden strategic expansion politically hazardous. The larger Iceworm became on paper, the harder it was to treat as an ordinary extension of an existing base. A continental-scale missile system would have been not only an engineering project but a transformation of Greenland's strategic status.

Later controversy over what Danish officials did or did not know is easy to overstate in both directions. It is not credible to portray Camp Century itself as an invisible installation unknown to Denmark. Danish liaisons and the legal framework for U.S. activity matter. But knowledge that Americans are operating an Arctic research and engineering camp is not equivalent to informed consent for a 600-missile buried network. The evidentiary work is to reconstruct who knew which layer, when, and with what authority.

That layered knowledge is more interesting than a binary secret. Governments are made of compartments. One official may understand the engineering. Another may know the diplomatic boundary. A liaison can observe a facility without receiving the strategic planning papers that explain how the sponsor hopes to scale it. Later investigators can therefore find evidence of both awareness and exclusion without contradiction.

The number six hundred should be treated accordingly. It is documented planning ambition, not proof of emplacement. Its value is architectural. It tells us the Army was not merely wondering whether a few missiles might be sheltered in snow. It was imagining the ice sheet itself as a weapon system: geography converted into uncertainty, with tunnels doing the work that mobility and ocean depth performed elsewhere.

That is the scale at which the moving ice becomes fatal to the premise. A local deformation can be repaired. A network whose strategic value depends on vastness must absorb deformation everywhere. The same multiplication that makes targeting harder makes maintenance harder too.

Six hundred missiles sound like deterrence. Six hundred maintenance problems sound like Greenland.
