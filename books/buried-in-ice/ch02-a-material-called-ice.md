# A Material Called Ice

A glacier is easy to misunderstand if you meet it first as scenery.

From a distance the Greenland Ice Sheet offers all the visual assurances of permanence. It is enormous, cold, old and heavy. Its surface can look like land with the color removed. That resemblance was strategically useful. If the ice sheet could be treated as terrain, then engineers might place roads, tunnels, storage and weapons inside it the way another military might bury them in rock.

But an ice sheet is not frozen ground. It is a moving body.

Snow falls, compacts into firn and eventually becomes glacial ice. Under sufficient stress, that ice deforms. It also flows downslope. Near the surface, the porous firn behaves differently from the denser ice below it. Heat from occupied structures alters local conditions. Excavation redistributes stress. Tunnels cut into snow and firn begin changing from the moment they are completed. The geometry of the site is therefore not a fixed background to the engineering problem. It is the engineering problem.

Camp Century was useful because it converted this general truth into maintenance records, warped structures and measurable closure rates. The Army's Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory did not need a philosophy of impermanence. It needed to know how wide to cut a trench, how much overburden an arch could tolerate, how quickly a room would narrow, whether a railway remained serviceable, and how often crews would have to excavate or reset structures. The answers could be written in inches, feet, labor hours and damaged steel.

There was a seductive asymmetry in the missile idea. Enemy intelligence would have difficulty observing motion beneath the ice. Soviet targeting would have difficulty knowing which launch position held a missile at a given moment. The proposed network could be large enough to make destruction expensive. Yet concealment required the same medium that created the maintenance burden. The more tunnel one built to gain strategic uncertainty, the more moving ice one had to manage.

This is a recurring feature of exotic military infrastructure. The feature that makes a location attractive is often the feature that makes it hard to operate. Deep ocean hides submarines and crushes hulls. Space offers line of sight and punishes mass. Deserts offer emptiness and attack seals, electronics and humans with heat and dust. Greenland offered concealment inside a continent-sized white volume whose material never agreed to remain where it had been cut.

The ice also confused timescales. A tunnel can perform well long enough to encourage extrapolation. An experimental railway may work. A reactor room may operate. A camp may survive several seasons. None of these demonstrates that tens of thousands of kilometers of strategic infrastructure can be maintained for decades at acceptable cost.

That difference between demonstration and system is where ambitious programs often get into trouble. A prototype proves that an object can exist. A deployed network has to prove that the object can keep existing while everything around it degrades, shifts, breaks, becomes obsolete or encounters politics.

The Greenland Ice Sheet added another clock no planner in the early 1960s could responsibly model the way a glaciologist does now: anthropogenic climate change. The Camp Century disposal assumption was shaped by a local observation that looked safe on the relevant planning horizon. Snow accumulation would bury the site deeper. If that continued, abandoned structures and wastes would remain sealed inside the ice.

The assumption was not absurd in 1967. That is what makes it worth studying.

Institutional mistakes are easiest to dismiss when they depend on obvious stupidity. Camp Century's afterlife is harder. Engineers had a real accumulation regime in front of them. The climate trajectory that could reverse the long-term fate of the site was not yet part of ordinary infrastructure accounting. The buried camp became a bet that one environmental regime would remain the governing regime for longer than responsibility itself.

The book's central object is therefore not a secret tunnel or a reactor. It is a category error measured in time. The Army needed ice to be terrain. Later it needed ice to be a tomb. Scientists would eventually use it as an archive.

It was never only one of those things.
