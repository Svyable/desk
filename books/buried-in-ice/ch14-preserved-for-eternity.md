# Preserved for Eternity

“Preserved for eternity” is the phrase that makes Camp Century sound like a parable written by climate change.

Parables are dangerous around technical history because they sand away what people actually knew. The operators who left Camp Century did not possess twenty-first-century regional climate models. They were not choosing between a quantified 1967 projection of permanent accumulation and a quantified alternative in which surface melting increased under anthropogenic forcing. They saw a site in an accumulation regime and a base already being buried by snow.

The disposal assumption was therefore not irrational. It was conditional.

That is exactly why the case matters.

In 2016, William Colgan and colleagues asked what happens if the condition changes. Their model experiments did not produce one inevitable year in which Camp Century becomes exposed. One forcing maintained positive surface mass balance through 2100; another produced a transition toward net ablation within the modeled period. The spread is important. A responsible reading is not “science says the base will emerge in 2090.” The result is that the historical assumption of indefinite sequestration is no longer safe enough to go unexamined.

Surface mass balance is the arithmetic of accumulation and loss at the ice-sheet surface. Snow adds mass. Melt, runoff, sublimation and other processes remove it. A site can remain buried even in a warming climate if accumulation continues to exceed loss. The threshold matters because once persistent net ablation dominates, the long-term direction of burial reverses.

Yet direct exposure is only one pathway. Surface meltwater can percolate downward through firn. Refrozen layers change hydrology. Contaminants can potentially move before a bulldozer-sized object reaches daylight. This means the intuitive photograph—the rusting camp emerging from white ice—may arrive long after the first environmentally relevant changes.

The word eternity also obscures political time. Governments rarely make decisions on infinite horizons. They make decisions under statutes, budgets, ownership agreements and practical expectations. A site can be “permanently” disposed of in an administrative sense while remaining temporary in a geological sense. The mismatch is common enough that we barely notice it until climate change moves the boundary.

Camp Century provides a particularly clean demonstration because the medium of disposal is also the medium of measurement. The ice sheet records past climate in its layers. The basal sediment records episodes when the ice was absent. Modern radar records the camp's present burial. Climate models estimate possible future surface conditions. The repository contains its own critique.

This should not be converted into an accusation that every 1960s engineer secretly knew Greenland would warm as it has. The stronger indictment is institutional rather than personal. No one owns an assumption forever. When evidence changes, responsibility should be capable of changing with it.

A disposal plan without a reinspection trigger is a promise that future evidence will not matter.

Camp Century is still buried. That fact is sometimes used as evidence that the old assumption remains correct. It proves only that accumulation has won so far. The relevant question is not whether the camp is visible today. It is whether the environmental regime that made abandonment acceptable can still be treated as stationary over the lifetime of the waste.

Eternity is not a duration engineers can validate. It is what people say when they have stopped scheduling the next measurement.
