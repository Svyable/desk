# Buried in Ice

## Camp Century, Project Iceworm, and the Future Greenland Refused to Keep

### Sven Hardy Benson

There are military ruins that survive because stone is durable. Camp Century survived for the opposite reason: because its builders trusted a material that never stops moving.

The United States Army cut a city into the Greenland Ice Sheet in 1959. The public story was audacious enough on its own. Engineers would learn how to live, build, drill, generate power and run machinery beneath Arctic snow. A portable nuclear reactor would supply electricity. Scientists would work from one of the strangest laboratories on Earth. Film crews were invited. The camp was not a rumor.

Behind that visible achievement sat a less public strategic question. Could the ice sheet hide a dispersed ballistic-missile force? Project Iceworm imagined an enormous tunnel network and mobile missiles under Greenland. The scheme did not become an operational weapons system. The ice moved too much, the engineering burden was severe, service politics mattered, and Denmark's nuclear policy made the political premise unstable. Yet the feasibility work was real enough to leave documents, machinery, tunnels, waste and an argument about what a sovereign ally had actually been told.

Then the story folded over on itself.

The ice core drilled at Camp Century reached the material beneath the ice sheet. Decades later, scientists found preserved plant remains and geochemical evidence in those basal sediments. Northwestern Greenland had been ice-free during warm periods of the Pleistocene. The military site built on an assumption of durable ice helped produce evidence that the ice was not durable on geological timescales at all.

A second reversal followed. When the Army left, much of the camp remained in place. The practical assumption was that continuing snowfall and accumulation would carry the abandoned infrastructure and wastes deeper into the ice. Climate warming changed the horizon of that assumption. In 2016, William Colgan and colleagues modeled the possibility that Camp Century could eventually pass from net accumulation toward net ablation, creating a pathway by which buried chemical, biological and radiological wastes could be remobilized. That paper did not predict an imminent toxic eruption from the ice. It did something more interesting: it showed that a decommissioning decision can contain an unstated climate model.

This book is about that hidden model.

It is not a story in which the secret missile base was fully built. It was not. It is not a story in which Denmark knew nothing about Camp Century. Danish officials knew the camp existed; the harder question is what they knew about the strategic program for which the camp supplied engineering evidence. It is not a story in which the nuclear reactor was simply abandoned in place. Major reactor components were removed. Nor is it a claim that Camp Century's wastes are already entering Greenland's environment at dangerous concentrations.

The stronger story survives those corrections.

Camp Century joins three kinds of time that institutions usually keep separate: the short time of military planning, the middle time of infrastructure and contamination, and the deep time recorded by ice and sediment. The Army wanted the ice to behave like ground. It behaved like ice. Later scientists wanted the ice to behave like an archive. It did that too, but what the archive said was inconvenient: this landscape has changed far more radically than the builders' disposal assumptions allowed.

The ice did not keep the secret. It kept the evidence.
