# Waste Is a Time Theory

A waste site is a prediction about the future disguised as a place.

The prediction can be modest. This drum will remain intact long enough. This trench will stay dry enough. This warning sign will still be legible when somebody needs it. At Camp Century the prediction was grander because the container was not engineered concrete. It was the Greenland Ice Sheet.

The camp's wastes did not all enter the ice in the same way. Sewage and grey water went to sumps. Reactor-associated liquid waste was discharged separately. Diesel was stored. Structural material remained in tunnels. Paints, electrical equipment and other building components may contain PCBs, though a modern site-specific chemical inventory is necessarily reconstructed rather than directly sampled from every buried room. Each category has a different mobility, persistence and hazard.

The distinction becomes crucial once meltwater appears. Direct surface exposure is the most intuitive image: climate warms, ice melts, a buried base emerges. That image is probably too simple. Colgan and colleagues emphasized that percolating meltwater could interact with buried material before the surface physically ablates down to the camp. In porous firn, liquid water can travel downward. Contaminants do not have to wait for a roof to appear in daylight.

This creates an awkward timing problem for remediation. Act too early and one may undertake an extraordinarily expensive Arctic excavation before there is a demonstrated pathway to environmental release. Act too late and the contaminants may have begun moving through a medium that is much harder to control than a conventional soil site.

The appropriate response is not alarm but surveillance with explicit triggers. Where is the waste now? How fast is the ice carrying it horizontally? How deep are the relevant structures and frozen liquid reservoirs? How is surface mass balance changing? Are melt layers thickening? Can radar distinguish infrastructure well enough to support a modern map? Which contaminants could be sampled indirectly, and which require drilling?

These questions turn decommissioning into observation science.

They also expose a political asymmetry. The generation that benefited from the strategic experiment did not have to pay for complete removal because burial looked stable. A future generation may face the opposite decision after climate change alters the premise. Responsibility becomes temporally separated from benefit.

Camp Century is not unique in that respect. Coastal military sites, chemical dumps, mine tailings and nuclear facilities all contain design assumptions about sea level, precipitation, permafrost, storms or groundwater. Climate change converts some of those assumptions into moving variables. The result is not merely more environmental hazard. It is more inherited uncertainty.

The useful lesson is therefore narrower than “do not bury waste in ice.” That situation is too unusual to generalize. The general lesson is that every claim of permanent containment should be translated into the environmental variable that makes permanence possible.

At Camp Century the variable is surface mass balance and the thermal-hydrologic behavior of firn. Once stated that way, eternity becomes a number that can be measured.
