# Who Owns a Buried Mistake

The physical problem at Camp Century is easier than the ownership problem.

Physical questions can be framed in measurements. How deep are the tunnels? Where has the ice carried them? What contaminants remain? What is the local surface mass balance? How quickly can meltwater penetrate the firn? Even when the answers are uncertain, the uncertainty has units.

Responsibility has treaties.

The United States built and operated Camp Century in Greenland under the 1951 defense agreement with Denmark. That agreement addressed American property and contemplated removal or disposal after consultation. The later climate problem was not imagined in those terms. If waste once considered safely sequestered becomes mobile because the host environment changes decades later, who has the duty to act?

The obvious candidates do not map neatly onto the history. The United States created the camp and much of the waste. Denmark was the sovereign party to the defense relationship at the time. Greenland now possesses self-government and bears the local environmental stakes. Climate change is global, not a force attributable to one historical actor. The site has moved with the ice. The original strategic program never became the deployed system it was meant to test.

None of those facts dissolves responsibility. Together they make responsibility expensive to state.

The 2016 Camp Century paper called attention to the potential for a new kind of climate-driven political dispute: pollutants once regarded as immobilized can become remobilized as environmental baselines shift. The legal system may inherit a physical problem that did not exist in the same way when the relevant treaty language was written.

There is also a colonial dimension that legal abstractions can hide. Greenlandic people did not occupy the same bargaining position as Washington and Copenhagen when Cold War installations were planned. A remediation regime that treats the issue only as a bilateral accounting problem risks reproducing the structure that made Greenland a strategic surface before it was treated as a political subject.

At the same time, modern analysis should not invent a current emergency simply to sharpen the justice argument. Camp Century remains buried. Exposure pathways are prospective and model-dependent. The correct response to uncertainty is not to declare contamination already occurring at harmful levels. It is to make sure uncertainty does not become an excuse for having no monitoring plan.

Ownership can begin with information. Establish a shared inventory. Reconstruct the historical record. Maintain updated radar mapping. Define thresholds for drilling and sampling. Agree in advance how evidence of contaminant mobility would change responsibility. Those acts are cheaper than emergency remediation and politically easier before a crisis creates incentives to deny causation.

Camp Century's administrative afterlife is therefore a test of whether institutions can inherit their predecessors' uncertainty without inheriting their denial.

A buried mistake does not need one owner. It needs someone who is not allowed to say that burial ended the matter.
