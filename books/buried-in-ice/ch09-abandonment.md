# Abandonment

Abandonment is a misleadingly clean word.

Camp Century did not vanish when regular operations ended. A camp cut into ice cannot be demolished the way a warehouse can. Some equipment left. The PM-2A reactor's principal components were removed. Other structures, tanks, pipes, wiring, building materials, sewage and process wastes remained. The tunnel network itself became an object inside the ice.

The 2016 reconstruction by Colgan and colleagues gives the leftovers uncomfortable scale: roughly 9,200 tonnes of physical infrastructure, an estimated 200,000 liters of diesel fuel, at least 24 million liters of grey water including sewage, probable PCB-bearing materials, and radiological waste associated with reactor operations. These are estimates assembled from historical records and modeling, not a contemporary excavation inventory. The distinction matters because the site is still buried.

The disposal logic depended on accumulation. Snowfall would continue. The camp would descend relative to the surface as new snow compacted above it. The ice would isolate what remained.

That is a familiar form of environmental reasoning: containment by expected continuity.

Every disposal site contains a forecast, whether anyone writes one down or not. A landfill assumes something about groundwater and liners. A tailings dam assumes something about rainfall and maintenance. A buried chemical site assumes something about land use. Camp Century assumed a particular future for the ice sheet. The waste could be left because the medium surrounding it was expected to keep doing what it had been doing.

On the timescale of the camp's operators, that expectation had evidence behind it. The site lay in an accumulation zone. The idea that the base would simply emerge a few years later was not credible. Indeed, Camp Century remains buried decades after abandonment.

The failure, if it becomes one, is subtler. Infrastructure outlives the planning regime that created it. Climate baselines move. Governments reorganize. Records scatter. The original polluter may no longer control the place. The people who inherit the site are asked to manage a forecast they never made.

This is why the phrase “preserved for eternity,” associated with period assumptions about the camp, is so potent and so dangerous. Nobody needs to believe the builders literally performed an eternal forecast. The phrase captures the operational mentality: burial by accumulating snow was treated as durable enough that retrieval was not built into decommissioning.

Once climate change enters the story, the buried camp stops being only a historical artifact. It becomes a delayed governance problem. If future surface mass balance turns negative at the site, melt and runoff can alter how wastes move. Meltwater percolation may matter before direct surface exposure. The relevant horizon becomes decades to centuries, not the last day a soldier walked out of a tunnel.

The most interesting residue may not be the one that sounds most frightening. The reactor gives the story its atomic glow, but persistent organic pollutants such as PCBs may be environmentally more important. Diesel may remain in tanks or have leaked. Sewage and grey water form another class entirely. The wastes behave differently and should not be collapsed into a single toxic mass.

Abandonment did not freeze Camp Century in 1967.

It merely transferred the experiment from the Army to time.
