# The Reactor Arrives

The PM-2A reactor is one of the details that makes Camp Century easy to mythologize, so it is worth being exact.

The Army installed a portable nuclear power plant under the Greenland ice. That is extraordinary enough. It does not need embellishment.

The logic was logistical. A remote Arctic camp consumed fuel simply to remain habitable. Every gallon delivered across the ice carried transport cost and risk. Nuclear power promised concentrated energy in a place where resupply was difficult. Army engineering reports from the period treated PM-2A as part of a broader military nuclear-power experiment: could compact reactors serve isolated installations where diesel logistics were punishing?

The reactor also demonstrated what Iceworm would demand. A buried strategic network required power that did not depend on constant visible convoys. Nuclear generation fit the fantasy of autonomous infrastructure hidden under snow. But the plant simultaneously exposed how hostile the environment was to machinery expected to keep exact dimensions.

The reactor room lived inside the same creeping medium as everything else. Snow and ice did not care that the equipment inside was nuclear. Structural deformation imposed maintenance on the enclosure. The point is not that the reactor suffered a cinematic accident at Camp Century. It did not. The plant operated, was shut down, and major components were removed. The important fact is quieter: even a high-value installation designed for reliability could not make its surrounding architecture stationary.

The plant ran for roughly thirty-three months before deactivation in July 1963. The reactor was later removed in 1964. The surviving record also matters for what was left behind. The 2016 Colgan study estimates that tens of thousands of liters of radioactive liquid waste associated with the reactor system were discharged into an unlined sump in the ice during operations. The paper gives an estimated bulk radioactivity at disposal of about 1.2 billion becquerels. That number sounds alarming until it is placed beside other Arctic nuclear contamination, including the much larger 1968 Thule bomber accident. Scale matters both ways: the waste was real, and it should not be inflated into a buried reactor core.

This distinction between removed machinery and abandoned process waste becomes central later. Decommissioning is rarely one action. Fuel can be removed while contaminated liquids remain. A reactor vessel can leave while pipes, structures, sumps and ordinary industrial materials stay. The phrase “the reactor was removed” is true and incomplete in exactly the way “Camp Century was a research station” was true and incomplete.

Nuclear technology also changes the duration of administrative responsibility. A diesel generator can be scrapped and forgotten in ways a radiological system cannot. Records, inventories and disposal decisions acquire a longer afterlife because the hazard is partly invisible. Yet Camp Century's deeper legacy may prove to be chemical rather than radiological. Colgan and colleagues argue that PCBs associated with building materials may be the most consequential waste class at the site. That conclusion is almost disappointingly ordinary. The atomic city may be remembered for paint, fuel and sewage.

There is a habit in historical writing to let the most exotic object dominate the risk story. Camp Century resists it. The reactor was dramatic, the missile plan was classified, but the environmental afterlife depends on mundane materials distributed through a site that was never designed for retrieval.

The reactor arrived because compact nuclear power solved a logistics problem. Its removal solved only part of what the reactor had brought into the ice.
