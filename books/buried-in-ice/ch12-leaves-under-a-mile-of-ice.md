# Leaves Under a Mile of Ice

The Camp Century basal sediment contained plant remains delicate enough to offend intuition.

A mile of ice suggests grinding, crushing and erasure. Yet researchers examining the old core material found preserved twigs, leaves and other biological traces in sediment taken from beneath the ice sheet. The significance was not that ancient Greenland had once been green in the vague way a popular headline might imply. The significance was that a specific location now buried beneath roughly 1.4 kilometers of ice had experienced ice-free conditions during the Pleistocene.

The 2021 study did not rest on one leaf. Its argument combined independent evidence. Plant macrofossils and biomarkers indicated terrestrial ecosystems. Stable isotopes in pore ice suggested precipitation at elevations lower than the present ice-sheet surface. Cosmogenic nuclides helped constrain periods when the material had been exposed rather than shielded beneath thick ice. Luminescence and stratigraphy contributed additional timing information. The resulting history was complicated: ice, absence of ice, vegetation, renewed glaciation.

That complexity is more useful than a slogan about Greenland melting before.

Past ice-sheet loss does not mechanically predict the timing or extent of future loss. Orbital forcing, greenhouse-gas concentrations, ocean conditions and the geometry of the ice sheet differ across episodes. Paleoclimate evidence constrains sensitivity; it does not hand us a calendar. The Camp Century sediment is therefore evidence against absolute stability, not evidence for a specific twenty-first-century deadline.

This distinction matters because the site's environmental legacy can otherwise become rhetorically overdriven. One can jump from fossil leaves to “the buried base will soon emerge.” That is not what the evidence shows. The 2016 waste-remobilization paper modeled a plausible future shift in local surface mass balance under a high-emissions scenario and emphasized uncertainty between climate-model forcings. The 2021 basal-sediment work addressed geological history on timescales orders of magnitude longer.

The books worth writing are often built from evidence streams that do not line up neatly. Here the mismatch is the point.

The Army's decommissioning assumption operated on an institutional horizon: decades. The 2016 climate study extends the question toward the end of this century and beyond. The basal sediment extends it into deep time. Put together, they show how the word permanent changes meaning depending on who is using it.

To an engineer designing a temporary military camp, permanent may mean safely beyond the service life of the installation. To a regulator, it may mean beyond foreseeable release. To a glaciologist, the ice sheet is a dynamic system with states that change over millennia. To the preserved plant under Camp Century, a million years is merely enough time for ice to arrive, leave, and arrive again.

The military base and the plant fossil therefore belong in the same book for a precise reason. Both are records of assumptions about ice.

One was built by people who needed the ice to hold still.

The other survived because, over deep time, it did not.
