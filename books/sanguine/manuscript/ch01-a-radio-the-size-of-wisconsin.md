# 1 — A Radio the Size of Wisconsin

The first strange thing about Project Sanguine is that the Navy was not trying to build a radio station in Wisconsin.

It was trying to make Wisconsin part of the radio.

That sounds like a line written after the fact to make a Cold War engineering program seem sinister. It is actually the least mystical way to describe the problem. A ballistic-missile submarine was useful because the ocean made it hard to find. The ocean also made it hard to call.

Radio is not one thing. Frequency changes what the world looks like to a signal. At familiar broadcast frequencies, seawater is a wall. A submarine can raise an antenna, trail one near the surface, or come shallow enough to receive. Every solution spends some of the concealment that made the submarine valuable in the first place.

Go low enough in frequency and the bargain changes. Extremely low frequency energy can penetrate seawater farther than ordinary radio. But the wavelength becomes grotesque. At tens of hertz, you are no longer designing an antenna in the ordinary mental picture of an antenna. The efficient object wants to be comparable to distances that belong on maps.

So the Navy proposed a bad antenna on a magnificent scale.

Long grounded conductors would drive current through the Earth. The local geology mattered. The conductivity of the ground mattered. The geometry mattered. The receiving submarine did not need a voice channel. It needed enough information to know that somebody with authority wanted it to do something else—most importantly, to rise into a communications posture where richer instructions could reach it.

That last point is easy to lose because the word *communications* carries too much bandwidth in our heads. Sanguine was not supposed to give a submerged commander a telephone call from Washington. Extremely low frequency was brutally narrow. The strategic value lay in getting a small amount of information into a place that was otherwise expensive to reach.

A little information can be worth an absurd machine.

The proposal acquired a name fit for a classified project and a footprint fit for a public revolt.

SANGUINE.

The Naval Facilities Engineering Command's own history records that the Navy began environmental research in 1967 because it anticipated the questions the system would raise. The work included interference with telephone and power lines and wire fences, along with laboratory and field experiments aimed at biological and ecological questions. The Wisconsin Test Facility became operational in 1969.

Notice the chronology. The health argument was not invented decades later by people searching old maps for secret antennas. The Navy knew before the test facility opened that a continental electrical system would force an argument about what its fields did to things other than submarines.

The same official history says that studies available by the end of 1974 showed no effects on humans, animals, plants, or microorganisms. That sentence will not be allowed to do more work than it can carry.

`No effects` is not a primitive scientific fact. It is the endpoint of choices: what organism, what exposure, what endpoint, what duration, what sample size, what statistical test, what background, what instrument, what definition of effect. If you measure body weight and the relevant endpoint is an arrhythmia, your null result is true and useless. If you test a hundred endpoints and report the two that move, your positive result can be exciting and useless. The only cure is to descend into the experiments.

We will.

But there is another temptation to resist first.

The existence of a biological research program is sometimes presented as evidence that the military already knew the field was dangerous. This is backwards. Research can be evidence of concern, uncertainty, regulatory anticipation, political pressure, engineering diligence, liability management, or all of them at once. The fact that an institution investigates a hazard does not establish the hazard. If it did, every safety test would be a confession.

The opposite shortcut is just as bad. The Navy's sponsorship of reassuring work does not establish harmlessness. Institutions are not disinterested observers of programs they want funded. They choose questions, contracts, endpoints, summaries, and thresholds. That is a reason to audit the work, not a reason to reverse its sign.

Sanguine is unusually useful because neither shortcut is necessary. There is enough surviving public material to ask better questions.

In April 1979, the Government Accountability Office—then the General Accounting Office—reviewed the Navy's strategic communications argument. Its conclusion was not a health verdict. It attacked something more basic: necessity. The Navy argued that an ELF system would let strategic submarines receive without putting antennas near the surface. GAO did not share the Navy's belief that the system was needed. It argued that submarines were already survivable, that existing antennas did not necessarily compromise them, and that the capability of ELF itself was questionable.

This matters because technological controversies often pretend the only question is whether the machine works.

There were at least four Sanguine questions.

Could it work physically?

Could it work operationally?

Was it strategically necessary?

Could it operate without unacceptable effects on people and the environment?

A yes in one column does not fill the others.

That seems obvious written as four lines. It was not obvious in the political life of the project, and it is not obvious now when Sanguine appears in the long afterlife of electromagnetic folklore.

Search the edges of the subject and the antenna changes shape. It becomes evidence of secret biological manipulation, mind influence, weather control, or an unnamed military capacity hidden behind the innocent phrase *submarine communications*. The transformation is understandable. A classified strategic program really did put current into the ground. It really did create fields across inhabited land. It really did sponsor biological experiments. It really did change names and designs under political pressure. Those are excellent raw materials for a mythology because the mythology does not need to invent the nouns. It only needs to replace the verbs.

`Studied biological effects` becomes `developed biological effects`.

`Could induce current` becomes `could control biology`.

`Classified military purpose` becomes `classified additional purpose`.

That grammatical slide is one of the subjects of this book.

It would be convenient if the answer were simply that weak electromagnetic fields cannot interact with biology. That answer is also too easy. Biology is electrical all the way down without being a radio receiver in the cartoon sense. Nerves depend on voltage gradients. Ion channels matter. Time-varying fields can induce currents. Strong enough fields unquestionably produce biological effects. Frequency, amplitude, waveform, orientation, tissue, duration, and scale all matter.

The serious question is never `can electromagnetism affect biology?`

Of course it can.

The question is whether a specified exposure produces a specified effect through a physically and biologically credible pathway, at a magnitude that survives controls and replication.

Sanguine forces the adjective *specified* back into the room.

There is a wonderful indignity in the engineering history. A machine conceived to communicate with the most secretive weapons platforms on Earth had to contend with fences.

A long wire fence is a conductor. Put it in a changing electromagnetic environment and it can participate. Telephone lines can participate. Power lines can participate. Pipelines can participate. The ordinary metal infrastructure of rural life becomes part of the engineering problem. The Navy's environmental program explicitly studied interference in such systems.

This is not a side note. It is the cleanest way into the whole book.

A fence can couple to a field without becoming a weapon.

A measurable induced voltage can be real without being dangerous.

A dangerous induced voltage, if demonstrated under some geometry, would not prove a neurological effect in a person standing elsewhere.

Every step needs its own evidence.

The Cold War did not reward this kind of patience. Neither did local politics. Neither does the modern internet.

Project Sanguine was born inside a strategic system where communication failure had apocalyptic stakes. If a submerged missile submarine could not reliably receive national command authority, that was not a consumer-electronics inconvenience. The Navy therefore had an incentive to value a communication path that looked ridiculous by peacetime engineering standards. Opponents living above the proposed infrastructure had the inverse problem: they were being asked to accept uncertainty generated by a strategic requirement they did not control and, in many cases, could not fully inspect.

Both positions can be rational at the same time.

That is not balance. It is geometry.

The person in the submarine and the person above the buried electrode do not inhabit the same risk calculation.

Sanguine would be reduced, renamed, relocated, fought over, tested, and eventually realized in a much smaller form than its earliest ambitions. Alternatives appeared. One of the strangest, Project PISCES, proposed adapting the Pacific Intertie power system to radiate an ELF signal. GAO records the proposal as an alternative to Seafarer. The idea has the quality many serious Cold War projects acquire in retrospect: it sounds fabricated until the government document is open in front of you.

This book will keep those documents open.

Not because official paper is infallible. Official paper lies, omits, protects, flatters, and forgets. But it has dates. It has institutional authors. It has budgets and engineering nouns and arguments that can be compared against what happened next. It lets us distinguish a secret from a rumor about a secret.

That distinction is the reason to spend a book on an antenna most people have never heard of.

Sanguine sits at the intersection of nuclear command, submarine survivability, geophysics, electromagnetic coupling, environmental politics, weak-field biology, statistical inference, secrecy, and folklore. It does not solve the modern argument about electromagnetic bioeffects.

It does something more useful.

It gives the argument a place where the wires are real.
