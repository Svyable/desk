## A note on evidence

This is a book about a machine that was controversial partly because most people could not see the machine.

That makes language dangerous.

A wire carries current. A field can be measured. A current can be induced in another conductor. A biological system can respond to a stimulus. A measured response can be statistically significant. A statistically significant response can be biologically trivial. A biological effect can exist without being an adverse health effect. An adverse effect can exist without being large enough to matter at the exposure a particular installation produces.

Those are separate sentences because they are separate propositions.

The manuscript uses four evidence classes.

**Class 1 — verified / declassified / documented.** Official program histories, budgets, engineering records, environmental records, congressional material, GAO reports, contemporaneous technical papers, and peer-reviewed experiments whose methods and findings can actually be inspected. This class establishes that Project Sanguine existed, that the Wisconsin Test Facility operated, that the Navy investigated environmental and biological questions, that successor ELF proposals were contested, and that federal auditors questioned parts of the strategic case.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete.** Serious reporting, participant recollection, technically plausible reconstruction, or scientific findings that are relevant but not yet replicated or not directly matched to Sanguine exposure conditions. These can change the book. They cannot silently become Class 1.

**Class 3 — disputed / unconfirmed.** Claims that Sanguine or ELF infrastructure produced specific illnesses, neurological effects, behavioral manipulation, or covert capabilities when independent causal evidence is absent or inadequate. They stay in the book when provenance matters. They do not get promoted because they are dramatic.

**Class 4 — synthesis / open conjecture.** My argument that Sanguine is valuable as an epistemic control case for modern electromagnetic-anomaly debates: a documented military system, a genuine physical coupling mechanism, a real research program, real institutional incentives, and still no license to jump from `field exists` to `field harmed me`.

The distinction cuts both ways. A Navy conclusion is not a law of nature because the Navy paid for the study. An opponent's claim is not false because it inconvenienced the Navy. Sponsorship is evidence about incentives. It is not a substitute for reading methods.

### Defeat condition

The book's central framing weakens if exposure-matched, independently replicated evidence shows a robust adverse biological effect from the actual field strengths, frequencies, duty cycles, and geometries encountered around the Sanguine/Seafarer system and the manuscript continues treating the controversy primarily as an inference problem. It also weakens in the other direction if the historical record shows that serious health objections were never evidence-bearing at all and were merely rhetorical decoration. Either finding should change the book.

The point is not to split the difference. The point is to find out what happened.
