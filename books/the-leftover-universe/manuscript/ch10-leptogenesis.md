# Leptogenesis

A lepton can become a reason for a proton without ever becoming a proton.

That is the first sentence one has to get straight.

Leptogenesis does not usually mean that an electron literally turns into a proton. It means an asymmetry in lepton number is generated in the early universe and electroweak processes redistribute part of that asymmetry into baryon number. The charges are coupled by anomalous Standard Model processes called sphalerons.

The route is indirect enough that it sounds contrived until you write the bookkeeping.

At high temperatures above the electroweak scale, sphaleron transitions are efficient. They violate baryon number \(B\) and lepton number \(L\) in equal amounts such that \(B-L\) is conserved while \(B+L\) changes. If some earlier process creates a nonzero \(B-L\)—for instance a lepton asymmetry with no initial baryon asymmetry—sphalerons drive the plasma toward an equilibrium distribution in which both \(B\) and \(L\) are nonzero.

When the universe cools and sphaleron transitions become ineffective, the baryon component freezes in.

Visible matter can therefore be a converted asymmetry.

This mechanism was proposed in the 1980s by Mikhail Fukugita and Tsutomu Yanagida in a form involving heavy Majorana neutrino decays. It has since grown into one of the major frameworks for baryogenesis because it connects naturally to the seesaw explanation of neutrino mass.

The appeal is almost suspiciously high.

Start with right-handed neutrinos, which the Standard Model does not contain but which are natural if one wants neutrinos to have Dirac masses. Because these new neutrinos are gauge singlets, they can also carry large Majorana masses. Their couplings to ordinary lepton doublets and the Higgs generate small light-neutrino masses through the seesaw. The same heavy states decay in the early universe. If their decays violate CP, they produce unequal numbers of leptons and antileptons. Sphalerons do the conversion.

One extension. Two mysteries.

This is the sort of theoretical economy that makes seminar rooms happy.

Then the calculations begin.

A successful asymmetry depends on the abundance of the heavy neutrinos, their mass spectrum, the strength and phases of their Yukawa couplings, the reheating temperature after inflation, flavor effects, quantum coherence, thermal corrections and washout processes. The simple cartoon of one heavy particle decaying slightly more often to leptons than antileptons survives as intuition but not as precision.

The source of CP violation deserves a closer look.

At tree level, a heavy Majorana neutrino decay amplitude may be CP symmetric. Loop corrections add additional amplitudes involving other heavy neutrino states. The interference between tree and loop terms can contain imaginary phases. If there are at least two heavy states with appropriate complex couplings, the decay rate into leptons can differ from the conjugate rate into antileptons.

The CP asymmetry parameter is a difference of rates normalized by their sum.

Once again, the universe is built from a numerator.

If the heavy neutrinos are very massive and hierarchical, classic thermal leptogenesis tends to require the lightest relevant state above a lower bound around \(10^9\) GeV under standard assumptions—the Davidson-Ibarra scale. This is not a universal commandment. Resonances, flavor effects, nonthermal production and alternate seesaw structures can lower the required scale. But it illustrates how quickly the mechanism can leave accelerator territory.

The Large Hadron Collider reaches parton collision energies of order ten teraelectronvolts.

A billion GeV is a million TeV.

No clever magnet closes that gap soon.

At that point the theory must be tested by shadows.

One shadow is neutrinoless double-beta decay.

Another is the pattern of light-neutrino masses and mixing.

Another might be charged-lepton flavor violation such as \(\mu \rightarrow e\gamma\) or muon-to-electron conversion, depending on the model.

Another could be cosmological constraints on reheating or extra radiation.

In extended models, collider signatures or gravitational waves may appear.

The shadows do not necessarily reconstruct the object uniquely.

This is the central epistemic problem of high-scale leptogenesis. The mechanism can be theoretically well motivated and empirically slippery.

Consider the seesaw formula. In its simplest schematic form,

\[
m_\nu \sim -m_D M_R^{-1} m_D^T,
\]

where \(m_D\) comes from neutrino Yukawa couplings and the Higgs expectation value, and \(M_R\) is the heavy Majorana mass matrix. Low-energy experiments can, in principle, determine the light-neutrino mass matrix: three masses, mixing angles and CP phases, though several pieces remain unknown.

But many combinations of \(m_D\) and \(M_R\) produce the same low-energy matrix.

The inverse problem is underdetermined.

The early-universe decay asymmetry depends on combinations of high-energy couplings not fixed by oscillation data alone. You can know the daughters and still not identify the parents.

This is why claims that measuring the neutrino CP phase will “solve why matter exists” require sanding down. A large PMNS phase would be important. It may support specific constrained leptogenesis models. But generic high-scale leptogenesis contains more freedom.

The better question is cumulative: how many independent shadows can we align?

Suppose future experiments establish that neutrinos are Majorana particles through neutrinoless double-beta decay.

Suppose DUNE and Hyper-K measure significant leptonic CP violation.

Suppose cosmology narrows the absolute mass scale and ordering.

Suppose no low-scale sterile states appear.

Suppose grand unified proton decay remains absent.

At that point high-scale leptogenesis becomes more attractive by comparison, though not proven. Scientific inference often works by a landscape becoming less crowded.

Alternatively, suppose a GeV-scale heavy neutral lepton is discovered in a beam-dump experiment, with mixing patterns compatible with neutrino masses and a pair of nearly degenerate states. Suddenly low-scale resonant or oscillatory leptogenesis gains a physical actor.

The same word can migrate from blackboard to event display.

Resonant leptogenesis deserves attention because it shows how nature can amplify a small structure. If two heavy Majorana neutrinos have nearly equal masses, self-energy loop effects can resonantly enhance CP violation in their decays. The asymmetry may become large enough at much lower mass scales than the hierarchical case.

Near-degeneracy acts like an amplifier.

But then a new question appears: why are the masses nearly equal? A symmetry may explain it. Or it may be tuning. The solution to one small number often hands you another.

ARS leptogenesis takes a different path. Sterile neutrinos with GeV-scale masses are produced in the early universe and undergo coherent oscillations. Their flavor-dependent interactions generate lepton asymmetries while the sterile states are still out of equilibrium. Sphalerons convert part of the active-sector asymmetry to baryon number.

Here oscillation itself is the engine.

Because the masses can be low, the same particles might be produced in decays of B or D mesons, W bosons or other accessible processes. Their tiny mixing makes them long-lived. A heavy neutral lepton can travel centimeters, meters or farther before decaying. Experiments look for displaced vertices—decays occurring improbably far from the collision point.

A baryogenesis particle might announce itself by being late.

This is one of my favorite experimental inversions. Detectors are designed around expected spatial and temporal relationships. New physics can appear as geometry: a vertex where no prompt particle should decay, a track that begins in empty detector volume, a timing delay.

The universe's early timing problem becomes our late particle.

Flavored leptogenesis adds another layer. At very high temperatures, charged-lepton Yukawa interactions may be too slow to distinguish electron, muon and tau flavors in the plasma. As temperature falls, these interactions enter equilibrium at different epochs. The lepton state produced by a heavy neutrino decay can therefore experience flavor-dependent washout.

The simple total lepton asymmetry is not always enough.

A component strongly washed out in one flavor can survive in another. Quantum coherence between flavors can matter. Modern calculations use density-matrix treatments to interpolate between regimes. The baryon abundance can depend on how the universe “measures” lepton flavor through interactions.

I put “measures” in quotation marks because this is not consciousness. Environmental interactions select effective flavor bases. No observer is required.

The distinction matters in a book that keeps asking about the human role. Quantum language has a tendency to drag minds into places where scattering rates will do.

Leptogenesis is a clean example of a universe performing complicated quantum kinetics long before there were nervous systems.

Our later role is to infer the kinetics.

Washout is the antagonist. Inverse decays and scattering processes can erase the lepton asymmetry. If heavy-neutrino couplings are too strong, the same interactions that produce an asymmetry keep the system close to equilibrium and destroy it. If couplings are too weak, the heavy states may not be produced adequately or may decay too late. There is a window.

The mechanism lives between too much interaction and too little.

This is not unique to leptogenesis. Relic abundance calculations across cosmology are full of such windows. Dark matter freeze-out depends on interaction strength. Big Bang nucleosynthesis depends on weak rates freezing out at the right time. Structure formation depends on particles coupling enough early and little enough later.

The universe is a sequence of missed appointments between reaction rates and expansion.

Leptogenesis turns one missed appointment into every atom.

The heavy neutrino decays when inverse processes cannot fully keep up. Its bias survives. Sphalerons remain active long enough to convert the bias. Then sphalerons shut down before erasing or redistributing it differently. QCD confines quarks later. Nucleosynthesis packages protons and neutrons into light nuclei. Recombination gives electrons stable atomic homes.

The final object—a rock—contains no visible trace of the scheduling.

This is a general feature of emergence that does not require philosophical decoration. Macroscopic stability can hide microscopic historical dependence. Once an atom exists at room temperature, it does not care which baryogenesis mechanism produced the net baryon number. Different cosmic histories can converge onto indistinguishable low-energy matter.

That is why reconstruction is hard.

The system lost information.

Not necessarily in a fundamental unitary sense; rather, the observables accessible in present baryons do not retain a detailed label of their origin mechanism. Many microscopic histories map to the same baryon abundance. Cosmology gives us aggregate relics, not serial numbers.

To distinguish origins, we need correlated relics.

A gravitational-wave background from a phase transition.

A proton-decay mode.

A sterile-neutrino mass and mixing pattern.

An EDM.

A Majorana mass signal.

A dark-matter relation.

The research strategy is to find another object that remembers.

Thermal leptogenesis has a difficult memory problem because the heavy particles are gone and high-scale dynamics can be decoupled from low-energy details. Its strength is theoretical coherence: it uses ingredients already motivated by neutrino mass. Its weakness is experimental nonuniqueness.

This tension is healthy.

A theory should not be rewarded for being hard to falsify merely because it is beautiful. Nor should it be dismissed because its decisive scale is high if it generates testable consistency conditions.

The correct stance is neither romance nor cynicism.

It is accounting.

What does the mechanism require?

Which quantities are measured?

Which are free?

Which regions are excluded?

What would force abandonment?

This last question is rarely asked loudly enough.

High-scale leptogenesis as a broad framework is difficult to falsify because its parameter space is large. Specific realizations can be. A measured reheating temperature too low to produce required heavy states could kill a thermal scenario. Certain neutrino mass patterns can make minimal versions difficult. Discovery of new interactions could either support or wash out the mechanism. Strong limits on lepton-number violation can constrain low-scale variants.

A good research program keeps narrowing until the framework becomes risky.

There is another speculative bridge worth naming: the relation between the baryon asymmetry and dark matter.

The observed cosmological mass density in dark matter is about five times the baryonic density. In standard WIMP stories, that rough ratio is an accident between unrelated freeze-out and baryogenesis processes. In asymmetric dark matter models, a shared mechanism may generate linked asymmetries in visible and dark sectors. The dark matter could itself be a leftover of a particle-antiparticle imbalance, with symmetric dark matter annihilating away much as baryonic antimatter did.

Two residues, one origin.

Some leptogenesis-like mechanisms can transfer asymmetry between sectors. If dark particle masses sit near a few times the proton mass, linked number densities can naturally produce the observed energy-density ratio. Other models use different masses and transfer operators.

No evidence establishes this picture.

But it exemplifies a broader possibility: the baryon asymmetry may not be an isolated oddity. It may be one visible projection of a more general charge-separation process across hidden sectors.

If so, “why matter?” is too narrow a question.

The real question would be: how did the universe distribute sign?

Leptogenesis already gives one answer in miniature. The sign we see in baryons can begin elsewhere.

The visible may be bookkeeping downstream of the invisible.

This is why neutrino experiments deserve a place beside antimatter traps in the same book. One compares matter directly with antimatter. The other asks whether a nearly invisible sector carried the initial imbalance. Both approach the residue from opposite directions.

At CERN, we synthesize antihydrogen and ask whether the mirror object differs.

At DUNE, we send neutrinos through Earth and ask whether propagation probabilities differ from antineutrinos.

At double-beta experiments, we ask whether the neutrino is its own mirror.

At a future heavy-neutral-lepton detector, we may ask whether a long-lived sterile state exists at all.

The universe did not leave a manual.

It left overlapping tests.

Leptogenesis is not the answer yet.

It is one of the few answers audacious enough to explain why the stuff we can touch may owe its sign to the stuff that almost never touches anything.
