# The Statistics of Existence

One in a billion is a terrible probability statement.

It is a useful abundance statement.

The distinction is small enough to lose in conversation and large enough to ruin an argument.

When physicists or CERN explain the baryon asymmetry by saying roughly one matter particle remained for every billion matter-antimatter pairs, they are describing the scale of a net excess after an early thermal history. They are not saying the universe had a one-in-a-billion chance of existing. There is no agreed probability distribution over possible universes from which that conclusion follows.

Yet the language almost forces the mistake.

A small ratio feels like a small chance.

A leftover feels lucky.

A symmetry broken in our direction feels selected.

This is where cosmology becomes vulnerable to counterfeit statistics.

The actual measured quantity is closer to a baryon-to-photon ratio. Cosmological observations infer that there are several baryons per ten billion cosmic microwave background photons today, after accounting for the thermal history. The baryon-to-entropy ratio is another common normalization. These numbers are outputs of whatever baryogenesis mechanism occurred.

They are not lottery odds unless a theory supplies an ensemble and measure.

This matters because “our existence was incredibly improbable” is one of those sentences that can sound profound while containing no defined sample space.

Improbable under what model?

Which parameters vary?

With what prior distribution?

Are universes sampled uniformly in a Lagrangian coefficient, logarithmically, through inflationary dynamics, quantum cosmology, a landscape of vacua, or not at all?

Does each universe count once, each causal patch, each observer, each baryon, each unit of spacetime volume?

Without answers, the probability is mood wearing a decimal.

The baryon asymmetry deserves better.

It gives us a concrete number and a dynamical puzzle. We should resist inflating that into a quantified claim about cosmic luck.

There are nevertheless legitimate statistical questions around the asymmetry.

A baryogenesis model can predict a distribution of outcomes under stochastic initial conditions. Inflation can create spatial fluctuations in a field controlling baryon production, leading to baryon isocurvature perturbations. A theory with spontaneous CP breaking may produce domains with positive or negative asymmetry. A parameter scan over a well-motivated model can show how much of its parameter space yields the observed value while satisfying constraints.

Those are defined probability or naturalness questions.

They are not the same as asking how likely existence itself was.

The difference between probability and typicality becomes important in multiverse arguments. Some theories of eternal inflation or string landscapes propose vast ensembles of regions with different low-energy parameters. Observers can exist only in a subset. One can then ask whether our measured values are typical among observer-compatible regions under some measure.

This has produced serious work and serious headaches.

The measure problem in eternal inflation is notorious. Infinite spacetime volumes make relative frequencies ambiguous. Different cutoff prescriptions yield different answers. Defining “observer” introduces selection effects. Landscape distributions are incompletely known. Anthropic predictions can become prior-sensitive.

Baryon abundance has been considered in anthropic contexts because too little baryonic matter could inhibit galaxy or star formation while too much could alter structure and cooling. But the fact that life requires some baryons does not pin the observed asymmetry sharply without a detailed model.

Observers need a floor, not necessarily our exact number.

This is a useful diagnostic for anthropic explanations. If a parameter could vary by orders of magnitude while still allowing observers, selection alone does not explain why it takes the particular observed value.

The baryon asymmetry may be such a parameter depending on how other cosmological quantities co-vary.

The word “depending” carries the honest weight.

There is another statistical issue: sign.

If a baryogenesis mechanism breaks CP spontaneously, different cosmic regions might choose opposite signs with equal underlying probability. In one region baryons dominate; in another antibaryons. Observers made of either would call their local substance matter. No observer can infer a global preference merely from being made of the local sign.

Our sign is not an observed cosmic moral.

If the theory is exactly symmetric before spontaneous breaking, a global ensemble may contain equal numbers of positive and negative domains. Inflation could enlarge one domain to encompass our observable universe. The baryon asymmetry inside it would be nonzero while the fundamental theory remains symmetric.

In such a model, “why matter rather than antimatter?” has a different answer from “why an asymmetry at all?”

The first may be spontaneous sign choice.

The second is dynamics.

These questions are often conflated because language privileges our sign.

Imagine discovering tomorrow that a region beyond some cosmological boundary is antimatter dominated. Would it change any fact about the chemistry of your body? No. Would it change baryogenesis theory? Possibly enormously. It might favor models producing domains over models generating a universal sign.

The local residue does not tell us global topology.

This is an example of conditional evidence. Our existence tells us our past light cone contains enough baryonic matter for our evolutionary history. It does not directly tell us whether all causally disconnected regions share the same sign or abundance.

Human location is a selection effect even when humans are irrelevant to the dynamics.

This can be stated without invoking the anthropic principle as a capitalized doctrine. Any observer samples conditions compatible with its existence and location. A fish measures a watery environment not because water was designed for fish but because fish in dry granite do not report data.

The analogy becomes dangerous if pushed. Cosmological selection has subtleties fish do not. But the basic logic is ordinary conditional probability.

Where it goes wrong is when conditioning substitutes for mechanism.

“We observe a positive baryon asymmetry because otherwise we would not exist” says nothing about whether the asymmetry arose through leptogenesis. It can explain why an observer should not be surprised to measure a nonzero value above some habitability threshold in an ensemble where values vary. It cannot generate the distribution.

Selection edits a sample. It does not create the samples.

The same caution applies to fine-tuning language.

The baryon asymmetry is around \(10^{-10}\) in common normalizations. Is that fine-tuned?

Maybe in a particular model.

Not as a bare fact.

A small dimensionless historical outcome can emerge naturally from loop suppressions, ratios of scales, weak couplings, Boltzmann factors and washout. The electron-to-proton mass ratio is small. The primordial density perturbation amplitude is small. Smallness becomes a naturalness problem only relative to the theoretical mechanisms expected to produce it and the stability of the result under corrections.

There is no universal small-number alarm.

Humans have a decimal aesthetic because our fingers gave us base ten. Nature does not become more surprised when a parameter acquires another zero.

This is why baryogenesis papers do calculations rather than exclaiming at \(10^{-10}\).

A heavy-neutrino decay asymmetry can be \(10^{-6}\), with washout and sphaleron conversion yielding a final baryon asymmetry near the observed value. An electroweak mechanism can combine CP-violating source terms with diffusion and sphaleron rates. The final number is an integrated result.

The question is whether the required parameters are allowed and motivated, not whether the output looks aesthetically improbable.

The statistical framing becomes more interesting when we consider the coincidence between baryonic and dark matter abundances.

Cosmological data indicate dark matter has a mass density roughly five times baryonic matter. In conventional scenarios these arise through independent mechanisms: baryogenesis sets the baryon asymmetry; thermal freeze-out or another production process sets dark-matter abundance. Why should the densities lie within an order of magnitude today?

This is sometimes called the cosmic coincidence of visible and dark matter, distinct from the more famous coincidence involving dark energy's present density.

Asymmetric dark matter models answer by linking the number densities. If dark matter has its own particle-antiparticle asymmetry generated together with baryons, the ratio of energy densities can reflect the ratio of particle masses and shared charge transfer. A dark particle mass of a few GeV can then make the five-to-one density ratio look natural if number densities are comparable.

This is an actual explanatory use of a coincidence.

It proposes a mechanism that predicts correlated quantities.

Whether nature uses it is unknown.

Direct-detection experiments, collider searches, cosmology and astrophysics constrain asymmetric dark matter. Many models predict suppressed present-day annihilation because the anti-dark-matter population was removed, analogous to ordinary baryons. Others retain symmetric components or have dark-sector interactions.

The dark sector could be another leftover.

If so, the phrase “the leftover universe” becomes plural.

Baryons left from matter-antimatter annihilation.

Dark matter left from a hidden asymmetry.

Axion dark matter left from coherent field misalignment.

Relic neutrinos left from thermal decoupling.

CMB photons left from recombination and earlier thermal history.

Black holes left from stellar collapse or perhaps primordial density peaks.

The present cosmos is, in a physical sense, full of relics.

This is not a metaphor. Cosmology works because relic populations retain information about earlier conditions.

The word “relic” can sound passive, as if the universe is an attic. But relics remain dynamically active. Dark matter shapes galaxies. Neutrinos suppress structure on small scales through free streaming. The CMB fills space. Baryons form stars.

History keeps gravitating.

This changes the intuitive relationship between past and present. Human historical records are fragile. Burn the archive and an event can disappear from collective knowledge. Physical relics are archives that continue participating in the system. The baryon asymmetry is both record and raw material.

Every star is made from evidence.

That sentence risks literary excess, but it is literally defensible in one sense: the existence of baryonic mass is evidence that an asymmetry existed before annihilation completed.

The star does not encode the mechanism uniquely. It encodes the outcome.

This distinction between outcome evidence and mechanism evidence is crucial.

A crime scene may prove someone entered a room without identifying who. The baryon inventory proves the early universe did not end with zero net baryon number in our observable region. It does not tell us whether the intruder was leptogenesis, GUT decay or electroweak bubbles.

We therefore need second-order relics.

A gravitational-wave spectrum could encode a phase transition.

A neutrino mass pattern could encode a seesaw.

An axion could encode Peccei-Quinn breaking.

A proton-decay mode could encode grand unification.

The science is a search for independent archives of the same event.

Statistical confidence grows when archives agree.

This is very different from the rhetorical confidence of one explanatory story being elegant.

Cosmology has several famous cases where independent archives transformed plausibility into knowledge. Big Bang nucleosynthesis and the CMB infer compatible baryon densities at widely separated epochs. Multiple observations—supernovae, CMB, baryon acoustic oscillations—support accelerated expansion. Different neutrino sources and detector technologies establish oscillation.

Redundancy is how we interrogate an unrepeatable universe.

Baryogenesis does not yet have that redundancy for its mechanism.

The final baryon number is one archive.

The source process remains unnamed.

This is why a claimed “solution” should be judged by how many additional archives it predicts.

There is also a statistical humility in recognizing cosmic variance. We observe one universe from one location. On the largest scales, there are only a limited number of independent modes available within our horizon. Even perfect instruments cannot beat the fact that we have one realization of the underlying stochastic process.

Some uncertainties are not engineering problems.

Baryon asymmetry itself is measured through many small-scale and early-universe observables, so cosmic variance is not the primary limitation on its value. But theories of large-scale baryon domains or inflationary correlations can run into horizon-limited evidence.

The universe can hide information not by being faint but by existing only once.

This is a hard concept for a species trained on repeatable experiments.

We compensate by finding repeated instances inside the universe: billions of CMB modes, many supernovae, trillions of collider events, huge ensembles of atoms. We use the one cosmos as a generator of subexperiments.

For baryogenesis, the early event itself is not repeated under our control. But the laws it used may be.

CP violation repeats in B decays.

Neutrino oscillations repeat in beams.

Antihydrogen spectroscopy repeats in traps.

EDM measurements repeat with reversals.

We test the ingredients because we cannot rerun the recipe.

This is one of the deepest methodological facts in cosmology and not usually described as such. Causal history is singular; law is portable.

If we identify the laws tightly enough, we can reconstruct the history probabilistically.

That is what “why matter exists” will look like if solved. Not a video. A network of measured parameters plugged into a dynamical model whose predicted relic abundances and correlated signals match the universe while alternatives fail.

The solution will be statistical in the best sense.

It will not say our existence had probability one in a billion.

It will say a particular process produced an asymmetry of the observed magnitude with quantifiable uncertainty.

There is a moral temptation to turn this into a lesson about being a rare survivor. We are not doing that.

Human populations contain enough real histories of survival that stealing cosmology to decorate them would be obscene.

The baryon asymmetry is not resilience.

It is an unresolved physical parameter.

Its human consequence is simpler and stranger: we are made of the sample whose frequency we are trying to explain.

That gives us no wisdom automatically.

It gives us access.

We can weigh baryons because we are surrounded by them. We can create antibaryons because accelerators let energy cross the mass threshold. We can compare the two because one side survived in sufficient abundance to build laboratories.

The experiment is conditional on the outcome.

This creates a mild circularity of circumstance, not logic. Baryons made experimenters; experimenters infer baryogenesis. The inference remains valid if the evidence and theory are sound. A thermometer being made of atoms does not invalidate thermodynamics.

But the circumstance is worth noticing.

The universe's unexplained residual built a device to measure its own residual ratio.

That sentence is as close as this book will get to mysticism.

Then we go back to error bars.
