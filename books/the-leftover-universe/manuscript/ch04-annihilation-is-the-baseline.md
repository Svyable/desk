# Annihilation Is the Baseline

The first thing to know about annihilation is that it is not punishment.

Matter does not hate antimatter. An electron does not recognize a positron as an enemy. A proton and antiproton have no grudge. The word survives because the process is so complete at the level of the original particles: their identities can disappear into other allowed excitations, often photons or other particles. But there is no cosmic preference in the word itself. There is only quantum field theory doing its bookkeeping.

This matters because the matter-antimatter problem is easy to narrate badly. Put two substances in a room. They touch. Boom. One substance somehow wins. That story imports a war into a thermal plasma.

The early universe was not a battlefield. It was a reactor with no walls.

At high enough temperatures, the distinctions that dominate present-day intuition become temporary. Bound atoms cannot exist. Nuclei cannot exist at still higher temperatures. Quarks and gluons inhabit a plasma. Particle populations are continually made and destroyed as reactions run forward and backward. The relevant question is not whether matter and antimatter can annihilate. They can. The question is whether the network of reactions, while the universe expands and cools, can leave a nonzero net charge associated with baryons.

If all the important reactions remain in thermal equilibrium and if the governing symmetries are too exact, attempts to produce an asymmetry wash away. Forward and reverse processes erase the difference. The universe is a patient accountant when given enough time.

Expansion takes time away.

That sentence is one of the quiet engines of cosmology. The universe cools because it expands. Reaction rates depend on temperature, density and coupling strength. At some point a reaction that once happened rapidly becomes slow compared with the rate at which cosmic conditions change. A species freezes out. A process falls out of equilibrium. A population decouples. The universe carries forward an incomplete chemical conversation.

Much of what exists now is a record of processes that stopped keeping up.

The cosmic microwave background is the most famous case. Before recombination, photons scattered frequently from free electrons. After electrons combined with nuclei into neutral atoms, the mean free path of photons increased enormously. The radiation decoupled and began traveling almost freely. We see that ancient light today, redshifted into microwaves. Its anisotropies retain information about the plasma it escaped.

Neutrinos decoupled much earlier. Big Bang nucleosynthesis froze particular nuclear abundance ratios into the light elements. Dark matter candidates, if they were once in thermal equilibrium, would have their present abundance set partly by their freeze-out or freeze-in histories. Cosmology is full of fossils that are not bones. They are distributions left behind when interactions lost a race against expansion.

Baryogenesis belongs to this family of ideas but at a more primitive layer. Whatever generated the baryon asymmetry had to make a difference and keep it from being erased.

This is why Sakharov's third condition—departure from thermal equilibrium—was so influential. In the classic formulation, if the universe begins with zero baryon number and evolves under CPT-symmetric physics in exact thermal equilibrium, generating a persistent baryon asymmetry is obstructed. A nonequilibrium episode gives asymmetric processes a chance to leave a mark before inverse reactions undo them.

There are important refinements and generalized formulations. Modern baryogenesis mechanisms can be described with density matrices, quantum kinetic equations and situations where “out of equilibrium” is subtler than a simple freeze-out cartoon. But the conceptual point survives: a bias must be processed in a history with direction.

Time has to matter.

That sounds obvious because everything happens in time. It is not obvious in equilibrium statistical mechanics, where detailed balance can make a system forget which way its microscopic events ran. Baryogenesis needs a universe that is not merely hot but changing.

Fortunately, the early universe was incapable of sitting still.

As temperature fell, symmetries may have broken. Fields acquired vacuum expectation values. Particle masses changed. Reactions switched on and off. Heavy species became rare. If the electroweak transition had been strongly first order, bubbles of a new phase could have nucleated and expanded through an old one, providing violent nonequilibrium interfaces. In the Standard Model with the measured Higgs mass, the electroweak transition is instead a smooth crossover, which is one reason minimal electroweak baryogenesis does not work. Extensions of the Higgs sector can change that picture, which is why collider measurements and gravitational-wave searches can become relevant to a question about why matter exists.

A phase transition in the infant universe might someday be heard as a stochastic gravitational-wave background.

There is a sentence Dirac would not have had on his bingo card.

The connection is not guaranteed. Proposed electroweak baryogenesis models vary. A first-order transition strong enough to generate gravitational waves detectable by future space interferometers would occupy particular parameter ranges. No gravitational-wave detection today establishes electroweak baryogenesis. Still, this is exactly the kind of bridge modern physics builds: particle content changes the thermal history; thermal history shapes symmetry breaking; symmetry breaking can source spacetime ripples; a detector millions of kilometers across can test whether an ancient phase boundary existed.

The universe keeps receipts in odd media.

Before getting to phase transitions, though, we need to understand why annihilation makes a tiny imbalance so consequential.

Consider a simplified population with \(N+\Delta\) matter particles and \(N\) antimatter particles, where \(N\) is enormous and \(\Delta\) is small. If annihilation efficiently removes matter-antimatter pairs, the symmetric component—roughly \(N\) of each—disappears from the population as those particular particles, while \(\Delta\) matter particles remain. The final matter abundance can therefore be controlled by the initial or dynamically generated difference rather than by the enormous total population that existed before annihilation.

This is subtraction selecting the signal.

Particle physicists encounter the same mathematical structure elsewhere. An asymmetry is often defined as a difference divided by a sum: one decay rate minus its conjugate, normalized by their total. The numerator can be tiny while the denominator is large. Cosmologically, a small normalized asymmetry can become the entirety of a surviving charge after symmetric components vanish.

It is useful to separate three statements that are often mashed together.

First: matter and antimatter annihilate when appropriate particles encounter each other through allowed interactions.

Second: the early universe appears to have contained a small excess of baryonic matter over antibaryonic matter, or generated one before annihilation completed.

Third: we do not know which mechanism produced that excess.

The first is routine physics. The second is cosmological inference. The third is the frontier.

When those categories blur, certainty leaks from the first into the third.

The observed baryon abundance is constrained independently in ways that agree remarkably well. Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts light-element abundances as a function of the baryon density and other parameters. Measurements of primordial deuterium are particularly useful. The acoustic structure of the cosmic microwave background also depends on baryon density because baryons add inertia to the photon-baryon fluid before recombination. Planck's measurements fit a cosmological model in which the baryon density can be inferred with high precision. These methods look at different epochs and physics, yet their answers are compatible.

The residue is not a philosophical guess. Its abundance leaves fingerprints.

What we cannot do is run the early universe again with the asymmetry dial set to zero.

This is where cosmology differs from a laboratory experiment and where careless language about causation creeps in. We infer the thermal history from relics. We create analogous or relevant processes in accelerators. We test candidate interactions. We use theory to connect accessible observables to inaccessible epochs. The chain can be extraordinarily strong without becoming direct cinematography.

A baryogenesis model earns credibility by surviving several kinds of attack.

Can it produce the observed sign and magnitude of the asymmetry under a plausible cosmic history? Does it require particles or interactions already excluded by colliders? Does it predict electric dipole moments larger than experiments allow? Does it violate proton lifetime bounds? Does it fit neutrino data? Does it spoil nucleosynthesis? Does it generate unwanted relics? Does it require a phase transition inconsistent with Higgs measurements? Does it leave any distinctive signal at all?

A mechanism that merely says “CP violation happened” is not enough. The universe is not graded on conceptual plausibility.

One of the most merciless constraints comes from washout.

Suppose a process generates baryon number at high temperature. If later baryon-number-violating reactions remain active in equilibrium, they may erase the asymmetry. A successful theory has to generate the difference at the right time, in the right charges, and shepherd it through subsequent epochs. In leptogenesis, for example, an asymmetry first created in lepton number can be partially converted to baryon number by electroweak sphaleron processes. But flavor effects, reaction rates and thermal histories matter. The slogan “leptons become baryons” hides pages of kinetics.

The universe has many opportunities to forget.

That is one reason the surviving asymmetry is such an interesting record. It is not merely evidence that something once made more matter. It is evidence that the entire subsequent sequence failed to erase the difference.

Survival is a dynamical achievement even when nobody is doing the achieving.

The word “baseline” in this chapter's title is meant in that technical emotional sense. Annihilation is what makes the asymmetry visible. Without efficient removal of the symmetric component, a one-part-in-a-billion difference would remain a tiny perturbation in a sea of both matter and antimatter. Because the pairs eliminate one another, the difference becomes the inventory.

There is a related point about entropy that deserves care. Annihilation does not make the universe “more empty” in an absolute-energy sense. Rest energy goes into radiation and other products. Entropy generally grows. Photons remain abundant. When people say matter “disappeared,” they mean the particle identities and conserved or approximately conserved charges in question are transformed. Energy does not evaporate.

So why is the present universe not glowing with an obvious special signature from the annihilation epoch?

Because cosmic expansion redshifts radiation and because the thermal history is old. The early universe was already radiation dominated. Energy released through annihilation participates in that thermal bath. Later observables depend on when annihilations occurred and what species were involved. Electron-positron annihilation, for instance, has a standard role in the thermal history and changes the relative temperatures of photons and decoupled neutrinos. Baryon-antibaryon annihilation occurred in a hot environment long before transparent space existed.

There is no visible scar shaped like an explosion.

The scar is the ratio.

This also helps explain why finding present-day antimatter does not automatically challenge baryogenesis. Antiprotons in cosmic rays can be produced secondarily when energetic cosmic-ray protons strike interstellar gas. Positrons have multiple astrophysical sources. Pulsars can produce electron-positron pairs. Dark matter annihilation or decay could in principle contribute to antiparticle excesses, which is why cosmic-ray spectra are watched carefully, but conventional sources must be ruled out before invoking new physics.

An antihelium nucleus would be more interesting because producing heavier antinuclei in ordinary collisions is increasingly suppressed. Even there, one must calculate backgrounds. Experiments such as AMS-02 search for antinuclei in cosmic rays. Candidate antihelium events have attracted attention, but no accepted discovery of primordial or anti-stellar antihelium has established a macroscopic antimatter domain. The interpretation remains demanding.

We are looking for a missing population through crumbs that can be manufactured locally.

That is the observational difficulty in miniature.

If a distant galaxy were made entirely of antimatter, its emitted photons would look almost exactly like those from a matter galaxy. An anti-star fusing antihydrogen into antihelium would produce electromagnetic spectra governed by the same atomic energy levels. Light does not arrive stamped “anti.” The strongest signatures would come from contact zones, cosmic-ray antinuclei, unusual annihilation products, or other boundary effects.

A telescope is bad at seeing a sign change in baryon number when photons forget the sign before they reach us.

This is why large antimatter domains are constrained indirectly. If matter and antimatter regions touched after recombination or during structure formation, annihilation at boundaries could generate gamma rays and disturb cosmological observables. Models can push domains apart or alter histories, but simple nearby anti-galaxy scenarios face strong constraints.

The absence is not established by looking at a spiral galaxy and declaring its handedness. It is established by asking what interfaces would do.

Interfaces are a recurring theme in baryogenesis too.

In electroweak baryogenesis, a bubble wall between phases can provide precisely the kind of nonequilibrium interface where CP-violating interactions bias particle densities. Those densities diffuse, sphaleron processes act differently in front of and behind the wall, and a net baryon number can be deposited. The details vary by model, but the image is instructive: the asymmetry may have been written at a moving boundary.

Matter's existence as we know it could be a boundary effect from a phase that no longer exists.

That possibility should feel less mystical than it does. We are familiar with materials whose present structure records how they crossed a phase transition. Heat steel differently and its microstructure changes. Cool a magnet below a critical temperature and domains form. Freeze water and crystal defects preserve local history. The early universe was a material system in the broadest possible sense, crossing regimes as its temperature fell.

The difference is that its “material” was the fields from which later matter emerged.

If a first-order cosmic transition generated our baryon asymmetry, the historical contingency is literal. The speed of bubble walls, rates of particle diffusion, CP-violating phases and sphaleron activity would be ancestors of every proton in the future.

That is a brutal compression of causality.

We can resist the urge to humanize it and still let the scale land. The atoms in a courthouse, a tumor, a violin and a bag of fertilizer share dependence on the same ancient failure of cancellation. Moral categories arrive incomparably later. Baryogenesis is indifferent to what baryons eventually organize themselves into.

This is one reason the human role in the story has to be handled without self-congratulation. Our existence does not reveal that the asymmetry was “for” observers. It reveals only that the branch of cosmic history containing observers like us requires a surviving material substrate. We are consequences with instruments.

The instruments can now interrogate the conditions of that consequence.

Electric dipole moment experiments are a good example. A permanent electric dipole moment of an elementary particle or certain composite systems would violate time-reversal symmetry and, assuming CPT, signal CP violation. Many beyond-Standard-Model baryogenesis scenarios introduce new CP-violating phases that could generate EDMs. Experiments looking at neutrons, atoms, molecules and ions have pushed upper limits to extraordinary smallness. Null results carve away parameter space.

A molecule in a laboratory can veto a story about the first fractions of a second.

The ACME experiment's use of thorium monoxide molecules to constrain the electron electric dipole moment is a particularly elegant case. Molecules can create enormous effective internal electric fields, amplifying sensitivity to an electron EDM. The experiment does not make antimatter. It does not reproduce baryogenesis. It asks whether electrons carry a tiny directional asymmetry that new CP-violating physics might imprint.

The answer so far is a stringent upper limit.

That puts pressure on models. It does not eliminate the need for new CP violation; it tells theorists where it cannot be hiding easily.

This is how the “why anything” question becomes engineering. Better magnetic shielding. Colder particles. Cleaner vacuum. More stable lasers. Larger detectors. Quieter electronics. Better lattice calculations. More exact nuclear matrix elements. Stronger statistical controls.

Cosmic ontology arrives wearing lab gloves.

There is no single experiment called WHY MATTER. Instead there are dozens of narrower machines, each asking a sentence precise enough to fail.

Does the neutron have an EDM above this value?

Does antihydrogen's 1S–2S transition differ from hydrogen's at this precision?

Does a B meson decay to one final state more often than an anti-B meson decays to its conjugate state?

Does a proton decay within \(10^{34}\) years in a particular channel?

Do neutrinos and antineutrinos oscillate differently over this baseline?

Does a first-order phase transition leave a gravitational-wave background?

Does the sky contain antinuclei too abundant for secondary production?

These are the sharp edges of a huge question.

Annihilation, meanwhile, remains the baseline because it explains why the huge question can turn on a tiny number. The symmetric part does not survive as matter. The net part does.

A civilization built from the net part eventually invents subtraction and discovers that subtraction built it first.

That is not a metaphor I entirely trust. It is too neat. The universe did not perform arithmetic. But the mathematics is exact enough to justify the discomfort.

The inventory we call ordinary reality may be a difference term.

The chair is not made of the dominant original population. It is made of what the dominant population could not cancel.
