# DUNE's Long Bet

A beam leaves Illinois and arrives in South Dakota having changed identity.

That sentence is literal enough to sound fictional.

The Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment, DUNE, is built around a baseline of roughly 1,300 kilometers. A powerful neutrino beam generated at Fermilab near Chicago will pass through Earth's crust toward huge liquid-argon detectors at the Sanford Underground Research Facility in Lead, South Dakota. The neutrinos require no tunnel between the states. They go through rock.

Most do nothing.

A tiny fraction interact in the detectors. Those rare events are enough to reconstruct how flavor changed during the trip.

The long baseline is not an inconvenience. It is the instrument.

Neutrino oscillations arise because flavor states—electron, muon, tau—are quantum superpositions of mass states. As those mass states propagate, their phases evolve differently. Over a suitable distance and energy, the composition changes. A muon-neutrino beam can develop an electron-neutrino component.

For antineutrinos, the probabilities can differ if the leptonic CP phase is nonzero.

DUNE will compare them.

The experiment is not a direct baryogenesis detector. It will not reproduce the hot early universe, observe heavy Majorana-neutrino decay or count primordial baryons. It is a precision machine for a parameter that may tell us whether the lepton sector violates CP.

That parameter matters because baryogenesis needs some source of matter-antimatter discrimination.

The danger is to turn “may tell us whether” into “will explain why matter exists.”

The distinction deserves an entire chapter because DUNE has become a magnet for compressed cosmological promises.

A discovery of leptonic CP violation would be profound. It would establish another fundamental difference between matter and antimatter behavior. It could constrain classes of leptogenesis models. It would complete a major piece of the three-neutrino mixing picture.

It would not uniquely determine the baryon asymmetry.

This is not a weakness of DUNE. It is a property of the inverse problem.

The experiment's power comes from combining several effects over a long baseline. In vacuum, oscillation probabilities depend on the mass-squared differences, mixing angles and CP phase. Passing through matter adds another effect: electron neutrinos interact differently with electrons in the crust through coherent forward scattering. Antineutrinos feel the corresponding potential with opposite sign. This matter effect helps DUNE determine the neutrino mass ordering and complicates the extraction of intrinsic CP violation in a calculable way.

Earth becomes part of the detector.

The density profile along the path is not a nuisance afterthought. It modifies the quantum evolution. A planet that formed from the baryon asymmetry becomes a refractive medium for particles that may have generated that asymmetry.

That is one of those loops reality produces without needing symbolism.

DUNE's far detector modules use liquid argon time projection chambers. When a neutrino interacts with an argon nucleus, charged particles emerge and ionize the liquid. An electric field drifts the freed electrons toward readout planes, allowing three-dimensional reconstruction of the event. Scintillation light provides timing information.

The result is closer to a bubble chamber made digital than to a simple counter.

A neutrino interaction becomes an image.

Tracks identify muons. Electromagnetic showers indicate electrons or photons. Hadronic fragments reveal the messy nuclear recoil. Reconstructing the incoming neutrino's energy requires understanding not just lepton kinematics but the argon nucleus—Fermi motion, correlations, final-state interactions, multinucleon effects.

The cosmic question runs through nuclear modeling.

If the experiment is to compare neutrinos and antineutrinos at percent-level precision, systematic differences matter. Beam flux must be characterized. Cross sections must be measured. Near detectors close to Fermilab will sample the beam before significant oscillation, constraining interaction models and flux. Detector calibrations must make matter and antimatter running comparable.

A CP phase is not read off a dial.

It is inferred through a global fit of event spectra under competing parameter values.

This is why the long bet is not merely financial, though DUNE is certainly expensive. It is a bet that enough different information can be made mutually constraining that degeneracies collapse.

Mass ordering.

The CP phase.

Mixing angles.

Matter effects.

Cross sections.

Energy dependence.

Neutrino and antineutrino appearance.

Muon-neutrino disappearance.

No single data point does the work.

The result emerges from structure across the spectrum.

This resembles the baryogenesis problem itself. One asymmetry measurement is rarely decisive. We need a network of constraints whose intersection becomes small.

DUNE is particularly valuable because its baseline and broad-band beam sample multiple oscillation features. Hyper-Kamiokande, with a shorter baseline in Japan and enormous water Cherenkov volume, will attack similar parameters with different systematics and a powerful beam from J-PARC. The experiments are complements, not duplicates.

Agreement would matter more than either result alone.

Neutrinos have already taught this lesson. The solar-neutrino problem persisted for decades because different detectors measured different parts of the spectrum and reaction channels. Super-K saw elastic scattering. SNO used heavy water to distinguish charged-current interactions sensitive to electron neutrinos from neutral-current interactions sensitive to all active flavors. The neutral-current rate showed the missing solar electron neutrinos had changed flavor.

One detector saw a deficit.

Another changed the ontology of the deficit.

For CP violation, the corresponding goal is to avoid mistaking a model imperfection for a fundamental asymmetry.

There is a human-scale absurdity to the infrastructure. The far detector sits about a mile underground in a former gold mine. Excavation creates caverns large enough to contain four detector modules. Each module will hold tens of thousands of tons of liquid argon kept at cryogenic temperature.

Argon is ordinary atmospheric residue. Underground it becomes a camera for particles born in an accelerator three states away.

The beam begins with protons accelerated at Fermilab. They strike a target and produce pions and kaons. Magnetic horns focus particles of a selected charge. Their decays produce predominantly neutrinos or antineutrinos depending on the focusing configuration. The remaining hadrons and muons are absorbed. Neutrinos continue.

To ask whether the universe prefers matter, we begin with matter smashing matter to manufacture a beam of almost-matterless particles.

The chain is ridiculous and precise.

DUNE also broadens beyond oscillations. Its underground argon can detect neutrinos from a core-collapse supernova, especially electron-neutrino channels complementary to water and scintillator detectors. It can search for proton decay and other rare processes. It can probe physics beyond the Standard Model in neutrino interactions.

A single cavern becomes a listening post for several ways the Standard Model might fail.

For baryogenesis, the most publicized quantity is the CP phase \(\delta_{CP}\).

If \(\delta_{CP}\) is zero or pi, the standard Dirac-type CP violation in oscillations vanishes. Other values produce CP-odd effects. Current global fits and experiments provide hints about preferred regions, but the picture is not yet definitive and tensions can move as data accumulate.

DUNE is designed to reach discovery sensitivity for CP violation over a substantial fraction of possible phase values with sufficient exposure, and then measure the phase precisely.

“Fraction of possible values” is important. If nature chose a phase close to a CP-conserving value, no amount of experiment can truthfully discover large CP violation that is not there. Sensitivity depends on the true value.

A future null result could therefore mean several things.

The phase is near zero or pi.

The three-flavor model needs extension.

Systematics or statistics are insufficient.

Other CP phases relevant to Majorana neutrinos or heavy states may still exist.

Leptogenesis may still work through high-energy phases invisible to oscillations.

No single null result kills the origin story.

This underdetermination can feel unsatisfying until you compare it with the alternative: pretending the experiment says more than it does.

The point of DUNE is not to provide a final sentence. It is to eliminate entire paragraphs.

Suppose DUNE and Hyper-K both find a strongly CP-violating phase. The lepton sector joins the quark sector as a measured arena of CP violation, perhaps with a much larger phase. Models of flavor gain a new boundary. Some constrained leptogenesis frameworks become predictive enough to test against the sign or scale. Others remain indifferent.

Suppose they find no CP violation in oscillations. Many high-scale leptogenesis models survive because the relevant phases live elsewhere. But proposals claiming a direct low-energy link lose support. The negative result matters.

This asymmetry between exciting and boring outcomes is one of the strange incentives of science communication. “Neutrinos violate CP” writes itself. “A class of flavor-linked leptogenesis models becomes less plausible” does not.

The universe is indifferent to headline ergonomics.

There is another measurement DUNE will help settle: mass ordering.

Oscillation experiments know two independent mass-squared splittings. They know that two mass states are relatively close and the third is separated. But is the third state heavier than the pair—the normal ordering—or lighter—the inverted ordering? Matter effects over DUNE's long baseline produce different energy-dependent signatures, allowing the ordering to be identified.

This matters to neutrinoless double-beta decay because the expected effective Majorana mass ranges differ between orderings. It matters to cosmology because total mass constraints interact with the minimum sums implied by each ordering. It matters to flavor models and leptogenesis structures.

Again, one measurement constrains several stories.

The best frontier experiments have this property. They are not built around a single fragile hypothesis. They create a durable map.

DUNE's relation to baryogenesis is therefore less like a key and more like a survey marker.

We do not know which road the asymmetry took.

A precise CP phase fixes one coordinate.

A mass ordering fixes another.

A proton-decay limit fences off a region.

A supernova neutrino burst tests propagation over astronomical distance.

The map improves even if the treasure remains buried.

There is something useful here about the human role that avoids the usual observer metaphysics. Humans are spatially small and temporally late, but we can create baselines larger than cities and integrate exposures over decades. We compensate for weak interactions with infrastructure.

Scale is engineered.

A single neutrino scarcely notices us. Ten years of a megawatt beam and forty kilotons of argon produce a data set.

Knowledge is a collective cross section.

That phrase is a little cute, but the sociology is real. DUNE is an international collaboration with thousands of scientists and engineers. The detector depends on mining, cryogenics, high-voltage systems, electronics, software, accelerator operations, civil construction, materials science and public funding. No lone genius can build it.

The theory question may fit in one line. The answer requires an institution.

This mismatch between conceptual simplicity and operational complexity is characteristic of mature fundamental physics.

Why is there more matter than antimatter?

Easy sentence.

To test one relevant phase, excavate a mountain.

The mountain is not overkill. Neutrino interactions are that rare and cosmic-ray backgrounds that persistent.

This is what it costs to ask nature a question it has made difficult.

DUNE will not tell us whether we were intended.

It will not tell us why there is something in the metaphysical sense.

It may tell us whether one of the three known neutrino flavor transformations carries a matter-antimatter asymmetry and how large it is.

That is better.

Precise questions are how a civilization earns the right to approach larger ones.

The long bet is that enough precision, joined with enough other precision, will corner the ancient residue.

Illinois sends a beam.

South Dakota waits.

Between them, Earth itself helps split the possibilities.
