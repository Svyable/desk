# A Universe That Should Have Vanished

The cleanest universe is empty of us.

Start with the idea most introductory explanations use because it is both simple and dangerous: matter and antimatter are created in pairs, have equal masses, carry opposite charges where charge applies, and can annihilate when they meet. If the early universe began with exact equality and if nothing subsequently distinguished the populations in the right way, then almost all of that matter and antimatter would have found partners and converted their rest energy into radiation.

The remaining universe would not be literally nothing. Radiation is something. Neutrinos are something. Dark matter, depending on its identity and history, is something. Gravity does not resign because hydrogen failed to form. But the particular world that became galaxies full of long-lived stars, rocky planets and biochemistry would be absent or radically different. No baryonic leftovers, no periodic table in the familiar sense, no ribosome, no telescope.

The phrase “matter won” is therefore convenient and slightly wrong. Matter did not need a majority in any democratic sense. It needed a net excess that survived annihilation. Once the symmetric matter-antimatter component was consumed, the tiny difference became nearly the whole baryonic inventory.

That is a peculiar form of victory: not defeating an opponent so much as remaining after almost everyone paired off and disappeared.

To understand why this is hard, it helps to stop picturing annihilation as a science-fiction explosion between blocks of substance. At the particle level, annihilation and creation are ordinary quantum processes constrained by conservation laws and interaction probabilities. An electron and positron can annihilate into photons. At sufficiently high energy, photons can participate in processes that produce particle-antiparticle pairs. In a hot thermal bath, creation and destruction can proceed both ways. The early universe was not a room full of marbles searching for opposite-colored marbles. It was a dense, changing population of quantum excitations in which reactions occurred rapidly while expansion changed the temperature and the rates.

Equilibrium is the important word.

When reactions are fast compared with cosmic expansion, populations can track thermal equilibrium. As the universe cools, different processes become inefficient; species decouple or freeze out. The history of what remains depends on reaction rates, masses, expansion, conserved quantities and symmetry violations. Modern cosmology extracts remarkable evidence from those transitions. The abundance of light elements records conditions during Big Bang nucleosynthesis. The cosmic microwave background records the universe when photons last scattered freely from ordinary matter roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Neither is a photograph of baryogenesis itself, which occurred far earlier in most scenarios, but both constrain the baryon density that later existed.

The baryon asymmetry is often written as a ratio. One common quantity compares the net number of baryons to photons. The number is tiny, of order parts in ten billion. Another compares baryon number to entropy density. The exact numerical expression depends on convention, but the physical statement does not: the early universe retained a small positive net baryon number relative to the enormous radiation bath.

“Small” is doing strange work there. The residual was small compared with what annihilated. It is not small compared with the contents of the observable universe today.

This is one of those places where human intuition, trained on middle-sized objects and household accounting, slips. If a warehouse loses 99.9999999 percent of its inventory, we call it empty. If the initial inventory is cosmological and the remaining fraction is the only stable raw material available for later structure, the remainder can become a civilization.

The problem is not just that matter and antimatter annihilate. The deeper problem is symmetry. Relativistic quantum field theory does not casually allow particles and antiparticles to have arbitrary unrelated properties. CPT symmetry, under broad assumptions, ties them together profoundly. Charge conjugation swaps particles for antiparticles. Parity reflects spatial coordinates. Time reversal reverses the direction of time. The combined CPT operation is foundational enough that a measured violation would force an uncomfortable reconstruction of basic theory.

Experiments have spent decades asking whether the mirror is subtly defective. The answer so far is almost irritating: in many precision comparisons, matter and antimatter agree beautifully.

Take the proton and antiproton. BASE has compared their charge-to-mass ratios at extraordinary precision. The antiproton's magnetic moment has been measured and compared with the proton's. ALPHA has compared antihydrogen spectroscopy with hydrogen. These are not casual confirmations. They are attempts to locate any tiny departure that could expose new physics. The more closely the partners match, the more the cosmic absence of bulk antimatter demands explanation elsewhere.

The asymmetry we do know appears not primarily as “antimatter has a different mass” or “antimatter ignores gravity.” It appears in the probabilities of certain weak-interaction processes. Matter and antimatter can decay differently. That is a subtler kind of crack.

The 1964 discovery of CP violation in neutral kaons mattered because it showed that replacing particles with antiparticles and reflecting the spatial coordinates did not leave all weak processes unchanged. Nature had a preference hidden in decay amplitudes. This was precisely the sort of ingredient Sakharov would soon identify as necessary for dynamically generating a baryon asymmetry.

Necessary does not mean sufficient.

That distinction should be tattooed onto every popular account of baryogenesis.

The Standard Model includes CP violation through the complex phase in the CKM matrix describing quark mixing. Experiments at the B factories and LHCb have mapped this territory with increasing precision. In 2019 LHCb reported CP violation in D mesons. In 2025 it announced the first observation of CP violation in baryon decays, using beauty-lambda baryons and their antimatter counterparts. Each result expands our map of how matter and antimatter differ.

And still the known CP violation is not enough to explain the universe's matter dominance.

That is not a failure of the experiments. It is the point. Precision can deepen a mystery by closing easy exits.

The same is true of gravity. For decades one could hear speculative suggestions that antimatter might “fall up.” General relativity does not expect this. CPT-respecting quantum field theories do not casually offer antigravity as a free option. But direct measurement matters more than theoretical impatience. ALPHA-g was built vertically so trapped antihydrogen atoms could be slowly released and their annihilation positions recorded. In 2023 the collaboration reported the first direct observation of gravity's influence on the motion of antimatter, consistent within the experiment's precision with attractive downward acceleration.

Another easy exit narrowed.

This pattern gives the baryon asymmetry problem its texture. It is not a mystery because antimatter is poorly understood in every respect. We understand enough for the mismatch to be sharp. Antimatter is routinely produced in particle collisions. Positrons are used in medical imaging. Antiprotons can be trapped. Antihydrogen can be synthesized. Its spectrum can be measured. It falls. Yet when we turn from the laboratory to the sky, the expected macroscopic counterpart is missing.

Where is the antimatter?

One answer is: mostly gone, annihilated in the early universe, because a slight excess of matter remained.

That answer immediately produces the next question: what made the excess?

There are ways to imagine avoiding the question. Perhaps matter and antimatter were simply separated into enormous cosmic domains. Our region is matter; distant regions are antimatter; the apparent asymmetry is local rather than global. This idea has been studied seriously. It runs into observational constraints because boundaries between matter and antimatter domains should generate annihilation signatures, particularly gamma rays, and because such domains can affect cosmological observables. The absence of expected signatures pushes simple versions of a matter-antimatter domain universe to very large scales.

Perhaps the initial condition simply contained more matter and no mechanism is required. That is logically possible as a stance, but it moves rather than solves the explanatory problem. Cosmologists prefer mechanisms when a dynamical account can connect observed asymmetry to testable physics. Sakharov's program begins with the harder premise: suppose the universe started with no net baryon number. What physics can generate one?

Perhaps the asymmetry is an artifact of what we can observe. Could antimatter galaxies exist so far away that their annihilation boundaries are hidden? Again, constraints matter. There is ongoing interest in cosmic antinuclei because a convincing detection of a complex antinucleus such as antihelium with an astrophysical origin could have enormous implications. But ordinary secondary production in cosmic-ray collisions already generates some antiparticles, so the evidentiary bar is high. An antiproton is not an anti-galaxy calling card. You need to understand backgrounds, production channels, detector systematics and propagation through the Galaxy.

The universe is rarely considerate enough to label its anomalies.

The reason I want to linger on annihilation is that it changes how we think about cosmic inventory. We often tell origin stories by accumulation. Dust accumulates into planetesimals. Mass accumulates into stars. Mutations accumulate. Capital accumulates. Knowledge accumulates. The matter-antimatter story begins with subtraction.

A huge population is removed pair by pair. The physically consequential quantity is the difference.

Differences can be more durable than totals.

That sounds abstract until you look at the microwave sky. The cosmic microwave background is a bath of photons left from a time when the universe became transparent to light. Its detailed anisotropies encode parameters of cosmology, including the amount of ordinary baryonic matter. Those photons vastly outnumber baryons. The ratio between net baryons and photons is part of the evidence that ordinary matter is a small residue relative to the thermal population of the early universe.

The night sky makes this hard to feel. Galaxies are bright because matter clumped gravitationally and stars ignited. Radiation from the early annihilation era is diluted and redshifted into backgrounds. The residue became structured; the enormous canceled component became comparatively uniform radiation. Our eyes are drawn to structure and mistake visual prominence for original abundance.

This is selection by consequence.

A surviving proton can join a nucleus. A nucleus can join an atom. Atoms can cool into clouds. Clouds can collapse. Fusion can manufacture carbon and oxygen. Chemistry can store gradients. None of this is guaranteed by the bare existence of a baryon asymmetry; dark matter and gravity, stellar evolution, nuclear physics and many other conditions matter. But without the ordinary matter inventory, the branch that leads to observers like us is cut very early.

The phrase anthropic principle often enters at this point and usually makes the conversation less precise. There are legitimate anthropic arguments in cosmology, especially when asking what values observers could measure under selection effects. But “we observe matter because observers require matter” is not a mechanism for baryogenesis. It does not tell you why there is a positive baryon asymmetry, what fields generated it, what symmetries were violated, or what experimental signature should appear next.

Observer selection can constrain interpretation. It cannot replace dynamics.

That is a recurring mistake whenever humans confront a parameter that seems necessary for our existence. Necessity for observers is not causal explanation. A drowning person necessarily observes that some air remained in the overturned boat. That does not explain who sealed the hatch.

The asymmetry problem is more interesting because physics gives us handles. We know conditions under which a net baryon number might be generated. We can test CP violation. We can search for baryon-number violating processes such as proton decay or neutron-antineutron oscillations. We can measure neutrino properties that bear on leptogenesis scenarios. We can search for new particles and phase transitions. We can improve cosmological constraints.

The gap is not mystical. It is experimentally surrounded.

Consider proton decay. Many grand unified theories naturally permit baryon-number violation and predict that protons, although fantastically long-lived, are not absolutely stable. Experiments such as Super-Kamiokande have spent decades watching enormous volumes of water for the characteristic signatures of a proton decaying. No confirmed proton decay has appeared. The resulting lifetime limits are huge. The absence constrains grand unified models and, by extension, some baryogenesis ideas.

The apparatus is wonderful in its audacity: gather tens of thousands of tons of water, put it deep underground, surround it with photomultiplier tubes, and wait to see whether matter eventually admits that baryon number was never sacred.

A null result can be a piece of cosmology.

Neutron-antineutron oscillation searches make a related wager. If a neutron can transform into an antineutron, baryon number changes by two units. Such a process would be spectacular evidence of physics beyond the Standard Model and could connect to mechanisms that created the cosmic asymmetry. No observation yet. Better searches are proposed and built because the question is not going away.

Then there are neutrinos, which complicate everything in the productive way neutrinos usually do. They have tiny nonzero masses, unlike the minimal Standard Model's original formulation. They change flavor as they propagate. The ordering of their masses and the exact nature of their CP-violating phase remain targets of major experiments. If neutrinos are Majorana particles—if, in a technical sense, the neutrino can be its own antiparticle—then lepton-number violation becomes possible in a particularly profound way. Heavy Majorana neutrinos in the early universe could decay asymmetrically into leptons and antileptons; electroweak processes could then convert part of that lepton asymmetry into the baryon asymmetry we see. This family of ideas is called leptogenesis.

It is elegant because it makes two mysteries touch: why neutrinos are so light and why matter survived.

Elegance is not evidence.

The experiments remain decisive. Neutrinoless double-beta decay searches ask whether a nuclear process can occur that emits two electrons without the neutrinos ordinary double-beta decay would produce. A confirmed signal would establish lepton-number violation and strongly support Majorana neutrinos, though translating that into a specific cosmic leptogenesis history would still require care. Long-baseline oscillation experiments such as T2K, NOvA, DUNE and Hyper-Kamiokande probe a different question: whether neutrinos and antineutrinos oscillate differently in a way governed by leptonic CP violation.

Every route has caveats. Low-energy CP violation may not map uniquely onto high-energy leptogenesis. A Majorana neutrino does not by itself prove the observed baryon asymmetry arose through leptogenesis. Proton decay, if discovered, would not hand us a timestamped video of baryogenesis. Physics advances by narrowing the space of stories until some survive contact with enough independent facts.

That brings us back to the empty universe.

The phrase “should have vanished” is deliberately provocative, not a literal consensus prediction that the universe had to be empty. We do not know the full high-energy theory or initial conditions. The statement is conditional: under exact matter-antimatter symmetry with the relevant processes in equilibrium and no asymmetry-generating mechanism, the ordinary matter component we inhabit would annihilate away to a far smaller residue than observed.

The real universe did something else.

It kept a difference.

For the rest of the book, that difference will be our thread. Sometimes it appears as a measurable decay asymmetry in a detector. Sometimes as a missing process. Sometimes as a theoretical condition. Sometimes as a future experiment under a mountain. Sometimes as a parameter inferred from microwave photons that have been traveling since the universe became transparent.

The scale will change. The question will not.

What prevented the universe from balancing its books?
