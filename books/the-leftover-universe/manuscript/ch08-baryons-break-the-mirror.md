# Baryons Break the Mirror

The proton is almost offensively stable.

Put one in a hydrogen atom and, as far as every experiment has established, you can leave it there. Civilizations rise. Continents move. Stars burn through fractions of their fuel. The proton continues being a proton.

This makes baryons feel like the wrong place to look for betrayal.

Mesons have always been the flamboyant members of the hadron family. Kaons mix with antikaons. B mesons decay through rich interference patterns. D mesons reveal tiny asymmetries after enormous data sets. Mesons made CP violation visible because their particle-antiparticle mixing creates natural interferometers.

Baryons, the three-quark states that make ordinary matter, seemed quieter.

Then in 2025 LHCb announced the first observation of CP violation in a baryon decay.

The result involved the beauty lambda baryon, \(\Lambda_b^0\), a composite particle containing an up quark, a down quark and a bottom quark. Its antiparticle contains the corresponding antiquarks. LHCb compared decay patterns of the baryon and antibaryon into final states containing a proton or antiproton, a kaon and pions. The measured difference crossed the five-standard-deviation threshold particle physics traditionally uses to call an observation.

For the first time, a baryon and an antibaryon had been caught decaying with demonstrably different probabilities.

This is easy to oversell.

The decay does not explain the cosmic matter-antimatter asymmetry. It is consistent with CP violation in the Standard Model's quark sector. It does not supply the missing orders of magnitude for baryogenesis. It does not show that protons themselves decay differently from antiprotons. The \(\Lambda_b\) is a heavy unstable baryon produced in high-energy collisions and lives for about a picosecond before decaying.

Still, there is something physically clarifying about seeing CP violation in the same broad class of particles that contains us.

Baryonic matter is not exempt from the crack.

The result came from Run 1 and Run 2 data of the Large Hadron Collider, where proton collisions produce heavy quarks in abundance. The LHCb detector is built asymmetrically along the beam line because bottom and charm particles tend to emerge in the forward region. Its geometry looks less like the barrel-shaped general-purpose detectors ATLAS and CMS and more like a long instrument aimed into the spray.

The collaboration reconstructs decay chains from tracks that exist for fractions of a nanosecond. A beauty hadron travels millimeters before decaying. That distance, microscopic to us and huge to a silicon detector, separates the secondary decay vertex from the proton-proton collision point. The pattern of daughter particles carries the information.

There is no little arrow marked CP.

A statistical difference has to be extracted from detector effects, production asymmetries and backgrounds. Matter and antimatter may be produced at slightly different rates in proton-proton collisions simply because the initial state is matter, not a CP-symmetric laboratory universe. Positive and negative particles can interact differently with detector material. Magnet polarity can bias tracking acceptance. The analysis has to disentangle instrumental asymmetry from decay asymmetry.

This is the part of modern discovery that newspaper language compresses away. “Scientists find difference between matter and antimatter” can mean years of calibration to prove the difference did not come from a cable, a magnetic field, a trigger threshold or the fact that the collider begins with protons.

The claim is only interesting if the apparatus is not the culprit.

The 2025 baryon result therefore belongs to a lineage of increasingly controlled asymmetry measurements. The kaon discovery showed CP is broken. B factories established large effects consistent with CKM physics. LHCb extended the map across charm and baryons. The pattern is coherent enough that the Standard Model remains annoyingly successful.

That adjective—annoyingly—belongs to theorists, not nature.

There is no requirement that a mystery provide us with a convenient failure.

The CKM framework can accommodate the observed baryon decay asymmetry while still being incapable of cosmological baryogenesis. That combination is a useful lesson in scale. A process can show a locally substantial CP asymmetry and yet contribute negligibly to the early universe's net baryon production because the relevant particles, temperatures, interaction rates and equilibrium conditions differ.

Laboratory size and cosmological relevance do not order themselves by percentage.

A ten-percent asymmetry in a rare modern decay is not automatically more useful than a one-part-in-a-million effect in an early-universe channel that acts on an enormous population. What matters is the entire kinetic network.

The LHCb result does something else: it forces us to remember that baryons are not elemental blocks.

A proton is made of quarks and gluons. Its mass is mostly not the sum of bare quark masses; much comes from QCD field energy and dynamics. The same is true, in its own arrangement, of the neutron. When we say the universe has a baryon asymmetry, we are talking about a conserved or approximately conserved net quantum number carried by composite objects today but associated with quark degrees of freedom in the early plasma.

The baryon was not born as a proton.

At temperatures above the QCD confinement scale, quarks and gluons were not bound into individual hadrons as they are now. As the universe cooled through the QCD transition, quarks became confined into baryons and mesons. A pre-existing net baryon number was repackaged into protons, neutrons and other hadrons. Most unstable hadrons decayed. Protons survived; neutrons survived when bound in stable nuclei or decayed when free.

The residue changed containers.

This is why the phrase “one extra proton per billion antiprotons” is pedagogically useful and historically imprecise. At the likely era of baryogenesis in many models, protons and antiprotons were not the relevant stable objects. The asymmetry lived in fields and quark populations before hadronization.

Later, the QCD transition translated that asymmetry into matter recognizable by a nuclear physicist.

There is an almost bureaucratic continuity to this. The form of the inventory changes, the net account carries forward.

Baryons also offer one of the most direct possible tests of baryon-number violation: proton decay.

If grand unified theories are right in certain forms, the proton's apparent stability is temporary. Quarks and leptons may belong to larger multiplets and interactions mediated by ultraheavy gauge bosons can turn a quark into a lepton. A proton could decay into, say, a positron and neutral pion. The predicted lifetimes are enormous because the mediating scale is enormous.

No proton decay has been seen.

Super-Kamiokande, a cylindrical tank containing 50,000 tons of ultrapure water under Mount Ikenoyama in Japan, watches for Cherenkov light from charged particles produced in neutrino interactions and possible nucleon decays. The underground location suppresses cosmic-ray backgrounds. Thousands of photomultiplier tubes line the walls. The water supplies roughly \(10^{34}\) protons to watch.

The detector converts patience into sample size.

For a decay mode with a lifetime far longer than the age of the universe, observing one proton is hopeless. Observing an astronomical number at once makes the expected number of decays per year finite enough to test. The logic resembles radioactive decay experiments taken to a delirious extreme.

Hyper-Kamiokande will expand this strategy with a far larger fiducial mass. DUNE, although designed primarily for neutrino physics, will also search for proton decay in liquid argon, with particular sensitivity to channels involving charged kaons because its imaging can distinguish detailed ionization tracks.

If proton decay appears, it will not merely establish that matter is mortal.

It will prove baryon number is not exact.

That would unlock Sakharov's first door experimentally at low energy, though the specific relation to early-universe baryogenesis would depend on the decay mechanism and theory. A proton-decay discovery could support grand unification and certain baryogenesis scenarios while ruling out others.

The absence already matters. Minimal SU(5), one of the earliest grand unified theories, predicted proton decay rates incompatible with modern limits. The idea of grand unification survived; the simplest implementation did not.

A missing flash in a tank can kill a beautiful theory.

There is another route: neutron-antineutron oscillation.

Unlike proton decay, which changes a baryon into leptonic and mesonic products in many GUT models, neutron-antineutron conversion would turn a baryon directly into its antibaryon. The change in baryon number would be two units. A free neutron could, in principle, develop an antineutron component through quantum mixing, analogous in broad mathematical spirit to neutral-meson oscillation but separated by an enormous suppression scale.

If that antineutron then encountered matter, it would annihilate.

Experiments search either for free oscillations in intense neutron beams or for related processes inside nuclei. Magnetic fields must be controlled because a neutron and antineutron have opposite magnetic moments; environmental energy splitting suppresses coherent oscillation. Long free-flight times help. The experiment is a strange attempt to give a neutron enough quiet to forget which side of matter it belongs to.

No oscillation has been observed.

Future facilities hope to improve sensitivity by orders of magnitude. A positive result would be among the clearest possible signs that baryon number is a contingent low-energy habit rather than an exact law.

Baryon-number violation also appears in electroweak theory in a less accessible form. Sphaleron transitions can change baryon and lepton number at high temperature, but at today's temperatures the rate is exponentially suppressed. We cannot simply wait for a proton in a laboratory to undergo an electroweak sphaleron event. The early universe had access to configurations our cold vacuum effectively forbids.

Temperature is a key to laws that look like conservation laws.

This is an unsettling but ordinary idea in field theory. The effective behavior of symmetries depends on the state of the system. Electroweak symmetry is restored at high temperature. The Higgs field's vacuum expectation value changes with thermal conditions. Processes that are negligible now can dominate then.

The universe's youth had permissions its old age revoked.

One of the hardest problems in baryogenesis is that our experiments occur in the old universe.

We can heat tiny regions in colliders, but not reproduce a homogeneous cosmic plasma at \(10^{10}\) or \(10^{15}\) GeV. We can create quark-gluon plasma droplets, but they last microscopic times and carry different initial conditions. We can infer finite-temperature field behavior through theory and lattice simulation. We can look for relics. We can test the low-energy descendants of high-energy interactions.

The reconstruction is forensic.

Baryons are unusually good forensic material because they are what survived. Their properties can be measured exquisitely. Their stability can be challenged. Their CP-violating decays can be mapped in heavy cousins. Their abundances can be inferred cosmologically.

But the proton itself has not confessed.

It has no measured EDM.

It has not decayed.

It has not oscillated into an antiproton.

Its antiproton partner matches its fundamental properties to extraordinary precision.

The ordinary building block remains conservative while the larger baryon family reveals small asymmetries.

This division is probably historical accident more than cosmic message. Heavy unstable baryons provide accessible weak decays with interference. Protons are the lightest baryons and are stable if baryon number is conserved, or extraordinarily long-lived if it is only approximately conserved. Nature is not hiding the answer in a symbolic contrast.

Still, the experimental juxtaposition is useful.

We can make a \(\Lambda_b\), watch it live for a picosecond and learn that its antimatter partner decays differently.

We can watch \(10^{34}\) protons for years and learn that none has obviously decayed at all.

Different time scales interrogate different violations.

The baryon asymmetry itself spans more time than either. Whatever established the net excess did so in the early universe. The resulting baryons survived through primordial nucleosynthesis, recombination, stellar formation and nuclear burning. Some were converted between protons and neutrons in reactions. Some spent billions of years inside stars. Some became the calcium in bone.

The baryon number migrated through history.

This makes humans a particularly ridiculous detector medium. A seventy-kilogram body contains on the order of \(10^{28}\) baryons. The exact count depends on composition. Almost every gram of ordinary tissue is baryonic mass. We are macroscopic heaps of the unexplained excess.

That does not make the body a precision baryogenesis instrument. It makes the explanatory gap impossible to externalize.

The problem is not “out there” in a distant quasar.

The sample is the hand holding the page.

If baryogenesis had produced the opposite sign, the hand—if some analogous chemistry still evolved—would be made of antimatter and call itself matter. Sign conventions are ours. Physics does not label baryons morally positive. The profound fact is not that our sign won. It is that one sign dominates.

This is why the 2025 baryon CP result should be read with modest excitement.

It closes no cosmological case.

It adds a new family to the list of places where the mirror is measurably imperfect.

It tells us baryons themselves can host weak-interaction CP asymmetry.

It tests the CKM framework in a domain where strong-interaction dynamics are more complicated than in many meson decays.

It challenges theorists to calculate hadronic effects accurately enough that any deviation from the Standard Model can be recognized.

And it gives future data something to sharpen.

Run 3 of the LHC and the upgraded LHCb detector will accumulate larger samples. Belle II offers complementary flavor measurements in a cleaner electron-positron environment. Future colliders could increase heavy-flavor precision further. The goal is not merely to catalogue CP violation. It is to find inconsistency.

A pattern that refuses to fit CKM physics could expose new phases.

New phases are exactly what baryogenesis would like.

This is an awkward scientific relationship: cosmology is waiting for particle physics to misbehave.

So far, it mostly behaves.

The first baryon CP violation is real. The cosmic residue is real. The bridge between them is not.

That distinction keeps the book honest.

We are baryons asking baryons whether their mirror broke badly enough to make baryons.

The answer, at present, is no.
