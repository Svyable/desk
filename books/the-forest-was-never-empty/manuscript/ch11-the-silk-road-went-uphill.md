# The Silk Road Went Uphill

The phrase Silk Road carries its own topography: caravans crossing open dry country between famous oasis cities.

Then drone LiDAR mapped two highland settlements in Uzbekistan and complicated the picture.

Tugunbulak and Tashbulak sit at elevations around two thousand meters. Archaeological work reported in 2024 used drone-based laser scanning to map fortified urban forms and associated features. Tugunbulak, the larger site, covered on the order of a square kilometer. The work suggested that high-altitude communities were more important to medieval Central Asian trade, industry and political geography than conventional Silk Road maps implied.

The case is different from tropical forest LiDAR because vegetation is not the primary villain. Here the instrument’s value is microtopography and rapid, detailed mapping across complex terrain.

That difference matters. “LiDAR archaeology” is not one method solving one visibility problem. The same measurement principle can remove canopy, detect slight relief, map fortifications, model watersheds or create high-resolution surfaces for architectural analysis.

Tugunbulak also helps break the book out of a tropical determinism. The preservation bias is global. Forest hides through vegetation. Highlands hide through remoteness, erosion and scholarly attention. Agricultural plowing can erase. Desert sand can bury. Urban redevelopment destroys while simultaneously exposing.

The highland cities challenge another assumption: that major trade networks are best reconstructed from the places later historians decided were major. Samarkand is visible in the historical imagination. A mountain city with fewer texts is not.

Remote sensing cannot repair textual inequality by itself, but it can identify physical systems that deserve excavation. If industrial zones, defensive walls and dense habitation survive as surface relief, the map can force economic history to look uphill.

The reported interpretation of Tugunbulak includes metal production and substantial population. Those claims need the same evidence discipline used elsewhere in this book. Mapped area is Class 1. Function inferred from archaeological material is stronger when excavated. Population is modeled. Importance to trade is synthesis requiring artifacts, routes, chronology and regional comparison.

The distinction protects the site from becoming another “lost metropolis” headline that outruns its stratigraphy.

There is a broader philosophical point. Historical narratives have centers because evidence has centers. Archives accumulate where states wrote. Excavations accumulate where archaeologists worked. Tourism reinforces famous locations. Funding follows significance already established.

LiDAR can behave like a mild anti-center technology. It samples surfaces continuously across places scholars might otherwise treat as between-sites. In doing so, it sometimes finds that the between was occupied.

That does not abolish historical centers. It changes the network around them.

The Silk Road did not move when Tugunbulak was mapped. Our representation of where consequential people could live along it did.
