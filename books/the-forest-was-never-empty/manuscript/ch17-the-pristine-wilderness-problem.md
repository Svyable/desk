# The Pristine Wilderness Problem

The phrase pristine wilderness contains at least three separate ideas that should not be allowed to travel together unnoticed.

One is ecological value: landscapes with low modern industrial disturbance are worth protecting.

Another is demographic history: few people lived there in the past.

The third is land-use history: past people altered the ecosystem only lightly.

LiDAR archaeology has made the second and third propositions harder to generalize in parts of the tropics without changing the first at all.

Southwestern Amazonian earthworks, terra preta, managed plant distributions, raised fields, causeways and settlement networks indicate long histories of landscape intervention in some regions. Upper Amazonian roads and agricultural systems do the same. None of this means the entire Amazon was built environment. The 2026 survey itself found low-density and apparently empty transects and emphasized heterogeneous soils and settlement patterns.

The important word is mosaic.

Some forests may regrow over formerly dense settlement. Some may preserve old orchards and useful species distributions. Some interfluves may have served as buffers. Some regions may have remained lightly occupied for long periods. The human history of a biome is not a binary between untouched and urbanized.

This becomes politically charged because different groups use precolonial population in opposite ways. One argument says extensive ancient human management proves modern extraction is natural or harmless. That does not follow. Indigenous burning, agroforestry or earthwork construction is not equivalent to industrial deforestation, mining or cattle conversion.

The opposite argument imagines conservation requires a people-free past. That is equally unnecessary and can erase Indigenous land management.

Archaeology can make conservation history more accurate without becoming a warrant for either dispossession or development.

There is a climate-model implication too. If precolonial land use was more extensive than previously estimated, models of vegetation, carbon cycling and human impact may need regional revision. The 2026 Nature authors explicitly raised the possibility that some current forest structure and even broader environmental modeling could be affected if similar patterns are found elsewhere.

“If” carries much of that sentence.

A handful of densely engineered landscapes cannot be extrapolated across six million square kilometers of Amazonia by rhetoric. This is exactly the kind of move the book’s evidence classes are designed to stop.

Class 1: mapped earthworks and excavated soils in specified regions.

Class 2: inferred settlement and management systems.

Class 3: basin-wide claims exceeding current survey coverage.

Class 4: the hypothesis that a meaningful fraction of modern ecological patterning contains a cultural inheritance we have not yet mapped.

That final possibility is compelling because it changes what recovery means. Forest can be both old ecological system and historical artifact. Biodiversity can contain signatures of people without becoming artificial.

The discovery is not that wilderness was fake.

It is that human and wild were never clean archaeological categories.
