# Roads in the Upper Amazon

The roads are what make the Upano Valley difficult to explain away as a collection of impressive mounds.

Archaeologists had worked at sites in Ecuador’s Upper Amazon for decades. Mounds were known. Excavations existed. Then LiDAR supplied the regional grammar. In 2024 Stéphen Rostain, Antoine Dorison and colleagues described in Science a dense system of pre-Hispanic urban centers occupied from around 500 BCE into the first millennium CE. The terrain models showed clusters of rectangular platforms and plazas tied together by remarkably straight, broad roads, with agricultural drainage and terracing threaded through the same landscape.

A mound can be local. A road several kilometers long is a decision about elsewhere.

The Upano system matters because it resists several inherited pictures at once. It is early. It is Amazonian. It is built heavily in earth rather than dressed stone. And its urbanism is distributed rather than compressed into the skyline people expect when they hear city.

That last point caused predictable trouble in the press. “Lost cities” is an irresistible phrase. It makes the forest a curtain and the archaeologist the person who pulls it back. Kathryn Reese-Taylor’s 2025 review of LiDAR coverage in news media is useful here because it asks what “lost” actually means. Some sites were known to local communities or archaeologists before scanning. What LiDAR changed was the mapped extent and connectivity.

Upano is a perfect example. Rostain had excavated major settlements long before the airborne survey. The laser did not create the archaeology. It showed how pieces fit.

The reported landscape contains thousands of earthen platforms, agricultural features and a road network extending over tens of kilometers. Some roads are strikingly straight across difficult terrain. Their existence implies labor, repeated traffic and a regional conception of space. Streets organize neighborhoods; longer roads connect settlements. The distinction between settlement and network begins to blur.

“Garden urbanism” is the phrase the authors used, and it is better than lost city because it preserves the agricultural fabric. Fields are not outside the city in the way a modern zoning map might place them. Cultivation, drainage, houses, plazas and roads coexist in a managed landscape.

That arrangement helps explain why earlier models of Amazonian complexity could undercount it. If the expected city is a dense masonry core, a dispersed earthen network looks rural. If the expected landscape is pristine rainforest, old field systems look natural after enough vegetation returns.

But the strongest version of the argument still requires restraint. LiDAR provides form, not function. A platform interpreted as residential needs excavation. Roads visible together are not automatically contemporary. Population estimates vary with assumptions about household numbers and occupation spans. Even the word urbanism carries theoretical commitments.

The safe claim is already large: excavation and LiDAR together document an early, extensively engineered settlement network in the Upper Amazon whose scale and connectivity were poorly captured by older maps.

The speculative claim is more interesting: there may be several kinds of city that archaeology has systematically under-recognized because they do not fossilize into the monumental forms by which Old World urbanism taught us to recognize itself.

Upano does not need to become Rome in the rainforest to matter. It matters because it was Upano.
