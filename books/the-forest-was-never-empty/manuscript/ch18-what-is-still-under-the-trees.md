# What Is Still Under the Trees

The most irresponsible number in this book would be an estimate of how many undiscovered cities remain.

So I will not give one.

The temptation is obvious. We now have examples where a relatively small amount of new scanning multiplied known features, connected isolated sites into systems or revealed structures of surprising scale. Why not extrapolate the surprise itself?

Because discovery rate depends on where we look, what survives, point density, canopy, geology, historical land use, existing scholarship and the feature class we are willing to recognize. There is no stable denominator called “the world’s hidden archaeology.”

What we can say is more useful.

Large areas of tropical forest have never been covered by archaeological-grade LiDAR. Some areas have been flown for forestry or topography but not systematically examined for archaeology. Existing datasets vary wildly in resolution. Dense canopy can defeat even good surveys. Machine-assisted analysis is only beginning to scale human inspection.

The unseen archive is therefore not hypothetical. It is partly sitting on hard drives.

The next decade will combine airborne LiDAR with satellite synthetic-aperture radar, multispectral and hyperspectral imagery, photogrammetry, historical aerial photographs, geophysics and automated pattern detection. Each sees a different property. LiDAR measures surface geometry. Radar responds to roughness and moisture in ways that can complement optical data. Spectral instruments can detect vegetation or soil differences. Magnetometry and ground-penetrating radar address subsurface features at smaller scale.

The powerful move is not choosing the best sensor. It is stacking sensors until a hypothesis becomes overconstrained.

A suspected road should exist as topography, perhaps vegetation difference, perhaps soil compaction, then finally excavation. A hidden settlement should generate more than rectangles: ceramics, dates, botanical residues, water use, paths and demographic consequences.

This is where the romance of the lost city finally becomes useful. It attracts attention to a technical revolution whose real product is not cities but completeness.

Archaeology has spent much of its history building extraordinary explanations from extraordinary samples. Monumental centers were excavated because they were visible and important. The ordinary landscape was sampled around them. LiDAR reverses the ratio. It can map the ordinary continuously and make the monument one feature among thousands.

That changes historical questions. Instead of asking where the king built, we can ask how far people walked to plazas. Instead of asking whether a reservoir existed, we can ask which neighborhoods it could serve. Instead of describing a road, we can model an entire network.

The danger is that technical abundance creates false certainty. More points are not automatically more history. Every scan must still pass through classification, interpretation, chronology and social theory.

The reward is that absence becomes harder to confuse with invisibility.

The forest was never empty in the universal sense suggested by the title. Some places were empty. Some were sparsely used. Some were crowded. Some were gardens, hunting grounds, ceremonial landscapes or buffer zones. The correction is not to replace one sweeping myth with another.

The correction is to map enough ground that we no longer need the myth.

There is an image I keep returning to. An aircraft crosses a canopy that looks continuous from the window. Below it are living trees, modern communities, old property lines, abandoned roads, ancient ditches, terraces, mounds, rivers that moved, fields that became forest, and places no person has occupied intensively for centuries. The laser pulse returns a fraction of that history as elevation.

Then we begin the harder work of deciding what the shape means.
