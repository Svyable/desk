# Roads, Ditches, Reservoirs, Fields

If I had to choose one category of archaeological feature to rescue from the word infrastructure, I would choose the ditch.

Infrastructure sounds abstract until somebody has to dig it.

A ditch records labor in negative. Soil was removed. Water, people or attackers were expected to behave differently afterward. The same is true of a road, reservoir, terrace or raised field. These are not merely background improvements around the real archaeology. They are frozen arguments about how a society expected the landscape to work.

LiDAR is unusually good at such arguments because topographic engineering persists as shape. A filled ditch remains a shallow trough. A causeway remains slightly high. Agricultural terracing repeats contour lines. A reservoir embankment can survive after the reservoir becomes forest.

The recent discoveries become more coherent when sorted by infrastructure rather than civilization.

Maya surveys reveal causeways linking centers and residential groups, defensive ditches and agricultural modifications. Angkor reveals water systems whose scale can be modeled hydrologically. Upper Amazon scans reveal roads crossing long distances among settlements. Southwestern Amazonian earthworks reveal enclosure geometry and ceremonial approaches. Bolivian lowlands reveal causeways, canals and reservoirs binding settlements to seasonal water regimes.

The shared fact is not that all these societies were secretly alike. It is that surface engineering is one of the most persistent archaeological archives available to remote sensing.

This shifts the unit of analysis from artifact to system. A ceramic vessel can move. A ditch usually cannot. Roads encode destinations. Field systems encode repeated cultivation. Reservoirs encode expected water stress.

They also expose coordination without telling us exactly who coordinated. A ten-kilometer road demands labor but not necessarily a king. Large public works can be organized through states, corporate groups, ritual obligations, seasonal gatherings or coercion. Archaeology must infer social mechanisms from more than scale.

The geometry can nevertheless constrain fantasies. A supposed ceremonial feature that connects directly into drainage channels may have had hydraulic functions. A road that terminates at a plaza suggests different traffic than one leading to fields. A reservoir above a settlement can potentially feed it; one below cannot do so by gravity.

This is where high-resolution terrain becomes almost forensic. Elevation, slope and flow direction are measurable. Claims about infrastructure can be tested against them.

The danger is functional overconfidence. Archaeologists love naming lines. Road. Wall. Canal. Boundary. One feature can change use over centuries. A channel may drain during one phase and mark a property edge later. LiDAR sees morphology at the final surface, often blending episodes.

The solution is not to become timid. It is to pair geometry with cuts through the geometry.

Excavate the ditch. Date sediment. Look for maintenance episodes. Sample pollen and phytoliths. Trace where the road surface was repaired. Test whether embankments were built once or repeatedly enlarged.

A point cloud gives the plan. Stratigraphy gives the verbs.

The most important recent LiDAR discoveries are full of verbs hiding inside nouns: people drained, raised, linked, diverted, defended, terraced, gathered and maintained.

The map becomes history when those actions can be dated.
