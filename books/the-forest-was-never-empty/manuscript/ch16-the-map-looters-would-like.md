# The Map Looters Would Like

An archaeological map can be scientifically incomplete and operationally perfect for a looter.

Remote sensing creates an ethical asymmetry. Researchers may need years to interpret a feature responsibly. Someone looking for saleable objects only needs coordinates.

High-resolution LiDAR therefore produces a heritage-security problem almost by design. It can identify previously unknown structures in places too remote for routine protection. Publishing exact locations can convert scientific discovery into a target list.

The issue is especially acute where state heritage agencies are underfunded, conflict is present, or antiquities markets reward objects detached from context. Once a tomb is emptied, the loss is not primarily the artifact. It is association. Stratigraphy, placement, neighboring materials and human remains turn objects into history. Looting converts history into inventory.

Researchers have responded in several ways: withholding precise coordinates, publishing generalized maps, sharing sensitive layers only with local authorities and collaborators, or delaying release until protection can be organized. Each choice conflicts with another scientific value. Open data improves reproducibility. Restricted data protects sites.

There is no universal answer because risk varies by place and feature type.

LiDAR complicates Indigenous sovereignty as well. A scan may cross Indigenous territories and reveal archaeological features that academic teams did not create and do not own culturally. The 2026 southwestern Amazon study explicitly withheld exact positions for some features in contemporary Indigenous territories. That is not an inconvenience to open science. It is an acknowledgment that archaeological knowledge has stakeholders other than researchers and readers.

The rhetoric of discovery can itself be extractive. “Scientists discover lost city” may erase the fact that local people knew a place, named it, traveled through it or maintained traditions about it. Technical novelty becomes a claim of epistemic ownership.

A better practice distinguishes discovery to remote sensing from discovery to humanity.

This matters because LiDAR is often funded and operated by institutions far from the landscapes being mapped. Aircraft, sensors, software and data storage cost money. Researchers with access to them can acquire a kind of aerial authority. The people living below become collaborators only if the project is designed that way.

The point cloud has no ethics. The survey does.

There is also a conservation paradox. Revealing ancient landscape engineering can strengthen Indigenous and historical claims about long-term occupation and management. The same data can attract tourism, development and looting.

Archaeological visibility is not automatically a public good.

For this reason I resist the explorer’s fantasy that more revelation is always better. Some sites are safer partly because nobody outside the region can find them. A responsible archaeology occasionally knows something and declines to publish the coordinate.

The laser can remove the trees from an image. It should not remove judgment from the people using it.
