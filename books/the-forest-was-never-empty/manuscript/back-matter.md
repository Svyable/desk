# Back Matter

The strongest claim in this draft is methodological: recent LiDAR work demonstrates that archaeological visibility has been an active source of bias in reconstructions of settlement scale, especially in forested and low-relief landscapes.

The book does not claim that all forests were densely populated, that every geometric LiDAR anomaly is archaeological, that modeled population maxima are observed counts, or that recent discoveries support a single global lost civilization. It treats those moves as failures of evidence classification.

A publication-length pass should deepen each case through primary-paper reconstruction, especially the 2018 PACUNAM Maya dataset, the 2025 Dzibanché work, the 2025 regional Maya demographic model, the 2024 Upano Valley Science paper, the 2026 Aquiry Nature paper, Casarabe LiDAR, Angkor and Mahendraparvata, and the 2024 highland Central Asian work. It should also add a technical appendix on return classification, point density, DEM derivatives, uncertainty propagation and machine-learning validation.

The unresolved question is not how many lost cities exist. It is how much historical inference has been built on landscapes whose archaeological visibility was never measured.
