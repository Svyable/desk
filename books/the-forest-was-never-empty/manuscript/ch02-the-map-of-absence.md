# The Map of Absence

Archaeology has always had a visibility problem disguised as a geography problem.

A stone city in an arid landscape advertises itself. A low earthen settlement in humid forest does not. That difference sounds obvious until one notices how easily it becomes theory. Regions with standing masonry acquire architectural histories. Regions where wood rotted and earthworks softened acquire arguments about sparse populations, simple political organization or environmental limits. The surviving object quietly becomes evidence for the society that built it; the missing object quietly becomes evidence that no equivalent society existed.

Sometimes that inference is right. Environments matter. Soils constrain agriculture. Disease, rainfall, transport and crop regimes shape settlement. The mistake is treating archaeological detectability as if it were one more environmental variable rather than a filter between the past and us.

Tropical archaeology made this problem unusually severe. Early explorers and later scholars could document temples, inscriptions and large mounds, but the ordinary built environment was harder. A masonry pyramid survives as a mountain with corners. A house platform may survive as thirty centimeters of relief. One enters history; the other becomes topography.

The result was not a single doctrine of “empty wilderness.” Scholarship was always more complicated than the caricature. Indigenous histories, excavations, soil studies, pollen, charcoal, ceramics and earthworks had been undermining simple emptiness stories long before airborne LiDAR became fashionable. What LiDAR did was change the scale and speed of the correction.

That distinction matters because technological origin stories tend to erase the people who already knew. Many “discoveries” were known locally. Some features had been recorded from the ground. Some were visible in satellite imagery after deforestation. LiDAR did not conjure their existence; it altered the completeness and relational detail of the map.

This book will use “discovery” sparingly for that reason. The word often means one of four different things: previously unknown to anyone; unknown to academic archaeology; known as an isolated feature but newly recognized as part of a system; or already documented and now mapped with a different instrument. News stories flatten those categories because “laser reveals lost city” is cleaner than “new terrain model changes inferred settlement connectivity around known and partially known sites.”

The flattening creates a second problem. Once LiDAR archaeology became associated with lost cities, every scan inherited a mythology. The image itself—a green canopy digitally peeled away to expose rectilinear forms—looks like revelation. It invites before-and-after storytelling. Forest, then civilization. Ignorance, then sight.

But the more important before-and-after is methodological. Before broad-area survey, archaeologists may have had a sample concentrated around known monumental centers. After broad-area survey, they can measure distributions across upland, wetland, slope and interfluve. That changes denominators. It exposes how much of the “empty” space was never systematically inspected.

The 2026 southwestern Amazon work is almost a laboratory demonstration. Before the new airborne survey, satellite imagery and aerial photographs had produced a growing inventory of geometric earthworks, especially where modern clearing made them visible. The LiDAR lines crossed forest and open areas. In comparable deforested zones, the researchers reported finding roughly five times more earthworks with LiDAR than previous satellite inspection had identified. That is not merely a new-site count. It is a measured estimate of how incomplete one mode of seeing had been.

Then the authors did something more dangerous: they extrapolated. From sampled flight lines, known density zones, detection limitations and assumed cultural associations, they estimated tens of thousands of earthworks across a much larger area. The extrapolation may prove broadly right. It may be revised sharply. What matters epistemically is keeping observed features separate from modeled totals.

The map of absence is built from exactly those separations. What did the instrument measure? What did an analyst classify? What did excavation confirm? What did radiocarbon date? What did a demographic model infer? Which parts of the landscape were never sampled? Which were sampled but effectively opaque even to laser returns?

A good archaeological map should show uncertainty as aggressively as it shows roads.

The oldest seduction in exploration is to color the blank spaces and then forget that they were blank. LiDAR has begun coloring some of them in. Its deeper value may be teaching us to stop treating the remaining white as evidence.
