# Twenty-Four Thousand Earthworks

On July 29, 2026, Nature published one of those papers that arrives carrying a number large enough to outrun its own methods.

“Over 20,000 precolonial earthworks in the Southwest Amazonia” was the title. The team, led by Martti Pärssinen, had flown 4,430 kilometers of airborne laser survey lines across parts of Acre and Amazonas in Brazil. The new LiDAR documented 432 earthworks. Only 36 had been previously known.

Those are the clean numbers.

The larger numbers came from extrapolation. By combining the new transects with earlier satellite mapping, estimated high-density and low-density zones, cultural classification and assumptions about detection, the researchers arrived at approximately 24,000–30,000 earthworks across a proposed Aquiry cultural area of about 183,000 square kilometers. They then estimated that perhaps 1.25–3 million people lived in the region around AD 100–300, with alternative assumptions pushing possible totals higher.

It is hard not to feel the floor move.

It is equally important to label which floor is moving.

The paper did not scan 183,000 square kilometers continuously and count thirty thousand structures. It sampled narrow airborne swaths, tested detection against other imagery, defined density regions, and extrapolated. That does not make the estimate weak by definition. Sampling is how large-area science works. But it means the confidence structure belongs in the headline.

One result deserves special attention because it depends less on the big extrapolation. In deforested areas where previous satellite imagery had been searched, LiDAR reportedly revealed about five times as many earthworks along comparable lines. That is a direct measure of detection failure. Even without believing the upper population estimate, we learn that a supposedly favorable mode of remote inspection had missed most of the relevant features in a tested sample.

The survey also encountered absence. Some long flight sections yielded no earthworks. Dense canopy reduced ground penetration. In portions of the study area, large fractions of terrain lacked sufficient ground points for ordinary earthworks to be visualized. The authors discussed whether some empty transects represented true low-density buffer zones or detection limits.

This is exactly the argument the book needs because it prevents LiDAR from becoming magic. The laser can reveal that satellite imagery missed features and still miss features itself.

The earthworks are not all the same age or function. The study distinguishes geometric ditched enclosures associated with the earlier Aquiry phenomenon from later mound villages and rounder embanked forms. Radiocarbon modeling suggests centuries of construction and use. Turning every mapped enclosure into a simultaneous town would be nonsense.

The authors instead tried to estimate contemporaneity, workforce and population using several approaches. Those models can be criticized and improved. What cannot be restored easily is the older comfort that terra firme forest away from major rivers was necessarily lightly occupied.

The paper also makes an ecological claim with implications beyond archaeology. If large precolonial populations altered soils and plant distributions, then portions of what looks like natural forest may carry long-lived human signatures. Domesticated and semi-domesticated trees, anthropogenic soils and earthwork distributions can overlap. Human absence today does not imply human insignificance in the formation of the ecosystem.

This is where the language becomes politically dangerous. “Human-made Amazon” is too strong. “Untouched wilderness” is too weak. The archaeology points toward mosaics: heavily modified zones, lower-density buffers, riverine settlements, ceremonial centers, managed forests, and places with little evidence of permanent occupation.

Twenty-four thousand earthworks is therefore not a fact of the same kind as 432 detected earthworks. It is a model built on those facts.

If the model survives additional scanning, it will redraw the demographic history of a huge region. If it contracts, the 432 remain.

That asymmetry is what makes the paper strong.
