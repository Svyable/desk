# Twenty-Four Thousand Earthworks

On July 29, 2026, *Nature* published one of those papers that arrives carrying a number large enough to outrun its own methods.

The title was [“Over 20,000 precolonial earthworks in the Southwest Amazonia”](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10835-7). The number that matters first, however, is not twenty thousand.

It is 432.

In 2024, Martti Pärssinen and colleagues flew ten airborne-laser-scanning lines across Acre and Amazonas. The full campaign covered 4,497 square kilometers. In the new LiDAR data they documented 432 earthworks. Only 36 had been known before.

That is an observation. The laser passed over terrain, enough returns reached the ground to build digital terrain models, and human-made geometry appeared where the existing archaeological inventory had mostly contained blank space.

The second number is 347.

For the part of the study designed to estimate regional density, the team concentrated on six long, roughly parallel survey lines. Each was about a kilometer wide, the lines were spaced about ten kilometers apart, and together they systematically sampled roughly 2,380 square kilometers. Those lines contained 347 earthworks of several kinds. The distribution was violently uneven: 320 of the 347 were concentrated within the first 210 kilometers of the Rio Branco side of the survey, while only 27 appeared in the final 235 kilometers toward Lábrea.

That matters because a regional extrapolation built from a uniform density would have been nonsense. The survey itself found a core and a sparse edge.

The paper's largest claim begins after that.

By combining the LiDAR transects with previous satellite mapping, field archaeology, radiocarbon dates, high- and low-density zones, cultural classification, and estimates of how many structures remain invisible even to LiDAR, the authors expand the proposed Aquiry cultural area from about 60,000 square kilometers to roughly 183,000. Across that area, they estimate approximately 24,000–30,000 earthworks, around two-thirds belonging to the Aquiry period.

The paper did not continuously scan 183,000 square kilometers and count thirty thousand structures. It measured a sample, divided the landscape into different archaeological-density regimes, and propagated assumptions outward.

That is not a criticism. It is the experiment.

The useful question is how much of the big number can be pulled apart and tested.

One piece is unusually clean. In a deforested part of the high-density block, the researchers compared LiDAR with the satellite imagery that had already been used to hunt for earthworks. Along the same lines, earlier visual inspection of Google Earth, Bing, and ESRI imagery had found 30 structures. LiDAR found 156.

Five times as many.

That comparison is more important than the headline because it measures a failure mode directly. Even where the forest had been cleared enough that ordinary overhead imagery ought to have been favorable, the previous method had missed four structures for every one it found.

So the problem is not only that rainforest hides archaeology. Archaeological visibility has layers. A feature can be invisible beneath canopy, weak in a satellite image after canopy removal, obvious in a digital terrain model, and still archaeologically ambiguous until somebody walks to it, excavates it, dates it, and works out whether the geometry is cultural, when it was used, and what it was for.

The LiDAR itself has a denominator problem too.

The aircraft flew about 1,125 meters above ground at roughly 130 knots. The laser operated at 1.6 million pulses per second. Average point density was about 31 returns per square meter, with the resulting terrain models interpolated to 50-centimeter resolution. Those are serious measurements, not a magical forest-removal filter.

Dense canopy still wins sometimes.

The paper's sensitivity analysis estimates that in the high-density block, roughly 11.5–41.9 percent of earthworks could remain undetected because too few laser returns reached the ground, with a mean estimate of 26.7 percent. In the low-density block, the corresponding range was 17.8–54.5 percent, with a mean of 36.2 percent.

This creates a pleasingly awkward fact: the technology that proves previous remote sensing missed structures also has to model what it missed.

There is another awkwardness. The 347 detected earthworks are not one thing.

Of 334 structures used in the main extrapolation, the authors classified 120 as ditched geoglyphs and 115 as embanked quadrilateral or multilateral enclosures associated with the Aquiry tradition. The remainder included later forms, particularly mound villages and rounder embanked settlements that appear after about AD 1000–1200.

A map can flatten time more efficiently than a forest can hide it.

Put twenty centuries of construction into one beautiful hillshade image and the landscape begins to look like a single city. It was not. The structures accumulated, changed function, were abandoned, reused, built near older ceremonial centers, and belonged to different social arrangements.

The population estimate has to solve that temporal problem.

The authors identify AD 100–300 as the period of densest chronological overlap. Their kernel-density treatment suggests about 25 percent of the Aquiry earthworks were constructed during that interval, while a separate phase model suggests roughly 60 percent of dated Aquiry earthworks could have been in simultaneous use around the peak.

That 25-to-60-percent range becomes one of the main levers in the demographic calculation.

The other lever is people per place.

The study estimates roughly 16,660 Aquiry-period earthworks before some visibility corrections. If approximately 25–60 percent were active around AD 100–300, that implies about 4,165–10,000 active centers. Assign roughly 300 people to an average center and the arithmetic produces 1.25–3 million people.

Three hundred is not a census count recovered from an ancient ditch.

The authors justify it using several lines of reasoning, including the labor implied by the earthworks and comparison with later mound villages in the same broad cultural landscape. Those later villages average about 1.28 hectares and around 11.75 elliptical mounds; treating the larger mounds as multi-family longhouses yields a community size on the order of 300. The authors also run a much lower alternative in which those houses represent single families. That drops the implied population to roughly 250,000–600,000 before the LiDAR-visibility correction. They regard that scenario as unlikely, but it is there.

This is the part that should be printed in large type beside every headline about millions of people.

The population is not one inference. It is a stack:

how many earthworks exist;

which belong to the relevant cultural phase;

how many were active at once;

how many people an average active center represented;

how much archaeological visibility the survey still lacked.

Change one term and the answer moves. Change several and it can move a lot.

But there is an important asymmetry in this uncertainty.

If the three-million estimate eventually contracts, 432 newly mapped earthworks do not disappear. The 156-versus-30 LiDAR comparison does not disappear. The high-density core and low-density edge do not disappear. The newly mapped extent of monumental landscape engineering does not disappear. Neither does the evidence that earlier ways of seeing the forest systematically undercounted what people had built there.

This is why the paper is stronger when its hierarchy is kept intact.

There is also a political reason to keep it intact.

The old error was not merely a bad population number. It was a habit of converting present-day forest cover into a story about past human absence. New work across Amazonia has been dismantling that habit for years. The 2026 survey pushes hard in the same direction: geometric centers, roads, managed soils, settlement forms, cultivation, forest stewardship, and long-lived cultural landscapes can survive the societies that made them and then be misread as evidence that nobody significant was there.

The correction can be overcorrected.

“Untouched wilderness” is a poor description for many Amazonian landscapes.

“The Amazon was one giant city” would be poor science for the opposite reason.

The survey itself contains dense zones and sparse zones. It contains monumental earthworks and later villages. It contains places where the laser sees well and places where the canopy still blocks it. Contemporary Indigenous territories below portions of the flight lines were not simply converted into archaeological database entries, which is another reminder that a blank cell in a research table can have legal, ethical, or methodological causes besides ancient emptiness.

What the paper gives us is not a replacement myth. It gives us a better instrument panel.

There are direct detections. There are classifications. There are dates. There are visibility corrections. There are density models. There are contemporaneity models. There are population assumptions.

They should not be collapsed into one number because they can now be attacked separately.

Fly denser transects through the predicted high-density blocks. Scan the proposed low-density buffers. Ground-truth a blinded sample of LiDAR candidates. Date more centers. Test whether the southeastern boundary really sits where the model places it. Compare the fraction missed by LiDAR in different forest structures. Replace the 300-person community assumption wherever excavation gives better local denominators.

The largest claim in the paper is therefore not that there were exactly twenty-four thousand earthworks.

It is that the number has become vulnerable to measurement.

Before LiDAR, a large part of this landscape was easy to describe as empty because the evidence was difficult to see. Now the argument has moved into a much less comfortable place.

We have to decide what the evidence means after we can finally see some of what was there.
