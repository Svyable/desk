# The Mountain Capital

Mahendraparvata was hiding in a place where history already said it ought to be.

Inscriptions and scattered archaeological evidence had long connected Phnom Kulen, northeast of Angkor, with an early Khmer capital associated with Jayavarman II. The problem was not that nobody suspected a city. The problem was seeing its form.

Airborne laser scanning changed that. Research published in Antiquity described a centrally organized urban landscape on the plateau, roughly forty to fifty square kilometers, with major axes, smaller parcels, shrines, mounds, ponds, dams and an unfinished reservoir. The pattern was consistent with an early Angkor-period capital.

This is a useful antidote to the discovery myth. Sometimes remote sensing confirms a historical hypothesis instead of generating one.

The plateau matters because it reveals planning that would be difficult to reconstruct from monumental points alone. A temple is a location. A grid is a decision about the space between locations.

Road axes subdivided the central zone. Smaller-scale boundaries subdivided blocks. Water-management works interacted with terrain. The arrangement of palace, state temple and other features resembled patterns seen in later Khmer capitals while remaining particular to the mountain setting.

If archaeology had consisted only of finding monuments, the result would have been a list. LiDAR made morphology available at city scale.

The unfinished reservoir is especially attractive because unfinished infrastructure preserves intention without success. A completed canal tells us what builders achieved. An incomplete one can expose what they attempted and where the environment or politics interrupted them.

There is a temptation to overread centralized planning whenever geometry appears. Straight lines feel governmental. But coordinated form can emerge at different scales of authority, and grids do not automatically imply a bureaucratic state directing every household. The right question is how much standardization, labor and sequencing the mapped pattern requires, then what excavated evidence says about political organization.

Mahendraparvata also demonstrates how a city can be archaeologically real without becoming a dense permanent metropolis in the modern sense. Settlement on the plateau may have been uneven. Some mapped features were ceremonial, some residential, some agricultural. Urban identity can depend on political and ritual centrality as much as continuous building density.

This makes the site a bridge between the book’s cases. The Maya Lowlands, Amazonian earthworks and Khmer landscapes do not represent one form of low-density urbanism. They show different solutions to climate, food, water, transport and authority. LiDAR makes them visually comparable, which is useful and dangerous. Similar-looking grids can have different histories.

The instrument standardizes representation before archaeology standardizes meaning.

That is why the best LiDAR scholarship spends so much time leaving the screen. A road has to be walked. A mound has to be sampled. A reservoir has to be dated. The city model becomes stronger when some features fail field inspection, because the error rate becomes knowable.

Mahendraparvata was not a lost city conjured from a scan. It was a historical problem whose shape suddenly became visible.

That is a quieter form of discovery and a more scientific one.
