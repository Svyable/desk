# The Forest Was Never Empty

There is a particular kind of archaeological embarrassment that arrives from above.

For years a team can walk a forest, record the mounds it can reach, cut trails, mark temples, argue over settlement density, and become properly expert in a landscape. Then an aircraft flies over with a laser. The computer subtracts the leaves. Roads appear. So do house platforms, terraces, reservoirs, ditches, causeways and walls. Some of them are under trails the archaeologists themselves used.

This book is about that embarrassment, because it is more important than the gadget.

LiDAR is usually sold as an archaeological X-ray. It is not. It measures surfaces by timing laser returns, and under favorable conditions enough pulses reach the ground through vegetation to make a digital terrain model. The archaeology still has to be recognized, dated, excavated, interpreted, and argued over. A rectangle in a hillshade is not a dynasty. A ridge is not a road because software colored it attractively. Population estimates remain models. Forest does not vanish merely because a point cloud allows us to suppress it on a screen.

Yet the cumulative effect is difficult to dismiss. In the Maya Lowlands, Cambodia, the Upper Amazon, southwestern Amazonia, the Bolivian lowlands and Central Asian highlands, airborne and drone LiDAR have repeatedly made old categories of settlement look too small. The revelation is not that archaeologists were fools. It is that visibility had been mistaken, sometimes quietly, for absence.

The argument here is narrower than the headline version and more disruptive. LiDAR is exposing a preservation bias in our picture of civilization. Stone towers on open ground are easy to inherit intellectually. Earthen platforms beneath tropical forest are not. Dense cities survive in textbooks; distributed settlement systems are harder to name. A landscape altered by roads, drainage, reservoirs, orchards and small residential platforms can look like wilderness once the people are gone and the plants return.

That does not mean every forest was a metropolis. It does not mean the Amazon was one continuous city. It certainly does not vindicate Atlantis, global lost-civilization stories, or any other narrative waiting to annex new archaeological work. The interesting discovery is almost the opposite. Real archaeology is stranger than those stories because it shows many different ways to become complex without converging on the same monumental form.

I use four evidence classes throughout the research trail. Class 1 is directly documented: measured terrain, excavated features, radiocarbon dates, published survey results. Class 2 is credible but incomplete interpretation: proposed functions, settlement relationships, demographic reconstructions with explicit assumptions. Class 3 is disputed or weakly confirmed: dramatic population claims resting on uncertain multipliers, media versions of “lost cities,” or cultural conclusions outrunning excavation. Class 4 is synthesis: what the accumulating discoveries may imply about preservation bias, urban definitions, ecological history and the amount of archaeology still effectively invisible.

The newest evidence makes the question urgent. In July 2026, a Nature paper reported 432 earthworks in newly flown southwestern Amazonian LiDAR lines, only 36 of them previously known, then extrapolated roughly 24,000–30,000 earthworks across the proposed cultural area. That extrapolation is not the same thing as detecting thirty thousand sites. The distinction matters. So does the underlying fact that the new scans found five times as many earthworks as satellite imagery along comparable lines in some tested areas.

The forest did not conceal one lost civilization. It concealed our sampling error.
