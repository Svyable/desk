# The Laser Through Leaves

The trick is not that the laser sees through a leaf. It usually does not. The trick is that a forest is not a roof.

A LiDAR instrument sends short pulses of light toward the ground and measures the time required for reflected energy to return. One pulse may strike the top of a tree. Another clips a branch. A few pass through gaps and reach soil, stone, ditch or mound. From millions or billions of returns, software estimates where the ground probably is. Archaeological LiDAR begins there, after most of what made the landscape look like a forest has been classified away.

That phrase—classified away—is worth holding onto. The famous bare-earth images are manufactured views. They are measurements transformed by algorithms that decide which points belong to vegetation, which belong to terrain, and how to interpolate between places where no clean ground return was obtained. A person looking at one of these images is not seeing the past exposed. He is seeing a model of the present surface with selected things removed.

And yet the effect can be astonishing because human earthworks are geometric in ways erosion does not always erase. A broad road cut into a slope may remain a shallow linear depression for a thousand years. A reservoir embankment can survive as a change in grade too gentle to notice while walking. House platforms can become low rises under roots. Agricultural terraces can turn an entire hillside into steps that vegetation makes visually continuous from ground level.

The technology altered archaeology most dramatically where traditional reconnaissance is slow, expensive and biased toward what a person can physically reach. Tropical forests are the obvious case. The canopy limits aerial photography. Ground crews may move only kilometers a day. Every survey strategy therefore becomes a sampling strategy, whether the team calls it one or not. Trails, rivers, known sites, modern clearings and local reports shape where archaeologists look.

LiDAR changes the geometry of looking. An aircraft can record wide areas without asking permission from every vine. It does not eliminate fieldwork; it reallocates it. Instead of cutting transects in hopes of finding something, archaeologists can walk toward a particular terrace, enclosure or causeway identified in the model. When Thomas Garrison described finding a Maya road after seeing it in LiDAR, the humiliating detail was that he could stand on the thing and still understand why it had been missed. The jungle had not made the road disappear. It had made the road look like jungle.

This is why the important unit is not the individual “discovery.” The individual discovery makes good copy: a pyramid, a fortress, an avenue. The larger change is that settlement can be mapped as a system. Roads connect things. Reservoirs sit at particular elevations. House platforms cluster around civic compounds. Defensive walls wrap ridges. Fields and drainage channels occupy the spaces that older maps left blank.

Once the blank areas begin filling in, old population estimates become unstable. So do old definitions. Is a city only the dense monumental core, or can it be a dispersed network of neighborhoods, gardens, causeways and ceremonial nodes spread over dozens of square kilometers? Does intensive landscape engineering count as urban infrastructure if buildings are earthen and low? How many people can live in a forested region before the word wilderness stops describing its history accurately?

LiDAR does not answer those questions. It makes avoiding them harder.

It also introduces new ways to fool ourselves. Hillshade direction can make one feature leap out and another vanish. Point density matters. Dense canopy can starve the terrain model of ground returns. Modern logging tracks and drainage works can masquerade as ancient features. Analysts primed to find rectangles find rectangles. Machine-learning systems trained on known mounds inherit the known sample’s biases.

The instrument is therefore most powerful when paired with its own humiliation. It makes archaeologists admit what they could not see, and then it requires them to admit what the laser still cannot know. It cannot date a road. It cannot tell whether two adjacent settlements were occupied simultaneously. It cannot infer kingship from a rectangle. It cannot turn an extrapolation into an observed site.

What it can do is reveal that the map on which those arguments were being made was missing a remarkable amount of ground.
