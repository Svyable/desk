# Cities Without Stone

Stone has bullied the archaeological imagination for a long time.

It survives. It photographs well. It can carry inscriptions. It makes hierarchy vertical. When an ancient society builds in stone, later societies inherit an argument about complexity almost automatically.

Earth is less cooperative. Earthen platforms slump. Ditches fill. Timber rots. Roads soften. Forest arrives and turns deliberate geometry into topography.

The lowlands of Bolivia, the Upper Amazon and southwestern Amazonia force a simple question: how much of our global map of ancient urbanism is really a map of durable building material?

LiDAR work in the Llanos de Mojos brought this issue into focus before some of the newer Amazon headlines. Survey of Casarabe-culture landscapes revealed monumental civic-ceremonial architecture, terraces, causeways, canals and reservoirs arranged within networks of settlements. The built environment did not need cut-stone towers to operate at regional scale.

The word city can become a trap here because arguments about vocabulary replace arguments about evidence. One researcher says low-density urbanism. Another says settlement hierarchy. A third says garden city. The disagreement matters when it concerns actual social organization, but sometimes the participants are measuring the same roads and mounds while defending different definitions.

I am less interested in winning that word than in noticing what the landscapes required.

A causeway requires a destination and labor. A reservoir requires hydrological knowledge and maintenance. A raised field system requires repeated investment. Monumental platforms require moving soil in volume. Networks of such features imply coordination that cannot be reduced to a few ceremonial monuments surrounded by empty forest.

This is why LiDAR’s most radical archaeological effect is quantitative rather than visual. It turns “there seem to be many mounds” into mapped density, orientation, distance and connectivity. Those measurements can be compared with ethnography, excavation and environmental data.

They can also be abused. A digital terrain model encourages counting because counting is easy. Every platform can become a house. Every house can acquire five inhabitants. Every mapped road can become simultaneous with every mound it approaches. Soon a population estimate has three significant figures and no honest way to deserve them.

The better approach is layered. The terrain model establishes morphology. Excavation samples feature types. Dating estimates overlap. Soil chemistry and botanical remains reconstruct subsistence. Population models report ranges and sensitivity to household assumptions. Indigenous knowledge enters not as decorative confirmation but as evidence about land use, plant management and social organization where appropriate.

Cities without stone are not invisible once we decide to look for their specific residues. Dark earth can preserve household activity. Phytoliths preserve crops. Charcoal preserves burning histories. Ditches preserve geometry. Roads preserve movement.

What LiDAR contributes is the skeleton that allows those organs to be located in relation to each other.

There is no need to make these landscapes secretly modern. They were not suburbs. They did not have municipal departments because they had drains. Their density, politics and seasonality varied. Some ceremonial enclosures may have served dispersed populations who gathered periodically rather than lived densely around them.

That possibility itself widens urban history. A place can be socially central without being continuously crowded.

The old archaeological hierarchy placed temples at the top and countryside around them. The new maps keep showing something messier: settlement, cultivation, ritual and infrastructure woven together.

The city may have been the weave.
