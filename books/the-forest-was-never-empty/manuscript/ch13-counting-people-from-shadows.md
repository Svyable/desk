# Counting People from Shadows

Population estimates are where LiDAR archaeology becomes most seductive because the arithmetic looks cleaner than the assumptions.

Count structures. Decide how many are houses. Estimate simultaneous occupation. Multiply by people per household. Correct for unmapped terrain. Produce civilization.

Every step can be defensible. Together they can generate a number with more confidence than its weakest assumption deserves.

The 2025 Maya population work extended LiDAR-informed modeling across tens of thousands of square kilometers and suggested a Late Classic population perhaps in the range of roughly ten to sixteen million across the Central Maya Lowlands, depending on model choices. The 2026 southwestern Amazon paper proposed 1.25–3 million in its study region at peak, again from explicit assumptions about sites, contemporaneity and people associated with each earthwork.

These are not fraudulent numbers. They are conditional statements that headlines strip of conditions.

The first uncertainty is classification. A mapped platform may be residential, ceremonial, agricultural or something else. Ground sampling estimates proportions, but rare errors multiplied across enormous counts matter.

The second is chronology. LiDAR collapses centuries into one terrain surface. If half the houses visible on the map were abandoned before the other half were built, raw structure density doubles the living city.

The third is household size. Ethnographic analogy helps, but households vary by time, status and architecture.

The fourth is detection. Dense vegetation can hide features even from airborne laser scanning. Erosion destroys small platforms preferentially. Survey parameters change the minimum detectable relief.

The fifth is extrapolation. Most regional studies do not scan every square kilometer. They sample and model density across unsurveyed zones.

The honest population estimate is therefore a distribution, not a monument.

This is one reason the new LiDAR papers are scientifically useful even when their largest numbers change. They expose assumptions in a form that can be tested by new scanning and excavation. If another 5,000 square kilometers are flown, the density model improves. If more residential platforms are excavated, classification priors improve. If radiocarbon sequences sharpen, simultaneity improves.

Older estimates were not assumption-free. They simply had fewer observed structures constraining them.

There is also a political sensitivity around population reconstruction in the Americas. Low estimates have historically been entangled with narratives that minimize the demographic catastrophe following European invasion and disease. High estimates can be recruited into romantic counter-myths. Archaeology should not choose a number because of which moral story it serves.

The record deserves to be large when it is large and uncertain when it is uncertain.

LiDAR’s contribution is not that it finally tells us how many people lived in a forest. It makes certain low estimates harder to maintain because the built landscape required more labor, habitation and coordination than the old map contained.

The rest is demographic modeling.

A shadow on a hillshade can become a person only after a chain of inference. The chain is legitimate. It should remain visible.
