# The City That Was Denser Than Tikal

Tikal has the unfair advantage of being famous enough to function as a unit of Maya-ness.

So when a 2025 LiDAR study of Dzibanché/Kaanu’l in Mexico compared the two, the comparison did more than supply numbers. It destabilized a mental map. The 103-square-kilometer Dzibanché survey recorded 13,928 structures, an average of 136 per square kilometer. In a roughly equivalent comparison area around Tikal, the authors counted 9,347, about 91 per square kilometer. On upland terrain suitable for settlement, the contrast was larger. Using standardized population multipliers, they estimated roughly 36,000 to 55,000 people for Dzibanché in the relevant period.

Those numbers are Class 2 evidence in this book, not Class 1. The structures are mapped. The population is modeled.

That separation lets us enjoy the stranger part of the finding. Dzibanché was not merely a dense pile of buildings. The survey revealed an urban and peri-urban system organized by causeways, monumental complexes, subsidiary civic-ceremonial nodes, fields and wetlands. The spatial arrangement suggested planning concerned with movement across the city and its surrounding settlement zone.

The political context matters. Dzibanché is associated with the Kaanu’l, or Snake, dynasty, whose reach across the Maya world is known from inscriptions at multiple sites. A dense, highly connected capital is consistent with that political importance. Consistent is the right word. LiDAR did not detect hegemony. It detected built landscape whose scale fits a history reconstructed from other evidence.

This is the productive way to use remote sensing: not as a replacement for history but as a second instrument reading the same event.

It also shows why “city” becomes slippery in tropical archaeology. A modern eye expects urban density to have an edge. Buildings stop, countryside begins. Maya settlement often grades outward through lower-density habitation, fields, small plazas and causeways. If the threshold defining “urban” is changed, the measured city changes. If wetlands are excluded, structure density changes. If all mapped platforms are treated as houses, population changes. If only simultaneously occupied structures count, chronology becomes decisive.

The authors themselves noted that wider coverage was needed to assess the full extent. That sentence should travel with every headline. LiDAR can make a city larger and simultaneously make its boundary less certain.

There is another reason Dzibanché belongs in this book. It reminds us that the laser does not only find things nobody knew existed. Archaeologists already knew the site and its political importance. The contribution was relational resolution. The settlement around the monumental core became measurable enough to compare with another city under similar methods.

That kind of result rarely gets described as revolutionary because it lacks the romance of a lost city. But archaeology advances when denominators become comparable. How many structures per square kilometer? On what terrain? Around what kinds of centers? With what road connectivity? How does density change with distance? Which fields and water-control features accompany it?

This is how a point cloud becomes social history, slowly and with arguments in between.

The Dzibanché comparison also warns against a common public misunderstanding: that earlier archaeologists somehow failed to notice a giant city sitting in plain sight. They noticed what their methods allowed them to map. The new survey altered scale. It made the peri-urban fabric legible.

Tikal did not shrink. Dzibanché acquired context.
