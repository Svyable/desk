# Contradictions and Incentives

## Central contradictions

LiDAR is marketed as canopy penetrating, yet dense canopy can still leave large areas with inadequate ground returns. The technology reduces one visibility bias without eliminating it.

The same dataset can support both a strong empirical claim and a weaker spectacular claim. Example: 432 newly documented earthworks is an observed survey result; 24,000–30,000 is an extrapolation across a much larger region.

“Lost city” reporting often describes sites already known locally or archaeologically. The real novelty may be network extent, urban morphology or regional context.

A terrain model is spatially continuous but temporally collapsed. Features that appear together may be separated by centuries.

## Incentives

Researchers benefit from significance and novelty, journals from broad relevance, universities from press attention, and news organizations from visually dramatic “lost city” framing. None of these incentives invalidates the work; together they create pressure to promote the most expansive interpretation.

Heritage agencies may benefit from stronger evidence of cultural significance but may also face security risks when precise site locations become public.

Local and Indigenous communities can gain evidentiary support for deep occupation and cultural landscapes, while simultaneously facing extractive research or tourism narratives that erase prior knowledge.

Technology vendors and remote-sensing programs benefit when LiDAR is described as revolutionary. This can obscure continuing dependence on excavation, chronology and local expertise.
