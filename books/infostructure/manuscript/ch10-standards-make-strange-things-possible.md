# Standards Make Strange Things Possible

The glamorous history of technology is built on boring agreements.

Thread pitches. Voltage ranges. Container dimensions. Character encodings. Time standards. Measurement units. Network protocols. File formats. The list is an anti-biography: nobody becomes a household name because two components fit.

Yet compatibility is one of the great multipliers of cultural information. A standard lets an invention inherit an ecosystem it did not create.

Without shared interfaces, every new component must solve neighboring problems. With them, a designer can treat large bodies of prior work as stable assumptions. This reduces the search space and increases recombination. Standards turn distant strangers into collaborators who never meet.

They also freeze choices. A bad standard can impose decades of cost. Incumbent interfaces can block technically superior alternatives. Compatibility is valuable precisely because deviation becomes expensive.

This tension reveals something important about infostructure. Progress is not maximized by novelty everywhere. It often requires extreme conservatism at one layer so experimentation can explode at another. The standardized shipping container constrains geometry and liberates logistics. Internet protocols constrain communication rules and permit astonishing diversity above them.

Metrology goes deeper. Before researchers can combine results, they need shared quantities, calibration chains and error conventions. A measurement without provenance may be data but is weak cultural inheritance. Standards make observations portable.

The same principle applies outside engineering. Legal forms, accounting conventions, citation systems and scientific nomenclature create interfaces among institutions. Their friction is easy to mock until one tries to coordinate without them.

Infostructure is therefore partly constitutional. It specifies what can connect without negotiation. A society with good interfaces can absorb novelty at the edges without renegotiating the center every time.

The strange thing about standards is that their success makes them disappear. When the plug fits, nobody writes a history of the socket.
