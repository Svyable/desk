# Science Is an Infostructure

Science is usually described as a method. In practice it is also a civilization-scale apparatus for making claims portable.

Journals, laboratories, peer review, conferences, repositories, metrology institutes, grant systems, professional societies, citation networks and replication norms do different jobs. Together they attempt something difficult: preserve observations while keeping them revisable.

The strength of science is not that scientists are unusually immune to error. It is that the infostructure can, under good conditions, allow errors to be found by people who were not present when they were made.

This depends on provenance. A number detached from apparatus, calibration, protocol and analysis choices loses much of its value. Reproducibility is partly an information-architecture problem.

Publication bias illustrates the inverse. If successful results are much more likely to enter the durable record than failures, the cultural archive becomes systematically distorted. Later researchers inherit not merely incomplete information but a biased map of possibility.

Specialization creates another problem. Science produces knowledge faster by dividing labor, then struggles to recombine what the division produced. Review articles, conferences and interdisciplinary institutes are retrieval devices. So are people whose careers cross fields.

The scientific literature is now too large for any person to search exhaustively. This is not a temporary inconvenience. It changes the epistemic environment. Discovery increasingly includes discovering that the discovery already exists under another vocabulary.

Machine assistance may help, but retrieval quality is not truth. A system that finds a paper still leaves the human problems of evidence, replication, causal interpretation and relevance.

Science works when it keeps these layers distinct: claim, evidence, provenance, criticism, reuse. Collapse them and citation becomes authority. Preserve them and old work can become new again for legitimate reasons.

The laboratory of the future may spend as much effort interrogating the inherited record as extending it.
