# Population Is Not Enough

There is a seductive demographic account of cumulative culture: more people mean more inventors, more models to copy, and less chance that rare skills disappear. Sometimes that is exactly right. Archaeological and theoretical work has explored how population size and connectivity can affect cultural complexity.

But headcount is a crude proxy for what matters.

Ten million people separated into impermeable communities may offer fewer useful recombinations than one million connected through trade, apprenticeship and shared standards. Conversely, perfect connectivity can homogenize a population so quickly that unusual local experiments disappear before proving useful. Infostructure has topology.

The relevant quantity is closer to effective cultural population size: how many distinct, competent sources can actually contribute to a problem, and how reliably can their contributions persist? Work on cultural evolution has used related concepts to explain why the distribution of knowledge matters, not only the number of individuals.

This reframes old arguments about isolated populations. Loss of technology need not imply declining intelligence. A small or disrupted network can fail to maintain skills whose reproduction requires enough specialists, enough learners, or enough complementary practices. The cultural phenotype can shrink while individual cognitive capacity remains unchanged.

The same mechanism operates inside modern organizations. A company with twenty thousand employees may have an effective problem-solving population of six if expertise is trapped behind reporting lines. Another firm may borrow competence from customers, open-source communities, universities and suppliers. Its legal headcount understates its infostructure.

Networks also have failure modes. Central hubs accelerate diffusion but create points of fragility. Dense local ties preserve trust but can exclude distant novelty. Weak ties introduce difference but may not carry the tacit detail required for difficult techniques. There is no universally optimal network because exploration, verification and transmission demand different structures.

The practical question is not whether a society is connected. It is connected *for what*? A rumor and a manufacturing process have different bandwidth requirements. A theorem can cross an ocean in a page; surgery cannot.

Population matters. But the number that fuels progress is not the census. It is the number of minds, practices and artifacts that can become relevant to one another before their useful differences are erased or forgotten.
