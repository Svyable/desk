# Firms, Supply Chains, and Buried Capability

A modern product is an argument no single employee can fully explain.

Consider an aircraft, semiconductor tool, pharmaceutical plant or power turbine. Capability is partitioned across firms, specifications, process recipes, software, specialized materials, inspection routines and regulatory knowledge. The final object is evidence that the network knows how to make it, even if nobody can write down the network's total knowledge.

This is distributed cognition with invoices.

Supply chains are usually discussed as logistics. They are also epistemic structures. Suppliers accumulate narrow expertise because repeated demand keeps a problem alive long enough for learning to compound. Customers externalize that expertise and become dependent on its survival.

The dependence is easy to miss while the chain works. A component may represent decades of unremarkable process refinement. Replace it after a disruption and the specification proves incomplete. The missing variables emerge only through failure.

This explains why industrial capability can be difficult to recreate from blueprints. The blueprint describes the product more readily than the production ecology.

Globalization expanded the cultural search space by connecting specialized capabilities across borders. It also concentrated some capabilities into fragile nodes. Resilience policy therefore faces a real tradeoff. Redundancy protects continuity but can be expensive; concentration accelerates learning but increases systemic dependence.

Infostructure makes the tradeoff visible. The question is not merely where a part is made. It is where the learning loop lives. If a critical process disappears, can another node reconstruct it from the surviving record, or must the culture relearn the process experimentally?

Inventory counts objects. Capability counts recoverable relationships.
