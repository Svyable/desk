# Culture Has a Dark Matter Problem

We measure culture by what leaves records. That is already a distortion.

The most consequential knowledge in a factory may never enter a manual. The most important negative result in a laboratory may never become a paper. A family can transmit a technique through demonstration without producing a sentence that describes it. An institution may know whom to call, which warning to distrust, or which nominally equivalent component will fail in winter. None of this is invisible to participants. Much of it is invisible to historians, economists and databases.

I do not mean dark matter literally. The analogy is useful only to mark an inference problem: observed outputs require more structure than the visible record contains.

Cultural evolution has often been modeled through transmitted traits, but the trait itself can be difficult to delimit. Is a boat-building tradition a set of dimensions, a sequence of gestures, a vocabulary, a social hierarchy that determines who may correct whom, or a coastal ecology that keeps the relevant materials familiar? Pull one item from the bundle and it may cease to reproduce.

This is why counting patents, papers or books can mislead. These are emissions from an infostructure, not the structure itself. Two places can possess the same documents and radically different abilities to turn them into action.

Tacit knowledge is one part of the missing mass. Michael Polanyi's famous formulation—that we can know more than we can tell—has become almost too comfortable. The stronger implication is institutional. If knowing can exceed telling, then copying the explicit record cannot copy the capability. Technology transfer is partly the transfer of people, routines, tolerances, judgment and error correction.

The dark matter also includes discarded paths. Cultures forget for reasons that have nothing to do with truth: fashion, language change, institutional death, destroyed archives, vanished markets, professional stigma. Some forgotten ideas deserved to die. Others merely lost their habitat.

A theory of progress that counts only successful, explicit transmission will therefore overestimate invention and underestimate preservation. It will attribute to genius what sometimes belongs to retrieval, and to ignorance what sometimes belongs to broken access.

The latent cultural inventory is not a warehouse of secret answers. Most dormant material is probably useless. The point is statistical: a large, heterogeneous culture contains more potential combinations than its current institutions can inspect. Progress depends on how effectively it samples that combinatorial space without drowning in nonsense.
