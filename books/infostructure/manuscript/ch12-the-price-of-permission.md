# The Price of Permission

Information can be available, comprehensible and still unusable.

A researcher may know an experiment worth trying but lack permission, equipment time, funding or career tolerance. An engineer may see a better design but face certification costs that make deviation irrational. An employee may possess a useful observation that cannot cross hierarchy without becoming a political act.

Infostructure includes these gates because access without agency is incomplete.

This is where simple celebrations of open information fail. Publishing every paper does not create a laboratory. Releasing data does not create time to inspect it. Teaching a method does not create legal authority to deploy it. Progress depends on the conversion of knowledge into trials, and trials consume scarce institutional permission.

The problem is not that gatekeeping is bad. Aviation should not let every clever idea become a passenger experiment. Medicine requires evidence. Nuclear engineering should be conservative. The question is whether the cost of trying is proportional to the downside and whether low-risk exploratory spaces exist.

Cultures differ in this margin. Some institutions punish failed experiments more severely than missed opportunities because failure is visible and omission is counterfactual. The resulting system can appear competent while quietly selecting against exploration.

Venture capital, skunkworks, research grants, prizes, sandboxes and discretionary budgets are all attempts to purchase permission for uncertain work. Their effectiveness varies, and each creates its own fashions and distortions.

The latent cultural inventory becomes economically meaningful only when somebody can place a bet on a retrieved combination. A culture can therefore suffer from a peculiar congestion: too many plausible ideas waiting at too few experimental gates.

Progress is not just a search problem. It is a queue.
