# The Knowledge That Cannot Be Written Down

If every useful thing could be written, the problem of progress would be largely clerical.

It cannot.

Polanyi's tacit knowledge is often invoked here, but the category deserves precision. Some knowledge is merely undocumented and could be written with effort. Some is difficult to verbalize but can be demonstrated. Some depends on perceptual discrimination acquired through repeated exposure. Some is distributed across a team so that no individual possesses the whole procedure. Some is embodied in equipment whose design silently constrains correct behavior.

These differences matter because they require different preservation systems.

A recipe is not a kitchen. A score is not an orchestra. Source code is not the operational history that tells maintainers which apparently ugly line exists because of a failure three years ago. The explicit artifact can be necessary and radically insufficient.

This is why apprenticeship persists in technologically advanced societies. It is not a primitive substitute for documentation. In many domains it is a high-bandwidth transmission channel for attention and judgment. The learner sees the expert react to variation, including cases no manual writer thought to specify.

Tacit transmission, however, is fragile. It is slow, local and vulnerable to demographic breaks. When a plant closes or a research group dissolves, the loss may not appear in any database because the missing information was never represented there.

The obvious response is to document more. We should. Video, process telemetry, annotated failures and richer provenance can convert some tacit material into durable traces. But documentation itself needs interpretation. A future reader must know what to look for.

The deeper response is to treat communities of practice as storage media. Preserving a capability may require preserving enough live practice for correction to continue. Museums can conserve machines; they rarely conserve the industrial ecology that made the machines ordinary.

Infostructure therefore has a living layer. A culture does not merely own information. It rehearses some of it often enough that the information remains executable.
