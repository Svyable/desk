# Infostructure

*The Latent Cultural Information Dynamics That Fuel Progress*

Sven Hardy Benson

A civilization knows more than any of its citizens. That sounds obvious until you ask where the excess knowledge is.

It is not all in books. It is not all in brains. It is not even all in institutions. Some of it exists only as a relationship between things: a machinist who can hold a tolerance nobody bothered to document, a supplier whose alloy is boring until another industry needs exactly its failure mode, a dead paper that becomes useful after a new instrument arrives, a standard that lets strangers build compatible parts, a word that makes an old observation newly searchable.

I call this larger substrate **infostructure**: the distributed cultural arrangement that stores, preserves, indexes, transmits, recombines, and sometimes loses the information from which new capability can be made.

The argument of this book is that progress depends less on the amount of information a society possesses than on the topology of access to it. The decisive variable is often not discovery but adjacency: whether an old fact can meet a new problem before either disappears.

This is not a claim that culture behaves like a computer, or that history can be reduced to bits. The word information is dangerous precisely because it can make unlike things look commensurable. A theorem, a hand movement, a taboo, a machine drawing and a professional reputation are not the same kind of object. But they can all alter what a later person is able to do. That common causal role is enough to begin.

The book follows the hidden inventory: knowledge that exists without being currently useful, useful knowledge that cannot be retrieved, capability stored in institutions rather than propositions, and the strange moments when several dormant fragments suddenly become an invention.
