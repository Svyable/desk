# The Library Nobody Can Read

There is a childish picture of progress in which humanity keeps a notebook. Somebody discovers something, writes it down, and from then on humanity knows it. The notebook grows. Progress is the growing notebook.

Almost nothing important works that cleanly.

A fact can be published and effectively vanish. A technique can be practiced for centuries and leave no adequate description. A scientific result can be correct but useless because the instrument needed to exploit it does not yet exist. A material can be commercially available before anyone notices that a property considered annoying in one industry is precious in another. Knowledge can survive in a language nobody in the relevant laboratory reads, in a trade journal outside a search index, in the muscle memory of workers whose plant closes, or in a standard whose significance is visible only after thousands of independent firms have quietly conformed to it.

This suggests a distinction between information that exists and information that is *available to a problem*.

The second category is much smaller.

Cultural-evolution researchers have long treated social learning and accumulated know-how as central to human adaptation. Boyd and Richerson's tradition describes culture in informational terms; Joseph Henrich and others emphasize cumulative culture, the way useful practices can become more complex than any single person could reconstruct alone. Archaeologists have repeatedly confronted the opposite phenomenon: complex skills and tool repertoires can also disappear. The interesting unit, then, cannot be the isolated idea. It is the idea plus the conditions that keep it legible.

A library is an easy example because its shelves make storage visible. But a library with no catalog, no shared language, no trained readers and no reason to suspect that the right book exists is mostly architecture. The information is physically present and socially absent.

Modern societies have built enormous libraries of this sort without recognizing them as libraries. Machine shops are libraries. Supply chains are libraries. Legal precedents are libraries. Failed experiments are libraries, although we are especially bad at shelving those. The habits of a laboratory are a library. So is the informal map in a veteran nurse's head of which written procedure becomes dangerous at three in the morning.

The word *infostructure* is meant to point at the whole arrangement rather than the documents inside it. Infrastructure moves matter and energy. Infostructure moves usable difference across minds, generations and domains. It includes storage, but storage is the least interesting part. The hard problems are retention, indexing, translation, trust, recombination and timing.

A civilization may therefore contain discoveries it has not yet made.

That sentence sounds mystical only if discovery is imagined as creation from nothing. Often the pieces are already distributed through the culture. What is missing is a path between them. Progress occurs when the path appears.
