# Institutions as Capacitors

Some knowledge needs a body larger than a person to survive.

Observatories, monasteries, navies, standards organizations, universities, patent offices, national laboratories, museums and long-lived firms all perform versions of the same improbable act: they allow information to outlast the immediate reason someone cared about it.

This is expensive. Archives occupy space. Expertise requires salaries. Specimens require climate control. Software rots. Instruments need calibration. Institutional memory is not memory in the human sense; it is a continuing expenditure that prevents selected traces from becoming unintelligible.

That expenditure creates latency.

A specimen collected for taxonomy can later be analyzed for pollutants its collector did not know existed. Astronomical plates made before satellites can answer questions about the sky before the orbital environment changed. Census records collected for administration can become demographic history. The institution stores optionality.

A capacitor is an imperfect analogy, but a useful one. Institutions can accumulate potential that discharges when a new question arrives. The energy is not free. Someone paid to keep the material, metadata and competence intact during the interval when its future value was unknown.

Markets systematically struggle with some forms of this storage because the beneficiary may be decades away and impossible to identify. Governments and philanthropy struggle for different reasons: budgets reward visible current outputs, not preserved possibility. Archives are easy to cut because the inventions they prevent from being delayed cannot testify at a hearing.

Institutions also suppress. Gatekeeping can preserve quality or freeze a paradigm. Classification can protect legitimate secrets while severing scientific feedback. Proprietary formats can retain data physically while destroying future access. A long-lived institution is not automatically good infostructure.

The test is whether it preserves not only objects but routes back into use. A box with no provenance is not an archive. A dataset with no schema is a future puzzle. A retired expert with no apprentice is a closing library.

Progress has a maintenance budget. We notice it mostly when someone stopped paying.
