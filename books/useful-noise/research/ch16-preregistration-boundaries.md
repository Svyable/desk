# Chapter 16 — falsification process and preregistration boundaries

Working note for *Useful Noise*, Chapter 16, “What Would Falsify This?”

## Why this matters

Chapter 16 argues that a research program becomes harder to fool when it predeclares kill and narrow gates, keeps null results useful, and forces preferred mechanisms to survive conventional explanations. That argument is directionally supported by metascience, but it should not turn procedural rigor into another talisman.

## Evidence that outcome-independent review changes what gets published

Anne M. Scheel, Mitchell R. M. J. Schijen, and Daniël Lakens, “An Excess of Positive Results: Comparing the Standard Psychology Literature With Registered Reports,” *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science* 4(2), 2021.

https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467

The authors compared 71 published Registered Reports with a random sample of 152 standard hypothesis-testing papers in psychology. For the first tested hypothesis, 96.05% of standard reports produced a positive result versus 43.66% of Registered Reports. The gap remained large after excluding close replications.

The useful inference is not that Registered Reports reveal the true null rate for science. The authors discuss multiple possible explanations, including the possibility that researchers choose riskier hypotheses for the format. The narrower result is that deciding publication before outcomes are known is associated with a literature containing far more disconfirming results and far less of the near-universal positivity seen in the conventional sample.

For Chapter 16, this supports the institutional intuition behind kill gates: a system is more capable of learning from failure when the decision to preserve the work is not contingent on getting the desired sign.

## Strongest counterargument: a frozen test can still be a bad test

Anne M. Scheel, Leonid Tiokhin, Peder M. Isager, and Daniël Lakens, “Why Hypothesis Testers Should Spend Less Time Testing Hypotheses,” *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 16(4), 2021.

https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620966795

This paper is useful counterevidence to any claim that preregistration or stricter confirmation automatically produces strong falsification. Its central argument is that a hypothesis test is only informative when the derivation chain connecting theory, auxiliary assumptions, measurement, and statistical prediction is sufficiently mature. A perfectly preregistered experiment can still be uninformative if the construct is poorly measured, the causal structure is unclear, boundary conditions are unknown, or auxiliary assumptions absorb every failure.

That maps directly onto the manuscript’s “immortal hypothesis” problem. Predeclaring a threshold prevents one class of after-the-fact rescue. It does not prevent the project from having chosen a weak discriminator in the first place.

## Boundary for the book

The defensible claim is therefore procedural but not bureaucratic:

- outcome-independent gates can reduce the incentive to preserve only favorable results;
- preregistration can make deviations and post hoc rescue attempts more legible;
- neither guarantees that the underlying theory, measurement, or discriminator is good;
- a serious falsification culture must allow not only a hypothesis to fail, but also a test to be judged incapable of deciding the question.

That last distinction would sharpen Chapter 16 without changing its thesis. The goal is not to make research obey a checklist. It is to make it harder for either the favored mechanism or the protocol itself to become unfalsifiable.
