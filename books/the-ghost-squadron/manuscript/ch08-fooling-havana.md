# Fooling Havana

The Cuban pilot said he had the intruding aircraft in sight.

That is the strangest detail in Gene Poteat's account of a PALLADIUM operation during the Cuban missile crisis. The aircraft the pilot had been sent to intercept was an electronic construction. A U.S. destroyer off Key West was receiving a Cuban radar signal, altering its timing, and sending it back so that the return moved across the scope like a fighter approaching from Florida.

Poteat published the story decades after the operation, first in the CIA's professional journal *Studies in Intelligence* and then in a 1999 engineering article. That makes his account unusually useful firsthand testimony about how PALLADIUM worked. It is not the same thing as a contemporaneous mission file. The public material does not give us the Cuban radar logs, the fighter's flight log, the intercept transcript, or a trial-by-trial record from which to reconstruct exactly what each participant saw.

The parts Poteat does describe are wonderfully physical. Soviet air-defense systems in Cuba gave U.S. intelligence a nearby chance to measure equipment that mattered to strategic reconnaissance. PALLADIUM could vary the delay of a captured radar pulse to create apparent range and speed. The team could also alter the strength of the returned signal, producing a target with a chosen apparent radar cross section. The problem was no longer simply whether Soviet-designed radar could see an airplane. It was how small an airplane-like return an operational crew could detect and track.

For the Cuban test, the destroyer was positioned so its antenna could work the Havana radar from beyond the coast. The electronic target was made to resemble a U.S. fighter coming out of Key West. A Navy submarine added the wonderfully excessive-looking part of the apparatus: timed, balloon-borne metallic spheres of different sizes, released so that the radar could encounter physical reflectors along the same geometry as the electronic target.

The spheres make sense once the experiment is treated as metrology rather than theater. The ghost supplied a controllable track. The spheres supplied known pieces of matter with different radar signatures. NSA listeners supplied another kind of measurement by monitoring Soviet and Cuban reactions. Poteat's stated aim was to infer the smallest radar cross section the system could reliably detect and track.

This was not deception for the pleasure of deception. It was a hostile calibration experiment.

According to Poteat, the Cubans then dispatched fighters. PALLADIUM's operators kept the phantom just ahead of the pursuing aircraft while NSA personnel listened. Then came the radio call: a Cuban pilot reported that he had the intruder in sight and was preparing to make a firing pass. Poteat says he signaled the engineer to switch the false target off.

The pilot's report is tempting because it sounds like corroboration. Radar saw something; a human being said he saw something too. Two sensors agree.

Except they were not independent.

The fighter did not enter the sky blind. He had been sent toward a position produced by the radar track. Whatever generated the later visual report, the search itself was conditioned by the electronic cue. The surviving account does not tell us whether the pilot misidentified something real in that patch of sky, used the language of visual acquisition under uncertainty, was responding to another cue, or whether some detail has blurred in recollection. What it cannot mean is that a delayed radio-frequency signal had become visible aluminum in daylight.

That makes the anecdote more useful, not less. It shows how quickly an injected observation can propagate into channels that look separate when the causal chain is hidden. A radar track launches a fighter. The fighter searches the indicated volume. A voice report comes back. Later, somebody looking only at the outputs can write down "radar plus visual" and count two kinds of evidence.

Modern multi-sensor arguments need this distinction badly. Two instruments are more persuasive than one when their errors and inputs are meaningfully independent. Agreement is weaker when one channel cues, tasks, filters, or trains the other. A camera pointed by radar is still valuable, but its pointing is not independent of the radar. An operator told where to look is a different witness from an operator who noticed the object before the alert. Sensor fusion can multiply information; it can also multiply one bad premise.

There is a counterweight here. The reported fighter scramble itself is an operational consequence, not a mere symbol on an American oscilloscope. If Poteat's reconstruction is substantially right, the false return survived enough of the Cuban air-defense process to cause real aircraft to move through real sky. That is stronger evidence of practical deception than a bench demonstration. It still does not tell us the complete success rate of PALLADIUM, how often operators rejected its ghosts, which sphere established which threshold, or how the episode looked in Cuban records.

The missile crisis adds another hazard. Anything secret done in October 1962 acquires strategic grandeur by proximity. Poteat's story supports a narrower claim: Cuba offered an unusually valuable opportunity to probe Soviet-designed radar under operational conditions, and PALLADIUM was used to turn that opportunity into a measurement. The public record does not establish that this episode altered the course of the crisis.

Its smaller lesson is stranger anyway. A state did not merely put a false dot on a radar. It arranged the dot so that an adversary's own procedures would do some of the work after the injection. According to the man who helped run the system, the phantom eventually acquired a pursuer who believed he could see it.

That is a better reason to care about PALLADIUM than the generic warning that sensors can be fooled. When several channels agree, the first question is not only how many there are. It is how they came to agree.
