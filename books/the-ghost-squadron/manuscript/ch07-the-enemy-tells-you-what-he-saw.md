# The Enemy Tells You What He Saw

A deception system without feedback is a magician performing behind a wall.

The difficult part of PALLADIUM was not only producing a plausible false target. The CIA needed to know whether the target had entered the Soviet picture. Poteat wrote that the team monitored reactions ranging from communications to the activation of additional radars. NSA support and real-time decryption turned the operation into a closed loop.

That loop is the intellectual center of the program.

The ghost goes in. The adversary reacts. The reaction comes back through another intelligence channel. The deceiver adjusts the ghost.

This is not merely electronic warfare. It is system identification performed against a hostile network.

The same logic appears in Wild Weasel operations in a far more violent form. U.S. aircraft exposed themselves to radar emissions in order to locate surface-to-air missile systems and attack them. Air Force histories describe crews deliberately allowing threat radars to reveal themselves. The enemy's attempt to sense became a source of targeting information.

PALLADIUM was subtler because the stimulus itself could be synthetic. Instead of waiting for an aircraft to trigger a radar, the CIA could present a controlled radar apparition and watch which parts of the network woke up.

There are two kinds of uncertainty in such a loop. The first is technical: did the radar actually detect the false target? The second is interpretive: what does the observed reaction mean? A fighter launch is stronger evidence of belief than a brief change in emission, but even a launch may be a training event, coincidence, or response to another cue. Good intelligence practice needs controls because the enemy environment is not a laboratory.

This is where declassified narratives can mislead if read too quickly. A participant recalling a successful operation decades later offers valuable primary testimony about design and purpose. It may not preserve every failed trial, ambiguous intercept, or statistical denominator. The public record is enough to establish the method. It is thinner on the complete performance distribution.

That distinction belongs in the manuscript because the temptation is obvious. PALLADIUM is exactly the kind of program that attracts mythology. It was secret. It created phantom aircraft. It involved CIA, NSA, ships, submarines, Soviet radars, and the Cuban missile crisis. One can inflate it into a universal explanation for anomalous tracks in a paragraph.

The better use is narrower and more consequential. The program demonstrates a mature intelligence idea: adversary sensors are not passive facts of the environment. They can be stimulated in ways that cause the adversary to disclose hidden characteristics of the network.

Modern military deception doctrine states the same logic more openly. Air Force doctrine describes electromagnetic deception as a way to create misleading information and notes that deceptive stories become more plausible when multiple information sources are simulated. Army writing on deception likewise emphasizes presenting false targets that force an adversary to reveal assets, expend munitions, or alter behavior.

The endpoint is behavior.

A convincing phantom is useful because somebody does something in response to it.
