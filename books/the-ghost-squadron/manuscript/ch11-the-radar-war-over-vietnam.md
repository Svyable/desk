# The Radar War Over Vietnam

By Vietnam, electronic warfare had stopped looking like a clever accessory and begun to look like infrastructure.

North Vietnamese air defenses used radar to detect aircraft, guide interceptors, support surface-to-air missiles, and direct antiaircraft fire. U.S. forces responded with specialized aircraft, jamming, chaff, radar-warning equipment, anti-radiation weapons, and tactics designed around the emissions of the defensive network.

The National Museum of the U.S. Air Force describes EB-66 electronic-warfare aircraft as scarce but heavily demanded assets that detected and jammed enemy radar. The Air Force's historical monograph on electronic countermeasures in the air war traces an adaptation cycle in which each side changed tactics and technology in response to the other.

That cycle matters more than any one jammer.

A radar operator can turn a transmitter off to avoid an anti-radiation missile. The attacker can then exploit the period of blindness. The defender can change frequencies or modes. The attacker can update jamming. Strike packages can lay chaff corridors or carry pods. Wild Weasels can try to force radars into revealing themselves. Every improvement changes the information available to the other side.

PALLADIUM belongs to this larger war over observability, but its goal was unusually diagnostic. Jamming often tries to reduce information. PALLADIUM tried to create a controlled piece of information and watch how the defense processed it.

The difference between denial and deception is important. Noise that makes a scope unusable tells the operator that something is wrong. A plausible false target can lead the operator to do something that appears reasonable. Deception preserves enough trust in the sensor to steer behavior.

This is why official doctrine distinguishes forms of electromagnetic deception rather than treating them as variants of brute-force jamming. Simulative techniques create notional or false capabilities. Imitative techniques reproduce enemy-like emissions. Manipulative techniques alter indicators. The intended output is not merely a bad signal-to-noise ratio. It is a wrong model of the battlefield.

Vietnam also shows why that ambition becomes harder in combat. Defenders are not passive receivers. They adapt. Losses teach. Captured equipment teaches. Signals intelligence teaches. Operators learn what jamming looks like. Networks develop alternative procedures. Deception that works repeatedly against the same opponent can become its own signature.

The arms race therefore migrates upward. First the fight is over whether a return exists. Then whether the return is coherent. Then whether the track behaves plausibly. Then whether other sensors agree. Then whether the tactical situation makes sense. Eventually the target is the fused model itself.

Modern discussions of “sensor fusion” sometimes treat fusion as an automatic cure for deception. Vietnam should make us less confident. More sensors can provide independence, but they can also provide more channels for a deception plan to exploit. If the attacker knows which cues the defender uses to resolve ambiguity, those cues become targets.

The only robust advantage comes from genuine causal diversity and adversarial testing.

Electronic warfare over Vietnam was deadly evidence that the electromagnetic spectrum had become contested terrain. PALLADIUM adds the stranger realization that terrain can be surveyed by planting a footprint that never belonged to a body.
