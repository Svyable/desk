# A Track Is Not an Object

A track is a promise made by software about continuity.

At some earlier point there were detections: returns, pixels, bearings, pulses, reports. The tracking system decided that some of them belonged together. It estimated motion. It propagated state between observations. It rejected other detections as clutter. It may have fused reports from several sensors. By the time an operator sees a symbol moving smoothly across a display, much of the mess has already been metabolized.

That is why tracks are so persuasive. They look like objects because persistence has been drawn for us.

PALLADIUM exploited a more basic version of the same trust. By changing delay smoothly, the system could create a target whose apparent range changed coherently from one radar observation to the next. Motion was not observed as a body crossing space. It was inferred from the controlled evolution of returns.

A modern tracker can impose even more continuity. This is usually helpful. Without association and filtering, a dense sensor environment would be unusable. But the same machinery can turn a designed sequence of false measurements into an apparently stable object if the sequence satisfies the tracker's expectations.

The epistemic rule is simple enough to say and difficult to practice: never ask what a track is before asking how the track was formed.

Was it initiated automatically or manually? What measurements seeded it? Which measurements were later associated? What gating thresholds were used? Were there track drops and reacquisitions? Did a human operator merge or split tracks? Was the displayed velocity measured directly through Doppler, estimated kinematically, or inherited from a fused source? What raw data survives?

These questions are mundane. They are also where extraordinary claims either become stronger or dissolve.

The rule applies equally to skeptical explanations. Saying “probably a sensor artifact” without identifying a mechanism is not analysis. A claimed spoof must satisfy geometry and signal constraints. A claimed tracking error must be compatible with the system's behavior. A claimed balloon must fit wind and altitude. A claimed aircraft must fit performance and signature. The standard should be symmetric.

This is one reason PALLADIUM is so valuable as a historical control. It gives us a documented case in which the “sensor artifact” was neither accidental nor vague. A state actor intentionally produced a coherent false target for a known intelligence purpose. The mechanism is described. The operational context is described. The feedback channels are described.

That existence proof should permanently remove one bad assumption: coherence by itself does not guarantee a corresponding vehicle.

It should not replace that assumption with its mirror image. Coherent tracks are still often produced by real moving objects. The base rate matters. Airspace contains aircraft, balloons, weather, birds, drones, satellites, and many ordinary sources of returns. Electronic deception is a special hypothesis that needs evidence of access and motive.

The mature position is less exciting than either extreme. A track is evidence. Its weight depends on the causal chain behind it.

Sometimes the causal chain ends in an airplane.

Sometimes it ends in a delay line on a ship.
