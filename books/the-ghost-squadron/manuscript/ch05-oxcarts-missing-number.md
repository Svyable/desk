# OXCART's Missing Number

Stealth is not a property you can inspect with a flashlight.

An aircraft has shapes, materials, seams, inlets, exhausts, surface treatments, flight profiles, and emissions. “Stealth” is the relationship between those things and a particular detection system under particular conditions. A target that is difficult for one radar may be easier for another frequency, geometry, processing method, or cueing network. OXCART forced the CIA to care about that relationship in operational terms.

Poteat described the problem plainly. Knowing Soviet radar power and spatial coverage was only half the answer. The agency also needed receiver sensitivity and operator proficiency. A map saying that a radar could illuminate a region did not tell planners how small an apparent target might be detected there.

PALLADIUM offered a way to search for that threshold without flying an actual A-12 into the test condition. Its false target could be adjusted to represent different radar cross sections. If the Soviets reacted at one apparent size and not another, the boundary could be narrowed.

This is experimental thinking under intelligence constraints. The variable of interest was not “can Soviet radar see aircraft?” It was closer to “under this geometry and configuration, what apparent return is sufficient to trigger a meaningful detection response?” The more specific the question, the more useful the answer becomes for mission planning.

There are obvious caveats. A radar operator may see something without reporting it. A communications intercept may miss the report. A network may be operating under unusual alert rules. Receiver sensitivity is not identical to track probability. Weather, clutter, interference, maintenance, and doctrine matter. PALLADIUM could measure an operational envelope, not reveal a Platonic number called Soviet radar capability.

That distinction helps with modern claims about classified sensor systems. Public discussion often asks whether a platform is “detectable” as though detection were binary. Operational systems live in probability distributions. Range changes signal strength. Aspect changes radar cross section. Integration time matters. Cueing matters. A system may detect without classifying, classify without tracking continuously, or track without achieving weapon-quality fire control.

The intelligence value of a controlled ghost lies in turning some of those abstractions into observed reactions.

It also explains why the program belonged near stealth development rather than in a theatrical deception shop. If your own aircraft depends on remaining below an adversary's threshold, then understanding that threshold is part of the aircraft design problem. The enemy sensor becomes a remote test instrument for your low-observable program.

This is a useful antidote to one of the habits created by secrecy. When details are classified, public imagination tends to fill the gap with either magical capability or total incompetence. The real historical record is usually more mechanical. Engineers confront uncertain parameters. Intelligence officers find ways to measure them. Operators build margins because the measurements are imperfect. Programs fail, adapt, and sometimes work well enough.

OXCART itself is a reminder that extraordinary-looking capability can be real without being supernatural. PALLADIUM adds a subtler point: sometimes the hidden capability is not a better vehicle. Sometimes it is a better way to ask the other side's instruments a question.
