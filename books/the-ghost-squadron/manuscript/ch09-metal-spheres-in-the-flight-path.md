# Metal Spheres in the Flight Path

The submarine is what saves the PALLADIUM story from becoming a metaphor.

According to Poteat, a Navy submarine was positioned near Havana Bay to release balloon-borne metallized spheres of different sizes into the path of the electronic aircraft. The spheres offered known physical radar targets against which the Soviet system's behavior could be compared.

A false target and a real target entered the same experiment.

This is calibration in its oldest form: put something with known properties into the instrument's field and see what the instrument does. The spheres convert a question about an invisible receiver threshold into a sequence of physical objects. If a sphere of one size produces a response and a smaller one does not, the boundary becomes less abstract. If the electronic ghost is adjusted to comparable apparent strength, its utility as a probe improves.

The experiment also highlights a distinction that deserves to survive into every modern sensor controversy: correlation is stronger when the channels are physically different.

Two software displays showing the same upstream radar track are not two independent detections. Two radars networked through the same tracker may share errors. An infrared camera slaved to a radar cue is more independent than a second rendering of the radar track, but not fully independent if the cue determines where and when the camera looks. A physical reflector known to occupy a measured location offers a different kind of check.

The metallized spheres did not solve every problem. Their radar cross sections would depend on size, material, frequency, aspect, and perhaps suspension geometry. Balloon motion introduces uncertainty. The Soviet radar's processing and operator behavior remain partly hidden. Yet the instinct is right: bring matter into the argument.

This matters because sophisticated deception often exploits abstraction. A track file is easy to copy. A message can be forged. A transponder can be imitated. A coherent radio-frequency return can be delayed. But each additional independent physical consequence becomes another obligation for the deception system. A real aircraft may produce radar, infrared, acoustic, visual, wake, contrail, communication, and eventually debris or maintenance records. A false aircraft must either reproduce enough of those consequences or prevent the defender from checking them.

Modern doctrine recognizes this from the attacker's side. Air Force guidance notes that multi-sensor deception can increase the plausibility of a deception story. The inverse follows naturally: defenders should value sensor diversity that forces the attacker to simulate distinct physical channels rather than merely duplicate data products.

The lesson is not “trust physical evidence and distrust electronics.” A metallized sphere itself becomes a designed electronic target. The lesson is to ask how independent the causal chains really are.

PALLADIUM's designers understood that a deception program intended to measure a threshold could not validate itself. They needed a known reference.

That may be the most modern part of the operation. In a world of synthetic media, networked sensors, autonomous classification, and machine-generated tracks, the question “what is the calibration object?” becomes more important, not less.
