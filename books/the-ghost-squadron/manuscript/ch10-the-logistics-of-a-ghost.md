# The Logistics of a Ghost

A nonexistent aircraft can require a great deal of trucking.

Poteat's history includes a detail I keep returning to because it refuses to behave like mythology. During one PALLADIUM deployment in northern Japan, winter weather closed airports. A large equipment van had to move by train, but tunnels were too small for an easy journey. The team repeatedly hauled the van off trains, moved it across difficult terrain by truck and sled, then loaded it again. The ghost aircraft depended on snow, tunnel clearances, rail schedules, and men wrestling hardware through the mountains.

Secrecy loves nouns. Program. Capability. Asset. System. Logistics returns the verbs.

Transport it. Power it. Align it. Repair it. Feed it the correct signal. Put the antenna in the right place. Coordinate the collection team. Keep the military support platform where geometry demands. Recover from weather.

This is not a side note. Logistics defines the operational envelope of deception. A technique that works in a laboratory but requires a conspicuous transmitter close to the victim radar may have limited wartime utility. A system that can travel on a ship but not an aircraft has a different set of targets. A deception requiring precise knowledge of the victim waveform becomes brittle against rapid waveform changes unless collection and adaptation move with it.

The physical burden also helps calibrate claims about hidden capabilities. Historical black programs could remain secret for years or decades, but they still generated equipment, specialized crews, deployments, failures, budgets, and operational dependencies. Some traces remain classified; others surface in declassified histories and participant accounts. The correct inference is neither “secrecy is impossible” nor “secrecy can hide anything.” Physical programs cast shadows proportional to how much physical work they demand.

PALLADIUM's shadow was smaller than a fleet of real reconnaissance aircraft because the target itself was synthetic. Yet the system still needed teams and platforms. That contrast is useful. Electronic deception can cheaply create apparent mass in the enemy picture, but only after a real support architecture is in place.

Modern expendable decoys make the trade even clearer. A small air-launched vehicle can imitate some signatures of a much more valuable aircraft and provoke an air-defense response. The decoy substitutes inexpensive physical mass and carefully designed emissions for expensive combat mass. It does not abolish logistics. It rearranges them.

The Japanese snow story also protects us from the clean diagrams that electronic warfare encourages. On paper, a variable delay line changes range. In practice, the box has weight.

That is a good habit for investigating every esoteric technology. Ask where the antenna sits. Ask who maintains it. Ask how much power it draws. Ask how close it must get. Ask what it needs to know about the target. Ask whether weather changes performance. Ask what happens when one component fails.

These questions are especially useful around alleged classified technologies because they are not demands for secret design details. They are demands for physical coherence.

A ghost that can only exist when a truck reaches the mountain is still an extraordinary intelligence capability. It is simply one that belongs to the world.
