# Decoys That Invite Fire

A successful decoy may be the thing the enemy is most eager to shoot.

That reverses an ordinary engineering instinct. We usually design expensive systems to avoid hostile attention. A decoy can be valuable precisely because it attracts attention cheaply, provokes emissions, consumes interceptors, reveals engagement zones, or forces the defender to expose the sequence by which a target moves from detection to weapon assignment.

Public acquisition records make the logic unusually concrete. A 2003 Government Accountability Office decision described the Miniature Air Launched Decoy as a small vehicle intended to create the impression of a fighter or bomber, provoke an enemy response, reveal or occupy air defenses, and help suppress an integrated system without exposing real aircraft to the same risk. Earlier GAO work on suppression of enemy air defenses described MALD as a platform meant to mimic aircraft and draw defenses away from real forces.

The family resemblance to PALLADIUM is obvious, but the differences are useful. PALLADIUM could create an electronic target without putting an aircraft at the apparent location. MALD is a real flying object designed to carry a misleading identity. One manufactures kinematics inside the receiver. The other manufactures a cheap body whose signatures resemble a more valuable body.

A defender trying to distinguish them must therefore ask different questions. Against a pure electronic false target, spatially separated sensors and non-RF modalities may expose the absence of an object. Against a physical decoy, those sensors may all confirm that something is there. The discrimination problem moves from existence to identity and intent.

This is why “multi-sensor confirmation” is not a universal proof of target value. A physical decoy can produce radar, infrared, optical, and kinematic evidence because it is physically present. The deception lies in what the object means.

The distinction matters outside warfare. Many anomaly debates treat the question as binary: real object or sensor artifact. Military deception reminds us of a third category—real object, false interpretation. A balloon can be real while its apparent significance is wrong. A drone can be real while its operator or purpose is misidentified. A decoy can be real while its implied threat is deliberately fabricated.

PALLADIUM supplies a fourth category: no corresponding object, deliberately fabricated track.

The taxonomy is not meant to make investigation impossible. It makes the required evidence clearer. To establish that an unusual track corresponds to a particular kind of vehicle, investigators need more than the fact that a sensor produced a coherent return. They need constraints that distinguish among physical targets, natural phenomena, errors, and active deception.

Decoys also reveal an economic asymmetry. If a defender uses an expensive missile, exposes a radar, or moves a scarce aircraft in response to a cheap target, the attacker gains even when the decoy is eventually identified. The deception can fail as belief and succeed as exchange ratio.

PALLADIUM could succeed similarly as a probe. The Soviets did not have to remain fooled for long. They only had to react enough to reveal something.

A phantom becomes strategically useful when the enemy spends reality on it.
