# The Microbarograph

The heroic object in this story is not a weapon. It is a pressure gauge.

Microbarographs and modern microbarometers turn tiny atmospheric pressure fluctuations into records that can be compared across an array. A single sensor hears weather and wind and machinery along with whatever event interests the analyst. Several separated sensors allow coherent arrivals to be distinguished from local noise and give direction-of-arrival information. Geometry turns pressure into evidence.

This is worth dwelling on because exotic subjects improve when the instrument becomes visible. Claims about distant explosions become questions about calibration, array aperture, timing, background noise and propagation models. The romance drains away and the interesting difficulty arrives.

Wind is especially troublesome. A pressure sensor sitting in moving air is not listening in a quiet room. Modern stations use spatial filters and pipe systems to suppress wind noise. Site selection matters. Forests can be useful because they reduce atmospheric turbulence at the sensor. A global listening system is therefore partly an exercise in gardening: find quiet air, lay out an array, maintain it for decades, and know exactly what its clocks are doing.

The instrument teaches the epistemology. Detection is never just a waveform. It is a waveform plus sensor health plus weather plus array coherence plus a model of how the atmosphere could have carried the signal there. Every later claim in this book, including the dramatic ones, owes the reader at least that much machinery.
