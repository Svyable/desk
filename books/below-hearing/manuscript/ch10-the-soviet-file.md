# The Soviet File

A 1972 U.S. intelligence assessment surveyed Soviet work on what it called controlled offensive behavior. One section addressed sound as a means of altering behavior. The document is fascinating less for what it proves than for how carefully it admits what it does not know: psychological and behavioral effects of infrasound were poorly documented, and Soviet work especially so.

That sentence should govern everything that follows.

The report gathered Soviet research, secondary references and anecdotes across infrasonic, sonic and ultrasonic regimes. It shows that U.S. intelligence analysts considered the possibility of behavioral acoustic effects worth cataloguing. It does not show that the Soviet Union possessed an effective infrasonic mind-control weapon.

This distinction is routinely destroyed in retellings. A declassified intelligence report becomes evidence that the subject was real; 'real' slides from real interest to real effect to real operational weapon. The archival fact is narrower and more interesting: amid Cold War uncertainty, analysts were asked to map a scientific territory whose borders were badly measured.

Government attention is evidence of attention.
