# Listening for the Bomb

Atmospheric nuclear testing made the air itself an intelligence collection surface. Large explosions produce pressure waves over a broad spectrum, including frequencies capable of long-range propagation. If another country detonated a device in the atmosphere, distant arrays could contribute evidence that something energetic had happened and where.

The distinction between detection and identification mattered from the beginning. An acoustic arrival is not a radionuclide. Volcanoes explode. Meteoroids enter violently. Chemical plants fail. Mining blasts occur. The problem is not merely hearing a bang below hearing; it is classifying an event in a world full of energetic impostors.

That is why modern treaty verification is deliberately multimodal. Seismic stations listen through rock, hydroacoustic stations through ocean, infrasound arrays through atmosphere, and radionuclide stations sample material evidence. No single modality is promoted to omniscience. The architecture is an institutional admission that nature is noisy and adversaries may be motivated.

There is a lesson here for anomalous research. The best sensor network is not the one with the most sensitive instrument. It is the one whose independent channels fail differently. A pressure wave can locate an event without proving nuclear origin. Xenon can supply a different kind of evidence. The strength is not accumulation of similar traces but collision of unlike ones.
