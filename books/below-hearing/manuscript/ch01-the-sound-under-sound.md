# The Sound Under Sound

On 15 February 2013, a meteor broke apart over Chelyabinsk, Russia. The visible event was local and spectacular: a white scar across the sky, a blast wave, windows breaking. The quieter record was planetary. Infrasound stations built to help detect nuclear explosions registered the event across the CTBT monitoring network; a station in Antarctica, roughly 15,000 kilometres away, heard pressure waves from the blast after they had crossed an ocean and a continent. The atmosphere had carried news of the explosion far beyond anybody who could have seen it.

That is the useful strangeness of infrasound. Below roughly twenty hertz, pressure still oscillates even as ordinary hearing becomes a poor guide to what is happening. Instruments register waves the ear may not. Structures can respond. The atmosphere can carry information for thousands of kilometres. The question is not whether something is "sound" in the everyday sense. The question is what pressure changed, where, by how much, and whether several instruments agree about where it came from.

States learned to care about that distinction for an obvious reason. An atmospheric explosion broadcasts into a medium no government controls. Energy couples into air, moves through a layered and moving atmosphere, and may arrive at distant pressure sensors long after the flash has disappeared. A sufficiently distributed network can therefore collect evidence about an event the source country might prefer not to advertise.

The modern version is wonderfully physical. The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization's International Monitoring System is designed to include sixty infrasound stations in thirty-five countries. Each station is an array, not a lone microphone: four or more elements measure minute pressure changes with microbarometers while pipe systems reduce wind noise. Analysts can compare arrivals across the array, separate coherent waves from some local noise, and estimate direction. The stations are deliberately placed away from airports, highways, industry, coasts and other noisy places when geography permits. Some sit in forest because trees help quiet the moving air. One of the great machines of nuclear diplomacy depends, in part, on finding somewhere the wind will shut up.

The network is not a nuclear detector in the magical sense. Volcanoes, storms, meteors, aircraft, rockets, mining blasts and large chemical explosions also produce infrasound. Nor can an infrasonic detection by itself establish that an explosion was nuclear; the treaty system combines several monitoring technologies, with radionuclide evidence playing a distinct role in identifying nuclear material. The strength of the system comes from calibrated instruments, multiple stations, complementary sensors and the slow work of attribution. False positives are not embarrassing exceptions. They are the normal environment in which detection has to work.

This is also why infrasound became such good material for stories about hidden influence. Invisible, inaudible and capable of travelling very far are almost indecently suggestive properties. But a pressure wave detectable by a sensitive instrument after a long journey is not therefore intense enough to injure or manipulate a person at that distance. Detection threshold and biological dose are different questions. So are source power, propagation loss, exposure duration and coupling into a body. Any serious claim about an infrasonic weapon has to survive those quantities rather than merely borrowing the eeriness of the frequency band.

The distinction will matter throughout this book. The same atmospheric physics produced two very different histories. One accumulated arrays, calibration, noise models, cross-station agreement and an international verification bureaucracy. The other accumulated suggestive experiments, intelligence interest, anecdotes and claims whose engineering is often left conveniently offstage.

Chelyabinsk offers the better starting point because nobody had to imagine the source. A rock entered the atmosphere; twenty stations heard what followed. The interesting part is what had to exist before those faint pressure traces could become evidence: instruments quiet enough to notice them, a network broad enough to compare them, and institutions willing to spend decades learning what the planet sounds like when nothing secret is happening.
