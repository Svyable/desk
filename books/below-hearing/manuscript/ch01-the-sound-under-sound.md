# The Sound Under Sound

A person can stand inside an acoustic event and hear nothing that explains it. Below roughly twenty hertz the familiar category of sound begins to fail as an everyday description. Pressure still oscillates. Instruments still register it. Structures can still respond. The atmosphere still carries information. The ear is simply no longer the instrument we should trust.

That mismatch made infrasound useful to states. An atmospheric explosion does not need permission to broadcast. Energy couples into air, propagates through a layered moving medium, and can be sampled at distance by a pressure sensor. The observer does not need to see the source. In the nuclear age this became an intelligence proposition: if the atmosphere is a transmission channel, a sufficiently distributed set of instruments can listen for events another government would rather keep private.

It also made infrasound ideal material for stories about hidden influence. Invisible, inaudible, long-ranging: three adjectives that practically write the conspiracy literature by themselves. But the very features that make a signal eerie do not make it biologically potent. The first task is to keep propagation, detection and physiological effect in separate columns.

The modern CTBT monitoring system makes the detection side wonderfully concrete. Its infrasound component is built around arrays of microbarometers. These measure minute pressure variations. The network exists because low-frequency atmospheric waves can propagate far enough to contribute to identifying explosions. The same stations inevitably record nature: volcanoes, meteors, storms and other large events. A machine built to hear cheating becomes an observatory of the planet.

That inversion is the book's subject. Secret listening became public verification. A military problem became geophysics. Meanwhile a poorly bounded bioeffects literature became a durable weapon legend. The same frequency band carries both histories, but not the same evidence.
