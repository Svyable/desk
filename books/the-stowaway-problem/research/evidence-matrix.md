# Evidence matrix

| Claim | Class | Current posture |
|---|---|---|
| Mars material naturally reaches Earth as meteorites | 1 — verified/documented | Established geological fact; not itself evidence that viable organisms made the trip. |
| Spacecraft assembly cleanrooms contain persistent microbial communities | 1 | Repeatedly documented by cultivation and molecular surveys. |
| Some spacecraft-associated *Bacillus* spores show unusual UV/peroxide resistance | 1 | Demonstrated for specific isolates, including SAFR-032. |
| Shielding strongly changes microbial survival in real/simulated space exposure | 1 | Demonstrated in EXPOSE-class experiments under specified durations and geometries. |
| Some organics measured in ALH84001 were terrestrial contaminants | 1 | Supported by amino-acid and isotopic work; does not settle every ALH84001 feature. |
| A viable cleanroom organism has reached and reproduced on Mars | 3 — disputed/not established | No public evidence used here establishes this. |
| Natural lithopanspermia has transferred viable life between Mars and Earth | 3 | Physically plausible stages exist; complete event remains unverified. |
| Cleanroom regimes can enrich the relative abundance of stress-tolerant survivors | 2 — credible/incomplete | Ecologically plausible and consistent with surveys; causal evolutionary claims require stronger longitudinal evidence. |
| A familiar-looking future martian organism could reflect ancient natural transfer rather than recent contamination | 4 — speculative synthesis | A provenance hypothesis, not a current finding. |
| Planetary protection and panspermia share a common transport-survival problem | 4 | Conceptual synthesis grounded in overlapping experimental variables; useful only if differences in geometry, time and mission context remain explicit. |

## Defeat condition

The book's central synthesis weakens substantially if comprehensive mission-chain data show that modern planetary-protection controls make biologically and molecularly relevant terrestrial carryover negligible at the sensitivities of life-detection instruments, while realistic transfer models simultaneously show no plausible overlap between microbial survival envelopes and natural ejecta trajectories. Either finding would separate the two literatures more sharply than this manuscript currently argues.
