# The Doppelgänger Test

Imagine the worst possible alien for a press conference: a bacterium that looks familiar.

It has DNA. It uses familiar amino acids. Its ribosomal machinery resembles ours. It carries genes that fall somewhere near terrestrial branches but not cleanly inside the sequences cataloged from the spacecraft.

Is it contamination?

The answer cannot come from resemblance alone. If life on Mars arose independently, very deep biochemical similarities might be surprising enough to favor contamination or shared ancestry. If natural transfer between early Earth and Mars occurred, shared architecture could be ancient. If the signal is a terrestrial stowaway that mutated during transport, exact sequence identity may be lost.

A serious provenance test would combine multiple forms of evidence.

First comes mission history: cleanroom isolates, reagent blanks, hardware swabs, environmental sequences, manufacturing records and the spatial route from possible contamination source to sampled material. Second comes phylogeny: does the organism nest inside a known terrestrial lineage with ordinary divergence, or does it branch in a way difficult to reconcile with recent contamination? Third comes biochemistry: genetic code, chirality, membrane composition, cofactors and molecular machinery. Fourth comes geology and ecology: is the organism distributed in protected martian material in ways that predate the spacecraft, or only near the hardware?

No single test is perfect. Together they can make one history more plausible than another.

This is why contamination archives should be treated as scientific assets rather than compliance debris. A swab taken years before launch may become decisive evidence after a surprising detection. A freezer containing cleanroom isolates may be part of the eventual proof that something on Mars is not ours—or is.

The doppelgänger problem also argues for predeclared thresholds. Teams should decide before detection what evidence would count against extraterrestrial origin. Otherwise every ambiguous result can accumulate ad hoc defenses. The history of anomalous science is full of claims that become harder to falsify as counterevidence arrives.

An alien-life claim should become easier to defeat when a close terrestrial match appears.

The strongest version of the claim would survive deliberate attempts to make it terrestrial. Investigators would search cleanroom databases for relatives, test extraction controls, reproduce the signal with independent instruments, examine isotopic and geological context, and invite adversarial laboratories to analyze blinded material.

If the result survives, the uncertainty does not disappear. It changes shape.

That is what good evidence does. It narrows the space in which doubt can hide.
