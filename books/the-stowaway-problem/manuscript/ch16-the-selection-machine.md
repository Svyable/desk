# The Selection Machine

Cleanrooms do not create extremophiles by wishing for them. They can still enrich for organisms that already tolerate extremity.

That distinction deserves protection because the phrase “NASA superbug” is almost irresistible. It is also usually wrong.

When investigators find unusually UV-resistant or peroxide-tolerant strains in spacecraft assembly facilities, at least three explanations are possible. The cleanroom may preferentially retain organisms with preexisting resistance. Repeated exposure may produce some adaptive change over generations. Or the sampling method may simply reveal lineages that ordinary environmental surveys overlook. Different organisms may reflect different mixtures of all three.

The strong claim—that planetary-protection procedures rapidly evolve uniquely dangerous microbes—needs evidence that is generally absent. The weaker claim is already consequential: stringent cleaning changes the composition of survivors.

Ecologists would not find this surprising. Every filter changes a community. Antibiotics enrich resistant bacteria. Desiccation favors organisms able to endure drying. Nutrient limitation favors strategies compatible with scarcity. A cleanroom is a set of filters arranged around engineering priorities.

The uncomfortable result is that total burden can fall while average resilience rises among what remains.

This does not mean cleaning is counterproductive. One resistant survivor is preferable to a billion mixed organisms. Probability still falls. But risk models should not assume the survivor has average physiology.

The insight connects directly to panspermia. Natural transfer also selects violently. Ejection kills the fragile. Space kills the exposed. Radiation kills the poorly protected. Entry and landing impose another filter. If viable life ever arrives by rock, it will be an extreme tail selected by transit.

Spacecraft cleaning and interplanetary transfer therefore perform analogous sorting. One is intentional, the other indifferent. Both make the surviving population unrepresentative of the starting population.

That analogy has limits. A cleanroom operates over years under terrestrial conditions. Lithopanspermia may operate over geological time with entirely different bottlenecks. The analogy is useful only at the level of selection logic.

Still, it suggests a practical research program. Instead of asking only how many organisms remain after a planetary-protection process, ask how the trait distribution changes. Measure resistance before and after cleaning. Sequence survivors. Test them under combined mission-relevant stresses rather than one stress at a time. Archive them for future provenance comparisons.

A planetary-protection program that knows its survivors is stronger than one that knows only its kill rate.

The same is true when the mission finally detects something biological. The question will not be merely whether it lives. It will be whether we have met it before.
