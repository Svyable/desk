# Humans Ruin the Clean Experiment

A human is a planetary-protection failure with a passport.

We shed skin, exhale aerosols, carry dense microbial communities in the gut and mouth, leak water, produce waste and require habitats warm enough for terrestrial biology to remain comfortable. Robotic missions can be assembled under cleanroom control and sent with measured bioburden. A crewed Mars mission will transport an ecosystem because the ecosystem is part of life support.

This does not make human exploration impossible. It makes a particular scientific fantasy impossible: a biologically pristine Mars indefinitely protected from Earth while humans live on it.

The relevant question shifts from prevention to zoning, documentation, containment and timing. Which regions should be explored robotically before human arrival? Which sites deserve special protection because they could preserve accessible martian biosignatures? How do we maintain microbial baselines around habitats, suits, vehicles and waste systems? What evidence would be strong enough to distinguish indigenous organisms from human-associated lineages after years of occupation?

Humans also change panspermia from hypothesis to policy. Once a settlement exists, transfer of Earth life to Mars is no longer a rare stochastic event inside ejecta. It is a continuous managed pressure. Even if surface conditions kill most releases, the number of releases becomes enormous.

The old category “forward contamination” begins to resemble ecology.

There is a useful humility in recognizing this early. Planetary protection can preserve some environments and scientific questions without promising eternal sterility. Antarctica offers an imperfect analogy: humans can conduct science under stringent environmental rules while still leaving impacts that must be measured and managed. Mars will be harder because the baseline biology, if any, may be unknown.

A human mission also undermines one common rhetorical move in planetary-protection debates. People sometimes treat contamination precautions as evidence that scientists secretly believe Mars is teeming with life. The actual logic is almost the reverse. When a potential signal is rare and precious, contamination matters more. We protect a low-background experiment because the expected signal may be weak.

The first crew will carry microbes that have already passed through hospitals, spacecraft assembly, closed habitats and preflight quarantine. Those microbes will experience another selection regime on the way to Mars. Radiation, recycled air, disinfectants, altered diet and confined surfaces will shape which lineages persist.

Mars may therefore receive not a random sample of Earth, but a human-filtered microbiome with its own history of surviving controlled environments.

By then the stowaway problem will have stopped being metaphorical. The stowaways will be residents.
