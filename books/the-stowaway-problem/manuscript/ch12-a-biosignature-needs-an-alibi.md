# A Biosignature Needs an Alibi

Suppose a future rover detects a complex organic molecule strongly associated with terrestrial metabolism.

The first question in popular imagination is whether nonliving chemistry could make it. The mission team will ask that too. But they will have another question that is less glamorous and often more immediate: was this molecule on the spacecraft before launch?

A biosignature is not a fingerprint unless the suspect list is controlled.

Modern astrobiology increasingly treats life detection as a ladder of evidence rather than a magic molecule. Morphology, isotopic fractionation, repeating molecular patterns, disequilibria, spatial associations and geological context can reinforce or undermine one another. That approach is partly a response to decades of false positives and near misses.

Terrestrial contamination can infiltrate several rungs at once. A dead bacterium carries cellular morphology, membrane fragments, amino acids, lipids and nucleic acids. A viable bacterium can add metabolism if conditions permit. Cleaning chemicals and manufacturing materials can contribute organics without biology. A single contamination event can therefore imitate convergence.

The solution is provenance engineering.

Witness plates, blanks, materials inventories, microbial surveys, sequencing of assembly-facility isolates and contamination knowledge bases are not bureaucratic extras. They are negative controls stretched across an interplanetary mission. If an unusual sequence appears in a martian sample, investigators want to know whether close relatives were present in the cleanroom. If an organic compound appears, they want a record of compounds associated with lubricants, adhesives, packaging and laboratory processing.

This makes life detection unusually dependent on memory. A mission must remember itself.

The cleanest sample can become scientifically weak if its contamination history is poorly documented. A messier sample can sometimes remain interpretable if the contaminants are characterized. The objective is not merely low background; it is known background.

The problem sharpens when an organism evolves during storage or transport. A terrestrial stowaway exposed to unusual radiation, starvation and bottlenecks could acquire mutations. It would not become martian, but a simplistic sequence match might become less obvious. Conversely, a genuine martian organism sharing ancient ancestry with Earth life could look suspiciously familiar.

This is where the panspermia hypothesis returns as an epistemic complication rather than a transport claim. If life can naturally move between neighboring planets, genetic similarity does not map cleanly onto contamination. Very close identity to a known cleanroom strain would be damning. Deep similarity across fundamental biochemistry could have multiple historical explanations.

The most interesting life-detection result may therefore be neither clearly alien nor clearly ours.

We should design the experiment before that ambiguity arrives.
