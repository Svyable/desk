# Five Hundred Fifty-Nine Days Outside

For 559 days, spores rode outside the International Space Station.

The ADAPT experiment on ESA's EXPOSE-E facility subjected *Bacillus subtilis* spores to real low-Earth-orbit conditions and simulated martian conditions. The most destructive agent was not a surprise: ultraviolet radiation. Monolayers directly exposed to the extraterrestrial solar spectrum were devastated. Yet when samples were shielded from solar irradiation, survival changed dramatically. Roughly eight percent of one UV-resistant strain's spores survived the low-Earth-orbit condition, while shielded spores under simulated martian atmospheric conditions performed far better relative to laboratory controls.

The important object in that experiment is almost the shield rather than the spore.

Space is lethal unevenly. Vacuum matters. Ionizing radiation matters. Temperature cycling matters. Solar ultraviolet is ferocious when it can reach biological molecules directly. A few millimeters or centimeters of the right material can change which mechanism dominates.

This is why the popular image of an exposed bacterium drifting naked between planets is a poor test of lithopanspermia. The hypothesis most worth testing is litho-panspermia: life inside rock. It is also why the exposed outer skin of a spacecraft is not the only forward-contamination concern. Hardware creates shadows, cavities and interfaces by design.

EXPOSE experiments are compelling because they replace simulated space with actual space for part of the chain. They also preserve an important limitation. Low Earth orbit is not interplanetary space. Earth's magnetic environment, mission duration and radiation spectrum differ. A year and a half is long for an experiment and short for many natural transfer trajectories.

A survival fraction measured after 559 days therefore does not extrapolate simply to a million years. Damage accumulates through multiple channels. Dormant spores cannot continuously metabolize their way out of trouble. Some lesions can be repaired after rehydration; others are fatal. Shield thickness changes radiation dose but also changes the mass and ejection probability of the carrier.

Panspermia becomes an optimization problem with no reason to optimize itself for our convenience.

The same experiments have immediate planetary-protection value without any appeal to natural transfer. They identify which spacecraft-associated or model organisms survive combinations of conditions and which environmental factors deserve the most aggressive controls. An organism that persists under desiccation and vacuum but dies under unshielded UV may be low risk on a sunlit panel and higher risk inside a protected interface.

This is the collision at the center of the book. An experiment designed partly to understand whether terrestrial contaminants could survive spaceflight also provides data used in arguments about whether life can move naturally between worlds.

One dataset, two anxieties.

The next step is to stop saying “shielded” as if all shields were equivalent.
