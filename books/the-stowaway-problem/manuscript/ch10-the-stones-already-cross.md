# The Stones Already Cross

There is a temptation to treat a martian meteorite as a message. It is first an object.

Its trapped gases, mineralogy, isotopic composition and crystallization history can tie it to Mars. Its cosmic-ray exposure can constrain how long it traveled. Its fusion crust records atmospheric entry. Its terrestrial weathering records the second life the rock acquired after landing here.

That last stage matters more than romantic accounts usually allow.

A meteorite arriving on Earth does not enter a sterile vault. It lands in ice, desert, soil or water. It encounters terrestrial organics, microbes, handling, storage materials and laboratory procedures. The longer the chain of custody, the more carefully a claim about indigenous molecules must distinguish preterrestrial material from what Earth added later.

This is the same provenance problem as spacecraft contamination with the direction reversed. On Mars we fear carrying Earth into the sample. With martian meteorites we receive Mars into Earth and then try to subtract Earth back out.

The fact of natural rock transfer is nevertheless profound. It means the planets are less biologically isolated in principle than they appear from a night sky. Early in solar-system history, impacts were more frequent. If microbial life existed on one world while another offered compatible habitats, rock exchange provides a physically grounded route worth quantifying.

But geological transfer does not tell us the sign of biological transfer. A rock can cross perfectly while every organism inside it dies. A viable cell can arrive and fail to reproduce. A lineage can establish and leave no detectable descendants. The inference from meteorite to panspermia remains a chain, not a leap.

There is also a complication rarely granted enough weight: if natural transfer between early Earth and Mars were possible, finding closely related life on both worlds would not automatically establish independent origins. Shared biochemistry could mean common ancestry rather than convergent emergence. The discovery everyone imagines as “a second genesis” could instead reveal a very old contamination event performed by impacts.

That possibility changes what counts as alien.

A martian organism with DNA, the same genetic code and familiar cellular machinery would be astonishing. It would also raise a provenance problem across billions of years. Was life independently invented twice, or transported? Phylogenetic distance, chirality, membrane chemistry and deep molecular architecture would become evidence in a historical reconstruction whose decisive records may be gone.

In that sense planetary protection is not merely preserving Mars from ecological harm. It is preserving the possibility of answering an origin question. Once terrestrial organisms are introduced widely, future investigators inherit a more complicated phylogeny.

We have already seen a smaller version of this ambiguity in one of the most famous meteorites ever collected.

ALH84001 did not settle whether Mars once lived. It became a lesson in how difficult a biological claim becomes after a rock has had two planets in its history.
