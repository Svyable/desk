# A Clean Room Is Still a Habitat

A spacecraft cleanroom is one of the least natural places on Earth, which is exactly why its ecology matters.

Air is filtered. Particles are counted. Surfaces are cleaned on schedules. People enter through controlled procedures wearing garments designed to keep hair, skin and ordinary debris away from hardware. Temperature and humidity are managed. The room is not supposed to be biologically interesting.

It is.

When microbiologists began applying culture-independent sequencing methods alongside the older spore assays, cleanrooms stopped looking empty and started looking selective. A 2012 study of Mars-bound spacecraft hardware and assembly environments recovered DNA representing bacteria, archaea and fungi. The spacecraft hardware itself carried fewer bacterial operational taxonomic units than floors and ground-support equipment, exactly what one would hope after cleaning. Yet recurrent lineages remained. The authors made the unsettling suggestion that cleanroom practices may inadvertently select organisms fit enough to endure both cleaning and, potentially, a long trip.

Selection is a word that can be abused here. Nobody demonstrated that a few years of spacecraft assembly created new radiation-resistant species. Cleanrooms are not miniature evolutionary laboratories in which NASA rapidly breeds superbugs. Most of the relevant organisms already possess traits that help them survive desiccation, starvation, oxidative chemistry or radiation. The environment filters. It repeatedly removes the easy organisms and leaves a biased sample of the difficult ones.

That is enough to create an epistemic asymmetry.

The organisms we most want to exclude are precisely those most likely to be overrepresented among survivors. If ninety-nine ordinary cells disappear and one spore-former remains, the room becomes cleaner by almost any practical measure while the composition of the residual population becomes more interesting for planetary protection.

This is why a raw microbial count is not the whole problem. Identity matters. Physiological state matters. Location matters. A cell on an exposed panel and a spore protected inside a hardware interface do not face the same journey. A DNA sequence does not tell us whether the source organism is alive. A colony assay tells us only about organisms that will grow under the chosen conditions. Different methods illuminate different slices of a low-biomass ecosystem.

The cleanroom also changes the story of contamination from accident to lineage. Once an isolate is cultured, sequenced and archived, it becomes possible to compare a future signal against a terrestrial reference. NASA-associated microbiologists have deposited extreme-tolerant strains from spacecraft assembly into public culture collections for exactly this reason among others. A contamination catalog is a kind of future alibi.

There is something strange about that archive. We preserve the organisms we tried to remove because their identity may later help us prove that we failed.

This is not pessimism. It is good experimental design. In ordinary molecular biology, contamination can often be rerun away. A tube is discarded. A batch is repeated. An extraction blank exposes the intruder. Planetary missions are different. The sample may have taken seven months to reach Mars. The rover may have drilled a rock that cannot be revisited. The contaminant may have shared the instrument's journey for years.

Cleanroom microbiology therefore belongs to the mission record in the same way calibration curves and engineering telemetry do. It describes the prior probability that a terrestrial organism, sequence or organic molecule could appear in a later measurement.

The most revealing organisms are not the average ones. They are the ones that make the cleaning regime look less like a wall and more like an obstacle course.

One of them acquired a name that now follows planetary protection like a small, durable ghost: SAFR-032.
