# A Rock Leaves Mars

We know Mars throws stones at Earth because some of those stones are in our museums.

That fact removes one of the old objections to interplanetary transfer before biology even enters the argument. Large impacts can eject martian material with enough velocity to escape Mars. Orbital dynamics can place fragments on trajectories that eventually intersect Earth. Isotopic and trapped-gas signatures tie a recognized class of meteorites to Mars. Natural exchange of rock is not a thought experiment.

The biological question begins after the geological one.

A microbe inside an ejected rock must survive a launch process violent enough to accelerate the rock beyond escape velocity. It must avoid being heated beyond recovery. It must persist through vacuum and radiation for however long the trajectory takes. It must be shielded enough from solar ultraviolet and energetic particles. It must survive atmospheric entry inside the fragment, then impact or fragmentation, then find conditions that allow repair and growth.

Panspermia arguments often blur these stages because each has a literature that seems encouraging in isolation. Some microbes tolerate high acceleration. Some spores survive shock pressures under particular laboratory conditions. Shielded organisms can persist in space-exposure experiments far better than exposed ones. Meteorite interiors can remain cooler than their fusion-crusted surfaces during atmospheric entry. None of those observations establishes the product of all probabilities.

This is the chain rule of extraordinary biology. If five stages each have a generous ten-percent success probability for the relevant organism and geometry, the combined probability is not fifty percent. It is one in one hundred thousand before ecological establishment is considered. Real probabilities are not known well enough to justify that numerical example as an estimate, but the structure is the point. Every link multiplies.

That is why “possible” is such a dangerous word in panspermia. Demonstrating that one stage is not absolutely lethal can be scientifically important while barely moving the probability of successful transfer. A door unlocked in the laboratory is not evidence that anyone walked through it in nature.

Yet the reverse mistake is equally common. If a microbe dies quickly under direct ultraviolet, one can declare space sterile while ignoring shielding. If a shock experiment kills most cells, one can ignore the surviving tail. Natural transfer, if it occurs, will be dominated by rare favorable geometries and unusually resistant organisms. Planetary protection has exactly the same statistical discomfort. We care about the tail because one survivor may be enough to compromise a measurement.

The two fields therefore share an obsession with boundary cases for opposite reasons. Panspermia researchers ask whether any route remains open. Contamination engineers ask whether every meaningful route has been closed.

A martian meteorite on a laboratory bench proves that one part of the route is real. It does not prove that the passenger survives.

To investigate that passenger, researchers have done something wonderfully blunt: they have put organisms inside rock analogues and hit them.
