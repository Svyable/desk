# Shock Without Death

The phrase “impact ejection” hides the part where a planet is struck hard enough to throw pieces of itself into space.

Laboratory shock experiments try to make that violence measurable. In one set of lithopanspermia experiments, bacterial spores, lichens and cyanobacteria were embedded in different rock types and subjected to pressures ranging into tens of gigapascals. Survival varied strongly with pressure, initial temperature and the nature of the host material. Low-porosity rocks and lower shock pressures were friendlier to life than porous materials and harsher conditions. Some organisms survived some shocks.

That sentence is both remarkable and easy to misuse.

The experiments do not recreate an entire planetary impact. A laboratory pulse has a particular duration and geometry. Natural ejection includes spatially variable heating, fracturing, acceleration, excavation depth and post-shock history. The organisms in an experiment are placed where researchers can recover them. Nature does not arrange its microbes for publication.

Still, shock survival changes the argument. “No cell could survive ejection” is too strong. The relevant question becomes how much habitable rock volume experiences survivable pressure-temperature histories while also acquiring escape velocity.

This is a geology problem married to microbiology. Numerical impact models identify regions of ejecta with different shock histories. Experiments supply biological response curves. The overlap between those two maps is the plausible passenger compartment.

The same habit of reasoning helps with spacecraft contamination. A mission does not need an organism that can tolerate every external condition on Mars. It needs a route from protected hardware to a protected niche. A tiny cavity behind insulation, a crack carrying salt, a shadowed interface or a temporarily wet microenvironment can matter more than the average surface condition.

This is not an argument that Mars is easily contaminated. Present-day Mars is extraordinarily hostile to familiar terrestrial life at the surface. Ultraviolet radiation, desiccation, oxidants, low pressure, cold and limited accessible liquid water are formidable. The point is geometric rather than optimistic: averages are poor descriptions of rare transfer events.

Shock experiments also give the book one of its recurring evidentiary rules. Survival is not flourishing. A spore recovered after a pressure pulse may form a colony when returned to rich terrestrial media. That demonstrates retention of reproductive capacity. It does not show that the same spore would wake in martian regolith, find nutrients, repair accumulated damage and establish a lineage.

The distinction matters because discussions of extremophiles often smuggle laboratory rescue conditions into claims about natural colonization. We should celebrate a survivor without inventing its future.

After ejection comes the part of the journey most people imagine first: open space. Here, direct exposure is often catastrophic. The interesting results appear when even a small amount of material stands between the organism and the Sun.
