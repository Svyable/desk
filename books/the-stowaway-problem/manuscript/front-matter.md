# The Stowaway Problem

*Planetary Protection, Panspermia, and the Life We Bring With Us*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a peculiar kind of laboratory error in which the contaminant is more interesting than the experiment.

NASA has spent decades trying to keep terrestrial microorganisms off spacecraft. The obvious reason is scientific hygiene. If a lander goes looking for life and finds a bacterium that rode from Florida, California, French Guiana, or a supplier's cleanroom, the mission has succeeded mechanically and failed epistemically. There is a second reason, more moral than methodological: another world should not be inoculated casually with ours.

The awkwardness begins when the organisms that survive those precautions become research subjects in their own right. Some make spores. Some tolerate peroxide, ultraviolet radiation, desiccation, starvation, or combinations of insults that kill ordinary laboratory strains. Some have been flown outside the International Space Station to see what survives actual vacuum, radiation, temperature cycling, and solar ultraviolet. Put shielding between the organism and the Sun and the answer changes sharply.

At that point planetary protection runs into panspermia.

Not the romantic version, where life drifts conveniently between stars because life wants to spread. The mechanical version. Can a cell or spore survive launch inside rock? Can it survive shock? Can it survive the trip? Can it survive arrival? Can it encounter a place where survival becomes metabolism rather than mere persistence? Each verb is a separate experimental problem. Rock exchange between planets is not speculative: we hold meteorites from Mars in terrestrial laboratories. Life exchange is another matter.

This book stays inside that distinction. It treats panspermia as a chain whose weakest link matters. It treats patents as patents, policies as policies, and survival experiments as survival experiments. It does not turn a resistant Bacillus spore into a martian immigrant. It does not turn a cleanroom isolate into a proven contaminant on Mars. It does not turn disputed structures in a meteorite into fossils.

The deeper problem is provenance. Once our machines have visited another world, the question “is this life?” is no longer enough. We also have to ask whose life, how it got there, and whether our own attempts to remove it selected for exactly the organisms most capable of confusing us later.

That is the stowaway problem.
