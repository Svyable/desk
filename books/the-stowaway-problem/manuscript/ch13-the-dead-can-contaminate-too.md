# The Dead Can Contaminate Too

Planetary protection often sounds as though its enemy is a living cell capable of colonization. Life detection has a larger enemy set.

A dead organism can still carry DNA. It can still carry lipids, pigments, proteins, cell-wall fragments and isotopic patterns shaped by metabolism. A sterilization procedure that kills every cell may therefore satisfy one biological objective while leaving a molecular crime scene for a sufficiently sensitive instrument.

This is why NASA's description of dry-heat microbial reduction notes a second effect: heating can bake off organic molecules from hardware surfaces. Killing and cleaning are related but not identical operations.

Molecular methods create a second inversion. Culture-based assays miss organisms that do not grow under test conditions. DNA sequencing sees much more, including organisms that are dead. Methods that target RNA, membrane integrity or metabolic activity add information but carry their own assumptions. No single assay answers all three questions: what was here, what is alive, and what could reproduce after transport.

The phrase “viable but nonculturable” is a warning against equating no colonies with no life. Dormancy makes the warning sharper. Spores are supposed to be metabolically quiet and hard to wake accidentally. Other bacteria can enter low-activity states that standard culture conditions fail to reveal. Recent work on cleanroom-associated organisms continues to uncover survival strategies that complicate simple counts.

For life detection, this means the contamination ledger needs layers. Viable burden matters for forward contamination of an environment. Total molecular burden matters for biosignature interpretation. Species identity matters for provenance. Spatial location matters for transport probability.

A spacecraft can therefore be biologically safe in one sense and scientifically dirty in another.

This distinction becomes especially important as instruments move toward exquisite sensitivity. Sensitivity is usually described as pure progress. But every order of magnitude gained at the detector requires another order of seriousness about background. Astronomy learned this with stray light and detector noise. Particle physics learned it with cosmic rays and radioactivity. Astrobiology learns it with skin cells, spores, reagent contaminants and polymer residues.

The alien signal, if it exists, does not arrive alone. It arrives inside the entire measurement system.

The most severe version of that principle occurs when the direction of biological risk reverses and the sample comes back to Earth.
