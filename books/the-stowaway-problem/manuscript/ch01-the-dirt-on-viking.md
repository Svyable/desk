# The Dirt on Viking

Before Viking went to Mars, it had to survive Earth.

The spacecraft was built for a biological question that no previous mission had been permitted to ask so directly. Its landers carried experiments intended to detect signs of metabolism in martian soil. That made every bacterium on the vehicle a potential author of the result. A positive signal could be extraordinary or embarrassing, and the difference might be a few cells hidden in a seam.

So the machine itself became part of the experiment.

Viking hardware was subjected to an unusually severe planetary-protection regime, including dry-heat microbial reduction after assembly. The logic is easy to admire and difficult to practice. Spacecraft are not glass Petri dishes. They are assemblies of electronics, lubricants, seals, adhesives, wiring, sensors, fasteners, cavities and materials with different tolerances. Sterilization is never merely a biological procedure. It is an engineering negotiation with the object you are trying not to destroy.

This is the first useful correction to the way people talk about clean spacecraft. “Sterile” sounds binary. Flight hardware is not. Planetary protection is an exercise in bounding probability: reduce the biological burden, characterize what remains, protect sensitive destinations and preserve the interpretability of measurements. The details change by mission and destination because the consequences change.

NASA's current planetary-protection material still contains the old practical wisdom. Dry heat can kill organisms on surfaces and within some hardware, but not every component tolerates it. Hydrogen peroxide, ultraviolet light and other approaches each come with limits. Alcohol wiping is useful, yet NASA explicitly notes that alcohol does not generally destroy bacterial spores; wiping removes them mechanically. A worker can therefore clean a surface without having sterilized the organisms that matter most to the hardest cases.

That distinction sounds small until a mission's scientific claim depends on it.

Viking is usually remembered for the arguments over its biology experiments: what the labeled-release response meant, what oxidants in martian soil could do, why later discoveries of perchlorates complicated retrospective interpretation, whether the instruments were asking the right biochemical questions. Less often remembered is the other experiment running underneath all of them: whether humans could deliver a complicated machine to another planet while keeping its terrestrial biology below a defensible threshold.

The lander had to be clean enough not only to protect Mars, but to protect the meaning of Mars.

That is why planetary protection belongs inside astrobiology rather than beside it. Contamination is not a facilities problem delegated to people in coveralls while the “real” scientists think about life. A contamination model is part of the hypothesis test. If the background is unknown, the signal is not interpretable.

This principle becomes more severe as instruments become more sensitive. A crude detector may miss both alien life and terrestrial dirt. A modern molecular instrument can detect astonishingly small traces, which is an achievement until one remembers that a spacecraft arrives with a manufacturing history. Every component has been handled, transported, tested, cleaned, exposed to air, assembled beside other components and enclosed in volumes that may later become inaccessible.

The fantasy of perfect cleanliness is therefore dangerous in two directions. It can make the public imagine that any terrestrial organism found later must have arrived independently, and it can make scientists underestimate the evidentiary work required to establish provenance. The honest version is harder and more interesting. We launch controlled biological histories.

Viking's heat treatment was not a ritual inherited from germ theory. It was an epistemic device. The cleaner the spacecraft, the more meaningful an unexpected biological result could become. But the same history also reveals the limit: there is no useful planetary-protection policy without knowing which organisms resist the policy.

That is where the book changes scale. We leave Mars for a floor in a cleanroom.
