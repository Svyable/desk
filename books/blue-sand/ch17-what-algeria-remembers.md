# What Algeria Remembers

English-language accounts of French nuclear testing in Algeria often cite French sources discussing Algerian victims. That is not the same thing as reading Algerian sources.

The difference is not cosmetic.

Algerian historians, legal scholars, local associations and witnesses have built their own archive around Reggane and In Ekker. Much of it appears in Arabic, some in French, some in bilingual journals and conference proceedings. The research is uneven. So is every national literature on a politically charged subject. What it contains, however, is a set of questions that French institutional histories do not naturally ask.

What did the tests look like from a nearby community rather than a command post? What happened to local laborers after the French facilities closed? Which illnesses became part of family memory? How did residents interpret strange objects, restricted areas and warnings? What stories were preserved because formal medical documentation was absent? How did the tests enter Algeria’s broader memory of colonial rule?

These are not secondary questions simply because they are harder to quantify.

One Algerian study preserved testimony from a man who had worked around the Reggane center and used that account to examine health and psychological effects. Other papers trace the legal argument over French responsibility, environmental contamination, waste and archival access. Local organizations have spent years collecting testimonies and pressing for recognition.

The language can be categorical. “Crime,” “nuclear crime,” “barbarity” and “colonial aggression” appear frequently. A researcher trained in an Anglophone scientific style may instinctively lower confidence when the conclusion seems announced before the methods. That instinct can protect against advocacy masquerading as measurement. It can also become a lazy excuse not to read the source.

The correct move is to disaggregate.

A legal article may overstate epidemiological evidence and still accurately identify the date of a French parliamentary admission. A witness account may be unable to prove radiation causation and still accurately describe a work task. An Algerian newspaper may use politicized language while reporting a verifiable government demand for waste maps. A French ministry can use measured language while selecting facts favorable to the state.

Tone is not evidence quality.

This book’s source log therefore treats Algerian material by proposition. If a paper claims 150,000 victims, the number must be traced to its underlying source before use. If it reproduces a named testimony, that testimony is logged separately. If it cites a French declassified document, the French document becomes the terminal source for the factual claim. If it preserves local terminology or memory not available elsewhere, the Algerian source is primary evidence for that cultural history.

This approach produces a more interesting picture than choosing sides.

One recurring Algerian demand is for complete disclosure of maps and archives concerning test locations, contamination and buried waste. That demand is independently visible in French parliamentary questions and answers, which confirms that the issue is not invented by local rhetoric. Another recurring claim concerns persistent health effects across generations. That claim has a much weaker evidentiary basis at the population level and requires dedicated epidemiology.

The distinction strengthens rather than weakens the Algerian case.

A government should not need to prove a multigenerational cancer catastrophe before receiving location data necessary to survey radioactive waste on its own territory.

Conflating those issues allows the stronger transparency claim to be dragged down by the weaker epidemiological one.

The Algerian literature also changes the geography of the story. French military accounts naturally center CSEM, CEMO, shot points, tunnels and instrument stations. Algerian accounts name regions, oases, communities and routes. They make visible a social map that technical reports compress into distance rings.

That map should be integrated, not appended.

A future research pass should geocode every named locality appearing in Algerian testimony, then layer it against known fallout contours, test dates and official monitoring stations. This would not prove disease causation. It would show where memory and measurement overlap.

Or fail to overlap.

A surprising mismatch could be as informative as confirmation. If a community with intense nuclear memory lies outside modeled significant fallout for the event it remembers, that may indicate another exposure pathway, inaccurate recollection, later contamination, or the cultural spread of a regional trauma. Each explanation can be investigated.

Translation itself needs care. Arabic terms for explosion, irradiation, contamination, victimhood and martyrdom carry political and cultural associations that can be flattened in English. French colonial administrative names may differ from current Algerian place names. Personal names can be transliterated multiple ways. A source log should retain original-script names wherever possible so future researchers can find the material again.

This sounds like bibliographic housekeeping. It is actually a barrier to discovery.

An English researcher searching only “Reggane nuclear tests health” will miss papers whose metadata and titles are primarily Arabic. A person searching a French transliteration may miss an Arabic spelling. Knowledge does not become globally searchable merely because it is online.

The user’s request to surface what English speakers miss is therefore not satisfied by adding foreign color. It requires building cross-language retrieval infrastructure inside the research notes.

For this book, that means every Arabic source should carry its original title, transliteration where useful, English gloss, author affiliation, publication venue, URL and the specific claim for which it is evidence. Strong claims should be checked against another source whenever possible.

Algerian testimony also carries a temporal urgency. People who experienced the tests as adults are now elderly or dead. Oral history can no longer wait for perfect archival conditions. Interviews should be recorded with dates, locations, names, roles and explicit separation between what the witness personally experienced and what they later heard.

That last distinction is critical.

Collective memory can absorb published narratives. A witness interviewed in 2026 may know facts about Gerboise Bleue from television that he did not know in 1960. Good oral history asks: what did you see then? What were you told then? What did you learn later?

The same question should be asked of French veterans.

Memory is not weaker because it is Algerian. It is human evidence and should be handled with human rigor.

France kept the technical archive because France ran the program.

Algeria kept another archive because people had to live where the program had been.

The history gets better when they are finally allowed to speak on the same page.
