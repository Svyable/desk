# When the IAEA Came Back

In 1999, at Algeria’s request, the International Atomic Energy Agency undertook a radiological assessment of the former French nuclear test sites. The resulting preliminary assessment was published in 2005.

The dates matter almost as much as the measurements.

Nearly forty years separated the end of French testing in the Sahara from the international assessment. That delay changes what an inspection can discover. Persistent radionuclides remain. Hotspots remain. Contaminated debris can remain. Short-lived radionuclides are gone. Workers are gone. Original land-use patterns have changed. An inspection can measure the present and infer parts of the past, but it cannot recreate the test range as it existed in 1960.

This is why the IAEA report should be read neither as a final verdict nor as an irrelevant late survey.

It is a bridge document.

The Agency’s role also changes the evidentiary ecology. French state records are interested-party records even when technically excellent. Algerian government claims are also politically situated. The IAEA brings an external technical institution with established radiological methods. That does not make every conclusion infallible. It makes the methods and institutional incentives different.

The assessment examined former sites at Reggane and In Ekker and provided recommendations to the Algerian government. The report’s very title—“preliminary assessment and recommendations”—should restrain anyone who wants it to close the case.

Preliminary means what it says.

The most useful feature of a radiological site assessment is its granularity. Instead of asking whether “the Sahara” is contaminated, investigators identify specific locations, radionuclides and pathways. External dose rates can be measured. Samples can be analyzed by isotope. Hot particles or contaminated fused material can be distinguished from broad background. Areas can be categorized by whether access controls, warnings or remediation are warranted.

This is what mature environmental science looks like after political language has worn itself out.

A community may describe a landscape as poisoned. A ministry may describe it as safe. A survey meter produces a spatial distribution.

The distribution rarely flatters either slogan.

Former nuclear test sites often contain highly uneven contamination. Near a detonation point, vitrified material or fused rock can retain radionuclides. At underground sites, material may remain largely trapped inside cavities while portal areas or ejected debris show different signatures. Atmospheric fallout creates another pattern entirely. Waste burial adds point sources that may have little relationship to original plume geometry.

The IAEA assessment therefore belongs in the manuscript as a site atlas, not a citation used once to prove safety or danger.

For each area it examined, the book should eventually extract measured radionuclides, activity concentrations, dose-rate findings, estimated potential exposure pathways and recommended controls. If the report says a location presents a hazard mainly if material is handled, that is different from saying nearby residents receive a continuing high whole-body dose. If it recommends restricting access, that is stronger than vague concern.

This distinction matters especially in public communication. Radiation frightens because its scale is difficult to intuit. A reading can be above background and still imply a small annual dose. A tiny object can have high contact dose and matter greatly if carried away. The word contaminated covers both.

The IAEA mission also depended on information France supplied. French authorities provided technical material about test locations, methods and earlier radiological conditions. Parliamentary answers later pointed to this cooperation. That is significant: the assessment was not an independent rediscovery of an unknown site. It used state knowledge to guide modern measurement.

Which brings us back to the archive.

If France possessed enough historical information to support the IAEA assessment, what portion of that underlying material is public? Can Algerian researchers access the same source documents? Are burial locations included? Does the IAEA report summarize information that remains more detailed in French files?

These are not accusations. They are document questions.

The mission’s timing also intersects with another political development: the gradual emergence of nuclear-test veterans as a recognized constituency in France. During the 1990s and 2000s, public attention to the health effects of French testing increased. The state published more historical material. Parliamentary inquiries examined consequences. Associations pressed for compensation. The Saharan sites were no longer merely old military geography.

Scientific reassessment and political reassessment moved together.

This can create suspicion that technical work is driven by scandal. Sometimes it is. That does not invalidate the measurement. Environmental health research often begins because affected people refuse to stop asking.

The IAEA report is also useful for testing claims in Algerian scholarship. If an article says contamination persists at a particular site, the assessment can provide independent technical support or contradiction. If a testimony describes people collecting particular materials, the report may show whether those materials plausibly contained hazardous radionuclides. If a legal paper claims the entire region remains lethally irradiated, measured dose rates can bound that language.

This is where bringing non-English sources into the book adds value rather than exoticism.

The point is not to prove that Algerian sources contain a hidden truth unavailable in French. It is to let local claims meet international measurement instead of being filtered only through French debate.

A future field-research phase could go further. Since 2005, instrumentation has improved, satellite positioning is routine, and two more decades of site use have passed. A modern joint survey could revisit IAEA coordinates, quantify changes, map unsurveyed areas and publish geospatial data in French, Arabic and English.

That would transform the argument.

Instead of debating whether France has provided “enough” information in diplomatic language, both governments could anchor claims to shared measurements. Historical accountability would remain contested, but present hazard could be separated from memory.

There is no technical reason a public radiological map of known hazard areas could not exist, subject to genuine security constraints.

The obstacle is institutional will.

The IAEA assessment therefore represents a partial answer to the title question of the book. Sand can be measured. Rocks can be sampled. Contaminated objects can be found. The unseen can be made visible.

What cannot be reconstructed so easily is the dose of a person who stood in the wrong place in 1962 and was never adequately monitored.

Environmental truth survives longer than human truth.

That is both the promise and the limitation of going back.
