# Into the Mountain

The appeal of underground nuclear testing can be stated in one sentence: let the rock hold what the atmosphere cannot.

After four atmospheric shots at Reggane, France shifted the center of Saharan testing to In Ekker, near the granite massif of Taourirt Tan Afella in the Hoggar. The engineering idea was different from the tower and surface shots of the first series. Tunnels would be driven horizontally into the mountain. A nuclear device would be placed deep inside. The gallery behind it would be sealed with a sequence of barriers intended to absorb shock and prevent radioactive gases and debris from escaping.

If the system worked, much of the radioactive inventory would remain trapped underground.

This was not an exotic French theory. Underground testing became the dominant model for nuclear powers once atmospheric fallout became politically and scientifically intolerable. The Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963 would prohibit nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, outer space and underwater for parties to the treaty while leaving underground testing available so long as radioactive debris did not cross national borders. The technology of containment therefore became part of the political technology of the nuclear age.

A mountain, however, is not a pressure vessel manufactured to specification.

Rock contains joints, faults, weathered zones and variations in strength. Explosive energy can open pathways that did not exist at useful scale before the shot. A tunnel is itself a pathway that must be deliberately defeated with plugs and stemming. Gas pressure and shock arrive faster than most intuitive accounts of “burying” a bomb suggest. The containment design must be correct not only in general but for the specific device yield, cavity geometry, geology and construction quality.

The French system at In Ekker used galleries with characteristic bends and engineered closures. Public technical accounts differ in detail, and some descriptions have been repeated so often that secondary diagrams can look more authoritative than the surviving primary record. The safe way to describe the design is functional rather than decorative: the tunnel was meant to prevent direct line-of-sight propagation and to close the route to the surface before radioactive material could escape.

The first underground shot, Agate, took place on November 7, 1961. It demonstrated that the new site could be used. The program then entered an awkward historical period. Algeria was moving toward independence while France’s nuclear testing infrastructure remained physically embedded in Algerian territory.

The Evian Accords and associated arrangements permitted continued French use of certain military facilities for a limited period after independence. The nuclear test sites were among the strategic assets affected by that transition. Algeria became independent in July 1962. French nuclear testing in the Sahara continued until 1966.

This fact is often deployed as if it settles the politics by itself. It does not.

One version says: France continued colonial nuclear testing after Algeria became independent, therefore nothing meaningful changed. Another says: independent Algeria agreed to continued French use, therefore the later tests cannot be called colonial. Both compress a negotiation between profoundly unequal parties at the end of a brutal war into a single legal adjective.

The documents require a more exact account. The legal authority changed. The infrastructure did not vanish. France retained access under negotiated arrangements. Algeria had formal sovereignty but inherited a relationship in which France already possessed the test complex, the technical personnel, the bomb program and the records. That is neither identical to pre-independence rule nor an ordinary arms-length lease.

In Ekker makes this transitional sovereignty visible because the mountain itself became a machine France would use for four more years.

The move underground also changed who could see risk. An atmospheric test announces itself. The cloud is visible. Fallout monitoring is expected. An underground test can be narrated as contained unless evidence says otherwise. Risk moves from obvious release to confidence in engineering.

That confidence is fragile because containment has a binary public image and a continuous technical reality.

A shot can be fully contained. It can vent small quantities of gas. It can leak through fractures. It can eject contaminated rock. It can expose workers near the portal while producing little wider public dose. Different releases can have different radiological significance. To call all of them “accidents” without scale is imprecise; to call any release trivial because most radioactivity remained underground is equally unsatisfactory.

French parliamentary reporting has acknowledged that at least four In Ekker shots were not completely contained: Béryl, Améthyste, Rubis and Jade. The wording matters. “Not completely contained” is an engineering statement. The health consequences must be investigated separately.

The second underground shot would demonstrate why.

Before Béryl, the tunnel system offered a promise: modern nuclear testing could continue without sending the experiment into the atmosphere. After Béryl, everyone involved knew that a mountain could fail as part of the apparatus.

That knowledge should have changed procedure. The questions for the archive are concrete. What did the post-accident investigation identify as the failure mechanism? Were tunnel geometry, stemming, yield estimates or firing criteria changed? Did personnel stand-off distances increase? Were protective requirements altered? How were later workers briefed? What new monitoring was added? Which recommendations were implemented, and how quickly?

These are better questions than “did officials know underground testing was dangerous?” Of course they knew there were hazards; otherwise no containment system would have existed. The historical issue is what they learned from failure and whether operations incorporated that knowledge.

This is where the archive can often outperform retrospective rhetoric. Safety systems leave paperwork because they are systems. A changed procedure has a date. A new exclusion distance has a number. A memo has recipients. A technical report names a failure mode. If a later accident repeats a known mechanism, the chronology becomes evidence of institutional learning or its absence.

In Ekker also complicates the common moral contrast between spectacular accidents and routine operations. Béryl is remembered because cameras captured the plume and senior officials were present. Yet routine work may produce cumulative exposure that is less visible: tunnel re-entry, contaminated equipment, dust, decontamination, sample handling, waste burial, vehicle maintenance. Occupational health history repeatedly teaches the same lesson. The cinematic event is not always the largest source of dose.

That possibility has to be tested rather than assumed. Personnel dosimetry, task assignments and contamination records are essential. The French veteran population includes people with very different jobs and durations. A minister visiting for a shot is not the same exposure category as a worker repeatedly entering contaminated zones. A local laborer without preserved dosimetry is not the same evidentiary problem as a monitored technician.

A good compensation system should understand those distinctions. A good historical account must first recover them.

The mountain was supposed to simplify the environmental problem by containing the radioactive source. Instead, it transformed the problem into one of engineering confidence, occupational practice and invisible failure.

The test was no longer above the ground where everyone could see it.

The evidence had gone inside too.
