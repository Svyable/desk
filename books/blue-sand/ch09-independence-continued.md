# Independence, Continued

Algeria became independent on July 5, 1962. French nuclear testing in the Algerian Sahara continued until February 1966.

Those two sentences are frequently placed beside each other as if they explain themselves. They do not.

The continuation of testing after independence emerged from the military provisions surrounding the Evian settlement. France retained temporary use of specified facilities in Algeria, including installations connected to nuclear experimentation. The arrangement reflected the practical reality of decolonization: sovereignty could transfer in a day; bases, tunnels, weapons programs and technical organizations could not.

The legal transition matters because careless language can obscure rather than clarify. Tests before independence occurred under French colonial rule. Tests after independence occurred on the territory of a sovereign Algeria under negotiated arrangements. Calling the entire 1960–1966 program legally identical erases that change. Treating the post-1962 program as an ordinary consensual contract between equal states erases the conditions under which the agreement was made.

The end of a war is not the beginning of symmetrical bargaining.

France entered the negotiations with an established test infrastructure, a functioning weapons program and a strong interest in avoiding a pause while new Pacific facilities were prepared. Algeria emerged from a war that had killed, displaced and traumatized enormous numbers of people and had destroyed much of the ordinary basis of governance. The post-independence test period sits inside that imbalance.

This is a place where the documentary record should discipline moral intuition. What exactly did the Evian arrangements say? Which sites were covered? For how long? What obligations existed regarding safety, access, cleanup and return? What did Algerian officials know about prior contamination and the Béryl accident? What information did France provide? What did later Algerian governments request and receive?

The answers should be reconstructed from the agreements and correspondence, not guessed from the political context.

The chronology nevertheless tells us something important about infrastructure. France had not yet completed the transition to its Pacific test center. The Saharan sites were strategically valuable because they already existed. A weapons program operates on schedules: device development, diagnostic equipment, trained crews, transport and political commitments. Abandoning a site immediately could interrupt that sequence.

This creates what might be called infrastructural leverage. Once a state has built a specialized facility on contested or dependent territory, the facility itself becomes an argument for continued access. The sunk cost is not merely financial. It is organizational knowledge.

In Ekker was a mountain full of work already done.

That fact does not prove coercion in a legal sense. It explains why the French state had a strong incentive to preserve access and why Algeria had to negotiate over something more consequential than empty land.

The post-independence shots also complicate responsibility for later site conditions. France designed and operated the test program. Algeria eventually received the sites. If contaminated material remained, questions of remediation cross the sovereignty boundary. A French official can argue that facilities were dismantled and returned according to agreement. An Algerian official can ask whether the returning state disclosed every contaminated burial location and hazard. Both propositions can be true until the inventory is complete.

This is one reason maps matter so much in present-day diplomacy.

A general acknowledgment that waste exists is not equivalent to coordinates. A statement that sites were surveyed is not equivalent to a list of every burial trench, contaminated vehicle or fused material zone. Cleanup is a geospatial problem. Without location data, sovereignty transfers responsibility without transferring knowledge.

French parliamentary answers in recent years have stated that installations were dismantled and the sites retroceded to Algeria and have cited the later IAEA assessment. Algerian officials and researchers continue to demand fuller information about radioactive and non-radioactive waste. The persistence of this dispute indicates that “retroceded” is an administrative endpoint, not an epistemic one.

The post-independence period is also where national memory splits most sharply.

In French strategic history, the Sahara is often the first stage before the Pacific: an early test phase that enabled the force de frappe and ended when facilities shifted to Mururoa and Fangataufa. In Algerian memory, the fact that tests continued after the flag changed can look like evidence that colonial power survived formal independence in military form.

Each narrative selects a different transition.

France marks the transition from atmospheric to underground testing and from Sahara to Pacific. Algeria marks the transition from colony to sovereign state and asks why the explosions did not stop there.

A book can hold both timelines at once.

The final Saharan shot took place in February 1966. The first French atmospheric test in Polynesia followed that July. The handoff was operationally tight. France did not abandon nuclear testing; it relocated it.

This relocation would reproduce several arguments familiar from Algeria: remoteness, strategic necessity, safety assurances, monitoring, later revelations, health disputes, compensation claims and demands for recognition. The geography changed from desert to atoll. The institutional logic traveled.

That continuity is one reason the Algerian and Polynesian histories should be compared without collapsing them. The exposure pathways, populations and political statuses differed. Yet both show the same structural problem: the nuclear state controlled the instrument, the measurements and much of the archive, while affected populations spent decades trying to reconstruct what those measurements meant for them.

Independence ended French rule in Algeria.

It did not instantly end French nuclear access to Algerian ground.

History is usually messiest in the gap between those two facts.
