# People Inside the Perimeter

Nuclear-test histories tend to organize people by nationality or status: French soldiers, French scientists, Algerian workers, local residents, nomads, ministers, veterans. Radiation does not recognize those categories. Archives do.

That difference matters.

A person inside a test complex could be exposed through several pathways: prompt radiation near an event, external gamma radiation from contaminated ground or equipment, inhalation of dust and aerosols, ingestion of contaminated material, or direct handling of radioactive samples. A person farther away could encounter fallout through air, food, water or resuspended dust. Job, location and timing mattered more to dose than citizenship.

But citizenship and employment status mattered enormously to whether the dose was measured and whether the measurement survived.

French military personnel were part of an institution with rosters, medical services and dosimetry procedures. Those systems were imperfect, and not every exposure was captured. But the structure existed. A film badge can be lost; a roster can omit a task; a dose limit can later look inadequate. Still, the existence of a personnel system creates evidence.

For Algerian local workers, the documentary trail can be thinner. Some worked within or near French facilities. Later testimony collected by Algerian researchers describes men performing support labor without understanding the radiological significance of the environment. These accounts are important and must be checked individually against surviving work records where possible. The category “local worker” is too broad to stand in for exposure. A driver, laborer, cook, guard and tunnel worker did different things.

The distinction between measured and unmeasured people can create a cruel retrospective paradox.

The person whose exposure was best managed is also the person most capable of proving it.

A monitored French technician may have a badge record, a unit assignment and an event history. An Algerian worker performing dirtier work without equivalent records may later have only memory and geography. Compensation systems built around documentation can therefore reward institutional inclusion after the original institution distributed protection unevenly.

This is not unique to nuclear testing. Occupational disease history is full of the same pattern. Workers with company medical files can sometimes prove exposure that informal laborers experienced more intensely but documented less well. Recordkeeping is not merely administration; it becomes part of the later distribution of justice.

The Saharan program also included conscripts. A young French soldier could arrive with little understanding of nuclear weapons and leave months later with no expectation that his service would become medically relevant decades afterward. Veterans’ associations later became important in forcing public attention to the testing program and compensation. Their testimony expanded the public archive, but veteran memory has the same limits as any retrospective account. Details merge. Distances are estimated. Code names are remembered incorrectly. Illness can reshape recollection.

The correct response is corroboration, not contempt.

A witness who says he was ordered into a contaminated area after a shot provides a research lead. Unit logs, duty rosters, photographs, test schedules, vehicle records and contamination-control orders can test it. If corroborated, the testimony gains specificity. If not corroborated, it remains testimony rather than becoming fact by repetition.

Local Algerian testimony deserves the same method.

Several Algerian academic articles preserve accounts from people who worked around Reggane or lived in affected regions. These sources are especially valuable because the English-language literature often reduces Algerians to an undifferentiated “local population.” Names, occupations, family movements and remembered instructions restore agency. They also reveal how nuclear memory is transmitted through communities whose official medical follow-up was limited.

The political language surrounding those testimonies can be forceful. French testing is often described in Algerian scholarship as a colonial crime or crime against humanity. Those legal conclusions require their own analysis. They should not be used to discount the empirical content of an interview any more than patriotic language in a French military memoir invalidates its dates.

Source criticism is not ideological sorting.

The strongest human history of the test sites will therefore resist representative characters invented from composites. Nuclear narratives often create a generic soldier, a generic Tuareg, a generic scientist. That technique makes prose smooth and evidence weak. Better to follow people whose records exist and say when they stop existing.

A named worker with a documented job can carry ten pages. A compensation claimant with medical and service records can show how historical exposure becomes an administrative case. A scientist’s notebook can reveal what was measured in real time. A local witness can show what official warning looked like from outside the command structure.

The perimeter itself should be treated as a changing social boundary.

Before a shot, it defined who was allowed near the device. After a shot, contamination could make new spaces restricted. Workers then crossed those boundaries to recover instruments, sample debris, decontaminate equipment and restore operations. Some of the highest occupational exposures in test programs can occur not during the detonation but during cleanup and re-entry.

This is why shot-day photographs are incomplete evidence of worker risk.

The critical question may be who went back the next morning.

For each test, the research appendix should eventually identify post-shot tasks: radiological survey teams, tunnel inspectors, instrument recovery, sample collection, transport, maintenance, decontamination and waste handling. The hazard profile of each task differs. External dose can be monitored with badges. Internal contamination requires different measurements. Protective equipment matters. So does time spent in the zone.

A state can comply with its own dose limits and still produce a later political scandal if the limits excluded pathways that mattered or if different worker classes were not monitored equally.

This brings the analysis back to information.

What were workers told?

The answer cannot be reconstructed from modern informed-consent standards. Military personnel accept occupational risks under command. Workers accept hazards as part of employment. The relevant historical question is whether people received information necessary to follow protective instructions and whether known hazards were concealed in ways that increased exposure.

A person does not need a lecture in radiobiology to be warned not to pick up contaminated metal. A local worker does not need to understand isotope half-lives to be told why a vehicle requires decontamination. A soldier does not need to know weapon design to understand that a film badge should be worn.

Warnings are operational artifacts. They can be found.

Orders, signs, manuals, training material, safety briefings and witness accounts can establish what different groups were expected to know. If French technical staff received detailed radiological instructions while local laborers received only task commands, that difference would be meaningful. If equivalent instructions existed, that would complicate the exploitation narrative.

The book should be capable of finding either result.

The people inside the perimeter were not one population.

That is exactly why “the victims of the tests” is too blunt a category for investigation, even when it is understandable as political language.

To discover who bore risk, we have to put people back into jobs, dates and places.

The archive classified them long before history did.
