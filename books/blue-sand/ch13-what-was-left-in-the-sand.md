# What Was Left in the Sand

A nuclear test ends quickly. A test site does not.

After the flash, blast and measurements come the material problems: contaminated soil, fused rock, cables, filters, protective clothing, vehicles, structures, tools, sample containers, debris and equipment that may have been deliberately exposed to radiation or fallout. Some items can be decontaminated. Some can be removed. Some are buried. Some remain too hot, too large or too inconvenient to recover economically.

Decades later, this is where the French-Algerian dispute becomes literal.

Where is the waste?

French parliamentary answers have stated that the Saharan installations were dismantled and the sites returned to Algerian authorities under the post-independence arrangements. They have also pointed to information supplied to the International Atomic Energy Agency and to radiological assessments conducted around the former test areas. Algerian officials, parliamentarians, researchers and antinuclear organizations have continued to demand fuller maps of burial locations and contaminated materials.

The disagreement is not semantic. A cleanup crew needs coordinates.

Radiological risk from abandoned test infrastructure is highly nonuniform. A broad survey can find generally low environmental levels while missing a small object or burial point with much higher activity. Scrap-metal scavenging makes this especially important. Material that is harmless at a distance can become significant if somebody picks it up, transports it, sleeps beside it, cuts it, melts it or incorporates it into another object.

This is one reason old test sites create a problem different from fallout.

Fallout is dispersed. Waste can be concentrated.

A concentration problem requires an inventory, and an inventory is only as good as the records created during dismantlement. What was removed? What was buried? Where? What was judged clean enough to leave? Which standards were used? Were locations surveyed after burial? Were markers installed? Did those markers survive?

The French military had reasons to keep such records, but whether complete maps exist publicly is another matter. The post-test program had an operational incentive to know where dangerous zones were so future personnel would not stumble into them. Dismantlement creates its own documentation: work orders, radiological-release surveys, transport manifests, burial plans and handover records.

Those are among the highest-value targets for archival research.

The question of waste also exposes a common failure in nuclear memory: attention sticks to the explosion because the explosion is visually overwhelming. Waste is dull. It is administrative. It often becomes politically important only after the strategic program has moved elsewhere.

At Reggane and In Ekker, the French weapons program departed. Algeria remained.

The IAEA’s preliminary radiological assessment, published in 2005, is a critical source because it approached the former sites as places requiring contemporary measurement rather than historical rhetoric. Its recommendations distinguish areas and hazards rather than treating “the Sahara” as one contamination zone. That geographic specificity should discipline both alarm and reassurance.

If an assessment finds localized radiological hazards, it does not mean the entire region is dangerously contaminated. If broad areas show modest levels, it does not make concentrated remnants irrelevant.

The right unit is the object or location.

This becomes especially important in stories about contaminated metal. Across the history of nuclear and industrial sites, abandoned sources and activated or contaminated scrap have caused exposures precisely because later users did not know what they were handling. The danger is not mystical persistence in “irradiated land.” It is physical material with measurable radionuclides and exposure pathways.

A responsible account of Saharan waste should therefore avoid the language of poisoned desert unless a specific environmental finding supports it. Sand is not a moral category. Some places can be substantially contaminated, some mildly affected, some near background. The task is mapping.

Algerian demands for French waste maps have another dimension: sovereignty over information.

A state may own the land yet still depend on a former colonial power to know what is beneath it. This is a peculiar continuation of technical dependency. France designed the experiments and controlled the documentation. Algeria inherited the site. If the archive needed to remediate the land remains incomplete or inaccessible, decolonization is unfinished at the level of hazard knowledge.

That claim is stronger when framed practically than rhetorically.

No conspiracy is required. Records can remain classified because they contain weapons information. They can be scattered across agencies. Some may have been destroyed under retention schedules. Locations can be recorded imprecisely by modern geospatial standards. What matters is whether the state holding the best surviving information has made a serious effort to identify and transmit what is necessary for safety.

This creates a testable standard for transparency.

France need not release weapon-design secrets to provide burial coordinates for contaminated debris. If a record mixes sensitive and safety-relevant information, it can be redacted. If uncertainty remains, joint survey teams can locate anomalies. If no map exists, saying so clearly would itself narrow the dispute.

The waste question is one place where “we cannot know” is not an acceptable endpoint, because instruments can still answer part of it.

Gamma surveys, soil sampling, remote sensing, ground searches, spectrometry and site-specific excavation can locate persistent contamination. The task is expensive and difficult across enormous terrain, but it is not metaphysical. Modern radiological remediation has a mature technical vocabulary.

The political obstacle is deciding who pays, who leads, who owns the data and what level of cleanup is required.

Those decisions are inseparable from history.

If France frames the sites as old military facilities transferred according to agreement, cleanup can look like an Algerian domestic responsibility supported by historical cooperation. If Algeria frames them as the residue of colonial nuclear experimentation, cleanup becomes a French obligation of repair. Technical teams may agree on becquerels while governments disagree on verbs.

A book cannot resolve that diplomatic question by moral assertion. It can reconstruct the chain of custody.

Who created each class of waste? Who buried it? What handover documents exist? What did the agreements say? What did the 1966–1967 French radiological state of the sites record? What did the IAEA measure three decades later? What areas remain unsurveyed? Which hazards are documented now rather than inferred from memory?

This is the kind of hidden history in which the astonishing document may be a dull table.

A line item can be more important than a speech: vehicle number, burial pit, isotope, activity, coordinate.

The bomb is the event everyone photographs.

The ledger is what the grandchildren need.
