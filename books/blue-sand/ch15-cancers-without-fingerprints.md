# Cancers Without Fingerprints

Radiation history invites a kind of certainty biology does not provide.

A person develops leukemia, thyroid cancer, lung cancer or another disease decades after exposure. The exposure is real. The disease is real. The causal sentence between them may still be probabilistic.

Ionizing radiation can cause cancer. That is not controversial. Risk depends on dose, dose rate, tissue, age, sex, radiation type and other variables. For some exposures and diseases, epidemiological evidence is strong enough to estimate excess risk across populations. The difficulty appears when history asks about one person whose dose is poorly known.

There is usually no molecular stamp reading: this tumor was caused by Gerboise Bleue.

That fact has been used in two opposite ways.

Institutions can demand impossible individual proof and then treat its absence as evidence that compensation is unwarranted. Activists can answer by treating any compatible disease after any plausible exposure as proven radiation injury. Both responses arise from the same uncomfortable feature of stochastic effects: causation is real at the population level and uncertain at the individual level.

The Saharan tests are an unusually difficult epidemiological problem because exposure reconstruction is fragmented.

For French military personnel, service records may identify location and dates, but individual dosimetry can be incomplete or may capture external dose better than internal exposure. For Algerian workers, records may be scarcer. For surrounding populations, precise residence histories, food pathways and contemporaneous environmental measurements are uneven. The relevant groups are relatively small compared with national cancer registries, and medical surveillance in southern Algeria during the period was not designed as a long-term nuclear cohort study.

This means claims about regional cancer burdens require unusual caution.

Algerian publications and media reports frequently describe elevated cancers, congenital anomalies and chronic disease around Reggane and In Ekker. Some include striking victim totals. Many of these claims express genuine local experience and deserve investigation. The problem is denominator and design. How many people were at risk? How were cases identified? What is the expected rate? Were diagnoses verified? Did the population change through migration? What other exposures matter? How does disease vary with distance or modeled fallout?

Without answers, a number may be testimony about suffering rather than epidemiological evidence.

French official skepticism can be equally misleading if it asks a small, poorly documented population to produce a statistically powerful signal decades after the event. An underpowered study cannot convert uncertainty into reassurance.

The right scientific posture is to separate several questions.

First: were people exposed to ionizing radiation because of the tests? Yes, in at least some occupational and environmental contexts; otherwise there would be no radiological protection system and no basis for compensation law.

Second: did some exposures reach levels capable of increasing cancer risk? For particular events and individuals, this is plausible and in some documented cases measurable. The range must be established from dosimetry and reconstruction rather than inferred from proximity alone.

Third: can every later illness among veterans or residents be attributed to the tests? No.

Fourth: can failure to attribute every illness be used to conclude that no excess disease occurred? Also no.

These propositions should sit beside one another in the manuscript.

The best way forward is cohort reconstruction.

For military personnel, a roster-based study can identify who served at which site, when, in what unit and in what job. Linkage to cancer and mortality registries can then compare outcomes by exposure category. Such studies are methodologically difficult because veterans differ from general populations and because dose data may be missing, but they can still provide more information than collections of anecdotes.

For Algerian populations, the challenge is greater. Historical population records, migration and cancer ascertainment may be incomplete. A geographic ecological study risks attributing to radiation any disease difference between regions. Individual-level case-control designs could be more informative if residence histories and exposure models can be reconstructed. Family testimony can help locate people in time and space even when it cannot diagnose causation.

The waste problem adds a second exposure era.

People could have been exposed during active testing and later through contaminated objects or sites. Those pathways should not be combined automatically. A person born after 1966 cannot have received test-day fallout directly but could encounter residual contamination. Different hypotheses require different evidence.

This distinction is often lost in political language that treats “the effects of the tests” as one continuous exposure.

The book should also be explicit about hereditary claims. Radiation can damage germ cells at sufficient dose, but dramatic claims of multigenerational genetic catastrophe require rigorous evidence. Human hereditary effects from radiation have been difficult to demonstrate at the scale often imagined in popular culture. Congenital anomalies around a former test site therefore cannot be attributed by intuition alone. Case definitions, parental dose and comparison rates matter.

Saying this may sound cold beside a family caring for a child with a birth defect. It is the opposite. False certainty can direct attention away from causes that might actually be preventable and can make legitimate compensation claims easier to attack.

The strongest moral case does not need the weakest science.

France’s eventual compensation law recognized this. It did not require claimants to demonstrate a unique biological mechanism linking their disease to a named radioactive particle. Instead, it defined eligible diseases and exposure conditions and allowed a presumption framework to carry some of the causal uncertainty.

That is what law often does when science can estimate probability but cannot narrate an individual cell’s history.

The design of the presumption then becomes political science in bureaucratic form. Make it too strict and almost nobody qualifies. Make it too broad and the state compensates disease with little causal connection to testing. Changes to the French system over time show that the first balance did not settle the controversy.

For Algerian claimants, documentary inequality reappears. A veteran with a French service file may be able to demonstrate presence in an eligible zone. A local resident may have no equivalent record. Geographic eligibility can help, but only if zones reflect real exposure rather than convenient administrative boundaries.

The health chapter therefore ends where the measurement chapter began: maps and records decide who exists.

A future definitive study of the Saharan tests would need French archival data, Algerian demographic and medical data, modern dose reconstruction, veteran rosters, local oral history and independent epidemiology. No single country possesses the complete evidence.

That fact should be politically useful.

It means the argument cannot be solved by France opening only selected archives or Algeria publishing only witness accounts. The data have to meet.

Cancer does not carry a flag.

Neither should the cohort.
