# A Map Both Countries Could Publish

The cleanest ending to this story is not an apology speech.

It is a map.

Not the famous declassified fallout map of Gerboise Bleue, though that belongs in the record. A new map: jointly produced, georeferenced, public, bilingual or trilingual, showing what is known today about the former French nuclear test sites in Algeria.

The map would have layers.

One layer for every detonation point and tunnel. One for known contaminated zones. One for areas surveyed by the International Atomic Energy Agency and later teams. One for measured radionuclides and dose rates with dates attached. One for known burial sites and contaminated debris. One for uncertainty: places the archive suggests may contain waste but that have not been adequately surveyed. One for settlements, roads, wells and access routes. One for historical fallout contours clearly labeled by what the underlying measurements can and cannot establish.

A map like that would do more for truth than another decade of governments exchanging the word transparency.

It would not settle cancer causation. It would not tell every family why someone became ill. It would not erase the colonial conditions in which the first tests were conducted or the unequal post-independence arrangements that allowed later ones. It would not answer every compensation claim.

It would answer a simpler question: what do we know about the ground?

That answer is overdue because the technical dispute is now mixed with a diplomatic one. French officials can point to declassification, parliamentary reports, the 2010 compensation law, IAEA cooperation and released archives. Algerian officials can point to continuing uncertainty about waste, very limited compensation of Algerian claimants, missing or inaccessible records and the obvious fact that France created the test infrastructure in the first place.

Both positions contain verifiable facts.

The path forward is to convert broad demands into datasets.

France should identify every surviving archival series relevant to Saharan environmental and personnel exposure: test plans, meteorology, fallout monitoring, dosimetry, containment investigations, worker rosters, decontamination logs, dismantlement records, waste inventories, burial maps and handover documentation. Records that remain legitimately sensitive for weapons-design reasons can be reviewed for partial release or redaction. Safety-relevant coordinates should not remain secret merely because they share a file with military information.

Algeria should consolidate local health, environmental and oral-history records into a research infrastructure that independent scientists can audit. Claims should be geocoded and dated. Disease data should use defensible case definitions. Witness testimony should preserve original language and distinguish direct experience from later knowledge. National memory does not become weaker when it accepts methodological discipline.

Then both countries should invite independent measurement.

The IAEA’s 2005 assessment was preliminary. A modern reassessment could use improved instrumentation, precise satellite coordinates and decades of accumulated remediation practice. It could publish machine-readable data rather than only prose summaries. Samples could be split between laboratories. Methods could be preregistered. Areas of disagreement could be resampled.

This would not depoliticize the history. It would deprive bad politics of some oxygen.

The same principle applies to health research. Build cohorts where possible. Recover French military and contractor rosters. Digitize historical dosimetry. Work with Algerian institutions to identify local workers and communities. Separate test-day fallout exposure from later residual-site exposure. Publish uncertainty ranges. Do not announce a causal conclusion before the study begins.

The result may disappoint everyone.

It may show that some popular casualty numbers are impossible to support. It may show that some official reassurances were broader than the underlying measurements justified. It may identify a small number of high-risk sites rather than a uniformly poisoned Sahara. It may find health signals too weak or data too poor for decisive epidemiology. It may uncover records that change current exposure models.

That is what an investigation is for.

The strongest truth in the archive so far is not that France secretly knew every future consequence. It did not. Nor is it that nothing important happened because exact long-term disease burdens remain uncertain.

The record supports a more durable conclusion.

France deliberately conducted seventeen nuclear tests in the Algerian Sahara. It possessed sophisticated systems for measuring radiation and tracking fallout. Four atmospheric shots released material by design; at least four underground shots experienced imperfect containment. The tests crossed the political transition from colony to independent Algeria. French institutions retained much of the technical archive. Persistent radiological concerns were serious enough for Algeria to request an IAEA assessment decades later. France eventually created a compensation regime, and the design and accessibility of that regime remain contested. Algerian communities and researchers preserve testimony and claims that cannot simply be replaced by French state records, even when some of those claims exceed what current epidemiology can prove.

That is already an extraordinary history.

It does not need invented experiments, hidden superweapons, secret mass graves or impossible certainty about every cancer. The surviving documents are enough.

The deeper lesson concerns classification.

Secrecy does more than prevent outsiders from seeing a fact. It changes which facts get integrated. Meteorologists can know the plume. Doctors can know the disease. engineers can know the tunnel failure. workers can know the task. villagers can know what they saw. If those knowledge streams remain separated for decades, later investigators inherit fragments.

Classification can therefore produce uncertainty without producing ignorance inside the institution.

That distinction appears again and again in hidden history. The state may know pieces no single person has assembled. After enough time, the pieces become legally, scientifically and politically expensive to join.

The Sahara is a particularly severe example because decolonization split the archive from the land.

France kept much of the paperwork.

Algeria kept the sites.

People on both sides kept bodies and memories.

A serious repair process would reunite those three forms of evidence.

There will still be disputes after that. Some are irreducible. Nuclear deterrence itself is a moral and strategic argument the archive cannot settle. The legal meaning of colonial responsibility will remain contested. Individual cancer causation will remain probabilistic. Memory will remain national.

But uncertainty can be narrowed.

The future of this history should not depend on another dramatic leak of a fifty-year-old map. It should depend on systematic release, translation, measurement and comparison.

The desert was never empty.

The archive should stop pretending to be.
