# Empty Is a Political Word

A map of the Sahara encourages a particular mistake. The towns are far apart. Roads thin out. Distances that would cross several European countries fit inside one administrative region. From Paris, a circle drawn around Reggane could look like a rational solution to a problem every nuclear state faced: where do you test a weapon whose effects cannot be confined to a laboratory?

The answer depended on what counted as presence.

Military range planners did not literally believe that no human being existed in the Sahara. French authorities administered settlements, military posts, airfields, wells, caravan routes and labor. The test program itself required thousands of people to build, transport, measure, guard and maintain it. Reggane was not beyond society. It was a node in a colonial system. Yet the language of remoteness made some kinds of presence easier to discount than others.

This is not uniquely French. American nuclear testing turned Nevada and Pacific islands into zones where distance from major political centers became a proxy for acceptable exposure. Britain used Australian land at Monte Bello, Emu Field and Maralinga. The Soviet Union used Semipalatinsk and Novaya Zemlya. China used Lop Nur. Nuclear history is full of places described as remote by governments whose capitals were elsewhere.

The word does several jobs at once. Remote can mean technically useful: far enough from dense population that blast and immediate fallout risks can be reduced. It can mean logistically difficult: supplies, medical care and instrumentation must be moved over enormous distances. It can mean politically weak: the people most likely to be affected have little influence over the decision. And it can mean culturally invisible: a mobile pastoral population can vanish on an administrative map more easily than a city does.

The Saharan test sites make those meanings collide.

Reggane sits in the southwest Algerian Sahara, near the Touat region and its chain of oases. The first French atmospheric tests were conducted at the Centre saharien d’expérimentations militaires, commonly abbreviated CSEM. The environment offered the military open space, long sight lines and controlled access. It also contained people whose relationships to land did not always resemble the cadastral assumptions of metropolitan planning. Sedentary communities lived in oasis settlements. Nomadic and semi-nomadic groups moved through wider territories. Workers were recruited into French facilities. Soldiers came and went. Traffic moved along routes that existed long before the test range.

A safety perimeter is easy to draw if exposure behaves like artillery. Radiation does not.

For prompt radiation and blast, distance can be modeled with considerable confidence. Atmospheric fallout adds weather, particle size, deposition, rain, resuspension, food pathways and time. The relevant geography may become a plume, not a circle. An exclusion zone can protect against one mechanism while doing much less for another. The people who appear safely outside a fence can still be downwind.

French planners knew this in the practical sense. Meteorology was integral to atmospheric testing. Monitoring stations existed because fallout location mattered. The program measured radioactivity because a bomb test is not merely an explosion; it is an event that produces a moving field of contaminants. The later declassified Gerboise Bleue fallout map is surprising to a public audience precisely because it reveals a system that was already watching movement beyond the immediate test point.

That map should not be misread. A line or shaded area indicating detected fallout does not mean every person beneath it received a dangerous dose. Atmospheric monitoring can detect radioactivity at levels far below those associated with acute harm. Some later reporting blurred that distinction because the geographic reach itself was dramatic. The correct response is not to dismiss the map. It is to ask what was measured, at what time, in what medium, at what activity, and how those measurements translate—or fail to translate—into dose.

This is where the word empty becomes scientifically dangerous as well as politically convenient.

If planners conceptualize a landscape mainly through population density, they can underweight low-density pathways that matter for exposure. A herder may spend more time outdoors than an office worker. A family may use local wells, livestock and foods in ways that alter ingestion pathways. Dust can be resuspended. Children and adults differ in vulnerability to some radionuclides. A small population can be statistically difficult to study later even if individual harms are severe. Low density does not equal low importance; it can mean low statistical power.

The archival problem deepens the asymmetry. The French state generated records in French, using military categories designed to answer operational questions. Algerian residents generated memory, local testimony and later scholarship in Arabic and French. These evidence systems do not align neatly. A military log may tell us wind direction at a precise hour but not who was herding livestock beneath the plume. A witness may remember a flash, dust, illness or instructions from soldiers without being able to reconstruct isotope-specific dose. Each source is strong where the other is weak.

Good history requires refusing to promote either into something it is not.

Algerian researchers have spent decades collecting testimonies and framing the tests within colonial history. Much of that work is barely visible in English-language discussions. Articles published through Algeria’s national academic journal platform describe Reggane not as a distant French weapons range but as a lived regional event. They preserve accounts from workers and residents, examine legal responsibility, document local memory organizations and argue that the continuing absence of complete waste maps has practical consequences. Some of these papers use language—crime, barbarity, aggression—that signals a normative position from the first paragraph. That does not make their factual material worthless. It makes source criticism necessary.

French official records carry their own framing. Parliamentary reports often describe the test program through chronology, technical design and compensation categories. They can be unusually candid about containment failures and pollution while still treating colonial governance as background. A report can accurately identify four atmospheric shots, thirteen underground shots and four imperfectly confined underground events without asking what authority made Algerian land available for the first shot in the first place.

Put the two archives beside each other and the missing question becomes obvious.

A test site is not merely where a weapon was detonated. It is a political arrangement for concentrating risk.

France concentrated immediate blast risk far from Paris. It concentrated occupational risk among soldiers, engineers, laborers and support personnel. It concentrated environmental uncertainty in places where post-test monitoring would later be difficult. It concentrated archival power in institutions that controlled classification. When Algeria became independent, the geography changed jurisdiction without instantly changing the testing schedule. France continued to use Saharan sites under the arrangements associated with the Evian process until the program shifted to the Pacific.

This continuity after independence is one of the most revealing details in the entire story. If the Sahara had been chosen only because it was French territory, independence should have ended its usefulness overnight. It did not. The sites had become infrastructural assets. Tunnels existed. Instrumentation existed. Staff and procedures existed. A nuclear program has momentum. Political sovereignty can change faster than a weapons-testing system can be rebuilt.

That is why the post-1962 period cannot be narrated simply as colonial France continuing unchanged. Algeria was now a sovereign state. The legal and diplomatic basis for French use of the sites changed. Yet the power imbalance built into the transition remained enormous. France had the bomb, the technical apparatus and the archival machinery. Algeria had just emerged from a devastating war.

The word empty, then, concealed several things at once: people, politics, and future claims.

It also concealed waste.

Testing produces hardware, contaminated structures, melted or activated material, scrap, instruments, protective gear and vehicles. Some material can be removed. Some can be buried. Some becomes part of the landscape. Decades later, questions about what remains and where it remains have become central to Franco-Algerian disputes. A landscape presented as empty before the test can be treated as available for burial afterward. The same conceptual erasure works twice.

The International Atomic Energy Agency’s later assessment of the former French sites is important partly because it returns the discussion to measured locations and pathways. It does not vindicate every alarming claim about the Sahara, and it does not declare the sites harmless. It identifies areas of radiological concern and makes recommendations. Its existence is itself evidence that the landscape could not simply be abandoned as historical background.

The difficulty is that remediation science arrives after decades of movement. People move. Sand moves. Scrap moves. Knowledge moves unevenly.

Reports from the region have long raised concerns about local people encountering or reusing materials from old test areas. The strongest versions of these claims require site-specific evidence; one should not imagine a continuous carpet of lethal debris across southern Algeria. But the basic hazard mechanism is straightforward. A concentrated radioactive object can matter even when average environmental levels over a broad area are low. Cleanup is therefore a problem of finding points, not merely calculating regional averages.

This distinction mirrors the human record. A population-level study may find limited evidence for a broad disease increase while missing a small number of highly exposed individuals. Conversely, a heartbreaking individual illness cannot establish the population effect. Radiation history constantly tempts us to move between scales without warning.

“Empty desert” makes that temptation worse because it starts with the population erased.

A better mental map begins with layers. Put the detonation point on one layer. Put the official safety zones on another. Add monitoring stations. Add measured plumes by date. Add settlements, wells and routes. Add worker camps and military posts. Add post-independence jurisdiction. Add known waste and uncertain waste. Add testimony. Add compensation claims. Add what the archive still withholds or has never preserved.

Only then does the test range become visible.

It is not a blank patch of sand. It is a system of overlapping geographies, each produced by a different institution and each answering a different question. The military asked where a bomb could be tested. Meteorologists asked where material moved. Doctors asked who was exposed. Lawyers later asked who qualified for compensation. Algerian researchers asked what was done to their country. Waste specialists asked what remained. Archivists asked what could be opened.

None of those maps is the territory.

Together, they are closer.
