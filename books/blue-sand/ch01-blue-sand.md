# Blue Sand

On the morning of February 13, 1960, France detonated its first nuclear device in the Sahara. The test was called Gerboise Bleue. The name is almost whimsical: a blue jerboa, one of the small desert rodents that move through sand on long hind legs. The blast was not whimsical. It made France the fourth state to test a nuclear weapon and announced, in the most literal way available, that the country intended to enter the thermonuclear age on its own terms.

The usual photograph shows the cloud. The more interesting document is a map.

For decades, the public story of the Saharan tests could be told as if geography itself had solved the moral problem. France had gone to the desert because the desert was empty. Reggane was remote. In Ekker was remote. Vastness did rhetorical work. A nuclear test in the Sahara sounded, to metropolitan ears, like a detonation in a blank space.

The map makes the blank space disappear.

A French military fallout chronology for Gerboise Bleue, later declassified, traced radioactive material far beyond the immediate test zone. Contemporary and later reconstructions differ over the significance of those measurements and over dose, which is not the same thing as detecting fallout. But the document destroys the simplest picture. The atmosphere did not respect the administrative fiction of an isolated range. Material moved across Algeria and into other parts of Africa; later reporting on the declassified chart described traces reaching the Mediterranean and southern Europe. That fact does not tell us who received a medically consequential dose. It does tell us that the phrase “the desert” is a poor unit of exposure.

This book begins there, with a distinction that governments, activists, journalists, courts and veterans have repeatedly had trouble holding steady: contamination is not dose, dose is not disease, uncertainty is not safety, and missing records are not proof of the worst allegation. Those four propositions can coexist.

France conducted seventeen nuclear tests in the Algerian Sahara between 1960 and 1966: four atmospheric tests near Reggane and thirteen underground shots at In Ekker. At least four of the underground tests experienced containment failures. The most famous was Béryl, on May 1, 1962, when the sealing of a tunnel in the Taourirt Tan Afella mountain failed and radioactive material escaped. Ministers, soldiers, technicians and local people were present in the broader area. Film of officials turning away from the advancing cloud became an almost too-perfect image for the later politics of the program: the people who had authorized the test could be seen running from something the public had been told was controlled.

But even Béryl is easy to simplify after the fact. The accident did not turn every person in the Sahara into a radiation casualty. Nor does the later recognition of contamination tell us precisely what every soldier, worker, Tuareg family or resident of the Reggane region absorbed. Dosimetry was incomplete, records were uneven, health surveillance was not designed to answer every question later generations would ask, and the most consequential exposures may not be the ones best documented. The archive is thick in places and thin exactly where a compensation claim needs it to be precise.

That unevenness is the real subject.

France was not improvising blindly. It had military radiological teams, meteorological stations, dosimeters, exclusion zones, safety procedures, technical committees and classified reporting. It measured. It mapped. It wrote. The surviving paper trail shows an institution that understood enough about fallout to organize around it, while still operating with assumptions about acceptable risk that would later be contested. Some of the most astonishing facts are therefore not rumors from outside the system. They are measurements produced by the system itself.

This creates a difficult kind of history. A story of total ignorance would be simpler: scientists did not know, soldiers did not know, nobody could have known. A story of perfect foreknowledge would also be simpler: officials understood the full future health burden and chose to expose people anyway. The documents support neither cartoon.

They support something more bureaucratic.

People inside the French state knew different things at different times. A meteorologist could know where a cloud traveled without knowing what disease would emerge twenty years later. A military doctor could know that a worker’s film badge remained below a threshold without knowing the worker’s complete internal dose. A senior official could be briefed on a containment failure without knowing the contamination history of every vehicle later reused. A government could know that radioactive debris existed while lacking—or not disclosing—a complete map of where every contaminated object had been buried. Knowledge was distributed across organizations and classification boundaries. Responsibility was distributed too, which is not the same thing as saying it disappeared.

The Algerian side has its own evidentiary problems. Testimony from people who lived and worked near the sites matters because official French records were never designed to preserve the entire lived world around the ranges. Algerian scholarship and survivor accounts describe cancers, reproductive problems, livestock losses, unusual disease and persistent fear. These claims deserve to be heard in their original context rather than flattened into an English-language paragraph about “victims.” But testimony is not a dosimeter, and a cluster reported decades later cannot by itself reconstruct causation. Some Algerian publications repeat casualty numbers whose derivation is difficult to verify. Some French accounts answer those weak numbers by retreating into the stronger parts of the state archive. Each side can end up pointing at the other’s weakest evidence.

The book will not do that.

Instead, it will keep four ledgers open at once. The first is verified and documented: dates, shot names, official measurements, known accidents, surviving reports, compensation rules, declassified maps, test-site inventories, treaty provisions and the findings of institutions such as the International Atomic Energy Agency. The second is credible but incomplete: veteran testimony, local recollection, plausible exposure pathways, historical reconstructions built from partial records. The third is disputed: claims of specific disease causation without adequate individual exposure evidence, large casualty totals with unclear denominators, allegations that cannot yet be tied to independent documentation. The fourth is synthesis: what secrecy, colonial geography and incomplete dosimetry did to the possibility of knowing the truth later.

That last category is not a license to speculate. It is where the book asks questions that the documents themselves create.

Why did the first French bomb have to be tested in Algeria? Why was “remoteness” treated as a safety property when people lived, traveled, worked and herded across the region? What did the French state know about the movement of fallout in real time? What changed between the atmospheric tests at Reggane and the underground program at In Ekker? What did Béryl teach the people running the program? What remained secret after independence? What was left behind when France departed the sites? Why did it take until 2010 for France to create a statutory compensation regime for nuclear-test victims? Why have Algerian claimants fared so poorly under that system? And what exactly would count as enough evidence now, when the best time to collect it was sixty years ago?

There is another question underneath all of them.

Who gets to call a landscape empty?

The answer matters beyond Algeria. Nuclear powers repeatedly chose places described as remote: Nevada, Semipalatinsk, Novaya Zemlya, Maralinga, the Marshall Islands, Lop Nur, Mururoa and Fangataufa. “Remote” usually meant remote from the political center making the decision. It rarely meant uninhabited. Distance converted other people’s land into strategic depth.

In the Sahara, France obtained the bomb it wanted. Algeria inherited part of the test range’s material after independence. Veterans and local residents inherited uncertainty. The archive inherited classification.

And the sand, which looked empty from Paris, kept the receipts.
