# White, Red, Green

Gerboise Bleue is remembered because firsts attract history. First bomb. First French nuclear test. First entry into the club. The other atmospheric shots at Reggane are more useful if the goal is to understand how a program learns.

They were called Gerboise Blanche, Gerboise Rouge and Gerboise Verte: white, red and green, completing and then extending the palette of the French tricolor. The sequence gives the tests an almost heraldic neatness. The physics was less tidy.

Each shot presented a different combination of yield, device configuration, meteorology and contamination. To treat the four atmospheric tests as one repeated event would erase the point of conducting a series. Weapons programs test because they want variation.

The second shot, Gerboise Blanche, was conducted on April 1, 1960. Publicly available technical histories describe it as a low-yield detonation conducted close to or at ground level. That geometry matters enormously. A surface or near-surface nuclear explosion entrains soil and debris into the cloud, creating a different fallout problem from an airburst. Material becomes radioactive or carries fission products and can deposit downwind in relatively concentrated patterns. A smaller yield does not automatically mean a smaller local contamination problem.

This is one reason nuclear-test history punishes simple rankings. People instinctively sort bombs by kilotons. Health and environmental consequences depend on more than yield.

A high airburst can generate less local fallout than a much smaller surface shot. A well-contained underground test can release less material than either. An underground shot with a containment failure can create a narrow but significant plume. The important variables are coupled: height of burst, geology, weather, source term, particle size, timing and human location.

The Reggane series forced French test planners to confront these interactions in real terrain.

It also created an archive whose existence is easier to establish than its completeness. Military and CEA organizations produced test reports, environmental monitoring records, radiological surveys and technical summaries. Later French parliamentary investigations and the Service historique de la Défense identify substantial holdings related to the nuclear test directorate. Yet the public record has emerged unevenly: official histories, declassified maps, litigation disclosures, veteran records, archival inventories and press revelations separated by decades.

That staggered release shapes public suspicion. When a dramatic map appears fifty years after an event, people reasonably ask what else remains unavailable. The wrong next move is to assume that every gap contains the most incriminating possible document. The right move is to inventory the gaps.

What records should a competent atmospheric test program have produced?

At minimum: shot plans, safety zones, weather forecasts, launch or firing criteria, instrumentation layouts, personnel rosters, dosimetry, field contamination surveys, aerial monitoring, sample analysis, decontamination records, medical reports, transport logs and post-shot access rules. Not every record will survive. Not every record will have been created in a form useful to later epidemiologists. But identifying the expected record classes converts a generalized demand for “the archives” into a testable archival program.

Gerboise Rouge followed on December 27, 1960. Gerboise Verte came on April 25, 1961. By then France had demonstrated that it could repeatedly field nuclear devices at Reggane. The next strategic problem was not whether France could make an atomic explosion. It was how to continue testing while reducing the political and radiological costs of atmospheric shots.

Underground testing was the answer many nuclear powers pursued.

An underground explosion promises containment. Place the device in competent rock at sufficient depth, engineer the tunnel or shaft correctly, and the surrounding geology becomes part of the safety system. The explosive energy melts and fractures rock. A cavity forms. Radioactive material can be trapped underground rather than injected into the atmosphere.

“Can be” is the crucial phrase.

Containment is not automatic. Gases seek paths. Rock fractures. Stemming structures must survive. Geology contains discontinuities that models can miss. A tunnel is also a man-made path to the surface. If the seal fails or the mountain cracks in the wrong way, the underground shot can vent.

France selected In Ekker, in the Hoggar, for the next phase. The location offered a mountain massif in which horizontal galleries could be driven into rock. The device could be placed deep within a tunnel, with engineered barriers between the explosive chamber and the outside.

This transition is often narrated as the environmental improvement it was intended to be. It should also be read as a change in failure mode.

At Reggane, atmospheric release was part of the design. Monitoring aimed to characterize and manage it. At In Ekker, atmospheric release would signify incomplete containment. The same instrument reading could therefore carry a different institutional meaning. Detection after an atmospheric shot might be expected; detection after a supposedly sealed underground shot could mean something had gone wrong.

That distinction would matter in 1962.

Before getting there, however, it is worth staying with the three lesser-known gerboises because they reveal something about memory. Gerboise Bleue has become a national symbol in both France and Algeria, though with opposite emotional valence. French histories describe the birth of an independent deterrent. Algerian histories describe a colonial nuclear crime. The later shots receive less attention because they lack the drama of firstness.

But exposure does not care about symbolism.

A worker near Gerboise Blanche did not experience it as an appendix to the first test. A local contamination hotspot created by a lower-yield surface event does not become less relevant because the code name is less famous. If the research question is human and environmental risk, the correct unit is not the iconic event. It is the exposure opportunity.

This suggests a different way to build the chronology. Instead of four rows labeled with code names and yields, construct a matrix: date, device type, height or geometry, meteorological conditions, source-term information if available, monitored plume, access restrictions, personnel present, settlements potentially affected, environmental samples, decontamination actions and surviving record locations. Repeat for every shot.

Such a matrix would probably be less dramatic than a scandal narrative. It would also be more useful.

One of the problems with the French-Algerian debate is that broad moral claims have sometimes outrun event-level reconstruction. French officials can point to uncertainties and reject inflated assertions. Algerian activists can point to secrecy and demand recognition. Both positions become harder to evade when the technical chronology is granular.

Suppose, for example, an archive shows that a particular atmospheric shot produced elevated readings at a named monitoring station. That fact should be frozen even if later health consequences cannot be quantified. Suppose a testimony places a local worker in a specific task area without protective equipment. That testimony should be preserved as testimony, then tested against rosters, photographs and work orders if available. Suppose a later article claims a high disease rate in a village. The denominator, diagnosis method and comparison population should be recovered before the number enters the manuscript as fact.

This method is slower than outrage. It is also more difficult to dismiss.

The colors—blue, white, red, green—invite a final observation. Nuclear code names can sanitize because they are designed for internal coordination, not public moral memory. They reduce an event to a word that can sit comfortably in a table. The same is true of terms such as “shot,” “experiment,” “event” and “incident.” Bureaucracies need compressed language.

Victims and activists often answer with their own compression: crime, contamination, irradiated, poisoned.

Neither vocabulary should control the evidence.

A book has room to restore the middle scale: what happened on a given day, what the instruments recorded, what people were told, who was present, what moved through the air, and what remained uncertain afterward.

Gerboise Bleue made France nuclear.

Gerboise Blanche, Rouge and Verte made the consequences harder to reduce to one morning.
