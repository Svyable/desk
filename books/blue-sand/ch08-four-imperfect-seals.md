# Four Imperfect Seals

Béryl dominates the memory of In Ekker because it produced the kind of failure humans remember: visible, filmed, embarrassing, close to ministers.

The official record is less cinematic and more important. French parliamentary sources identify at least four underground tests in the Sahara as having imperfect containment: Béryl on May 1, 1962; Améthyste on March 30, 1963; Rubis on October 20, 1963; and Jade on May 30, 1965.

Four is not most of thirteen. It is also not zero.

This arithmetic matters because public arguments about the underground program often swing between two misleading summaries. One says the mountain tests were contained except for one notorious accident. The other says the underground series simply continued atmospheric contamination under another name. Neither matches the documented pattern.

Underground testing reduced the routine atmospheric release inherent in open-air shots. It also introduced a containment technology that sometimes failed. The correct question is not whether underground testing was “safe” or “unsafe” in the abstract. It is which events released what material, through which pathways, and who was present when they did.

Améthyste, Rubis and Jade deserve separate technical biographies.

Later summaries often group them because they share the label “non-contained” or “partially contained.” That grouping hides the scale. A small release of radioactive gas and a violent ejection of contaminated rock can both be failures of containment while posing very different hazards. A book that treats them as equivalent would reproduce the same mistake it criticizes in official reassurance: replacing mechanisms with labels.

The archival task is therefore to recover for each shot the predicted yield, actual yield if publicly established, device placement, gallery design, stemming sequence, geological observations, venting or leakage path, environmental measurements, personnel locations, re-entry rules and follow-up. The most useful document may be an engineering sketch rather than a ministerial memo.

This emphasis on engineering does not depoliticize the story. It tells us what officials had reason to know.

If a post-Béryl technical review identified a particular failure mode and a later shot experienced a similar one, the chronology could show a known hazard recurring. If the later failures arose from genuinely different geological mechanisms despite changed procedures, the history looks different. Words such as negligence require this level of specificity.

The program’s own vocabulary can help and mislead. A release may be described as a “leak,” “vent,” “incident” or “failure.” Each term has technical uses. None automatically communicates human consequence. A release could be operationally disappointing but radiologically modest beyond a controlled zone. Another could look minor in a weapons-development report while mattering greatly to a worker at the portal.

The scale problem appears again.

National histories count shots. Radiological protection counts dose. Engineers count failure modes. Compensation systems count eligible claimants. Communities count sick people. Those are all real counts, but they are not interchangeable.

For the In Ekker series, the strongest book would build a table no one needs to argue with. Thirteen rows. One row per underground shot. Columns for date, code name, containment status, release description, monitoring evidence, known personnel exposure, decontamination actions, public disclosure history and archival source. Blank cells should remain blank rather than filled by inference.

Blank cells are part of the story.

They tell us where research has not recovered the evidence or where records may not be public. The temptation in controversial history is to treat a blank cell as incriminating. Sometimes it is simply a blank cell. But if the state holds the missing record, classification changes the meaning of absence. The government has the power to convert an empirical question into a trust question.

French authorities have gradually released more information about the test program. Parliamentary reporting in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries acknowledged environmental and health concerns that earlier official narratives minimized. The Service historique de la Défense now publicly describes a substantial archive of the Direction des centres d'expérimentations nucléaires. The existence of that finding aid matters. It makes the documentary terrain more concrete.

It also clarifies what declassification is not.

Opening an archive does not automatically make evidence accessible to every affected person. A French military file in Vincennes may be legally public and practically unreachable to an elderly resident of Reggane. Language, travel, archival literacy, document classification rules and reproduction costs remain filters. Digital publication can narrow that gap. Translation can narrow it further.

This is one reason the Algerian-language record belongs inside the book, not in a footnote. Algerian scholars have documented how local communities remember the tests, how legal claims are framed, and how the absence of maps of buried material continues to shape political demands. Some papers make causal claims stronger than their methods support; others preserve details that French state-centered histories ignore. The solution is source comparison, not source hierarchy by nationality.

Take the recurring claim that birth defects became widespread around the Saharan sites because of testing. It appears in Algerian reporting and scholarship. Radiation can cause reproductive and developmental harm at sufficient doses. But a historical regional claim requires baseline rates, case ascertainment, geography, timing and plausible dose. Without that, the claim belongs in the credible-or-disputed ledger depending on the evidence, not in the verified ledger.

Now reverse the burden. An official report that cannot demonstrate a statistically significant health increase does not establish that no one was harmed. Small populations, mobile communities, poor historical medical records and uncertain dose reconstruction can make absence of statistical proof unsurprising.

This is not symmetrical skepticism for its own sake. The evidentiary defects point in opposite directions. Activist claims can over-attribute disease. Official studies can under-detect it.

The four imperfect seals at In Ekker are valuable precisely because they are not allegations. They are documented failures around which the uncertain human record can be organized.

Start with what cannot reasonably be disputed: the shots happened; at least four had containment problems; radioactive material escaped to some degree; monitoring existed; people worked at and around the test complex. Then narrow the contested questions. How much material? Where did it go? Who received what dose? Which later illnesses are consistent with those exposures? Which claims exceed the evidence?

This structure does something politically useful. It removes the false choice between declaring the Sahara a proven mass-casualty catastrophe and declaring the program adequately controlled.

A state can run a sophisticated safety program and still expose people.

A community can have legitimate claims and still repeat bad numbers.

A contaminated site can require remediation even when broad population epidemiology is inconclusive.

An archive can contain remarkable evidence without containing a smoking gun.

The mountain at In Ekker did not fail every time. That is exactly why the times it failed can be studied as experiments in institutional learning.

Four imperfect seals are enough to ask whether the system learned what it claimed to learn.
