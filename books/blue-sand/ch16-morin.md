# Morin

In 2010 France passed a law recognizing and compensating victims of its nuclear tests. Half a century had passed since Gerboise Bleue.

The law is commonly called the Morin law after defense minister Hervé Morin. Its existence marked an important change in official posture. A state that had long defended its testing program as controlled and necessary now accepted a statutory responsibility to compensate certain people who developed specified diseases after being present in defined test-related areas and periods.

Recognition, however, is not a switch. It is an algorithm written in legal language.

Who counts as exposed? Which diseases count? Which places count? What dates count? How strong is the presumption of causation? What evidence must a claimant supply? Who decides whether another cause overwhelms the nuclear presumption?

Every answer includes and excludes people.

The early compensation system was criticized for rejecting an overwhelming share of applications. French parliamentary review later concluded that the law had not yet achieved its objectives and reforms altered the causation standard. These changes are important because they show that the original framework was not merely administered poorly; its underlying evidentiary architecture was contested.

This is where historical research becomes operational.

A compensation law relies on the state’s own reconstruction of exposure geography. If a claimant served inside a recognized zone during a recognized period and developed a recognized disease, the law can spare that person from proving an exact individual dose. But the zone must first be drawn.

Where did its boundaries come from?

For atmospheric tests, fallout patterns vary by shot and weather. A static administrative polygon is a simplification of moving plumes. For underground accidents, exposure may be highly directional and task-specific. A worker just outside a standard perimeter during an unusual release could have more exposure than someone inside it on a quiet day.

Law needs categories where physics supplies gradients.

The Morin system also exposes a national asymmetry. French veterans have organizations, legal representation, service records and political access. Polynesian claimants have built powerful local advocacy structures and benefited from increasing national attention to the Pacific tests. Algerian claimants face the additional barriers of nationality, geography, archival access and diplomatic history.

Recent reporting and parliamentary debate have repeatedly noted how few Algerian victims have received compensation compared with the scale of political claims surrounding the Sahara. The exact numbers change over time and should be frozen from current official CIVEN data before publication. The pattern is what matters here: formal eligibility does not automatically produce practical access.

An application is a technology of proof.

It asks a person to translate life into fields: date, place, disease, document. The nuclear test program generated some of those records. The claimant supplies the rest. If the state’s original monitoring system did not document a local worker, the later compensation office can treat that absence as the claimant’s problem.

This is how archival inequality becomes administrative inequality.

The obvious remedy is not to abandon evidence. It is to distinguish which evidence the state had a duty to preserve.

If France controlled entry to a test site, it should be expected to have personnel or contractor records. If official fallout monitoring identifies a community under a plume, residents should not be required to produce personal dosimeters that never existed. If a claimant alleges presence in a zone for which records are missing, corroborating testimony and employment evidence should have defined weight.

A mature compensation regime also needs to separate two functions that are often confused: scientific causation and social repair.

A government may choose to compensate a person because the probability of test-related causation is meaningful even if it cannot be proved more likely than not in an individual case. That is not scientific dishonesty. It is a policy decision about who should bear uncertainty created by a state program.

The harder question is how much uncertainty the state should absorb.

Nuclear veterans argue, with force, that the state created the exposure and controlled the evidence, so doubt should not fall entirely on the sick individual. Governments worry about causal overreach and open-ended liability. Both concerns are legitimate. The history tells us where to put the thumb on the scale.

If records are missing because claimants ignored available monitoring, that is one situation. If records are missing because the test program did not monitor a category of people or kept results inaccessible, that is another.

The Saharan tests contain many of the second kind.

Compensation data can themselves become a research source. Approval and denial decisions reveal which diseases, locations and claimant groups have been recognized. Anonymized case patterns could show where the evidentiary bottlenecks are. How many Algerian claims fail for lack of presence documentation? How many fail on disease criteria? How many never reach the system because potential claimants do not know it exists?

These are policy questions that can be counted.

The compensation process also creates incentives around historical narrative. Advocacy groups benefit from emphasizing broad harm. Governments benefit from administrable boundaries. Lawyers search for precedents. Scientists can be pulled into battles over thresholds their studies were never designed to settle.

A book should not become an advocate’s brief for either side.

It should identify where the design of the law conflicts with the design of the historical record.

The most important reform may be boring: proactive archival assistance. If the state already possesses service, employment or monitoring records relevant to a claimant, the claimant should not have to know which archive box contains them. A dedicated historical unit could reconstruct presence using government records as part of the application process.

For Algerian claimants, bilingual or trilingual access matters. A French statutory right that can only be navigated through French administrative literacy is not equally available in practice. Arabic documentation, local intake support and coordination with Algerian institutions would be part of meaningful access.

None of this determines who should receive money in a particular case.

It determines whether the process asks the right party to carry the uncertainty.

The Morin law was a delayed acknowledgment that nuclear-test risk could not remain a purely military historical matter.

Its continuing revisions reveal something less flattering.

It is easier to detonate a bomb than to design a fair rule for everyone who stood downwind of it.
