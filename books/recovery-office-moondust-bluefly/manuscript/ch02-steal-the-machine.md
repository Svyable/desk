# Why You Steal the Machine

The cleanest way to understand crash retrieval is to remove the crash.

Suppose an adversary offers you one of its frontline fighters. You would take it.

The United States did. In the late 1960s, American intelligence and test organizations secretly examined acquired Soviet-built MiGs. The programs later known publicly as HAVE DOUGHNUT, HAVE DRILL, and HAVE FERRY were not rumors invented after the Cold War. Official Air Force and Defense Intelligence Agency histories now describe them. A MiG-21 and MiG-17s were flown, disassembled, analyzed, and used to change American tactics and training.

This is foreign materiel exploitation in its purest form: get the machine, touch it, fly it if you can, measure what propaganda and photography cannot tell you.

Technical intelligence has a stubborn physicality. Signals intelligence can reveal emitters. Human sources can describe a cockpit. Imagery can estimate dimensions. A captured object answers different questions. What fasteners did the designer choose? How much does the part actually weigh? What happens when a pilot pulls the aircraft through a particular maneuver? How does the radar behave at an angle no brochure discusses? Where does the structure flex? What is beautifully engineered and what is crude?

The mythology of secret technology often begins by imagining that possession is equivalent to mastery. The historical record suggests the opposite. Possession creates work.

A foreign object enters a chain of exploitation because it is not self-explanatory. Specialists from different disciplines may disagree. The part may have been damaged. Its operating context may be missing. A circuit board without firmware can mislead. A material can look exotic until ordinary manufacturing history is recovered. A high-performance feature can carry an ugly tradeoff that only appears when the complete system is run.

The HAVE programs are important to this book because they give us a known answer to a question otherwise vulnerable to fantasy: what does the United States do when it genuinely gets hold of strategically important foreign aerospace technology?

It builds teams. It creates cover. It limits access. It performs instrumented tests. It compares the hardware against prior intelligence estimates. It turns the results into tactics. It eventually creates training institutions that embody what was learned. The object does not remain a sacred relic in a warehouse. Its value is realized by translation into doctrine, acquisition, countermeasures, and design.

This gives modern reverse-engineering allegations a second benchmark. If a government had possessed radically superior non-human hardware for decades, what kind of exploitation signature should exist? The answer cannot be reduced to “better technology would appear,” because technological diffusion can be slow, compartmentation can be severe, and an alien artifact might be incomprehensible. But neither can the answer be “there would be no signature at all.” A real exploitation enterprise has people, facilities, budgets, security rules, failure reports, test fixtures, contractors, rejected hypotheses, and eventually disputes about what has actually been learned.

We should expect seams.

Foreign materiel exploitation also complicates the word “reverse engineering.” The phrase is often used as if it describes one act. In practice, it contains levels. You can characterize a material without knowing how it was made. You can copy a mechanical feature without understanding why it works. You can learn how to defeat a system without being able to reproduce it. You can reproduce a component while failing to recreate the industrial base that made it cheap and reliable.

The MiG programs did not require American engineers to rediscover Soviet society. They needed enough understanding to fight the aircraft.

This matters because a hypothetical non-human artifact raises the difficulty by orders of magnitude. If the machine depends on unknown energy sources, manufacturing methods, materials, or physical principles, possession might not yield a working copy. That is a fair speculative defense against the argument that “we would have seen the technology by now.” It is not, however, evidence that such an artifact exists. It merely prevents one weak falsification.

The point of historical exploitation is not to prove or disprove the exotic case by analogy. It is to discipline our expectations.

Governments steal machines because machines leak truth. They also hide the theft because revealing possession can burn diplomatic relationships, intelligence sources, and countermeasure advantages. These incentives are sufficient to explain a large amount of secrecy around recovered hardware without invoking anything beyond human competition.

That is uncomfortable for both poles of the UAP argument. Skeptics sometimes treat secrecy itself as theatrical. It is not. Believers sometimes treat secrecy as evidentiary weight for exotic origin. It is not that either.

Secrecy is what you would expect around a MiG.

The payload still has to be proved.
