# HAVE DOUGHNUT

The MiG-21 arrived with an answer attached: Soviet.

That did not make the intelligence problem easy. It made it testable.

In 1968, a Department of Defense team led by the Air Force’s Foreign Technology Division clandestinely examined a Soviet-built MiG-21F-13. Official Air Force history now describes the effort. DIA’s museum goes further, placing HAVE DOUGHNUT alongside HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY as programs in which Soviet aircraft were flown, analyzed, disassembled, and studied at Groom Lake.

This is what an actual hidden aerospace exploitation program looks like after declassification.

There is a machine with a known provenance. There are test objectives. There are pilots. There are performance findings. There are downstream consequences. The knowledge does not remain trapped inside the project. It influences how American aviators train and fight.

The contrast with alleged non-human reverse-engineering programs is not that one story is classified and the other is not. HAVE DOUGHNUT was classified. The contrast is evidentiary maturity.

Once the program emerged, its pieces locked together. Institutional histories corroborated the general mission. Aircraft acquisition made sense within known geopolitical relationships. Test facilities matched the need. Tactical lessons matched the hardware. Later Constant Peg operations used acquired MiGs at greater scale. The secret had a technical afterlife.

This provides a powerful standard for evaluating claims that cannot yet show equivalent material.

A real exploitation program produces friction. Aircraft break. Parts are scarce. Pilots need conversion training. Engineers discover that an adversary’s design is better in one regime and worse in another. Security requirements interfere with normal maintenance. The object resists mythology by becoming specific.

A non-human artifact, if real, should create even more friction.

Consider what a program would confront. Unknown materials might be impossible to machine. Power systems might be hazardous or inert. Controls might not map to human physiology. Components might be integrated at scales or through manufacturing methods unavailable to us. Analysts would disagree over whether a structure was functional, damaged, or incidental. Contractors would propose competing models. Decades could pass without reproduction.

This is one plausible reason technological spinoff might be limited.

But difficulty leaves records too. Failure is administrative. Someone has to explain why a funded line of effort has not produced results. Security compartments can hide the explanation from most people without eliminating budgets, personnel, test equipment, injuries, storage requirements, or disputes.

The HAVE programs also show that exploitation is not the same as reverse engineering for production. The United States did not need to build a domestic MiG-21 factory to gain enormous value. It needed to learn performance envelopes and tactics. A hypothetical exotic program might likewise focus first on characterization rather than reproduction.

This narrows one common skeptical argument: “If we had alien craft, we would obviously have anti-gravity by now.” Not obviously.

It also narrows one common believer argument: “Some modern technology appeared after alleged retrievals, so it may be reverse engineered.” Temporal sequence is not enough. Semiconductor, materials, aerospace, and computing advances have documented terrestrial research lineages. A reverse-engineering claim has to show a transfer mechanism, not simply technological surprise.

HAVE DOUGHNUT shows us what a transfer mechanism can look like. Captured hardware changes threat understanding. Threat understanding changes tactics and training. Institutions such as Red Flag and adversary-aircraft programs embody the learning.

The chain is visible after the secrecy relaxes.

That visibility did not require every classified detail to be released. Enough independent structure emerged for the historical claim to stabilize.

This is the standard the crash-retrieval debate should want, not fear.

If a non-human exploitation program existed, establishing it would not cheapen the subject by demanding paperwork. It would rescue the claim from folklore. The most extraordinary intelligence discovery in human history deserves at least the evidentiary architecture of a borrowed Soviet fighter.

Until then, HAVE DOUGHNUT remains the useful control: secret, real, consequential, and terrestrial.
