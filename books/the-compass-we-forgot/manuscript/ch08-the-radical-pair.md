# 8. The Radical Pair

A compass made of chemistry sounds impossible until the numbers are stated correctly.

Earth’s magnetic field is too weak to yank proteins around in the way a bar magnet moves a nail. But the radical-pair hypothesis does not ask it to. Light can create molecules containing unpaired electron spins. Those spins evolve between quantum states while chemical reactions compete with that evolution. A weak magnetic field can slightly alter the spin dynamics and therefore the probabilities of reaction products. The organism does not have to feel magnetic force. It has to amplify a tiny magnetic influence on chemistry.

The idea traces through work by Klaus Schulten and others and became one of the dominant frameworks for the avian magnetic compass. In modern versions, cryptochrome in the retina is the leading candidate molecule. Direction could be encoded as a pattern across differently oriented molecules, potentially overlaying magnetic information on vision rather than producing a separate sensation.

This is beautiful enough to attract bad explanations.

The mechanism still faces serious unresolved constraints. Earth-strength sensitivity must survive thermal and biochemical noise. Radical lifetimes and molecular alignment matter. The role of radiofrequency fields has generated both striking behavioral results and difficult theoretical questions. A 2026 *Physiological Reviews* synthesis notes a particularly awkward mismatch: behavioral compass disruption has been reported from extremely weak radiofrequency fields, in some cases around nanotesla levels, while standard radical-pair models struggle to produce effects that large. Proposed three-radical or scavenger mechanisms can enhance sensitivity, but the theory is not closed.

There is also a common category mistake. Demonstrating a magnetic field effect on a chemical reaction is not the same as demonstrating a sensory receptor. The chain must continue from altered chemical yield to a transduction pathway, neural signal and organismal behavior.

That chain is why animal work remains so valuable. Light-wavelength dependencies, cryptochrome genetics, radiofrequency disruption and neural mapping provide independent handles. No single one proves the mechanism; together they make a structured hypothesis.

Human work has fewer handles.

The 2011 human-CRY2-in-Drosophila experiment established molecular capability in a foreign sensory system. Some studies have reported geomagnetic influences on human visual sensitivity, a result that would be conceptually comfortable for retinal radical-pair models. But the literature is small and not yet independently converged. Meanwhile the 2019 EEG polarity dependence points away from the simplest axial radical-pair compass.

There is a productive way to handle this contradiction: stop asking which mechanism “wins” and force each one to predict an experimental fingerprint.

A radical-pair human response should care about illumination spectrum. It may show characteristic sensitivity to weak oscillating fields at frequencies related to spin dynamics. It should respond to field axis in ways separable from polarity. If the pathway is retinal, eye state and retinal adaptation should matter. Pharmacological or genetic perturbations could eventually help, although invasive manipulations appropriate in animals are not available in healthy people.

A magnetite-based system predicts a different fingerprint. Static polarity can matter. Darkness need not abolish the response. Mechanical coupling and field gradients may matter. Strong brief pulses could in principle remagnetize or disrupt particle orientation, though ethical and interpretive issues make such tests nontrivial.

The radical-pair hypothesis is therefore valuable even if it turns out not to explain the human EEG result. It demonstrates how to convert an apparently mystical sense into a molecular model with failure conditions.

That conversion is the theme of this book.

The magnetic field is invisible. The mechanism cannot be allowed to be.
