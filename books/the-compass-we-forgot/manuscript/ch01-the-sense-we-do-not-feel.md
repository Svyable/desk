# 1. The Sense We Do Not Feel

If you stand outside with your eyes closed, Earth is still running through you.

Not metaphorically. The field at the surface is on the order of tens of microtesla, tilted and varying with latitude, altered by crustal geology, solar weather, buildings, wires, vehicles, elevators and the little magnets hidden inside ordinary machines. A compass needle participates openly in this environment. Human experience does not.

That absence of sensation has done enormous argumentative work. We tend to assume that because north does not arrive with the immediacy of red or hot or loud, there is no biological north to arrive. But sensory biology gives no such guarantee. Most computation in a nervous system never becomes reportable experience. The vestibular system edits vision and posture without announcing most of its arithmetic. Interoceptive systems regulate blood pressure, oxygen, carbon dioxide and stretch with only occasional promotion into consciousness. A signal may be biologically real and phenomenologically silent.

That is the narrow opening through which human magnetoreception returned.

For decades, the subject was poisoned by exactly the kind of story that makes scientists protective of their time. People were said to possess a sixth sense. Blindfolded volunteers were driven around and asked to point home. Magnets near the head allegedly ruined the ability. The stories migrated faster than the replications. Joseph Kirschvink, who would later become one of the most important advocates for a renewed human magnetoreception experiment, was initially among the people trying to break the early claims. They often broke.

That history matters because the modern question is not the old question with better publicity. The old question was usually behavioral: can a person orient, point, home or choose using the geomagnetic field? The newer question is physiological: does the human nervous system produce a reproducible response when the magnetic vector changes under controlled conditions?

Those questions can have different answers.

A sensory transducer can exist without supporting useful navigation. It can survive as an evolutionary remnant. It can feed a circuit whose outputs are suppressed by stronger cues. It can be developmentally calibrated and become unreliable in an artificial environment. Or the apparent physiological response can be artifact and the whole resurrection can collapse under independent replication.

In 2019 Connie Wang, Isaac Hilburn, Daw-An Wu, Yuki Mizuhara, Christopher Cousté, Jacob Abrahams, Sam Bernstein, Ayumu Matani, Shinsuke Shimojo and Joseph Kirschvink published an experiment in *eNeuro* that moved the argument into that narrower territory. Subjects sat in a magnetically shielded chamber while three orthogonal coil systems rotated Earth-strength magnetic fields around the head. The investigators measured EEG. They reported that certain rotations produced a transient reduction in alpha-band amplitude—an alpha event-related desynchronization—that appeared only for particular combinations of rotation direction and vertical-field polarity.

The strongest part of the result was not simply that an EEG trace moved. EEG traces move constantly. The stronger claim was selectivity. Reverse the static vertical component and the response disappeared. That mattered because the induced electrical fields generated by changing magnetic fields depend on the change, not the polarity of the static field. The authors therefore argued that a simple induction artifact in electrodes or tissue could not explain the pattern.

They went further. The response was sensitive to magnetic polarity, which does not fit the simplest axial radical-pair compass models usually associated with cryptochrome. They argued that a ferromagnetic transducer remained compatible.

This is where a bad book would declare victory.

A better book notices that the experiment creates a stack of separate problems. Is the EEG effect independently reproducible? Is it magnetic rather than some coil-correlated artifact nobody has yet isolated? If magnetic, is it sensory transduction or a nonsensory biophysical perturbation? If sensory, where is the receptor? If there is a receptor, where does its signal go? If the signal reaches cortex, does it influence behavior? If it influences behavior, under what ecological conditions? If our ancestors used it, why can almost nobody tell north with their eyes shut?

Each step is a different book-sized claim.

The 2019 experiment has an unusual quality: it makes the mystery smaller and larger at the same time. Smaller, because it gives us a specific waveform, stimulus geometry and set of controls to reproduce. Larger, because it suggests the possibility that a sensory channel can remain active after conscious access to it has faded.

That possibility is biologically ordinary enough to take seriously. Evolution does not perform clean software uninstallations. It repurposes, degrades, suppresses, duplicates and leaves machinery hanging around. A structure may persist because it is cheap, pleiotropic, or still useful in some context no laboratory has identified.

The first task, then, is not to prove that humans have a magnetic compass.

It is to stop asking the nervous system only questions that require it to answer in words.
