# 2. The Planetary Field

The Earth does not offer north as an arrow. It offers a vector field.

At any location the field has direction, inclination and intensity. A compass needle lying level mainly reports the horizontal component. An animal can, in principle, use more. Inclination changes systematically with latitude: in much of the Northern Hemisphere field lines angle downward into Earth; in much of the Southern Hemisphere they angle upward. Intensity also varies geographically. Those quantities create a crude planetary coordinate system without satellites, maps or radio.

That matters because the phrase “magnetic sense” hides several different jobs. A compass answers which way. A map provides position. A polarity compass distinguishes magnetic north from south; an inclination compass may instead distinguish poleward from equatorward without caring about polarity. An animal might use magnetic information only when celestial, olfactory or visual cues fail. It might calibrate one compass with another. It might learn local magnetic anomalies the way a person learns a skyline.

The sensory problem is therefore not merely detecting a weak field. It is extracting useful variables from a field that is weak, noisy and slowly varying while an animal itself rotates, accelerates, breathes and moves through electric machinery.

Physics provides three broad mechanism families often invoked in animal magnetoreception. One uses ferromagnetic material such as magnetite: a small magnetic particle experiences torque or force in a magnetic field and could mechanically couple to a membrane or cytoskeleton. A second uses spin chemistry, especially light-dependent radical pairs in cryptochrome proteins, where weak fields can alter reaction yields under some conditions. A third uses electromagnetic induction, particularly plausible in conductive animals moving through magnetic fields, as proposed for some aquatic species.

None is a universal solution. Different lineages may have evolved different mechanisms. Even within one animal, a compass and a map could be physically distinct.

This plurality is important for humans because our favorite mistake is to turn the mechanism debate into an election. If human CRY2 can support magnetosensitivity in a fly, cryptochrome wins. If magnetite exists in the brain, magnetite wins. If an EEG response is polarity-sensitive, cryptochrome loses. Biology is under no obligation to provide one receptor, one organ or one mechanism.

The 2019 human EEG result is especially interesting because the stimulus dependency looked ecological. The reported response occurred for horizontal rotations under a downward-pointing vertical component resembling Northern Hemisphere conditions, and disappeared when the vertical component was inverted. One interpretation is that the nervous system was responding to a magnetic configuration it had developed under, rather than to any arbitrary coil movement.

But ecology can be imitated by artifact. A piece of ferromagnetic equipment might react differently to polarity. A nonlinear component can create directional asymmetry. A chamber can be cleaner than a city and still contain physics the experimenters did not model. This is why the geometry of the field matters. A replication should not merely copy the published rotation. It should deliberately permute inclination, polarity, intensity, rotation speed and coordinate frame until the competing explanations make different predictions.

The geomagnetic field also changes naturally. Solar storms perturb it. Crustal magnetization produces regional structure. Lightning, power networks, trains, cars and buildings contribute local fields. Modern life has made our magnetic environment more complicated at exactly the moment when our technologies have made magnetic measurement exquisitely precise.

If a human receptor exists, the city becomes part of the experiment.

That claim can be made too quickly. There is a long history of attributing vague health or behavioral effects to weak electromagnetic fields without a convincing mechanism or reproducible dose-response relationship. Magnetoreception should not become a back door for importing that literature wholesale. A sensory receptor tuned to Earth-strength fields would not imply that every technical field is biologically harmful, any more than having eyes implies that all light exposures are dangerous.

But a receptor would force a more specific question: which field parameters does the organism actually encode?

Frequency matters. Direction matters. Static versus oscillating matters. Spectral noise matters. Light may matter. Motion may matter. The animal’s own coordinate frame may matter more than laboratory coordinates. A fifty-microtesla field rotating around a stationary head is physically different from a head rotating through a stationary planetary field in some ways and equivalent in others. A serious sensory model must say which differences the receptor can detect.

The Earth’s field is weak. That is not an argument against biology using it. Biology routinely exploits weak signals by filtering, averaging, amplifying and restricting bandwidth.

The real question is whether evolution built a detector and, in us, forgot to build the conscious dashboard.
