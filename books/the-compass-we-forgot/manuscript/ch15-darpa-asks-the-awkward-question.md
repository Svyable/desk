# 15. DARPA Asks the Awkward Question

The military has a habit of funding questions respectable people prefer not to ask in public.

That fact is often abused. The existence of a defense program is turned into proof that the underlying phenomenon is real, operational, weaponized or secretly mature. Sometimes the program exists because the government knows something. Sometimes it exists because the government does not know something and would like to stop being surprised.

DARPA’s RadioBio program belongs in the second category on the public record.

Launched in 2017 through the Defense Sciences Office, RadioBio aimed to determine whether purposeful electromagnetic signaling between biological cells exists and, if it does, what mechanisms and information might be involved. The public program description framed the problem as a validity test. Phase 1 performers were to model hypothesized signaling pathways and test them experimentally. A second phase envisioned independent test beds to replicate and confirm pathways found in the first.

That design is epistemically interesting. DARPA did not need to assert that cells possess hidden radio systems in order to justify investigating the claim. The potential payoff—a biological strategy for communication or sensing in noisy environments—was enough to make a bounded experiment worthwhile.

Caltech’s human magnetoreception work received support from the RadioBio program. This creates a temptation to narrate the EEG result as a military discovery program for a human sixth sense. The public record does not support that framing. RadioBio’s stated scope was broader biological electromagnetic signaling. Funding indicates interest, not validation.

Still, defense interest belongs in the book because it reveals something about anomalous science. National-security organizations often face asymmetric incentives around low-probability, high-consequence phenomena. If an effect could enable navigation without GPS, communication through clutter, remote sensing or biological control, the cost of a careful test can be small compared with the cost of dismissing it prematurely.

This is why state interest appears repeatedly in fields that later become magnets for conspiracy. Remote viewing, nonlethal effects, exotic propulsion, biological sensing and unusual materials all produce the same rhetorical loop: the government studied it, therefore it works; because it works, the real program must be classified; because it is classified, absence of evidence becomes evidence of secrecy.

That loop has no natural stopping point.

RadioBio offers a better model. Publish the question. Specify the phase gates. Demand mechanism. Build independent replication into the program structure. Treat a null as an answer.

The history of magnetoreception suggests why this approach matters. Biology clearly achieves magnetic sensing somewhere. We still do not know exactly how in many vertebrates. That gap is scientifically legitimate even without a human result. If biological systems solve weak-field detection with architectures engineers have missed, the mechanism could inspire technology.

The important word is *if*.

Defense research is at its healthiest when “if” survives contact with budget.

The public RadioBio record should therefore be kept in Evidence Class 1 only for what it documents: DARPA considered the question worth a structured research program, and the program explicitly sought to establish whether the claimed signaling existed. It is not evidence of a classified human magnetic-navigation capability. It is not evidence of weaponization. It is not evidence that every performer produced a positive result.

The same government can fund a mystery and remain uncertain about it.

That may be the least conspiratorial and most useful lesson in the entire subject.
