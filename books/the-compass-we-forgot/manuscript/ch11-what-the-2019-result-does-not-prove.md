# 11. What the 2019 Result Does Not Prove

A result can be important and still prove much less than its headline.

The 2019 study does not prove that humans can navigate by magnetism. It does not prove that a dedicated magnetic sensory organ exists. It does not prove that magnetite is the transducer. It does not prove that cryptochrome is irrelevant. It does not show conscious perception. It does not show ecological usefulness. It does not establish that the effect is universal across populations. It does not show that modern electromagnetic environments damage a magnetic sense.

Most of the public argument skips one or more of these boundaries because the phrase “human magnetic sense” compresses them.

A cleaner claim ladder begins with stimulus control. Did the experimental apparatus generate the intended magnetic fields without relevant side cues? Then physiology: did a preregistered neural measure change selectively with those fields? Then transduction: is the change caused by a biological mechanism that detects the geomagnetic variable rather than a generic physical interaction with equipment or tissue? Then receptor: which cells or molecules perform that transduction? Then coding: how is the signal represented neurally? Then behavior: does it alter a choice or action? Then ecology: did or does the effect improve orientation or fitness?

The 2019 paper reaches most confidently into the first two layers and argues mechanistically into the third. Everything higher remains open.

This is not nitpicking. Different layers demand different experiments.

Suppose a multicenter replication reproduced alpha-ERD exactly. A skeptic could reasonably accept a physiological magnetic response while rejecting human navigation. Suppose a future histology study found a beautifully organized magnetite structure with innervation. That would not by itself show that the EEG effect came from it. Suppose a forced-choice study showed weak above-chance discrimination of field rotations. That would not prove participants could use the signal to home over kilometers.

The temptation to collapse these steps is strongest when a subject carries cultural baggage. A “sixth sense” feels like a single discovery. In reality it would be a chain of discoveries.

The chain also protects the field from reverse inflation. If behavioral navigation studies fail, that does not automatically erase a neural response. If a magnetite receptor candidate fails, that does not erase cryptochrome chemistry. If the EEG effect fails replication, that does not erase animal magnetoreception or the physical plausibility of the broader mechanism families.

The book therefore refuses to use one negative result as a referendum on all magnetobiology.

It also refuses the symmetrical believer’s move: using robust animal magnetoreception to make human claims feel pre-validated. Evolutionary continuity is a reason to ask, not an answer.

The strongest current statement is narrower and more interesting than the popular one. A published experiment reports that the human brain can respond selectively to rotations of an Earth-strength magnetic field under conditions designed to exclude several ordinary artifacts, while subjects remain unaware of the stimulus. A major 2026 review considers EEG evidence the first substantial indication of a subconscious human magnetic response but emphasizes the need for independent replication.

That is enough to justify serious work.

It is not enough to redraw the sensory homunculus.
