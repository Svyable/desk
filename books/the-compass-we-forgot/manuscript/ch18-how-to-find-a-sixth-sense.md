# 18. How to Find a Sixth Sense

The phrase “sixth sense” is almost perfectly designed to ruin an experiment.

It invites conscious reports, extraordinary claims, intuitive subjects and a hunt for people who already believe they possess the faculty. That is backwards. If human magnetoreception exists, the strongest way to find it is to treat it as ordinary sensory physiology whose receptor happens to be unknown.

Start with a preregistered magnetic stimulus space: field strength, inclination, declination, polarity, rotation speed and spectral noise. Measure the field at the head, not merely at the coil. Log vibration, sound, temperature and broadband electromagnetic noise. Blind the participant and experimenter to condition. Use sham currents that mimic heating and electrical operation. Randomize wiring mappings across sessions.

Use outcomes that do not require belief. EEG alpha-ERD is one candidate because it has a published effect to replicate. Add pupillometry, autonomic measures and carefully designed forced-choice tasks. Do not search every frequency band and every electrode until something becomes significant. Choose primary outcomes before the first participant enters.

Then replicate across laboratories before optimizing the effect.

That ordering is essential. Many fragile phenomena become impressive when a single team learns the idiosyncrasies of its apparatus and participant pool. A robust sensory system should survive hardware built by people who did not discover it.

If replication succeeds, begin perturbation.

Test light spectrum to probe cryptochrome involvement. Test static polarity separately from axis. Add weak RF fields over frequencies predicted to interfere with radical-pair chemistry. Compare darkness with controlled retinal illumination. Rotate the participant through a static field and compare with rotating the field around a stationary participant. Apply field gradients to probe ferromagnetic-force sensitivity while keeping local vector magnitude controlled.

Then use people as longitudinal subjects rather than disposable rows in a spreadsheet. Is responder status stable across weeks? Does the preferred field geometry persist? Does sleep, circadian phase or migraine change it? Does adaptation occur after prolonged exposure to an inverted field? What happens after crossing the magnetic equator?

If the effect is stable, receptor hunting becomes rational.

At that point, tissue biology should be tied to phenotype. Genome-wide association may be premature if samples are small, but candidate genes can be investigated cautiously. Retinal cryptochrome expression, iron-handling pathways and rare sensory-cell markers become more interesting when linked to a measured response. Imaging and postmortem work can search for anatomical correlates in people with known physiological phenotypes whenever ethically and practically possible.

The experiment also needs negative controls that are conceptually close. Use field transformations a proposed receptor should not detect. For a polarity-sensitive mechanism, compare axis-preserving reversals. For a radical-pair mechanism, include wavelengths outside effective absorption bands. Create synthetic stimuli whose coil currents resemble active trials while magnetic fields cancel at the head.

Finally, publish the failures.

A sixth-sense literature cannot afford a file drawer. The combination of small effects, many stimulus dimensions and individual heterogeneity is a perfect factory for selective reporting. Registered reports and shared raw data should be standard.

The reward for all this discipline is not merely avoiding embarrassment.

If the effect is real, rigorous nulls will map its boundaries. A sensory mechanism becomes visible by the conditions under which it disappears.

The goal is not to make magnetoreception harder to believe.

The goal is to make belief irrelevant.
