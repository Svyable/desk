# 7. A Protein That Can Notice North

In 2011 Lauren Foley, Robert Gegear and Steven Reppert published one of those experiments that is both less and more exciting than its headline.

They took human cryptochrome 2—CRY2, a protein expressed in the human retina—and put it into fruit flies engineered so that their own cryptochrome-dependent magnetic response was impaired. Human CRY2 restored light-dependent magnetosensitivity in that fly system.

The result did not show that human beings consciously sense magnetic fields. It did not even show that human CRY2 is used for magnetoreception in humans. What it demonstrated was narrower: a human protein can participate in a biological magnetosensory pathway under the right cellular conditions.

That matters because magnetoreception had often been discussed as though humans lacked the molecular parts. The fly experiment said at least one candidate part had not disappeared.

Cryptochromes are flavoproteins best known for roles in circadian biology. In many magnetoreception models, light creates a pair of radicals whose electron spins evolve in ways weakly influenced by magnetic fields. The chemical yield can then depend on field orientation. The mechanism is attractive because it offers a route from a field too weak to move ordinary tissue into molecular chemistry operating near the relevant energy scale.

It is also easy to romanticize. The word quantum appears and immediately lowers the reader’s skepticism while raising everyone’s appetite for grand interpretation.

The right response is almost the opposite. Radical-pair magnetoreception is interesting precisely because it is constrained. It predicts dependencies on light, field geometry, reaction kinetics and radiofrequency perturbation. The mechanism can be made to fail.

Human CRY2 complicates the story because mammalian cryptochromes are not simple copies of the canonical light-sensitive insect proteins. Whether human cryptochrome binds the relevant cofactor under physiological conditions, whether the chemistry is sufficiently long-lived and anisotropic at Earth strength, and whether any resulting signal reaches a human sensory circuit remain open.

The 2019 EEG result added another complication. Wang and colleagues reported polarity sensitivity. The simplest radical-pair compass is axial: reversing north and south while preserving the field axis should not matter. The authors therefore argued that their human response did not fit that simple quantum-compass mechanism and was more compatible with a ferromagnetic sensor.

This should not be turned into a funeral for cryptochrome. “The simplest version of this mechanism does not explain this particular reported response” is not the same as “cryptochrome cannot contribute to human magnetosensitivity.” There could be multiple sensors. There could be downstream asymmetries. The fly result could reflect molecular capability unused in humans. Or the EEG effect could fail replication.

The useful tension is that two legitimate experiments point in different mechanistic directions.

The 2011 experiment says a human protein can carry magnetic information in another animal’s sensory architecture. The 2019 experiment says the reported human neural response has a property not expected from the simplest version of that chemistry.

That is what frontier science should look like before it becomes a textbook. The pieces do not yet click into a single diagram.

A decisive human cryptochrome experiment would need more than correlating behavior with blue light. It would manipulate spectral conditions, field axis, polarity and weak radiofrequency interference while measuring a preregistered neural response. Ideally it would connect genotype, retinal chemistry and physiology. If a radical-pair pathway is operating, the dependencies should hang together quantitatively.

If they do not, human CRY2 remains what the fly experiment actually proved it to be: a molecule with an intriguing capacity, waiting for evidence that the human organism ever uses it that way.
