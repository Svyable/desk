# The Compass We Forgot

## Front Matter

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There are senses we possess so completely that we confuse them with reality. Light becomes color. Pressure waves become sound. Molecules become smell. The machinery disappears behind the world it makes.

Magnetism is different. Earth is wrapped in a field strong enough to turn a suspended needle, weak enough that most of us live our entire lives without once feeling it. Yet birds cross oceans, turtles return to natal beaches, bacteria align with field lines, insects alter behavior, fish and mammals appear to use magnetic information. Somewhere in biology, fifty microtesla is not nothing.

The unnerving question is whether it is nothing to us.

This book does not begin from the claim that humans possess a hidden compass. That sentence is too easy and too imprecise. It begins from a stranger collection of facts. Human brains contain magnetic minerals, although some are almost certainly pollution and none has been shown to be a receptor. A human cryptochrome protein can participate in magnetosensitivity when placed into a fly, although that does not prove a human magnetic sense. In 2019, a Caltech–University of Tokyo team reported that controlled rotations of an Earth-strength magnetic field produced a repeatable drop in alpha-band EEG power in some people, with polarity and vertical-field dependencies that argued against several simple artifacts. The subjects did not consciously feel north move.

If that result is real, the interesting thing is not that people are secretly pigeons. It is that a sensory channel can survive without becoming experience.

The history is useful because it is full of embarrassment. Robin Baker spent the 1970s and 1980s blindfolding people, driving them around, spinning them in chairs and asking where home was. Some results looked extraordinary. Replication attempts failed. Alleged magnetic deposits in human sinuses turned out to be contamination. The subject became an example of how an alluring biological claim can outrun its controls.

Then the animal literature got harder to dismiss. Then molecular biophysics got stranger. Then the human EEG result arrived.

The book is about that sequence: not vindication, but the reopening of a question after the original version of the question had earned skepticism.

The evidence classes used throughout are explicit.

**Class 1 — verified/documented:** robust animal magnetoreception; geomagnetic-field physics; magnetite and related ferrimagnetic material measured in human tissue; human CRY2’s magnetosensitive function in a Drosophila system; the published 2019 human EEG experiment and its controls; the existence and goals of DARPA’s RadioBio program; well-documented failures and contamination problems in candidate-receptor research.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete:** the interpretation that the 2019 EEG alpha-event-related desynchronization reflects sensory transduction; some human visual-threshold and neurophysiological effects of Earth-strength fields; the possibility that a residual human magnetic sense is unconscious or vestigial.

**Class 3 — disputed/unconfirmed:** reliable human magnetic navigation; a specific magnetite-containing receptor cell in humans; cryptochrome as the operative human receptor; claims that weak environmental magnetic fields routinely affect health or cognition through a magnetoreceptive pathway; a reproducible ferritin-based magnetic remote-control mechanism.

**Class 4 — speculative synthesis:** humans may retain a neural detector whose original behavioral function has degraded; urban electromagnetic environments may mask or detune a weak sense; population differences could reflect developmental geomagnetic ecology; a correct human result may require treating magnetoreception as a distributed sensor rather than looking for one organ.

The standard is not whether a claim sounds weird. The standard is whether the experiment can lose.
