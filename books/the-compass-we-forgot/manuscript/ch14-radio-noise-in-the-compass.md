# 14. Radio Noise in the Compass

A magnetic sense evolved under a planet, not under a router.

That statement invites two opposite mistakes. One is to conclude that modern electromagnetic noise must be scrambling human biology. The other is to assume that technical fields are irrelevant because most are far weaker than those used in obvious stimulation experiments.

The animal magnetoreception literature makes the middle position unavoidable. In some bird experiments, weak radiofrequency fields have disrupted magnetic compass orientation. The reported intensities can be surprisingly small, in some cases far below what simple radical-pair theory would predict. A major 2026 review treats this mismatch as one of the field’s unresolved mechanistic problems rather than as settled proof of a particular quantum receptor.

For human research, radiofrequency interference could be both biology and confound.

If a radical-pair mechanism contributes to magnetosensitivity, ambient spectral noise might alter its reliability. Two laboratories running nominally identical static-field protocols could obtain different results because their RF environments differ. A shielded chamber might produce a stronger effect not merely because it suppresses EEG interference but because it changes the biological stimulus environment.

That possibility is testable.

A replication should record broadband electromagnetic spectra during every session. It should deliberately add weak RF fields at preregistered frequencies and amplitudes. The sham should reproduce transmitter operation without the relevant field at the participant. If the magnetic EEG response changes, the frequency dependence should be compared with concrete spin-chemical predictions rather than described loosely as “EMF sensitivity.”

This boundary is important because magnetoreception can easily be hijacked by the enormous literature on electromagnetic hypersensitivity, wireless fears and nonspecific symptoms attributed to technical fields. A validated magnetic receptor would not validate those claims. Sensory detection is not injury. Disruption of a navigation cue is not toxicity. An effect on reaction chemistry is not evidence of disease.

But the receptor question could improve the broader conversation by making exposure variables specific. Biology rarely responds to the unit “electromagnetic field” as one thing. Static fields, low-frequency oscillations, radiofrequency carriers, pulses, gradients and induced currents are physically different interventions.

Magnetoreception researchers already live inside those distinctions because their mechanisms require them.

The city therefore becomes a useful experimental contrast rather than a villain. Compare participants or animals across low-noise and high-noise environments while measuring the spectrum. Move the same chamber. Introduce controlled noise. Test whether any effect adapts.

If nothing changes, the claim that modern electromagnetic environments suppress a hidden human compass should be weakened.

If something changes reproducibly, the effect still has to be explained without turning every antenna into a threat.

A compass can be jammed without the traveler being poisoned.
