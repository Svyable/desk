# Chapter 10 — The Inherited Crowd

The first thing a newborn inherits is not solitude.

A baby arrives into a population.

Some members are human: parents, siblings, nurses, relatives, neighbors. Some are microscopic. Bacteria and other microorganisms begin colonizing surfaces and body sites through birth, feeding, touch, environment, food, caregivers, household contact, and countless later encounters.

The word *microbiome* has become so familiar that it can create a new version of the soul problem.

People say, “We are more microbe than human,” or “Your microbes control your personality,” and a complicated ecology becomes another hidden agent supposedly operating the person from inside.

The reality is more interesting and less theatrical.

Human-associated microbial communities are genuine parts of our biology. They participate in digestion, metabolism, immune development, colonization resistance, and many other processes. Their composition varies by body site, age, diet, geography, medication, disease, household, and environment. They are neither a fixed organ nor a random contamination.

They also move between people.

A large 2023 *Nature* study led by Mireia Valles-Colomer analyzed more than 9,700 human metagenomes and looked for bacterial strains shared among individuals. The researchers found substantial person-to-person sharing with distinct patterns. Mother-to-infant gut transmission was prominent early in life. Oral strains showed extensive horizontal sharing, especially among people who lived together. Cohabitation time mattered.

Your microbial history is partly social history.

That sentence is strange enough without exaggeration.

Two people sharing a house do not become one organism because some bacterial strains are shared. A baby does not inherit a mother's microbiome the way a computer copies a folder. Microbial communities assemble, compete, disappear, return, and respond to a changing environment. Strains can be transmitted from several people and sources. Diet and medication can alter the ecology. Later contacts matter.

The child receives a crowd, not a clone.

This gives us another inheritance system with rules unlike genetics.

DNA sequence is primarily transmitted vertically through reproduction.

Microorganisms can move vertically in a broad family sense, but also horizontally among unrelated people, through cohabitation, food, environments, and other contacts. The boundaries are porous. One person can acquire living descendants of microbes carried by another person without acquiring any human genetic relation to that person.

Inheritance does not always follow the family tree.

Culture will make that point even more dramatically in the next chapter.

The microbiome makes it biological first.

It also complicates what we mean by “my body.”

A human organism has a developmental lineage. Most of its cells descend from the fertilized egg, with important exceptions and complications. The associated microbiota have many lineages, acquired at different times, capable of reproducing on their own terms. Some are transient. Some are long-term residents. Some become harmful in one context and benign in another. Some metabolic functions depend on community structure rather than one species.

If personal identity required complete identity of biological occupants, you would be replaced constantly.

It does not.

The body is an ecosystem with a proprietor we identify at another level.

Even “proprietor” is misleading. You do not consciously govern most of the ecology. Immune systems, chemistry, anatomy, diet, microbial competition, and chance help shape it.

The self has borders without total control.

That is a useful correction to the way reincarnation imagines a self as a sealed packet.

We are not sealed during life.

We exchange matter with the world every second.

We inhale other organisms and particles. We eat molecules assembled by other organisms. We acquire microbes. We shed cells. We exchange viruses. We learn words. We absorb norms. We incorporate medicines. We are altered by people we touch and people we never meet.

The boundary around a person is biologically necessary and causally leaky.

Microchimerism makes the leak more literal.

During pregnancy, cells can cross the placenta in both directions. Fetal-origin cells can persist in a mother's body after pregnancy; maternal cells can persist in offspring. Researchers continue to investigate what these cells do and how they relate to health and disease. The phenomenon is real enough to make a philosophical point without attaching speculative meaning to every finding.

A mother can carry cells that originated in a child.

A child can carry cells that originated in a mother.

The phrase “part of you lives in me” can be biologically true in a narrow cellular sense.

It is not personal reincarnation.

The cells do not contain the other person's whole mind.

But microchimerism reveals how crude the folk boundary can be. “My cells” and “your cells” do not always map perfectly onto “my body” and “your body.” Pregnancy creates biological traffic whose traces can persist.

Bone marrow transplantation creates another kind of chimerism. A recipient's blood-forming system can become donor-derived. Forensic and medical genetics must account for this because different tissues in one individual can yield DNA associated with different people.

Again, the conclusion is not mystical.

The conclusion is that identity operates at levels.

Genetic identity of a cell is not personal identity of a human.

A donor-derived blood cell belongs functionally to the recipient's living system after transplantation even though its nuclear genome descends from the donor.

This is what nested identity looks like in biology.

It also warns us against a recurring error in stories about transplant recipients.

There are reports of people developing new preferences or vivid experiences after organ transplantation and interpreting them as traits from donors. Popular accounts sometimes call this *cellular memory*. The term sounds mechanistic before a mechanism has been shown.

Cells have many forms of persistent state. Immune cells can retain memory-like responses. Epigenetic states can persist. Tissues carry biological histories. That does not establish that a heart stores a donor's taste in music or that a kidney contains autobiographical memories available to a recipient.

Extraordinary personal correspondences require more than the fact that cells can remember things in a cellular sense.

The carrier must have the bandwidth.

A heart is richly innervated and participates in a body-wide regulatory system. Transplantation profoundly changes a person's physiology and life. Immunosuppressive drugs can have effects. Recovery can change appetite and activity. Knowing details about a donor can shape interpretation. Living with another person's organ can change self-concept.

There are many routes for change.

The idea that the organ carried personality should therefore compete with ordinary pathways rather than leap over them.

This book will keep returning to bandwidth because reincarnation claims often ask a small carrier to move a large person.

Genes are asked to carry trauma narratives.

Organs are asked to carry memories.

Names are asked to carry identity.

Digital text is asked to carry consciousness.

The microbiome is asked to carry mood or personality as though one microbial composition maps to one mind.

The more serious approach asks what information a system can plausibly encode and transmit.

Microbiomes absolutely can transmit biological consequences.

A strain carries a genome. Microbial metabolism can produce compounds that interact with the host. Community composition can affect ecological functions. Pathogens can spread. Antibiotic-resistance genes can spread. Colonization can alter later microbial assembly.

Those are substantial forms of inheritance.

They do not require the microbe to know whose grandmother it came from.

This is pattern reincarnation in a particularly literal form: a living lineage moves from one human carrier to another and continues reproducing.

The microbe reincarnates more straightforwardly than the person.

That line sounds like a joke, but it exposes scale again.

When a bacterium divides, the descendants continue a cellular lineage through replication. When bacteria move between humans, the lineage can persist across host boundaries. The human hosts are environments in that story.

In the human story, the bacteria are inhabitants.

Which story is “the” story depends on scale.

This matters for evolutionary thinking. Humans are not independent of microbial evolution. Our behaviors create microbial transmission networks. Sanitation changes them. Antibiotics change them. Cesarean delivery, feeding practices, household size, travel, diet, hospitals, sewage systems, agriculture, and urbanization all change microbial ecologies.

Culture alters the conditions of biological inheritance.

The inherited crowd therefore gives us a bridge from genes to culture.

A baby inherits microbes partly because humans have caregiving practices.

A household shares strains partly because people choose or are forced to live together.

Foodways determine repeated exposures.

Medicine changes selection pressures.

Built environments determine contact.

Biology and culture cross the same doorway.

This is why simple nature-versus-nurture language fails so quickly.

A cultural practice can change a microbial exposure that changes immune development that changes later health.

A biological vulnerability can change family behavior that changes social learning.

A genetic variant can influence diet preference that changes microbiome composition that changes metabolites.

Causation loops.

Reincarnation stories prefer arrows.

Person A dies, then Person B appears.

Inheritance systems are usually networks.

A child receives from two biological parents, several grandparents through social contact, siblings, peers, teachers, media, microbes, institutions, and environments. The pattern that later resembles one ancestor may have been assembled from many sources.

That is why the phrase “you are your grandmother” can be emotionally true and causally wrong.

You may carry a few of her DNA segments, a microbial strain that traveled through household lineage, a recipe she taught your parent, a name inherited through family choice, money from an estate, and stories about what she valued.

The recurrence is real.

The singular source is invented.

Human identity emerges from crowds of causes.

This also changes how we think about originality.

We like the idea that the self is authored from inside. Even secular culture often preserves a miniature soul in the form of autonomous individuality: my thoughts, my tastes, my body, my voice, my decisions.

Those possessives are socially and legally useful.

Causally, they are crowded.

Your vocabulary came from other speakers.

Your immune system developed in response to organisms and exposures.

Your microbiome is partly acquired from other people and environments.

Your genome is inherited.

Your first beliefs about normality came from a household.

Your body was built through a pregnancy you did not direct.

Autonomy develops inside inheritance.

That does not make autonomy fake.

It makes it an achievement rather than an origin condition.

A child begins as a new organism already dependent on patterns older than the organism. Over time, the child can inspect some of those patterns, reject some, amplify some, recombine them, and create new ones.

This is where the crowd becomes a self.

Not by becoming pure.

By becoming organized enough to choose among influences.

The analogy to immune identity is tempting and should be handled carefully. Immune systems distinguish, imperfectly and dynamically, among self, non-self, tolerated organisms, threats, damaged cells, and other states. The system is not a philosopher of personal identity. Yet its existence reminds us that biological selfhood is active boundary management rather than isolation.

The body is constantly deciding what may remain.

Culture will reveal a similar process at another scale.

A society receives traditions and innovations. It does not preserve all of them. Some are selected, taught, ritualized, institutionalized, commercialized, banned, forgotten, or rediscovered. People function as transmission nodes, but they are also filters.

The microbiome's lesson is therefore not that we are secretly collective organisms in some totalizing sense.

It is that inheritance can be intimate without being identical.

Other lineages can live with us.

Other people's cells can persist in us.

Microbes can cross households.

Biological material can have a social history.

The border of one human life is permeable long before death.

That makes the ancient image of reincarnation—a complete traveler leaving one closed vessel and entering another—feel increasingly unlike ordinary biology.

Ordinary biology is messier.

Things cross all the time.

They cross partially.

They cross through contact.

They cross under selection.

They change after arrival.

They coexist with material from many other sources.

A new organism is not an empty house waiting for an old tenant.

The house is already crowded before it learns its own name.
