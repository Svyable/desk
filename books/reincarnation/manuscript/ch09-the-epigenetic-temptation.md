# Chapter 9 — The Epigenetic Temptation

Every generation wants a mechanism for the feeling that families inherit more than genes.

For a while, epigenetics seemed ready to become that mechanism for almost everything.

A grandparent starved. A grandchild has a metabolic problem. Epigenetics.

A parent experienced trauma. A child is anxious. Epigenetics.

A family repeats a behavioral pattern nobody can explain. Epigenetics.

The word acquired the prestige of molecular biology and the flexibility of folklore.

That combination is dangerous.

Epigenetics is real. The popular version often is not.

At its broadest, epigenetics concerns heritable or persistent changes in gene regulation and cellular state that are not changes in the underlying DNA sequence. DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin organization, regulatory RNAs, and related processes help cells with essentially the same genome maintain very different identities and responses. A liver cell and a neuron do not become different because one discarded most of the genome. They use the genome differently.

Development depends on regulated memory.

A cell divides and daughter cells often need to preserve aspects of cell type. Regulatory states can persist. Environmental signals can alter gene expression. In plants and several experimental organisms, epigenetic information can cross generations in ways biologists can measure.

Then humans make the problem unusually difficult.

The first reason is biological reset.

Mammalian reproduction includes extensive epigenetic reprogramming. Many methylation patterns are erased and rebuilt during germ-cell development and early embryogenesis. Some signals can escape or influence the next generation through other mechanisms, but a claim that a specific acquired epigenetic mark moves stably through multiple human generations has to survive those reprogramming events.

The second reason is that humans do not reproduce in laboratories.

We inherit environments with our genes.

A woman exposed to famine may later raise children under economic, dietary, medical, and cultural conditions shaped by that famine. A man who survives violence may change his parenting, neighborhood, stress physiology, relationships, work, substance use, beliefs, and resources. His children can be affected through many routes before any germline epigenetic mechanism is considered.

Grandparents affect grandchildren directly too. They provide care, money, housing, stories, food, rules, stress, and social position. Families share geography. Social policies persist. Discrimination persists. Wealth persists. Pollution persists.

Human generations are causally entangled.

That is what makes them interesting and what makes strong molecular claims hard.

Bernhard Horsthemke's 2018 critical review of transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans made the problem explicit. Such inheritance is clearly documented in some nonhuman systems, but human evidence is confounded by genetic, ecological, and cultural transmission. Even technically strong animal studies may await independent replication, and translating them into claims about human multigenerational inheritance requires care.

A 2026 review in *Trends in Genetics* put the point more directly: epigenetic inheritance remains unproven in humans, in large part because of biological barriers and the difficulty of separating germline transmission from social, environmental, and genetic pathways.

This does not mean there are no intergenerational biological effects in humans.

There obviously are.

A mother's physiology during pregnancy affects a developing fetus. Nutritional state matters. Hormones matter. toxins can matter. Disease can matter. Maternal antibodies cross the placenta. The prenatal environment is biological inheritance in a broad sense even when it is not *transgenerational epigenetic inheritance* in the strict germline sense.

The language matters because generations overlap.

If a pregnant woman is exposed to an environmental factor, her fetus is directly exposed. If that fetus is female, the developing germ cells that may someday contribute to grandchildren are also present during the pregnancy. Calling an effect in the grandchild “transgenerational” without specifying which generation was directly exposed can therefore exaggerate what crossed an unexposed generational boundary.

The problem becomes more complicated in paternal studies, where sperm can carry molecular consequences of environment or physiology. Animal research has implicated small RNAs, methylation, chromatin, and other mechanisms in some intergenerational effects. That work is scientifically important.

It is not a permission slip to say trauma is stored in sperm as memory.

A traumatic episode has narrative content: who was there, what happened, what it meant, what was feared, what was lost.

An epigenetic mark is molecular state.

Even if an exposure changes sperm RNA and that change alters offspring development, the sperm is not carrying an autobiographical recording of the event. It is carrying a biological consequence.

This distinction sounds obvious until popular language erases it.

We say the body “remembers” trauma.

Sometimes that is a useful metaphor. Nervous systems learn. Stress responses can change. Immune systems adapt. Hormonal regulation shifts. Avoidance behavior becomes automatic. People can react physically to cues before consciously narrating why.

But metaphorical memory can slide into literal inheritance without a mechanism.

“The body remembers” becomes “the genes remember.”

“The genes remember” becomes “the grandchildren remember.”

Three rhetorical steps later, a family resemblance has become molecular reincarnation.

The evidence rarely traveled that far.

Pattern reincarnation requires us to follow the carrier, and epigenetics is where that discipline earns its keep.

Suppose a grandmother survived severe food scarcity and decades later her grandchild has elevated metabolic risk.

There are multiple causal pathways to examine.

Shared DNA variants may influence both generations.

The grandmother's nutritional state may have affected a pregnancy directly.

Her child may have grown up with particular food availability or eating practices.

Economic conditions associated with the famine may have persisted.

The family's later socioeconomic status may be affected.

The grandmother may transmit beliefs about food, waste, body size, or security.

A germline epigenetic pathway may also be hypothesized.

The presence of that hypothesis does not make the other channels noise.

They are inheritance.

This is the larger revelation.

Human beings are so good at cultural transmission that proving a weak molecular inheritance effect may be harder precisely because the social effect is large.

Horsthemke made a version of this point: even if molecular mechanisms can transmit epigenetic information across human generations, cultural transmission through communication, imitation, teaching, and learning may overwhelm our ability to detect the epigenetic contribution.

The irony is almost perfect.

We reach for epigenetics because ordinary social inheritance seems too intangible.

Then ordinary social inheritance becomes the confounder preventing us from seeing epigenetics clearly.

Culture hides in the control group.

This should change how we talk about intergenerational trauma.

The phrase describes something real enough at the family and social level. Harm can recur across generations. Adults who experienced maltreatment as children can face elevated risks in later parenting, although the size of the association varies by study and methodological quality, and many people do not repeat the pattern. Meta-analyses find continuity, not destiny.

That last distinction matters most.

If you tell a survivor that trauma is written into descendants' biology, you may intend validation. You can accidentally create fatalism.

The biology is not settled enough for that certainty.

The psychology is not destiny either.

Intergenerational risk is a population pattern. Individuals break it constantly.

Protective relationships matter. Treatment can matter. Social support matters. Economic conditions matter. Choice matters. Different partners and communities matter. A parent can know what happened and deliberately construct a different environment.

The pattern can fail to reincarnate.

This is where the epigenetic temptation becomes morally consequential.

People like molecular explanations because molecules feel objective. A family story can be dismissed as soft. A methylation mark feels hard.

But a mechanism does not become more important merely because it is smaller.

If a grandmother teaches her daughter to fear hunger and the daughter teaches a child to stock food obsessively, the transmission is real. It does not need a methyl group to become respectable.

If housing discrimination concentrates disadvantage for generations, the mechanism is real. It does not need an inherited molecular scar to matter.

If a father's trauma changes his ability to regulate anger and that shapes the emotional environment of his children, the pathway is biological, psychological, and social at once. Demanding that the effect pass through sperm before calling it intergenerational misunderstands the system.

Scale is not seriousness.

This also explains why epigenetics attracts reincarnation language so easily.

It promises a bridge between experience and heredity.

Classical genetics makes a hard distinction: the sequence inherited by children is not ordinarily rewritten to encode the events of a parent's life. Epigenetic research softens that boundary because environmental state can influence gene regulation and, in some organisms and circumstances, some of those influences can affect descendants.

The temptation is to hear Lamarck returning.

Lamarck is often caricatured as the man who thought giraffes stretched their necks and handed the stretch to their offspring. Historical Lamarckian thought was more complex, but the modern shorthand survives: acquired characteristics inherited directly.

Epigenetics is sometimes marketed as proof that Darwin was wrong and Lamarck was right.

That is a bad framing.

Evolutionary biology already includes plasticity, parental effects, niche construction, culture, maternal effects, and several forms of non-genetic inheritance alongside DNA sequence. Epigenetic inheritance can be incorporated into evolutionary theory without turning every acquired feature into a heritable one or replacing natural selection.

The reality is richer than a nineteenth-century rematch.

For this book, the important point is that inheritance has layers.

Genetic sequence can persist.

Regulatory states can persist across cell division.

Some biological effects can cross generations.

Prenatal environments influence development.

Parents construct environments.

Culture transmits learned information.

Institutions preserve conditions.

Wealth and disadvantage shape opportunity.

All of these can produce recurrence.

A child can therefore carry consequences of an ancestor without carrying the ancestor.

That sentence should become almost boring by now.

Boring is progress.

It means we no longer need a totalizing mechanism.

The strongest version of pattern reincarnation is not “everything is inherited.”

It is the opposite: different things are inherited through different channels with different error rates, durations, and constraints.

Epigenetics is one possible channel for particular biological states.

It is not the underground railway for personality.

This becomes clear if we ask a deliberately unfair question.

Where is the epigenetic mark for a joke?

There is none in any useful sense.

The joke travels through language.

Where is the methylation pattern for a surname?

There is none.

The surname travels through social convention and law.

Where is the histone modification for a trust fund?

There is none.

The wealth travels through institutions.

Where is the sperm RNA for a family's rule that nobody discusses divorce?

The rule can cross generations perfectly well without one.

Human inheritance is multi-channel.

The molecular channel receives disproportionate attention because it looks deepest.

Depth is another misleading metaphor.

A law can affect millions of bodies.

A story can change reproductive behavior.

A religious rule can shape diet for centuries.

A school system can alter cognitive development across a population.

A tax policy can reorganize intergenerational wealth.

Culture reaches biology from the outside all the time.

Gene–culture coevolution makes this literal over longer timescales. Human practices can change selection pressures. The classic examples include subsistence practices interacting with genetic adaptations. The causal arrow runs both ways: biology shapes what culture can do, and culture changes environments in which biological traits matter.

There is no clean border where inheritance stops being “real.”

There are mechanisms.

Some are molecular.

Some are behavioral.

Some are institutional.

The responsible question is not which one sounds scientific.

It is which one the evidence supports.

That standard will make some readers unhappy because it removes an emotionally satisfying claim. People who feel they carry the pain of previous generations may hear scientific caution about epigenetics as denial of their experience.

It should not be.

You do not need germline molecular proof to be shaped by a family past.

You can inherit a silence.

You can inherit poverty.

You can inherit a vigilance taught by a parent who needed it once.

You can inherit a neighborhood produced by earlier policy.

You can inherit a funeral ritual, a coping style, a relationship to authority, a fear of institutions, a pattern of saving, a pattern of drinking, a pattern of tenderness.

These are not lesser inheritances because they are carried in behavior and structure.

They may be easier to change precisely because they are visible.

The epigenetic temptation says the deepest truth is hidden in chemistry.

Sometimes it is.

Human beings have another habit.

We write the past into one another in plain sight.
