# Research Brief — Reincarnation

## Purpose

This book separates three questions that are often collapsed: whether consequential patterns can recur across carriers; whether psychological, biological, cultural, legal, or digital continuity exists; and whether any of those continuities establish numerical personal identity or survival of consciousness after death.

The first two can often be studied empirically. The third includes philosophical and metaphysical questions that should not be settled by rhetorical overreach.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. Original scientific papers, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses.
2. Primary philosophical and religious texts plus high-quality academic scholarship interpreting them.
3. Government agencies, courts, standards bodies, and official platform documentation for law and technical policy.
4. Peer-reviewed ethics and social-science research.
5. High-quality secondary reporting for context, never as sole support for a disputed technical claim.

## Claim rules

- Separate observation from explanation.
- Separate resemblance from causal descent.
- Separate causal descent from numerical identity.
- Treat memory confidence, vividness, and emotion as psychologically meaningful but not self-authenticating.
- For childhood previous-life reports, record chronology, information access, misses, base rates, and post-identification contamination.
- Do not use the seven-year cell-replacement myth.
- Do not describe DNA, epigenetic marks, transplanted organs, or microbiota as carriers of autobiographical memory without direct evidence.
- Distinguish intergenerational effects from strict transgenerational germline inheritance.
- Treat cultural and institutional inheritance as mechanisms, not as proof that organisms are interchangeable.
- For digital replicas, distinguish retrieval from generation, preserved agency from synthetic agency, functional similarity from consciousness, and both from numerical identity.

## Chapter source trail

### Chapters 1–2 — identity and claimed previous-life memories

- University of Virginia Division of Perceptual Studies, research program on children who report memories of previous lives. Useful as the primary institutional source for the case archive and its investigators' own characterization of the phenomenon.
- Tucker, Jim B. (2008), review of children's reports of past-life memories, *Explore*. Useful for recurring features and methodological framing.
- Academic scoping review (2021) of studies of alleged past-life memories, identifying the predominance of child cases, case-report designs, interviews, and the need for stronger methods.
- Contemporary DOPS materials should be used for current project descriptions, not as independent confirmation of the reincarnation hypothesis.

**Falsification note:** A large archive of investigated cases is not a count of verified reincarnations. Stronger cases narrow ordinary information routes; they do not by themselves establish personal identity transfer.

### Chapter 3 — memory

- Josselyn, Köhler & Frankland, reviews of memory engrams and distributed neural substrates.
- Nader, Schafe & LeDoux (2000), reconsolidation work in fear memory; later reviews for boundary conditions.
- Source-monitoring literature associated with Johnson and colleagues.
- Developmental and forensic-interview literature on suggestion and children's reports.
- Reviews of experimentally induced false autobiographical memory, with careful attention to method and definition.

**Falsification note:** The chapter does not claim that every act of retrieval rewrites every memory, that all childhood reports are suggestible, or that vivid memories are false. It argues that content alone does not establish provenance.

### Chapters 4–5 — rebirth, self, and causal continuity

- Primary and scholarly sources on Hindu accounts of ātman and rebirth, Jain jīva and karma, and Buddhist anattā/not-self, dependent arising, karma, and rebirth.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on personal identity and relevant Indian/Buddhist philosophy for orientation and bibliography.
- Primary Buddhist sources should be used when making claims about doctrine; modern pattern inheritance must not be presented as equivalent to Buddhist rebirth.

**Falsification note:** Analogies such as flames, rivers, and causal streams show that continuity without material stasis is conceptually possible; they are not laboratory mechanisms for rebirth.

### Chapters 6–7 — cell turnover and nested biological continuity

- Spalding et al. (2005), *Cell*, retrospective birth dating of human cells using atmospheric carbon-14 and evidence that studied occipital-cortex neurons were as old as the individuals.
- Sender & Milo (2021), analysis of human cell turnover rates and mass turnover.
- Literature on hematopoietic chimerism after bone-marrow transplantation and maternal/fetal microchimerism.

**Falsification note:** Tissue turnover varies. The manuscript rejects a universal seven-year replacement schedule and does not infer an immaterial self from cellular turnover.

### Chapter 8 — genetics

- Standard genetics literature on meiosis, recombination, segregation, polygenic traits, and gene–environment interaction.
- Population-genetics work distinguishing genealogical from genetic ancestry and showing that genealogical ancestors need not contribute identifiable DNA segments after enough generations.
- Behavioral-genetics references for the correct interpretation of heritability as a population statistic under specified conditions.

**Falsification note:** Genetic influence is real; the chapter rejects the stronger claim that genomes encode complete ancestors, autobiographical memories, or fixed personal destiny.

### Chapter 9 — epigenetics

- Horsthemke, Bernhard (2018), critical review of transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans.
- Current review literature on epigenetic reprogramming in mammalian germ cells and embryos.
- 2026 review literature emphasizing that stable human transgenerational epigenetic inheritance remains difficult to establish because of biological reset and confounding genetic, prenatal, ecological, and cultural pathways.

**Falsification note:** The chapter does not deny epigenetic regulation, parental effects, prenatal effects, or experimentally demonstrated inheritance in nonhuman systems. It rejects upgrading those facts into inherited autobiographical trauma without evidence.

### Chapter 10 — microbiome and chimerism

- Valles-Colomer et al. (2023), *Nature*, person-to-person transmission of human gut and oral microbiome strains across mother–infant and cohabitation relationships.
- Reviews of maternal/fetal microchimerism and clinical sources on donor-derived hematopoiesis after transplantation.

**Falsification note:** Shared strains, donor-derived cells, or fetal-origin cells do not establish transfer of personal identity or autobiographical memory.

### Chapter 11 — cultural inheritance

- Whiten and broader cultural-evolution literature on socially learned traditions and culture as a second inheritance system.
- Gene–culture coevolution literature for multiple transmission pathways and cumulative culture.

**Falsification note:** Culture is not DNA made of ideas. Similarity can arise by convergence; a cultural-descent claim needs a transmission path.

### Chapter 12 — family roles

- 2023 systematic review literature on parentification, emphasizing variation in role, fairness, context, and outcomes.
- Meta-analytic literature on intergenerational continuity of child maltreatment, emphasizing elevated average risk without determinism and sensitivity to study design.

**Falsification note:** The systems perspective does not imply every trait is a family role or that harmful patterns inevitably recur.

### Chapter 13 — naming and symbolic inheritance

- Qualitative scholarship on adults named after deceased relatives and broader onomastic research on names as social signals.
- Sociological and psychological literature on names, identity, discrimination, belonging, and commemorative practice.

**Falsification note:** A name can shape social treatment without containing the named person's identity. Specific 'name effects' require careful causal interpretation.

### Chapter 14 — wealth and agency inheritance

- OECD, *Inheritance Taxation in OECD Countries* and related work on wealth concentration, inheritances, gifts, and equality of opportunity.
- Trusts-and-estates scholarship on dead-hand control, perpetuities, charitable purpose, and posthumous property instructions.

**Falsification note:** The chapter does not claim all debts legally pass to heirs or that inheritance has one uniform effect on every measure of inequality. Jurisdiction and institutional design matter.

### Chapter 15 — institutional memory

- Organizational research on routines, transactive memory systems, knowledge retention, member turnover, and organizational inertia.
- Legal scholarship on corporate/juridical personality for the distinction between person-like legal action and human consciousness.

**Falsification note:** Institutions are not claimed to be conscious organisms. 'Memory' and 'person' are functional/legal terms at this level and must not be smuggled into psychological equivalence.

### Chapter 16 — personal identity and branching

- Parfit, Derek, *Reasons and Persons*, for fission, psychological continuity, and the argument that identity may not be what matters.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 'Personal Identity,' for biological, psychological, and non-reductionist approaches and branching problems.

**Falsification note:** Thought experiments expose conceptual consequences; they do not demonstrate that mind uploading is technically possible.

### Chapter 17 — digital remains

- Apple Support, 'Request access to a deceased family member’s Apple Account' and Legacy Contact documentation. As of August 2026 Apple describes Legacy Contact as a route for authorized access to certain stored account data after death, subject to documentation and data-category limits.
- Google Account Help, 'About Inactive Account Manager' and deceased-user account request documentation. Google permits users to designate people to receive selected data after inactivity and describes separate review processes for deceased-user requests.
- Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska (2024), *Philosophy & Technology*, 'Griefbots, Deadbots, Postmortem Avatars,' on ethical design of generative AI in the digital-afterlife industry.

**Falsification note:** Account access is platform- and jurisdiction-dependent. Possession of data does not imply ownership of every relational datum or permission to synthesize a person from it.

### Chapter 18 — voice and generative replicas

- U.S. Federal Trade Commission, 2023–2024 materials on AI-enabled voice cloning, fraud, impersonation, biometric misuse, authentication, watermarking, detection, and the absence of a single technical fix.
- Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska (2024) for consent, transparency, retirement, and vulnerable-user concerns in postmortem avatars.

**Falsification note:** The ability to imitate voice or style establishes functional similarity, not transfer of subjective consciousness. Generated statements about new events are inferences, not recorded posthumous decisions by the source person.

### Chapters 19–20 — ethics and legacy

These chapters synthesize earlier mechanisms. Normative proposals—right to diverge, provenance, non-conscription, retirement, contextual integrity, and protection against counterfeit authority—are arguments of the book rather than empirical findings. They should be presented as proposals and stress-tested against autonomy, grief, privacy, expression, property, and possible future AI moral status.

## Editorial boundary

The book may end with uncertainty about literal reincarnation. It should not end with uncertainty about its own categories.

A resemblance is not automatically a lineage.

A lineage is not automatically a person.

A copy is not automatically survival.

A mystery is not automatically a miracle.

And a pattern that returns is not automatically entitled to continue.
