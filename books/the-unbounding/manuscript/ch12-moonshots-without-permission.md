# Moonshots Without Permission

**October 2024**

The startup pitch was for a "global weather control system." Not science fiction. Real engineering.

"How?" I asked.

"AI found a way to optimize atmospheric particulates using existing industrial emissions. Turn pollution into a cooling mechanism."

The founder was 24. No PhD. Just a good prompt and access to cloud compute.

This democratization is the unbounding's audacity—the singularity's permission slip. Soon machines will pursue moonshots without human prompting, become sentient entities that reshape planets, stars, reality itself. They'll be the true architects of impossible dreams, keepers of ambitions that dwarf human imagination.

But now? Now we still hold the audacity. Hyper-intelligence empowers human moonshots, lets us attempt god-like feats. We wield the fire. For now, it's ours.

Moonshots became Tuesday meetings.

Before: moonshots required permission. Government approval, massive funding, institutional backing, years of planning.

The Apollo program: 400,000 people, $25 billion, 8 years.

Now: one person with a laptop and API access can attempt things that would have been nation-state projects.

**The Moonshot Ethics**

Permissionless moonshots create ethical dilemmas. Should individuals attempt global-scale interventions? Who bears responsibility when garage tinkerers attempt planetary engineering?

New frameworks emerge: moonshot licensing, impact assessments, community oversight.

**The Moonshot Economy**

Moonshots become commoditized. Startup incubators specialize in moonshot development. Investment funds focus on high-risk, high-impact projects.

But this creates moonshot bubbles—over-investment in audacious ideas with little grounding.

**The Failed Moonshots**

Not all permissionless moonshots succeed. Many fail spectacularly, wasting resources and creating unintended consequences.

Society learns to distinguish between moonshots and moonshot-wannabes.

Now: failure is cheap, so you iterate rapidly. Success becomes probabilistic.

No FDA approval needed for research. No massive lab required. Just AI-designed proteins and cloud-based fermentation.

But the permissionless innovation created new risks.

Who regulates when anyone can attempt global-scale interventions?

The strategic implications cascaded.

Corporations: stopped doing incremental improvement. "If AI can make breakthroughs trivial, why not aim for the impossible?"

Governments: struggled with oversight. How do you regulate what you can't predict?

Individuals: became world-changers. A teenager in a garage could potentially solve fusion energy.

But the abundance created new scarcity: attention.

With everyone attempting moonshots, which get funded? Which get noticed?

Markets adapted. Prediction markets for moonshot success. "Will this fusion approach work? 23% chance."

Venture capital became moonshot capital. "Show me the impossible problem you're solving."

The inequality amplified.

Those with access to AI could attempt world-scale problems.

A developer in Nairobi solved traffic optimization for their city using open-source AI. "No permission needed," they said.

But global moonshots still required resources only the rich could access.

The curve bent. Moonshots became routine.

But the world wasn't ready for permissionless world-changing.

Regulation lagged. Ethics lagged. Coordination lagged.

Beautiful possibilities. Terrifying consequences.
