# Through the Earth

*The Particle That Makes Secrecy a Detector Problem*

Sven Hardy Benson

There are secrets you protect with a wall. There are secrets you protect with distance, water, mountain, encryption, patrols, classification stamps, armed guards, treaty language, and the useful fact that most instruments cannot see through very much planet.

A nuclear reactor has one irritating property in this arrangement. It talks anyway.

Not in radio. Not in sound. Not in a channel its operator selected. Fission produces antineutrinos in enormous numbers, and the particles leave the core, the containment building, the site boundary, and eventually the country with almost comic indifference to the things humans build to stop information from traveling.

The difficulty is symmetrical. Matter barely stops neutrinos, which is why they can escape. Matter also barely notices neutrinos, which is why hearing them has historically required detectors measured in tons, caverns, accelerator beams, heroic background rejection, and patience.

That symmetry is this book.

It is tempting to write the story as inevitable technological conquest: first physics discovers an unshieldable messenger, then engineering miniaturizes the receiver, then secrecy ends. That would be a bad book and, more importantly, bad physics. In 2012 a Fermilab team really did transmit an encoded message through 240 meters of rock with neutrinos. The decoded rate was about one tenth of a bit per second. The apparatus around that stunt was not a radio you could slip into a submarine. In 2023 the U.S. Department of Energy highlighted SNO+'s detection of reactor antineutrinos from reactors at least 240 kilometers away using a thousand-ton water target. That is remarkable. It is also a thousand tons.

DARPA's QuSeN program now states the engineering problem plainly: push neutrino detection toward fundamental sensitivity limits, reduce the mass of detectors, and make distributed standoff sensing of reactors and nuclear materials more plausible. A program objective is not a capability. The distinction will be enforced throughout this manuscript.

The governing question is narrower and stranger than whether neutrinos are useful. It is what happens to intelligence, verification, and concealment when the best carrier of information through matter is also one of the hardest things in nature to detect.

## Evidence posture

This manuscript keeps four classes separate.

**Verified, declassified, or documented facts.** The Standard Model interactions used here; the NuMI/MINERvA communication demonstration and its published rate/error performance; SNO+ reactor-antineutrino detection; existing reactor-monitoring experiments; and DARPA's publicly stated QuSeN objectives belong here when supported by primary or authoritative records.

**Credible but incomplete evidence.** Projected detector masses, standoff ranges, costs, deployment concepts, and future performance belong here when they come from serious technical work but have not been demonstrated at the claimed system level.

**Disputed or unsupported claims.** Claims that neutrino systems can presently locate arbitrary hidden nuclear weapons, provide practical high-bandwidth submarine communications, or make nuclear secrecy obsolete do not become facts because the underlying particles penetrate matter.

**Speculative synthesis.** The book's larger argument—that neutrinos create a distinctive intelligence regime in which concealment shifts from shielding the source toward overwhelming, confusing, outranging, or economically defeating the detector—is a synthesis. It has to earn itself quantitatively.

The book will fail its own thesis if detector physics keeps the relevant intelligence channels impractical at meaningful ranges, if source degeneracy prevents useful inference, or if conventional sensing remains superior after cost, false alarms, geography, backgrounds, and operational constraints are counted.
