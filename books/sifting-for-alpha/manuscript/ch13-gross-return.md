# Chapter 13 — Gross Return Is Not a Trophy

Twenty point six percent is a much better sentence than "fourth place by the contest's final allocation procedure."

That is the problem.

After Q23, I said I believed I had the highest gross return, about 20.6 percent. I also said I had been so close to winning.

Those statements capture something real about how the contest felt from my side of the screen.

They also reveal the most human bias in quantitative finance:

We love whichever metric loves us back.

## Every portfolio has many scoreboards

Take a single strategy and you can rank it by:

raw return;

annualized return;

Sharpe ratio;

Sortino ratio;

maximum drawdown;

calmar ratio;

volatility;

hit rate;

profit factor;

turnover;

capacity;

beta-adjusted return;

factor-adjusted alpha;

worst month;

best month;

time under water;

skew;

kurtosis;

and whatever else makes the chart look persuasive.

The abundance of metrics is useful because portfolios have many dimensions.

It is dangerous because every mediocre strategy can become world-class in a carefully selected category.

If I tell you only the number my strategy won, I am not reporting performance.

I am doing public relations.

## The metric hierarchy

The cure is to establish a hierarchy before the result.

Primary objective.

Secondary constraints.

Diagnostic statistics.

For Q23, the primary competitive objective was not gross return. Quantiacs ranked eligible strategies according to live Sharpe under its contest rules.

Gross return remained important. It tells you something about economic magnitude.

But it did not outrank the objective function simply because my number looked good.

This sounds obvious when written calmly after the fact.

It is less obvious when your strategy has made more money than strategies above it and the leaderboard still disagrees.

That disagreement is educational.

The metric hierarchy forces you to ask whether you actually wanted the thing you optimized.

## Return without the path is an incomplete sentence

Suppose Portfolio A returns 20 percent with a 10 percent maximum drawdown.

Portfolio B returns 21 percent with a 50 percent maximum drawdown.

Which is better?

There is no universal answer. It depends on objective, leverage, liabilities, risk tolerance, horizon, and alternatives.

That is why raw return cannot stand alone.

The path matters because investors live through paths.

Capital constraints live through paths.

Margin calls live through paths.

Clients redeem through paths.

A 20 percent return delivered through violent oscillation is a different financial product from 20 percent delivered smoothly.

Sharpe is one attempt to summarize that distinction. It is imperfect and directionally useful.

The correct debate is not "return versus Sharpe."

It is "what kind of return are we trying to create, and what risk are we willing to accept to create it?"

## A contest rank is not an investment recommendation

Fourth place sounds like an endorsement.

It is an achievement inside a defined experiment.

Do not confuse the two.

A strategy can rank well during a four-month contest and still be unsuitable for long-term capital.

Maybe the factor exposure was regime-specific.

Maybe concentration was higher than a client could tolerate.

Maybe turnover becomes expensive at scale.

Maybe the live period was unusually favorable.

Maybe the strategy's edge decays after publication or funding.

This is why the honest next question after a prize is not "How much should we invest?"

It is "What did the prize update our belief about?"

The answer might be:

The ranking process contained useful information.

The portfolio construction was robust enough to survive a live window.

The research pipeline was capable of producing an eligible funded system.

Certain factor families deserve more investigation.

Those are valuable updates.

None says "risk everything."

## Benchmarking can change the brag

Suppose the market rose sharply during the contest.

A long-biased strategy could have a high gross return without much stock-selection skill.

Suppose the market fell.

The same return could be extraordinary.

Gross return without context does not tell you what the model added beyond common exposures.

This is why benchmark-relative and factor-relative thinking matters even when the contest does not use it directly.

Ask:

How much came from broad market beta?

How much came from sector tilts?

How much came from size, momentum, value, quality, or low-volatility exposures?

How much remained after those were accounted for?

The answer may be less flattering than 20.6 percent.

It may also be more durable.

Alpha begins where the obvious exposures end.

## The thirteenth sieve: report the metric that hurts

Here is a practical discipline.

Every performance report should include at least one statistic you wish were better.

High return? Show drawdown.

High Sharpe? Show out-of-sample decay.

Low drawdown? Show return and capacity.

Great contest rank? Show the scoring metric and the limitations of the live period.

Strong factor IC? Show unstable regimes.

The painful metric prevents the report from becoming an advertisement.

This is especially important when you are the researcher and the marketer, which is almost always true in personal investing.

Nobody else is coming to make your slide deck less flattering.

You have to do it yourself.

## Losing the argument with my own number

I still like 20.6 percent.

Of course I do.

A competitive person should be allowed one satisfying number.

The discipline is to keep it in its proper box.

Gross return tells one story.

Sharpe tells another.

Drawdown tells another.

Eligibility tells another.

Final allocation tells another.

The full performance is the intersection.

This became one of the larger lessons of Q23: quantitative investing does not eliminate storytelling. It creates enough numbers that storytelling becomes even easier.

The sifter must therefore filter narratives too.

Especially the ones that make the author look good.
