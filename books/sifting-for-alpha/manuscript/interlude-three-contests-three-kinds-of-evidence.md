# Interlude — Three Contests, Three Kinds of Evidence

A single contest result is a story.

Three contests begin to look like a research design.

Q23, Q24, and Q25 are not directly comparable experiments. The first was an S&P 500 long-short contest. The next two moved into long-only crypto. The scoring periods differ. The available data differ. The eligible universes differ. The market regimes differ.

That is exactly why the sequence matters.

If all three contests had been identical, I could tell myself that repeated success or failure proved something stable about one model. Instead, the contests changed enough to expose a more useful object:

**the research process itself.**

Q23 tested whether the process could produce something competitive enough to earn an allocation.

Q24 tested whether success transferred when the problem changed.

Q25 is testing whether the process learned anything from the gap.

That gives me three kinds of evidence.

## Q23: outcome evidence

Q23 is the cleanest headline.

Svyable finished in the fourth allocation slot in the S&P 500 Long-Short contest and received a $100,000 allocation under the Quantiacs funding program.

That is a public outcome.

It answers one narrow question:

**Could a Svyable strategy survive the contest process well enough to finish near the top?**

Yes.

The mistake would be expanding that answer into questions the result did not settle.

Did it prove a permanent stock-selection edge?

No.

Did it prove every factor in the research lineage was useful?

No.

Did it prove the same workflow would dominate a different asset class?

Q24 answered that one.

## Q24: transfer evidence

Q24 changed the problem to Crypto Top-10 Long-Only.

The strongest public Svyable result finished thirtieth with a contest Sharpe of 1.50.

No allocation.

More important, several Svyable entries arrived with in-sample Sharpes around or above two and then produced mediocre or negative contest Sharpes.

That is not merely a disappointing result.

It is transfer evidence.

It says a research machine can be very good at producing historically attractive systems without producing equally attractive future performance.

Q24 therefore tells me more about the reliability of the research pipeline than Q23 alone ever could.

Q23 says the machine can find something that works.

Q24 says the machine can also manufacture confidence.

Both statements can be true.

That is the useful tension.

## Q25: process evidence

Q25 has not begun its live contest period as I write this.

So there is no outcome evidence yet.

There is something else.

Process evidence.

The research workflow now records more explicit freezes, more causal checks, more cost stress, more archive-correlation analysis, more refusal to use already-seen recent data as a promotional vote, and more willingness to leave contest slots empty.

This matters because process changes are the only part of the next result I can influence before seeing it.

I cannot choose whether October through January rewards trend, reversal, liquidity, event response, defensive convexity, or something none of the research archive understands.

I can choose whether I cheat after seeing the answer.

I can choose whether a weak candidate is promoted because I like its recent chart.

I can choose whether two highly correlated systems get counted as two independent alphas.

I can choose whether the exact source that was reviewed is the exact source that gets submitted.

I can choose whether failed tests remain visible.

Q25 is therefore a test of epistemic behavior before it becomes a test of portfolio behavior.

## The scoreboard

The three contests can be summarized without pretending they are identical:

| Contest | Market / mandate | State | Best verified Svyable evidence | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q23 | S&P 500 Long-Short | Completed | Fourth allocation slot; $100,000 allocation | A sifted process can produce a funded result |
| Q24 | Crypto Top-10 Long-Only | Completed | Best public Svyable entry rank 30; contest Sharpe 1.50; no allocation | Strong historical metrics do not guarantee transfer |
| Q25 | Crypto Top-10 Long-Only | Pre-live | Research slate, frozen gates, rejection logs, redundancy audits | The process can become stricter before the outcome is known |

The table is deliberately asymmetric.

Q23 has a final allocation result.

Q24 has a final live rank and a useful family of failed transfers.

Q25 has no live contest result.

The asymmetry is not a formatting problem.

It is the state of the evidence.

## Three different ways to fool yourself

Each contest exposed a different failure mode.

### Q23: narrative inflation

A fourth-place finish can become a first-place story in memory.

You remember the good calls.

You compress the failed variants into a sentence.

You start describing an allocation as if it were proof that the underlying model is permanently superior.

The antidote is provenance.

Keep the exact contest facts separate from the reconstructed research lineage.

### Q24: backtest inflation

A 2-plus historical Sharpe can start to feel like a property of the strategy rather than a result produced by a search process.

Then the live period arrives.

The antidote is family-level out-of-sample evidence.

Do not inspect only the best survivor. Look at the siblings.

If several related systems looked excellent historically and degraded live, the family is telling you something about the common assumptions.

### Q25: process inflation

A sophisticated pipeline can become a new source of vanity.

Preregistration.

Hash locks.

Cost ladders.

Correlation matrices.

Causal prefix tests.

These are useful controls.

They can also become theater if the researcher quietly changes the rules whenever the preferred candidate fails.

The antidote is the decision log.

A rule only counts if failure has consequences.

## The unit of progress changed

In Q23, progress felt like a higher backtest statistic.

In Q24, progress became a live contest result that disagreed with the backtest.

In Q25, progress increasingly looks like a rejected model.

That sounds backwards until you think about what rejection means.

A rejected model is a hypothesis that encountered a rule the researcher respected.

A model that fails a frozen criterion and remains rejected is evidence that the process has a boundary.

A process with no rejected models has no visible boundary.

It may be a research process.

It may also be a chart-selection process.

The distinction matters more than the sophistication of the code.

## The denominator matters

One of the most dangerous numbers in quantitative research is the number you do not report.

The denominator.

A single 2.5 Sharpe strategy sounds exceptional.

One 2.5 Sharpe strategy selected from ten trials sounds less exceptional.

One selected from hundreds sounds different again.

One selected from thousands of related transformations of the same daily bars should make you ask whether the research program is finding alpha or finding favorable noise.

This does not mean large searches are invalid.

It means the search itself belongs in the evidence.

Q25's increasingly formal research logs are partly an attempt to preserve the denominator.

Not perfectly.

The historical search family is too large and too entangled to pretend that every trial can now be reconstructed into one pristine significance calculation.

That limitation is itself part of the record.

The appropriate response is not to manufacture a clean p-value.

It is to become stricter about what future evidence is allowed to mean.

## The archive became an opponent

At first, an archive is a memory aid.

Later, it becomes a benchmark.

A new factor does not merely need a positive historical Sharpe.

It needs to answer a more difficult question:

**What does this return stream know that the archive does not already know?**

That question changed Q25 research.

Different formulas were often producing highly similar return streams.

Different names were often packaging the same broad exposure.

Trend appeared in multiple costumes.

Defensive behavior appeared in multiple costumes.

Liquidity appeared in multiple costumes.

Residual momentum appeared in multiple costumes.

The archive made those disguises easier to see.

That is why "one family, one seat" became useful.

It is not a sponsor rule.

It is a defense against counting duplicated beliefs as diversified evidence.

## Winning and learning are not substitutes

It would be convenient if the contest that teaches the most is always the contest that pays the most.

It is not.

Q23 paid the best.

Q24 may have taught more.

Q25 may end up teaching more than either of them, regardless of rank.

This does not mean results are irrelevant.

A research philosophy that celebrates losing is just another way to avoid accountability.

The live result matters because it is external evidence.

The point is narrower:

**the usefulness of an outcome depends on whether the process allows the outcome to change your beliefs.**

A win that causes overconfidence can make the research program worse.

A loss that causes better controls can make it better.

Then the next live period gets to judge whether the improvement mattered.

## The evidence ladder

After three contests, I think about evidence in roughly this order:

1. **Idea evidence** — the economic story is coherent.
2. **Historical evidence** — the signal appears in past data.
3. **Stress evidence** — the result survives worse assumptions.
4. **Neighborhood evidence** — nearby parameters do not collapse.
5. **Family evidence** — related variants do not reveal an obvious search artifact.
6. **Originality evidence** — the return stream adds something beyond the existing archive.
7. **Causality evidence** — prefix and implementation tests show the system does not depend on future information.
8. **Platform evidence** — the exact strategy passes the actual contest or deployment environment.
9. **Outside-time evidence** — performance arrives after the relevant decisions are frozen.
10. **Capital evidence** — the strategy survives with real economic consequences.

Most research claims become weaker because they skip rungs.

A backtest jumps directly to "alpha."

A preview jumps directly to "live."

A contest allocation jumps directly to "persistent edge."

The ladder is a way to stop nouns from borrowing status they have not earned.

## What Q25 is actually trying to beat

The obvious opponent is the leaderboard.

The more important opponent is the researcher's own selection process.

Can I prevent a high historical Sharpe from receiving automatic promotion?

Can I keep an attractive recent diagnostic from voting twice?

Can I reject a candidate that is profitable but redundant?

Can I preserve a failure after discovering a clever way to explain it away?

Can I leave a slot empty?

Can I keep the exact pre-live chapter unchanged after the result arrives?

If the answer to those questions is yes, then Q25 will have improved the research process before the first live return is recorded.

That still does not mean the strategy will win.

It means the result will be harder to misinterpret.

That is a different kind of alpha.

Not portfolio alpha.

**Epistemic alpha.**

The ability to update from evidence without quietly changing what counted as evidence.

Q23 gave me a result.

Q24 gave me a contradiction.

Q25 is trying to give me a cleaner experiment.

That is the three-contest story.
