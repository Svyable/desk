# 8. The 1940 Answer

In 2017, Colin Carlson, Alexander Bond and Kevin Burgio published a blunt result in *Conservation Biology*. Using mixed-certainty sighting data and extinction models, they estimated the most likely thylacine extinction date around 1940. Across alternative models the dates clustered near 1936–1943; their most optimistic treatment did not carry the species beyond 1956.

Their conclusion was not only zoological. They argued that continued searches could drain limited conservation resources.

This is what a model becomes when translated into policy. A posterior distribution becomes a recommendation about where people should stop looking.

The analysis had an attractive principle: physical evidence, expert-validated sightings and unconfirmed reports should not be treated as equally reliable. A Bayesian framework can represent true and false sighting processes separately. Late low-certainty reports do not automatically drag the extinction date forward forever.

That is necessary because false sightings have no terminal date. People can report extinct animals indefinitely. A naive 'last report' estimator therefore guarantees immortality to charismatic species.

The Carlson result is powerful precisely because it resists that trap. But every power has a price. Estimates depend on how records are classified and how the rate of valid versus invalid observations is modeled. If expert-quality late observations are undervalued, the posterior will collapse too early. If low-quality reports are overvalued, it will stretch too late.

Brook and colleagues challenged deficiencies in the treatment soon afterward. The dispute should not be narrated as one paper debunking another. It is more revealing than that. Both sides agreed the thylacine is almost certainly gone. They disagreed over what the same contaminated historical tail can say about *when*.

That difference matters scientifically. An extinction in 1940 implies only a short undocumented survival after the final captive animal. Persistence into the 1960s or 1980s implies hidden reproduction, decades of non-detection, and a very different story about the effectiveness of searches.

The date is therefore a compressed test of assumptions about biology and evidence. 1940 is not merely a year. It is what falls out when certain sighting reliabilities and observation processes are believed.
