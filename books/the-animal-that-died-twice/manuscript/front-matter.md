# The Animal That Died Twice

## Thylacine, False Sightings, and the Science of Proving Absence

Sven Hardy Benson

### Author's note

There is a date on which the last known thylacine died. There is almost certainly another date on which the last thylacine died. They need not be the same date, and that gap is the book.

The distinction sounds pedantic until money, habitat, law, and hope depend on it. We can photograph an animal into existence. Extinction is harder. Nobody photographs the first morning on which no members of a species remain anywhere. The event produces no carcass, no alarm, no clean instrument trace. It leaves a sequence that simply stops.

The thylacine gives this problem teeth. Its final captive died in Hobart in September 1936. The last known wild animal shot was earlier. Protection came obscenely late. Sightings continued. Searches failed. Statistical analyses disagree about how long the tail should be believed. Modern cameras make prolonged survival harder to hide, while the growing cultural fame of the animal makes false reports easier to generate.

This is not a cryptozoology book wearing a lab coat. Nor is it a book in which skepticism is defined as laughing before looking. It is about what one can infer from a missing animal when the observations have different reliabilities and the absence of observations is itself produced by changing search effort.

If a living thylacine is documented tomorrow, much of the historical argument remains useful. The question would simply change from how we proved absence badly to how an animal escaped the proof.
