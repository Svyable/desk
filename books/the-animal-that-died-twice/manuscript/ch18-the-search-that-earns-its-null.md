# 18. The Search That Earns Its Null

The decisive thylacine experiment is not another expedition with a television crew.

It is a detection program designed so that failure means something.

The first line of the protocol should define what “survival” means before a single camera is mounted.

Not `a thylacine might be somewhere`.

Several explicit states:

- one residual individual;
- a tiny remnant no longer capable of sustained reproduction;
- a minimally reproducing population;
- a larger population capable of having persisted through multiple generations into the present.

Those are biologically different hypotheses and they should not be allowed to borrow one another's invisibility.

One animal can be absurdly hard to find. A population that has reproduced for decades has to keep solving mate-finding, prey acquisition, juvenile survival and territory use. It has to make more bodies, more movements and more traces. The survey should exploit that difference.

Use the 2023 spatial work and historical habitat to define search strata, but freeze the strata before contemporary rumors arrive. Rumors can become a secondary test set. They should not continuously drag the cameras toward wherever the newest story happens to point.

Then construct a forward model.

Populate the landscape with simulated thylacines under each survival state. Give them plausible home ranges, movement distances, road-use probabilities, habitat preferences and reproductive structure. These values should have uncertainty distributions, not one convenient point estimate.

Now put the proposed camera array into the same landscape.

Run it thousands of times.

How often does it miss every simulated population?

That number—not the number of cameras—is the first real measure of search power.

Do the same for every other sensor.

For cameras, measure usable camera-days after battery failures, vegetation triggers, theft, memory faults and dead units are removed. Calibrate trigger distance against animals of similar body size and speed. Estimate whether likely thylacine routes actually intersect the detection cone.

For tracks and scat, use controlled observers and known carnivore sign to estimate both miss rate and misidentification rate. Save rejected candidates. A good false-positive archive is more useful than a gallery of only the most exciting impressions.

For eDNA, begin with museum-derived reference sequence and specificity controls, but do not confuse assay specificity with ecological detectability. Spike environmental samples to measure inhibition and extraction loss. Measure persistence under Tasmanian temperature, UV, flow and microbial conditions. If possible, use extant carnivores to estimate how often an animal passing through a watershed leaves a detectable signal under the actual sampling scheme.

A clean laboratory assay can still be a bad wildlife detector.

Acoustics should be included only if the target vocalization is defined well enough to recognize. An algorithm trained on uncertain historical descriptions would manufacture confidence from folklore.

Every modality gets two numbers:

**sensitivity** — probability of registering the animal when it is present under the modeled conditions;

**false-positive rate** — probability of producing a thylacine-like signal when it is absent.

Then combine methods carefully.

A camera image and an eDNA sequence can be powerful because their error mechanisms are largely different. Two cameras sharing the same road bias are less independent than they look. A track found because an observer walked to the exact place where a blurry camera image was taken is partly cued evidence, not a clean second channel.

Every positive should therefore trigger a second protocol chosen for physical independence.

A plausible image triggers genetics, hair traps, scat search and additional cameras outside the exact original viewpoint.

A DNA detection triggers replicate extraction, an independent laboratory, a second genetic target, field resampling and contamination reconstruction.

A track sequence triggers photogrammetry, substrate controls, stride analysis, casting where appropriate and blind comparison against dog and devil tracks.

The protocol should specify those escalations before any positive arrives.

The null needs equal discipline.

Do not write: `we deployed 500 cameras and found nothing`.

Write: under survival model A, the calibrated system would detect at least one thylacine in 62% of simulations; under model B, 93%; under model C, 99.7%. Then report the real operating performance and rerun the analysis with the actual downtime and missing data.

If the null arrives, update each survival state separately.

Perhaps one residual animal remains almost unconstrained because even an excellent network misses that state frequently.

Perhaps a breeding population large enough to have persisted since the 1980s becomes extremely difficult to reconcile with the accumulated camera, eDNA, road and field-survey record.

That is a scientifically useful outcome because it says exactly what kind of ghost the evidence still permits.

No abundance threshold should be inserted because it sounds reasonable.

The phrase `minimum viable population` is not a magic integer. It depends on sex ratio, age structure, demographic variance, inbreeding, catastrophe risk, habitat connectivity and time horizon. The search model should therefore bracket several demographic states rather than pretend that one round number separates viable from doomed.

The historical record should enter as a prior, not as decoration.

Confirmed kills and specimens anchor the early twentieth century. Expert sightings and ordinary reports enter with different reliability. The 2023 database gives a spatially explicit possible tail. Historical searches, where their actual effort can be reconstructed, become negative observations. Modern calibrated surveys should then dominate later updates because their detection process is measured instead of guessed.

This is also how the old dispute between early and late extinction estimates can be made productive.

Do not ask which paper has the better date.

Ask which models make different predictions about what a modern search should find.

A late-survival model that implies a reproducing population through the 1980s should leave a different modern evidence distribution than a model in which only a handful of terminal animals persisted into the 1940s. Demography connects historical tail length to the amount of population mass that must exist between sightings.

That connection is where many cryptozoological arguments quietly cheat.

They allow a species to persist for decades as if it were one immortal animal.

Real persistence reproduces.

Reproduction creates detectability.

The project should be geographically honest too. Southwestern Tasmania is large, difficult and heterogeneous. A survey that saturates accessible roads while leaving remote habitat untouched should not claim state-wide power. Publish a map of **detection probability**, not merely a map of camera locations.

The blank parts of that map are part of the result.

The project should also earn conservation value regardless of the thylacine outcome. The same camera network can estimate occupancy for Tasmanian devils, spotted-tailed quolls, feral cats and prey species. eDNA can inventory aquatic and terrestrial biodiversity. Track surveys can document invasive predators. Habitat work can identify corridors worth protecting for animals that definitely still exist.

That changes the economics of uncertainty.

A dedicated expedition whose only possible success is a thylacine photograph becomes irrational as the posterior falls. A biodiversity monitoring network that also tests thylacine persistence can keep accumulating useful null evidence for years.

The stopping rule should be written before the project begins.

Not `stop when the team feels convinced`.

Stop or de-escalate expensive targeted searching when the expected information gain from another campaign falls below its cost and when all survival states relevant to conservation have been pushed below a declared decision threshold. Continue cheap passive detection if the marginal cost is low and the network serves other ecological purposes.

This is not surrender to statistics.

It is how statistics stop becoming theater.

What would overturn the conclusion?

A verified modern specimen with secure provenance would reset the entire problem instantly.

A fresh carcass would do it.

Unambiguous DNA obtained under contamination-controlled field conditions and replicated independently would do it.

A sequence of high-quality imagery with recoverable site evidence could do it.

The standard is high because the prior is now low and the false-positive ecology is demonstrably rich.

What would strengthen extinction inference?

A multi-year, geographically explicit survey with high modeled detection probability for every plausible reproducing-population state, coupled to calibrated eDNA and sign surveys, returning no credible positives.

A demographic analysis showing that late historical sightings require population sizes that modern calibrated search would almost certainly detect.

A reconstruction of historical search effort showing that the habitat most favored by late-tail models was repeatedly sampled with better power than assumed.

The important thing is that either direction can win.

There is no shame in ending with a null if the null has earned its weight.

The animal probably died decades ago. The scientific opportunity did not.

The thylacine can still teach us how to stop confusing the last thing we saw with the last thing that lived—and how to stop calling silence evidence until we have measured what silence should sound like if the animal were still there.
