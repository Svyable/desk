# 11. Naarding at Two in the Morning

In 1982, wildlife officer Hans Naarding reported waking in heavy rain and illuminating an animal at close range with a spotlight in northwest Tasmania. The sighting became exceptional because of the observer's field experience, the reported viewing conditions, and the institutional response. Officials kept the report quiet while a substantial search was mounted.

No thylacine was found.

This is exactly the kind of case that breaks binary thinking. If all testimony is discarded, Naarding contributes nothing. If expert testimony is treated as physical confirmation, the species survives to 1982 by definition. Neither is satisfying.

The correct object is a likelihood ratio: how much more likely is a report with these properties if a thylacine existed nearby than if it did not?

Answering requires a false-positive reference class for experienced observers, which is rarely available. Experts misidentify animals less often than tourists, but 'less often' is not zero. Memory, expectation and brief illumination still operate.

The subsequent search matters too. A genuine sighting followed by intensive failure is not the same evidentiary state as an isolated report. The report raises survival probability; the search lowers it. The final posterior depends on the search's detection power.

Descriptions of the 1982–83 effort include automatic cameras, tracking beds and broad field investigation. But to turn that into mathematics we need camera-days, placement, field of view, trigger reliability, target movement rates, habitat use and the probability that a remnant animal would cross monitored sites.

Without those denominators, 'extensive search' is narrative evidence. Useful, but not enough.

Naarding's report should therefore remain where uncomfortable evidence belongs: neither dismissed nor promoted. It is a high-quality unconfirmed observation followed by a serious null search. Any model claiming extinction before 1982 owes an account of how often such false positives occur. Any model claiming survival through 1982 owes an account of why the follow-up failed.

The case is valuable precisely because both sides have a bill to pay.
