# 7. The False-Positive Ecology

Once a species becomes famous for being missing, the environment changes.

Before 1936, somebody who saw a striped animal in Tasmania could report a thylacine because thylacines existed. After decades of cultural attention, a person can report a thylacine because the idea of the thylacine exists. The signal declines while the template becomes more available.

This creates an ugly statistical regime: true positives become rarer as false positives may become more common.

The standard intuition that 'many reports cannot all be wrong' fails when reports are generated by a shared cause. A thousand sightings are not a thousand independent experiments if witnesses have seen the same photographs, watched the same documentaries, driven the same roads, and learned the same diagnostic stripe pattern.

Nor are all errors random. A dog seen from behind is more likely to be reported when its gait, tail and lighting happen to match the cultural template. Confirmation operates before the report is submitted.

Media incentives amplify the ecology. Ambiguous images travel; ordinary dogs do not. A fuzzy frame labeled POSSIBLE TASMANIAN TIGER is selected precisely because uncertainty survives compression. By the time analysts see a public corpus, mundane negatives have been filtered out.

The proper comparison is detection theory. Set a threshold low enough and sensitivity rises along with false alarms. Set it high enough and only specimens survive, at the cost of ignoring potentially informative testimony. The optimal threshold depends on the consequence of misses and false alarms.

For a conservation search, missing a surviving population can be catastrophic. For a scientific claim that the species persists, accepting a false positive can redirect years of work. Those are different decisions and can rationally use different thresholds.

This explains why officials can investigate reports while maintaining an extinct listing without contradiction. Operational caution and evidentiary declaration are not identical.

A useful future database would preserve every report, including obvious misidentifications, with blinded quality ratings and eventual resolutions when available. False reports should not be discarded as clutter. They are the calibration set. Without them, we do not know the background rate against which a remarkable sighting must compete.
