# 3. Already Small

A satisfying extinction story has a villain and a healthy victim. The thylacine refuses the second part.

Genetic work on historical and subfossil material shows that Tasmanian thylacines carried low diversity before Europeans finished the species. Mitochondrial reconstructions suggest deeper population structure and contraction extending well before the bounty era. This does not exonerate hunting. It changes the initial conditions.

A small, fragmented predator population can be pushed by pressures that a large one absorbs: persecution, habitat conversion, disease, prey change, demographic bad luck. Once females and males rarely encounter one another, the arithmetic becomes cruel. A species can remain geographically present while already being demographically doomed.

That distinction matters to post-1936 sightings. Suppose a few thylacines did survive the disappearance of hard specimens. 'Survival' can mean one breeding population, one sterile old female, three scattered animals, or a metapopulation barely replacing itself. Those states have different expected durations and different probabilities of leaving evidence.

One cannot infer a hidden 1980 population merely by extending a 1936 animal forward. A population must reproduce. Each extra decade requires a genealogy. If wild life expectancy was on the order reported by historical accounts rather than many decades, survival into the 1960s demands births after the last specimen. Survival into the 1980s demands several hidden generations.

This is where romantic survival claims often become biologically expensive. They count years but not litters. A credible persistence model must contain enough animals, sex structure, habitat and reproduction to bridge the interval. Small populations can hide more easily; very small populations also disappear more easily.

The same argument prevents skeptics from using genetics lazily. Low diversity is not a magic extinction switch. Species can persist with severe bottlenecks. A genetic warning is a prior about fragility, not a dated death certificate.

The useful object is therefore a coupled model: demographic persistence plus detection. How many animals would have to exist to make survival through each decade plausible? Given that number, how often should roads, hunters, wildlife staff, cameras or carcasses have intersected them? The hidden-population hypothesis must pay both bills simultaneously.

This is the shape of the whole problem. The more animals required to keep a lineage alive, the harder invisibility becomes. The fewer animals invoked to explain invisibility, the harder reproduction becomes. Somewhere between those constraints is the narrow corridor in which a late-survival thylacine can still fit.
