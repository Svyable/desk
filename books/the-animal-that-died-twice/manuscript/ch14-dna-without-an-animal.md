# 14. DNA Without an Animal

Environmental DNA changes the romance of rediscovery. An animal no longer has to stand in front of a lens. It can leave cells, hair, feces, saliva or decomposing tissue in water and soil.

For the thylacine, eDNA is both promising and treacherous. Historical museum DNA provides reference sequences, but environmental assays must discriminate target fragments from close relatives, contamination, laboratory carryover and damaged old DNA. A positive amplicon is not automatically a living animal.

This is where chain of custody returns in molecular form.

A convincing modern detection would need independent sample collection, extraction blanks, field blanks, multiple target loci, sequencing confirmation, replicated laboratories and geographical coherence. Ideally the genetic signal would be paired with another modality: a camera image, scat morphology, tracks, or repeated sampling at neighboring sites.

A null eDNA survey also needs calibration. DNA persistence varies with temperature, UV exposure, microbes, flow and substrate. The absence of amplifiable thylacine DNA means little unless experiments show how often a thylacine-like mammal using the same habitat would be detected under the same sampling design.

The central rule is symmetric: molecular technology does not abolish the denominator.

Old cryptid debates often imagined that better technology would simply end uncertainty. In practice better instruments create new classes of false positive. Camera traps create image artefacts. eDNA creates contamination and index hopping. Machine-learning classifiers create confident labels on out-of-distribution inputs.

The solution is not technological pessimism. It is multimodal preregistration. Specify in advance what combination of independent signals will count as rediscovery and what controls must pass.

If the thylacine remains absent under that protocol, the null becomes much stronger than another decade of anecdotes. If it appears, the discovery can survive first contact with skepticism.
