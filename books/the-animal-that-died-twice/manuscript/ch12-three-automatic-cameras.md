# 12. Three Automatic Cameras

In 1980, Tasmanian wildlife officers Steven Smith and Adrian Pyrke deployed three automatic camera systems at selected sites. No thylacine appeared.

Three cameras sounds almost comically small in the age of cheap digital camera traps. That reaction is unfair and useful.

It is unfair because automated wildlife photography was difficult, expensive and technically crude compared with current systems. It is useful because it reminds us that the phrase 'we searched' hides orders of magnitude of detection effort.

A camera is not a transparent patch of forest. It has a trigger, field of view, battery life, film capacity, downtime, placement strategy and species-specific detection distance. A rare wide-ranging carnivore can pass fifty metres away and remain statistically invisible.

Later searches used more cameras and better equipment. None produced conclusive evidence. At some point repeated nulls must dominate even strong sightings, or extinction becomes unfalsifiable.

The challenge is cumulative accounting. We need a ledger of camera-nights through time, corrected for technology and habitat. Add road observations, hunter effort, scat surveys, carcass discovery rates and museum submissions. Estimate how many independent opportunities existed to detect a surviving population.

Conservation science now has occupancy models designed for imperfect detection. A non-detection at one site does not equal absence; repeated non-detections under known detectability can drive occupancy probability down sharply. The thylacine problem should be recast in that language.

This also reveals why modern silence is more informative than 1940s silence. Tasmania in 2026 is not Tasmania in 1937. Roads, phones, cameras, ecological surveys and citizen-science image archives create an accidental sensor network.

A survival hypothesis must update with that network. 'The wilderness is big' is not enough. One must ask how much of the habitat is effectively sampled by all the organisms, machines and humans moving through it.

Absence becomes evidence when detection opportunity is measured.
