# 1. Two Deaths

The last known captive thylacine died at Beaumaris Zoo in Hobart on 7 September 1936. That sentence is firm enough to engrave. The second sentence is not: the species became extinct on 7 September 1936.

Those statements are often allowed to collapse into one another because human beings like witnessed endings. We prefer the body in the cage to the probability distribution in the forest. Yet the zoo animal was only the last individual whose continued existence was documented. A wild population, if one remained, could have died earlier, later, or on the same day by coincidence.

Tasmania's own wildlife history preserves the awkwardness. Officials list the thylacine as extinct or presumed extinct and state that no conclusive post-1936 evidence has been found, while also describing hundreds of reported sightings and multiple organized searches. This is not bureaucratic indecision. It is what an extinction record looks like when the animal becomes rare before observers become systematic.

The thylacine therefore died twice in public memory. The first death was biological: the unknown moment at which the final organism ceased to exist. The second was epistemic: the point at which institutions became willing to act as if survival no longer deserved serious probability.

The biological event is unobservable after the fact. The epistemic event is reconstructable. It depends on what counts as evidence, how quickly false sightings accumulate, where people look, what technologies they use, what a surviving population would have to do to remain hidden, and how costly it is to keep searching.

The strange thing is that this is not a small zoological problem. Conservation biology repeatedly faces species that have not been seen for years or decades. Search too little and a remnant population can be abandoned. Search forever and scarce conservation money follows ghosts while living species decline in plain sight.

The thylacine is famous enough that almost every failure mode is amplified. Witnesses know what they hope to see. Newspapers reward ambiguity. Camera traps now occupy landscapes that once contained almost no automatic observers. Tasmania's forests are difficult but not metaphysical. Every year without a specimen changes the odds, but not by the same amount, because effort changes too.

The book begins with a proposition that sounds colder than it is: extinction should be treated as a detection problem with asymmetric consequences. A false declaration can kill protection for survivors. A refusal to declare can waste decades. There is no morally pure threshold. There is only a threshold made explicit or hidden inside habit.
