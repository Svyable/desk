# 6. People as Detectors

A human witness is a detector with excellent pattern recognition, terrible calibration records, moving thresholds, memory, pride and a face.

This is why arguments about sightings become personal too quickly. Calling a report unreliable sounds like calling the witness a liar. But a false positive does not require deceit. Tasmania contains dogs, quolls, shadows, wet roads, poor depth cues, and an animal people intensely want either to find or to mock.

The useful question is not whether witnesses are trustworthy in the moral sense. It is what their error distribution looks like.

Tasmania's own wildlife department offers an unusually good calibration point. In a review of 320 sightings reported between 1934 and 1980, Steven Smith judged just under half to be good sightings. None became conclusive. More awkwardly, the department notes that mainland Australia produces just as many sightings of equally good quality.

The mainland is not merely another place where nobody has found a recent carcass. Fossil and subfossil evidence puts the thylacine's disappearance there roughly two thousand years ago, long before the modern sighting culture. Yet people still generate reports that can survive an initial quality screen.

That is a rare gift in an extinction argument: a partial false-positive control assembled by history rather than by an experimenter. It does not tell us that every Tasmanian sighting is wrong. It tells us that a persuasive thylacine report can be produced where the animal is overwhelmingly unlikely to be present.

The strongest objection is that the control is badly matched. Tasmania and the mainland do not share the same fauna, habitat, road geometry, observer population, or cultural relationship to the animal. A tourist's roadside glimpse should not receive the same weight as a wildlife officer's prolonged close observation. Professional status, however, cannot be allowed to become a magic category either. What matters is how often observers with comparable experience, distance, light, duration and expectation make the same mistake.

That suggests a better use for old sighting archives. Do not keep only the most impressive reports. Keep the dogs, the misread tracks, the impossible mainland animals, the photographs that resolve into ordinary species, and the cases that remain ambiguous. Those are not rubbish at the edge of the dataset. They are the data needed to estimate the background.

The danger appears when classification is tuned after seeing the desired extinction date. If survivalists upgrade late reports because they are late, or skeptics downgrade them for the same reason, the analysis contains its conclusion in the labels.

Witness evidence is not embarrassing science. Natural history was built from observers, and astronomy spent centuries trusting trained eyes. The trouble starts when testimony is treated as either a carcass or nothing.

A person is a noisy biological instrument. The mainland record gives us something unusually precious: noise with the animal mostly taken out.
