# 2. The Pound Bounty

Before the thylacine became a ghost, it was a receipt.

Tasmania paid bounties for dead thylacines from 1888 to 1909. Official histories record more than two thousand bounty claims. The administrative logic was ordinary for its time: a predator believed to threaten sheep was converted into a unit of removal, one adult worth a pound and a juvenile worth less. A living carnivore became a payable nuisance.

The bounty ledger matters for more than moral atmosphere. It is one of the few quantitative traces of population collapse. A stream of claims rises, then falls. But a falling kill total can mean at least three things: fewer animals, less hunting effort, or lower incentive. When the program ends, one of the best observation systems ends with it. The species becomes rarer at roughly the same time the state stops paying thousands of people to report bodies.

This is the first recurring trap in extinction inference. Observation effort is not stationary. The probability of seeing an animal in 1895 is not the probability of seeing it in 1935. Roads, farms, guns, prices, law, public interest, cameras and habitat all alter detection.

The livestock accusation is also less settled than the old policy implied. Tasmania's modern account notes that the extent of sheep predation was exaggerated. Anatomy work has likewise complicated the cartoon of the thylacine as a wolf in stripes. It was a marsupial predator built under a different evolutionary history, convergent with canids in silhouette but not interchangeable with them in ecology.

That makes the bounty doubly instructive. The program generated evidence about abundance while helping destroy the thing being measured. And its justification may itself have been inflated by the same cultural machinery that later inflated survival sightings: people are capable of seeing a thylacine both when blaming a missing sheep and when hoping for a lost species.

An honest extinction model should want the bounty data and distrust it. It should ask how hunting intensity changed, where claims originated, whether claim verification shifted, and how the terminal decline compares with habitat contraction. The ledger is neither a clean census nor mere folklore. It is a biased sensor.

The thylacine's story is full of sensors like this: traps designed to kill, witnesses trained by illustrations, automatic cameras placed where witnesses already reported animals, statistical models built from records whose existence depends on all of the preceding choices. There is no view from nowhere. There are only instruments, some made of steel and some made of people.
