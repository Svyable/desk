# 13. How Much Silence Is Enough

There is no universal number of years after which a species becomes extinct by arithmetic.

A whale, fungus, cave insect and island carnivore have different detectabilities. Search area, abundance, generation time and observation technology matter. Fifty years is an administrative rule, not a law of nature.

The thylacine exposes the temptation to confuse institutional thresholds with biological certainty. Tasmania describes the species as presumed extinct while acknowledging continuing reports. The IUCN framework defines extinction in terms of no reasonable doubt that the last individual has died, reached through exhaustive surveys over appropriate habitat and time.

'Exhaustive' is the dangerous word. No survey is literally exhaustive.

The operational version is probabilistic: enough survey effort has accumulated that the chance of failing to detect the species, conditional on a viable population still existing, has become extremely small.

That conditional matters. A single dying individual can be almost impossible to detect. A viable breeding population is harder to hide. Conservation policy generally cares about the second state because rescue requires reproduction.

This leads to a useful split between *biological persistence* and *recoverable persistence*. A species might technically survive as one or two individuals after conservation has become impossible. Declaring it extinct would still be biologically wrong, but the policy consequence of continued searching changes.

The threshold should therefore be tied to decisions. Continue low-cost passive detection longer than expensive targeted expeditions. Preserve habitat where it benefits extant species regardless of thylacine status. Archive ambiguous samples rather than destroy them. Escalate only when evidence crosses a preregistered threshold.

The worst design is an all-or-nothing hunt funded by headlines. The best design makes searching for the ghost improve knowledge of the living forest even when the ghost never appears.

Then silence is not wasted. It becomes ecological data.
