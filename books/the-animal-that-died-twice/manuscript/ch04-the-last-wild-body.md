# 4. The Last Wild Body

In 1930, Wilfred Batty shot a thylacine at Mawbanna after it was found near his poultry. The photograph is grotesquely useful. A man, a dead animal, a place, a date: this is the sort of record extinction statistics love because the false-positive rate is effectively negligible.

Yet even the phrase 'last wild thylacine' must be handled carefully. It means the last one known to have been shot and documented, not necessarily the last one alive in the wild. Later live captures occurred. One animal entered Hobart Zoo in the 1930s and survived until 1936. Hard evidence was thinning, not switching off cleanly.

There is an important asymmetry between the last body and the first decade without a body. One confirmed specimen is enough to prove persistence at its date. A decade without specimens is not enough by itself to prove extinction. The evidentiary burden is directional.

That asymmetry is why extinction models exist at all. If every animal carried a transmitter, the problem would collapse into telemetry. Historical extinctions instead give us a last-seen series. The statistical task is to infer how likely it is that a detection process could stop while the species persists.

But the thylacine's detection process was violently nonrandom. Bodies were produced by hunters and trappers. As the species became protected, producing a body became legally and culturally different. A specimen curve spanning persecution and protection cannot be interpreted as if sampling effort were constant.

The 1930 body is thus both anchor and warning. It establishes that thylacines remained wild at least that late. It also comes from an observation regime we cannot reproduce: thousands of armed people living in and around habitat where killing the animal had recently been normalized.

Later searchers had better cameras and worse abundance. Earlier hunters had worse cameras and more animals. Which era had greater detection power is not obvious without calculation.

The photograph tempts us to declare a boundary because physical evidence feels categorically superior to testimony. It is superior. But the difference between 'superior' and 'exclusive' is the difference between sensible inference and a rigged model. If physical remains are the only admissible evidence, extinction will always be estimated close to the final specimen. If every plausible witness counts as real, extinction can be pushed arbitrarily late.

A serious model does neither. It gives different evidence different error rates, then lets those assumptions remain visible enough to argue about.
