# 17. Resurrection Money

De-extinction changes the economics of extinction stories even before it changes any animal.

Once a lost species becomes a candidate for genomic reconstruction, every preserved tissue acquires new value and every argument about the date of extinction gains a second audience. The extinct animal becomes not only an object of mourning but a platform: biotechnology, conservation fundraising, intellectual property, tourism, identity.

This does not make de-extinction research fraudulent. It changes incentives around the evidence.

The first discipline is linguistic. A genetically engineered proxy carrying selected thylacine traits would not erase the historical extinction. Nor would it prove that post-1936 sightings were correct. A recreated phenotype and an undiscovered remnant population are different claims.

The second discipline is ecological. Reconstructing a genome is not reconstructing a lost ecosystem. The historical thylacine occupied prey communities, disease environments and landscapes that have changed.

The third is archival. De-extinction programs can produce valuable science regardless of whether a surrogate animal ever exists: improved assemblies, developmental biology, marsupial reproductive techniques, museum genomics. Those gains should be stated separately from spectacle.

For this book, the important point is that extinction now has a market afterlife. That increases the need for clean epistemic bookkeeping. Investors, media, advocates and critics all have incentives to compress uncertainty into a stronger sentence.

The thylacine was once worth a pound dead. A century later, its absence can be worth far more. The currency changed. The need to audit the claim did not.
