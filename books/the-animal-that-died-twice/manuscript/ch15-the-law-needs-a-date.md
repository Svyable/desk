# 15. The Law Needs a Date

Nature tolerates probability distributions. Statutes prefer categories.

A species is listed, delisted, protected, extinct, extant. Funding rules and land-use decisions often require a state even when evidence supports only a range of probabilities.

This mismatch can create perverse incentives. Declaring extinction may release agencies from search obligations. Refusing to declare can lock scarce funds into charismatic lost species. Private groups can gain attention by keeping possibility alive. Skeptics can gain status by treating uncertainty as gullibility.

The thylacine has occupied this administrative border for decades. It is culturally alive, biologically almost certainly gone, and legally treated as extinct/presumed extinct while reports continue.

The better policy architecture would decouple several questions now forced into one label. What is the probability any individual survives? What is the probability a viable population survives? What is the expected conservation value of additional search? Does maintaining habitat protection benefit other species? What passive monitoring would occur anyway?

Those questions can have different answers.

A species with a one-in-ten-thousand chance of persistence may not justify a dedicated helicopter campaign. It may still justify adding a validated DNA assay to water samples already being collected for other purposes.

Probability should therefore control search intensity, not dignity. The animal does not become less important because the posterior shrinks. The conservation decision simply changes.

This is the practical reason to get extinction epistemology right. A wrong year in a paper is an academic problem. A wrong threshold embedded in conservation institutions can decide which species receive the last available field team.
