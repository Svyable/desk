# Dulles's Flight Home

He did not look like a man who had just crossed six time zones, because in 1956 there was no accepted way to look like that. John Foster Dulles was sixty-eight years old, a Presbyterian elder's grandson turned Wall Street lawyer turned Secretary of State, a man whose face in photographs from the period carries the same expression in a courtroom, a cabinet meeting, or a receiving line — flat, unamused, entirely occupied with the matter at hand. He had flown to Cairo the way senior American officials flew to the Middle East in the mid-1950s, which is to say slowly, on a propeller aircraft that stopped at least once to refuel, through a night that his own body registered as roughly nine or ten hours shorter than the one everyone waiting for him on the ground had just lived through. Egypt sits some six hours ahead of Washington. There was no getting around that gap by flying faster; jet passenger service across the Atlantic was still two years away, and even after it arrived it would only shrink the number of hours spent in the air, not the number of hours by which a traveler's body clock and the local clock had come to disagree. Dulles landed, was met, and within a day or so was sitting across a table from officials representing Gamal Abdel Nasser, discussing the largest and most consequential infrastructure project in the modern history of Egypt.

The Aswan High Dam was meant to do for Egypt what the Nile itself had never quite managed on its own: tame a river that had flooded and receded on its own schedule for the whole of recorded history, store its water against the dry years, and turn its current into electricity for a country trying to industrialize a generation after most of its neighbors had begun. Nasser wanted the dam more than he wanted almost anything else available to an Egyptian head of state in 1956, and Washington and London, along with the World Bank, held the financing that could make it real. The dam sat, in other words, exactly where Cold War calculation and postcolonial ambition tend to sit together — a genuine national need that also happened to be a lever two superpowers could use to pull a strategically located country toward one camp or the other. Dulles's job in Cairo was to move that lever, and by the account of it that has come down to us, he moved it while running on whatever reserve of alertness a man in his late sixties has left after a long transoceanic flight, several time zones behind his own nervous system, in meetings that by diplomatic convention did not wait for him to catch up.

A 2015 Washington Post account of the episode, headlined "How Jet Lag Hurts Diplomats Without Them Even Realizing It," lays out what happened next with a bluntness the original participants never had reason to use about themselves. Dulles negotiated the American commitment to help finance the dam, in a state the article treats as jet-lagged in the clinical sense, and flew home. The flight back to Washington was, if anything, longer and more punishing than the flight out, crossing the same six hours in the opposite direction against a body that had barely finished adjusting to the first crossing before being asked to reverse it. It was sometime around his return, according to that account, that Dulles learned Egypt had, during the very period surrounding his visit, arranged to purchase a substantial quantity of weapons from the Soviet bloc — arms that would arrive by way of Czechoslovakia, a channel Moscow used through the 1950s specifically so that a deal with a nonaligned state would not read, on paper, as a deal with the Soviet Union itself. Whatever Dulles had believed he was securing in Cairo, he came home to evidence that Nasser had been hedging, or worse, at the very moment the two countries were sitting down to discuss American money.

What followed did not happen quickly, and no single meeting in Cairo caused it by itself. Within the year, amid rising anger in Washington and London over Egypt's tilt toward Moscow and over Nasser's recognition of Communist China, the American and British financing offer for the dam was withdrawn. Nasser responded within days by nationalizing the Suez Canal Company, seizing the waterway that carried most of Western Europe's oil and had been under substantial British and French control since the previous century. Britain, France, and Israel invaded within months, in a coordinated military action that the Eisenhower administration had not sanctioned and moved swiftly to shut down, in one of the more consequential ruptures between the United States and its oldest European allies of the entire Cold War. Historians have argued for decades over how much weight to put on any single cause in that chain — the arms deal itself, the recognition of Beijing, the financing terms Egypt found insulting, the domestic politics of an American election year, British and French colonial anxiety about a canal they had run for generations — and a fair reading of the record resists pinning the Suez Crisis on any one man's fatigue in any one meeting. What makes the Cairo trip worth returning to is narrower than a claim about causing a war. It is that a Secretary of State conducted one of the more delicate negotiations of his career while, by a fairly ordinary and well-documented physiological measure, not fully in possession of the judgment he would have brought to the same table a week later, and that almost nobody around him, least of all Dulles himself, would have had a vocabulary for describing what was wrong.

That absence of vocabulary is worth sitting with, because it is not a small detail. The word jet lag did not exist yet in 1956; it would not enter common use for roughly another decade, once jet aircraft had shortened flight times enough that ordinary business travelers, not just diplomats and generals, began crossing five or six time zones in a single day and noticing, with some consistency, that they felt wrong on arrival in a way a good night's sleep did not fix. Before the word existed, the condition it named did not stop existing; it simply had no name, which meant it had no official standing in anyone's account of why a decision went one way rather than another. A negotiator who arrived rattled from a bad flight, snapped at an aide, or agreed to terms he would reconsider a week later had, at the time, no framework for attributing any of that to the clock in his own skull rather than to plain bad judgment or a bad night's sleep of the ordinary kind. The affliction was doing its work in the body of every diplomat, executive, and head of state who had ever crossed an ocean for a meeting; it simply went unrecorded, because there was nothing yet to record it as.

What that Washington Post account, and the research it draws on, describes is more specific and more mechanical than "tired from travel." Jet lag is a circadian rhythm disorder — a mismatch, not a mood, between the internal clock a traveler's body has carried since it last stood still and the local clock of wherever that body has just landed. The internal clock in question runs on a schedule of its own, generated by a small cluster of neurons and reinforced by nearly every organ in the body, and it does not reset the moment a plane's wheels touch the tarmac. It resets gradually, at a rate that the same account puts at roughly a day to a day and a half of adjustment for every time zone crossed — which means a traveler moving across six zones, as Dulles did in each direction, is looking at something close to a week before the internal clock and the local clock are fully back in agreement. Almost nobody waits that long. Diplomatic itineraries, then and now, do not build in a week of acclimatization before the substantive talks begin; a delegation lands, rests for a day if it is lucky, and sits down to negotiate on a schedule set by the host government, the traveling party's other commitments, and the general institutional assumption that competent adults simply manage. Until the clock finishes catching up, the account notes, jet lag can affect cognitive and physical performance in ways that matter a great deal for people whose job is to weigh evidence, read another party's intentions, and decide what to commit their country to.

None of that machinery announces itself the way exhaustion does. A traveler who has missed a night's sleep outright tends to know it — the eyes feel heavy, the coffee stops working, the body all but files a complaint. Circadian misalignment is quieter and, in some ways, more dangerous for exactly that reason. A jet-lagged negotiator can feel reasonably alert, can sit upright and speak in full sentences and follow the thread of a conversation, while the parts of cognition most useful for negotiation — patience under pressure, the willingness to sit with ambiguity rather than resolve it prematurely, the fine calibration of risk that separates a good deal from a bad one, the temper that keeps a frustrating morning from curdling into a rash afternoon — are running on a body that believes, at some deep physiological level, that it is the middle of the night. The headline the Washington Post chose for its 2015 account is precise on this point in a way that is easy to read past: diplomats are hurt by jet lag without them even realizing it. The realizing part is the whole difficulty. A negotiator who knew, in the moment, that his judgment was compromised could adjust for it, defer a decision, ask an aide to double-check his reasoning. A negotiator who feels approximately fine has no reason to do any of that, and neither did the institution around him, which had built its entire model of a competent diplomat around the assumption that a man capable of sitting upright in a meeting was capable of the judgment the meeting required.

This is not a problem unique to one Secretary of State on one trip in 1956, and it has not gone away simply because the word for it now exists. Business executives fly overnight into acquisition talks and are expected to be sharp by nine the next morning, because a chartered flight and a hotel room read, to the organizations paying for them, as sufficient preparation for a meeting that will determine the shape of a company for the next decade. Heads of state arrive at summits having crossed as many time zones as Dulles did, often on schedules more compressed than his, because modern communications have collapsed the excuse for taking a slower boat and left only the expectation of arriving ready. Central bankers, trade negotiators, generals conferring with allied counterparts — an entire class of people whose decisions carry outsized consequences routinely make those decisions within the window this chapter has been describing, the one to ten days after a long-haul flight during which the body has not yet finished renegotiating its own schedule. Institutions that would never let a surgeon operate without checking whether she had slept, that build fatigue rules into how airline pilots and truck drivers are scheduled, have historically applied none of that scrutiny to a diplomat stepping off a plane and directly into a negotiating room, because the diplomat's fatigue does not show up on any of the instruments the institution is used to reading.

The trouble with using an episode like Dulles's Cairo trip as evidence of anything, however, is the trouble that runs through most of history when it is asked to double as data. It happened once. It happened inside a set of Cold War pressures, personal relationships, and competing intelligence assessments that cannot be isolated from the state of one man's circadian rhythm, and no one can rerun 1956 with a well-rested Dulles to see whether the financing offer would have been drafted differently, or whether Nasser's turn toward Moscow would have played out any other way regardless. A single anecdote, however well documented, can illustrate a mechanism. It cannot, by itself, measure one. To actually put a number on what jet lag does to high-stakes judgment and performance — to move from a plausible story about one Secretary of State to something closer to a law of physiology — requires a domain that runs the same basic experiment thousands of times over, with outcomes precise enough to count and a sample size large enough to swamp everything else that might be going on in any single instance.

Professional sports turns out to be almost exactly that domain, for reasons that have nothing to do with sports being important and everything to do with sports being unusually well recorded. A baseball team crosses time zones dozens of times a season, in both directions, on a schedule set well in advance and largely indifferent to how anyone happens to be sleeping. Every plate appearance, every pitch, every stolen base attempt across more than a century of professional play has been logged, and for the more recent decades those logs come with enough surrounding detail — which city, which direction, how many days since the last game — to let a researcher match a specific flight to a specific outcome on the field. Nobody has ever done that for the private judgment of a Cabinet officer meeting a foreign head of state; the meetings are too rare, too consequential to run as an experiment, and too poorly instrumented for anyone to separate the clock's contribution from everything else in the room. Nobody has to do that for a shortstop's reaction time on a ground ball hit to his right. It is simply there in the record, waiting to be counted.

A team of researchers that included Ravi Allada, a circadian biologist at Northwestern University, did exactly that counting, working through two decades of Major League Baseball data and publishing the results in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, with the findings drawing wide coverage in 2017. The approach was straightforward in outline even though the underlying statistics were not: match each team's travel — how far, in which direction, how recently — against its performance in the games that followed, while holding constant the ordinary variables that already explain most of what happens in a baseball season, things like opponent quality, home-field advantage, and simple rest. What was left over, after all of that had been accounted for, was a residue attributable to jet lag specifically, and it did not spread itself evenly across the game. Certain skills were hit harder than others. Pitching, which depends on a pitcher repeating a precise, practiced motion with fine control over release timing, showed measurable degradation. Base running, which depends on split-second reaction and the kind of explosive first step that a well-rested body produces more reliably than a misaligned one, showed it too. Skills with more margin for error — a batter's raw power, for instance, which tolerates a slightly late swing better than a pitcher's control tolerates a slightly early release — were less affected, which is itself a clue about what jet lag actually degrades: not strength or stamina in any simple sense, but the fine temporal precision that separates a well-timed act from a poorly timed one.

The finding that drew the most attention, though, was not about which skills suffered but about how much. Home-field advantage is one of the most durable and well-documented edges in professional sports, a statistical regularity found across nearly every league and nearly every era, generally attributed to some combination of crowd noise, travel fatigue for the visiting side, and the comfort of familiar surroundings. It is not a subtle effect, and it does not normally disappear. What the Northwestern-linked research found was that jet lag, under the wrong travel conditions, was large enough to erase it — specifically, that a team returning home from a long road trip that had carried it across several time zones could show up in its own ballpark, in front of its own crowd, and play like a visitor rather than a host, because the advantage the building and the fans were supposed to confer had been cancelled out by a clock still running on the road trip's schedule. A team can walk back into the stadium it knows best, sleep in its own beds, and still be operating, in every measurable sense the data could reach, as though it had never come home at all.

The direction of travel mattered as much as the distance, and this is the part of the finding that connects most directly back to the physiology sitting underneath Dulles's Cairo trip. The effect was consistently worse for eastward travel than for westward travel covering the same number of zones — a team flying from the West Coast to play a series on the East Coast, and then flying home, showed a larger performance cost than a team making the reverse trip. This asymmetry is not an accident of baseball scheduling; it is close to a universal feature of how the human circadian clock behaves, discovered and rediscovered in one domain after another once anyone has bothered to measure it. Left alone, without any external cue at all, the human internal clock does not run on exactly twenty-four hours — it runs, on average, on something a little longer, which means the body's natural inclination, absent correction, is to drift later rather than earlier, to want one more hour of day before agreeing to sleep. Flying west hands the body more or less what it already wants: extra hours to fill before nightfall, a schedule that asks the clock to delay rather than advance, which is the direction its own internal tendency was already leaning. Flying east asks for the opposite — for the body to fall asleep and wake up earlier than its internal clock is prepared to accept, forcing an advance against its own natural drift rather than a delay along it. Advancing a clock is simply harder than delaying one, for reasons built into the biochemistry of the pacemaker itself, and that asymmetry shows up reliably enough, across enough different measurements, that researchers treat it as one of the more dependable regularities in the whole field. A pitcher's control after flying east into a hostile crowd's ballpark is being asked to fight the same current Dulles was fighting when he sat down in Cairo already six hours behind himself, except that nobody was logging his release point, and nobody was comparing it to what it would have been on a normal Tuesday in Washington.

That asymmetry might have stayed a baseball curiosity — a striking number attached to one sport, one dataset, one research team — if it had not shown up again somewhere else. A 2022 study published through Frontiers in Physiology, and made available via the PubMed Central archive, examined jet lag's effect on the National Basketball Association, a league whose demands on the body look almost nothing like baseball's. Basketball asks for explosive jumping, constant lateral movement, split-second shot selection under a defender's outstretched arm, dozens of times a game, across an eighty-two-game season that routes teams through nearly every time zone on the continent, often on back-to-back nights that leave almost no room for recovery of any kind. Adjusting for the other variables that ordinarily explain a team's performance — rest, opponent strength, the rhythm of the schedule generally — the researchers found that eastward travel specifically, not travel in general, was associated with impaired performance in a team's home games, the same directional pattern the baseball research had already surfaced in a completely different sport with a completely different set of physical demands.

It is the convergence, more than either finding on its own, that makes the case. A single study can be an artifact of its own dataset — a quirk of one league's scheduling quirks, one era's rules, one set of statistical choices that might have come out differently with a different research team asking the question. Two studies, in two sports that share almost nothing physically, using different data and different methods, and arriving at the same directional asymmetry, are harder to explain away as coincidence. What they are converging on is not a fact about baseball or a fact about basketball. It is a fact about the pacemaker sitting behind the eyes of every pitcher, every point guard, and every Secretary of State who has ever stepped off a long flight and directly into a room where a decision was waiting.

Which is, in the end, what makes a pitcher's ruined release point and a diplomat's clouded judgment the same story told in two very different registers. Nobody counted Dulles's decision-making the way researchers counted a strikeout rate or a made three-pointer; the record of what happened in that room in Cairo survives as narrative, not as data, and always will. But the mechanism the sports data lays bare in such fine grain — the fine motor and cognitive precision that degrades first, the asymmetry between the direction that is merely uncomfortable and the direction that is genuinely impairing, the fact that a person operating under this condition typically has no internal signal telling him so — is not a mechanism that stops at the edge of a baseball diamond. It was running in Dulles's body in 1956 as surely as it runs in a shortstop's body flying home from a road trip today. The difference is only that one produced a box score and the other produced a foreign policy, and only one of those leaves a number behind for anyone to check.

What a franchise does with a number like that is a different question from what a Cabinet department in 1956 could have done with an anecdote, because a number invites a response an anecdote cannot. A team that knows, in the aggregate, how much an eastward flight costs its pitching staff can decide to travel a day earlier, to charter rather than fly commercial, to build a schedule that clusters eastward trips differently than it clusters westward ones — not out of any particular tenderness toward its players' sleep, but because the data says a specific, quantifiable edge is sitting on the table for whichever front office bothers to pick it up. That is a different relationship to the body's clock than the one this book has spent its previous chapters describing, where the clock mostly showed up as a source of hazard — a reactor operator's blunted judgment at four in the morning, a resident physician's degraded reflexes on hour twenty-eight of a shift, a ship's crew making a fatal course correction in the small hours because nobody had accounted for what the hour was doing to them. Those were all stories about institutions that had ignored the clock and paid for it. What baseball's box scores and basketball's shot charts describe instead is the same clock treated as a variable that can be planned around, hedged against, even turned to advantage, once someone with enough data and enough motive bothers to measure it precisely.

Medicine, it turns out, got there before professional sports did, and for reasons that have nothing to do with box scores. A chemotherapy dose delivered at the wrong hour can do meaningfully more damage to a healthy cell and meaningfully less to a cancerous one than the identical dose delivered twelve hours earlier or later — a finding built not on a single dramatic anecdote but on decades of careful measurement of how the body's own rhythms change what a drug does once it enters the bloodstream. That is where the same clock that blurred Dulles's judgment in Cairo, and that still measurably degrades a pitcher's control after a long flight east, starts to look less like a liability to be managed and more like an instrument waiting for someone patient enough to learn how to play it.
