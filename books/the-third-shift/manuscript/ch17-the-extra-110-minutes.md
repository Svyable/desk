# The Extra 110 Minutes

A Division I basketball season does not leave much room in the day. Practice often starts before the sun is fully up, because it is the only block of time that does not conflict with class, and class cannot move, because the university has other students to run a schedule around. Weight room sessions get wedged in around it. Film review eats the early evening. Road games mean a bus to an airport, a flight that lands after midnight, a hotel bed slept in for five or six hours, and a shootaround the next morning before the game that night. None of this is unusual; it is simply what a serious college athletics program looks like, and it has looked roughly that way for decades. What it produces, reliably, is a population of young adults who are chronically short on sleep in more or less the same way office workers, medical residents, and night-shift nurses are short on sleep — not because anyone intends it, but because the schedule was built around everything except the one variable that determines how well a nervous system performs the next day.

Athletic culture, for most of its modern history, has treated this arrangement as simply the cost of doing business, and has often gone further than that, treating short sleep as something close to a badge of honor. The coach who runs the earliest practice is the disciplined one. The player who shows up for a 6 a.m. lift after a late film session is the dedicated one. Sleep, in this framework, sits in roughly the same category as a sore back or a missed meal — an inconvenience to be pushed through, evidence of the toughness the sport is supposed to build, rather than a variable that might be worth managing on purpose. Strength coaches counted reps. Nutritionists counted calories. Almost nobody on a college athletics staff, in the middle of the 2000s, was counting hours of sleep, or treating them as a lever a coaching staff could actually pull.

It was into exactly this population that a Stanford researcher named Cheri D. Mah decided to ask a fairly simple question: what happens if you give some of it back.

Mah worked out of Stanford's sleep research world at a moment when that world already had unusual authority behind it. William C. Dement, one of the four co-authors on the paper that would eventually come out of this work, had spent the preceding half century more or less inventing the field. Dement had helped identify the basic architecture of REM sleep in the 1950s, working alongside Nathaniel Kleitman at the University of Chicago, and had gone on to found Stanford's sleep research program, where he spent decades studying what happens to the mind and body when sleep is cut short — and coining, along the way, the concept of "sleep debt," the idea that lost sleep accumulates rather than simply evaporating with the missed night. By the time Mah began her work with the basketball team, in the middle of the 2000s, sleep debt was a well-established idea inside sleep medicine. What was much less established was what happened when a person actually paid the debt off — not in a laboratory, on a task designed to isolate alertness from everything else, but in the middle of a real season, doing a real, physically demanding job in front of a stopwatch and a scoreboard.

That distinction mattered more than it might sound. Most of what science knew, going into this study, about the cost of insufficient sleep and the benefit of additional sleep came from research conducted on ordinary volunteers performing narrow, artificial tasks — pressing a button when a light appeared, tracking a moving dot on a screen, answering simple math problems against the clock. That kind of research is valuable; it is how sleep scientists established, with real precision, that a sleep-deprived brain reacts more slowly and lapses more often than a rested one. But it does not tell you much about a five-foot free throw released under fatigue, or a full-court sprint executed for the fortieth time in a two-hour practice, or the accumulated toll of a season's worth of short nights on an athlete whose job requires sustained, complex physical judgment rather than a single isolated response to a single isolated stimulus. Nobody had put a group of elite, actively competing athletes through a real sleep-extension protocol, across a real season, and measured what happened to the actual skills their sport required. Mah's study, published in the journal Sleep in 2011 with co-authors Kenneth E. Mah, Eric J. Kezirian, and William C. Dement, was the first to do it.

The design was, in outline, almost plain. The researchers worked with members of the Stanford University men's varsity basketball team across two competitive seasons, from 2005 to 2008, tracking each player through a baseline period of two to four weeks in which he simply kept whatever sleep schedule he had always kept — the ordinary compressed rhythm of practice, class, and travel described above — and then through a subsequent period of five to seven weeks in which he tried to extend his sleep, night after night, for as long into the season as the study ran. There was no drug, no supplement, no piece of equipment, no change to the practice plan or the playbook. The only variable the researchers were manipulating was time spent asleep, and the only thing standing between the baseline numbers and the extension numbers was players getting more of it.

That simplicity was itself a kind of methodological discipline, and it is worth noticing what the study did not do. The researchers did not put players on a new supplement regimen, did not alter their conditioning program, did not change their diet, and did not adjust the practice schedule the coaching staff had already built around the season. Eric J. Kezirian, one of the paper's co-authors, brought to the project a background in sleep medicine focused on how sleep disruption affects daytime function — the kind of clinical grounding that kept the study's design narrow rather than sprawling. A study that changed five things at once and then found an improvement would have left open the question of which of the five things had actually mattered. A study that changed one thing, and nothing else, did not have that problem. Whatever showed up in the sprint times and the shooting percentages had to be traceable to the additional sleep, because the additional sleep was the only new fact introduced into the players' weeks.

They got, on average, a great deal more of it. Across the extension period, players' mean total sleep time rose by 110.9 minutes a night compared with their baseline — nearly two full additional hours, night after night, for five to seven weeks. That number is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is not a marginal adjustment. It is the difference between a sleep schedule built to survive a punishing season and something closer to what sleep researchers had been recommending for young adults for years, largely without much success in getting anyone to actually follow the recommendation. The players did not need new information to make this happen. They needed, and were given, the structure and the accountability of a study asking them to try.

What the extra time bought them showed up first on a stopwatch. As part of the study, players ran a timed sprint of 282 feet — three lengths of a regulation basketball court, down and back and down again, the kind of full-court conditioning sprint that shows up in some form in nearly every serious basketball program's practice plan. At baseline, the average sprint time was 16.2 seconds. After the extension period, it had dropped to 15.5 seconds. Seven-tenths of a second sounds small until it is placed next to what elite athletes ordinarily do to shave time off a sprint — the strength programs, the technique coaching, the years of repetition aimed at exactly this kind of margin. Here, the improvement came from an intervention that involved no additional physical training at all. The players were doing the same conditioning work they had always done. The only thing that had changed was how rested they were while they did it.

The shooting numbers told a related but distinct story, because shooting a basketball well is not primarily a test of raw speed. It is a test of fine motor control repeated under fatigue, of a shot mechanic that has to reproduce itself identically release after release, late in a game, with a crowd making noise and a defender in the way. Following the sleep-extension period, both free-throw and three-point shooting accuracy improved by 9 percent — a substantial gain in a skill where professional and elite college programs measure success and failure in single percentage points, where the difference between a good shooting team and a mediocre one in a given season is often smaller than that. A faster sprint time reflects a body working better. A better shooting percentage reflects something closer to a mind working better — sustained attention, fine coordination, the capacity to execute a well-learned motor sequence without the small errors that fatigue tends to introduce into exactly this kind of task. Nine percent, on that skill, is not a rounding error. It is the kind of number that shows up in a box score and, over a season, in the standings.

None of this is mysterious, in light of what sleep researchers already understood about what sleep actually does for a body and a brain. A free throw is not, mechanically, reinvented each time a player steps to the line; it is a motor sequence rehearsed tens of thousands of times until it becomes close to automatic, and the consolidation of that kind of procedural memory — the process by which a practiced movement gets encoded more durably, more reliably reproducible under pressure — happens disproportionately during sleep, not during the practice reps themselves. A player who is short on sleep is not just groggier. He is running the same motor program on a system that had less time overnight to finish filing it away, and asking a fatigued prefrontal cortex to catch and correct the small deviations — a slightly off elbow angle, a rushed release, a flinch at contact — that separate a make from a miss. Extend the sleep, and both sides of that equation improve at once: better consolidation of the skill itself, and a better-rested set of neural resources available to execute it cleanly under the fatigue and pressure of an actual game.

It is also worth being honest about what a single study of this kind can and cannot establish, because the paper's own authors would have been the first to say so. This was one team, one sport, at one well-resourced research university, studying a small number of elite, highly conditioned young men across two seasons — not a large randomized trial across many programs, many sports, and many kinds of bodies. Whether the same 110 minutes would buy the same seven-tenths of a second and the same nine percentage points for a lacrosse team, a marathoner, or a factory worker running a machine on a night shift is an inference, not a finding the study itself proved. What the study did prove, cleanly and specifically, is that for this population, doing this sport, the relationship between sleep and performance was not a rounding error or a statistical illusion. It was large enough to see with a stopwatch.

The researchers also tracked a set of measures that did not show up on a stopwatch or a shot chart but were, in their own way, just as central to the study's argument. Players' reaction times grew faster during the extension period. Daytime sleepiness — the kind that shows up as a heavy afternoon, a fight to stay alert through a lecture or a film session — decreased. And mood improved, a finding that is easy to treat as a soft footnote next to the sprint times and shooting percentages but that mattered a great deal to the researchers' broader case, because a tired, irritable athlete is not simply a slower one. He is a worse decision-maker, a worse teammate, a less coachable player in the specific moments — a late timeout, a disputed call, a coach's correction delivered in front of the rest of the team — where a season is often actually decided.

Taken together, the results amounted to something researchers studying fatigue rarely get to hand anyone: a clean, largely uncomplicated demonstration that a single, freely available intervention — more sleep, and nothing else — produced measurable gains across nearly every category the researchers thought to check. There was no confound to untangle, no question of whether the players had also changed their diet or their training regimen or their supplement stack over the same stretch of weeks. The instruction was to sleep more. The players slept, on average, 110.9 more minutes a night. And in the categories that actually decide a basketball game — how fast a player covers the floor, how reliably he makes a shot he has made ten thousand times before, how sharp his attention is by the fourth quarter of a close one — the numbers moved, and they moved in one direction.

It is worth pausing on why this particular study, of all the sleep research produced in the years around it, became one of the most frequently cited pieces of evidence in the growing conversation about sleep and athletic performance. Part of the answer is the size of the effects, which were larger than most sleep scientists, working from the existing laboratory literature, would have predicted from two extra hours a night. But a larger part of the answer is what the study was measuring in the first place. A psychomotor vigilance task administered to undergraduates in a windowless lab room tells you something true and important about attention and reaction time, but it does not translate cleanly into anything a general reader, or for that matter a professional sports franchise, can picture. A 282-foot sprint and a free-throw percentage translate immediately. Everyone who has ever watched a basketball game has an intuitive sense of what seven-tenths of a second off a sprint means, and what a 9 percent jump in shooting accuracy would do to a team's chances in a close game. The study's power came not only from its numbers but from the fact that its numbers were fluent in a language — feet, seconds, made shots — that did not require translation to be understood as significant.

There is something almost restful about this study, set against the material that fills most of the rest of this book. Nobody in this chapter died. No investigators had to reconstruct a control room from wreckage and testimony, weighing which of a dozen contributing factors deserved how much blame. No regulator had to be shamed into rewriting a rule only after a disaster forced the issue into public view. Mah, Kezirian, Dement, and their colleague simply asked a group of athletes to sleep more for a few weeks and wrote down what happened. The intervention required no new technology, no policy fight, no lawsuit, no grieving family testifying before a legislature. It required only that a group of young men be given permission, structure, and encouragement to do something their bodies had presumably wanted to do all along, and the results were as close to a controlled, real-world demonstration of the book's central claim as this account will offer anywhere: that the body's clock, respected rather than fought, is not merely a hazard to be managed but an asset that can be actively drawn on.

That reframing is the real point of including a college basketball study in a book that has otherwise spent its chapters in control rooms, cockpits, cargo ships, and hospital wards. Everywhere else in this book, the argument for honoring the body's clock has had to be made defensively — as a case for what disaster, error, or harm a given institution could avoid if it took circadian biology seriously. The nuclear operator, the airline pilot, the resident physician: in each case the claim has been that ignoring the clock costs something, sometimes catastrophically, and that the burden of proof sat with anyone arguing that the old way of scheduling things could continue unexamined. Here the argument runs the other way. Nobody was in danger during the Stanford study. The team was not going to explode a reactor or crash a plane if a player's reaction time stayed a beat too slow. What the study demonstrated instead was upside — that respecting the clock is not only about the floor beneath which things go wrong, but about how high performance can climb when biology is finally allowed to cooperate with effort rather than quietly working against it.

It is not a large step from a basketball court to nearly any other setting where the underlying skills are the same. Sprinting and shooting are, at bottom, a body executing fast, precise, well-rehearsed actions under time pressure and fatigue, guided by a mind that has to keep making small correct judgments quickly and keep making them correctly as the task wears on. That description covers a great deal more of modern work than the phrase "athletic performance" usually calls to mind. It covers a surgeon's hands late in a long operation. It covers an air traffic controller reading a radar screen during a busy stretch, a trader executing an order in a fast-moving market, a long-haul driver holding a lane at highway speed after ten hours behind the wheel, a software engineer debugging production code at 2 a.m. because that is when the outage happened. Very little of the modern economy still runs on raw physical strength, the kind of labor sleep loss barely touches. Increasingly, it runs on exactly the combination the Stanford study measured — speed, precision, and judgment, sustained over hours, under fatigue — which means the study's finding is a great deal less narrow than "college basketball players shoot better when rested." It is closer to a finding about the operating conditions of a very large share of the work being done in any given hour of the day, in offices and hospitals and cockpits that have nothing to do with a basketball court at all.

The chapters before this one made that case largely through the language of what could go wrong — a reactor pushed past its own stability at 1:23 in the morning, a cockpit reading its instruments during the low point of the body's own alertness curve, an intern's judgment thinning out somewhere past hour twenty of a shift no one had bothered to shorten. Those are true stories, and the institutions in them eventually had to reckon with the clock because the alternative was measured in bodies. But an argument built entirely out of catastrophes carries a hidden cost of its own: it lets an institution that has not yet had its disaster conclude, not unreasonably, that the argument does not apply to it. Nobody at a mid-sized accounting firm or a regional call center or a suburban high school is likely to cause a meltdown by ignoring the clock. It is easy, from inside an institution like that, to file circadian biology under things that matter to nuclear plants and hospitals and leave it there. What a study like this one offers instead is a case where nothing was at stake but a season, where no one's safety depended on the outcome, and the finding still came back loud and clear. If a team of already elite, already well-trained athletes could get measurably faster and more accurate simply by sleeping more, the same logic does not require a control room or an operating table to be worth taking seriously. It only requires a task that rewards speed, precision, or judgment — which is to say, it requires very little at all.

None of this required a single team, or a single sport, to have discovered something no one had ever suspected. Coaches have always had a rough intuition that tired players play worse; parents have always suspected that an exhausted teenager does poorly on a test the next morning. What the Stanford study did was replace intuition with a number, attached to a stopwatch, a shot chart, and a peer-reviewed journal, and in doing so it gave the broader argument of this book something it badly needed: a case where the connection between sleep and performance was not buried under the wreckage of a disaster, contested for years by dueling investigations, or diluted across dozens of confounding variables. It was 110.9 minutes, isolated as cleanly as a study of this kind allows, followed by 15.5 seconds and a 9 percent jump on the stat sheet — cause and effect, laid out about as plainly as circadian biology is ever likely to lay itself out for anyone outside a laboratory.

The players in that study were, for the most part, nineteen and twenty and twenty-one years old — young enough that their bodies still carried some of the same circadian tendencies that mark adolescence: a natural pull toward later sleep and later waking, not yet fully settled into the earlier rhythm most adults eventually default to. A few years earlier, most of them had been in high school, waking for a first class that started, as it does at most American high schools, well before eight in the morning, at an hour that a large and consistent body of research says runs directly against the timing of the adolescent body clock. Nobody offered them a research protocol back then. Nobody gave them 110 minutes back. They got up when the bell told them to, the way tens of millions of teenagers across the country still do every school morning, and whatever performance they were leaving on the table — on a test, in a classroom discussion, behind the wheel of a car on the way to school — went unmeasured, because no one was standing there with a stopwatch.

That gap — between what a single, well-resourced study can demonstrate about a few dozen college athletes and what almost nobody has been willing to do about an entire population of adolescents whose biology points in exactly the same direction — is where the next chapter goes.
