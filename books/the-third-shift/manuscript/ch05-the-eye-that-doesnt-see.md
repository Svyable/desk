# The Eye That Doesn't See

In the 1920s, a Harvard geneticist named Clyde Keeler was breeding mice with a hereditary defect that destroyed their retinas within weeks of birth. The rods went first, then whatever cones the animals had ever possessed, and within a couple of months the mice were blind by every test Keeler had for blindness. They could not learn a maze that required telling light from dark. They walked into things placed in their path. And yet when Keeler shined a bright light into one of these mice's eyes, its pupil still contracted, smoothly and on cue, exactly as a sighted mouse's would. He wrote up the finding and moved on to other work, because there was no mechanism available to explain it. For the better part of sixty years it sat in the literature as a curiosity — the kind of result a field notices, cannot use, and quietly sets aside.

It resurfaced, still unexplained, in the 1980s and 1990s, and by then the puzzle had grown a second head. Researchers working on circadian rhythms — the roughly twenty-four-hour cycles the first chapters of this book have already traced down into individual cells — began using mice with the same inherited retinal degeneration Keeler had studied, mice with no functioning rods or cones by adulthood, and found that many of them still synchronized their activity to a light-dark cycle in the cage. They woke and ran on schedule as the room lights came on and off, as precisely as mice with a fully intact retina. Around the same time, researchers working with totally blind human volunteers turned up something just as strange. Some of these volunteers, people with no rod or cone function and no conscious experience of light whatsoever, nonetheless kept a body-temperature rhythm and a melatonin rhythm locked to the twenty-four-hour day, rising and falling on schedule with a sunrise and sunset they could not perceive. Others did not. Their rhythms drifted, arriving a little later each day, as though nothing from outside were reaching the clock at all. If rods and cones were really the only route by which light reached the brain, none of this should have been possible. Something else in the eye had to be sensing it.

By the time researchers were asking that question in earnest, the retina's basic wiring diagram had been settled for the better part of a century, and it left no obvious room for a second system. Rods handled dim light and motion. Cones handled color and fine detail in daylight. Both fed their signals into a mesh of intermediate cells, which in turn fed a final layer called retinal ganglion cells, whose long axons bundled together into the optic nerve and carried the finished picture back toward the brain. In this diagram, ganglion cells did not detect light. They forwarded it. They were the wiring, not the sensor — the stage of the retina that had already finished its job of assembling an image and was simply relaying the result onward. One branch of that relay, identified in the 1970s, ran not toward the visual centers that produce conscious sight but toward a cluster of neurons behind the eyes called the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the SCN, the body's master clock. This pathway was assumed to be carrying the same rod-and-cone-derived signal as everything else in the retina, simply routed to a different address. A dimmer switch built from the same parts as the picture.

The mouse and human puzzles were pointing at the same crack in that assumption. If the image-forming machinery were also the machinery that told the SCN what time it was, destroying the rods and cones should have destroyed both functions together, vision and photoentrainment failing as a pair. Instead the two were coming apart in front of researchers. Mice with no rods or cones left could still set their clocks by the light in the room. Some totally blind people could too. Whatever was reaching the SCN in these animals and these patients was not coming from either of the photoreceptors anyone thought existed in a mammalian eye.

The person who found it had not set out to solve a problem in retinal physiology at all. Ignacio Provencio spent the early part of his career studying a different kind of light sensitivity entirely: the pigment cells embedded in frog skin, which darken or lighten in response to ambient light with no involvement from the frog's eyes or brain. These cells, called melanophores, are photoreceptors in their own right, sitting in tissue that has nothing to do with vision, doing their job directly, cell by cell, wherever they happen to be on the animal's body. Provencio and his colleagues went looking for the opsin, the light-sensitive protein, that let melanophores respond to light this way. When they found the gene and cloned it, they named the protein melanopsin, after the cells in which it had first turned up.

That would have been a modest discovery about frog skin, if melanopsin had stayed in frog skin. It did not. Searching related tissue for genes similar to the one they had just found, Provencio and his colleagues turned up melanopsin in the mammalian retina — not in rods, and not in cones, but in a small, distinct population of the very cells the old wiring diagram had written off as passive relay wire: the retinal ganglion cells. Only a sliver of them, by proportion. But this sliver had axons running, disproportionately, straight into the suprachiasmatic nucleus, along the same puzzling pathway that had been assumed for two decades to be carrying secondhand information from rods and cones.

What followed, worked out across a run of experiments by Provencio's group and several others through the early 2000s, amounted to a genuine revision of the textbook. These melanopsin-containing ganglion cells did not merely relay a signal assembled upstream by rods and cones. Isolated in a dish, stripped of any input from the rest of the retina, they depolarized when light hit them directly. They were sensing light themselves, using melanopsin roughly the way a rod uses rhodopsin, and they had earned a name for the occasion: intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, ipRGCs for short — a third class of photoreceptor in the vertebrate eye, distinct from rods and distinct from cones, built for an entirely different job.

The functions that turned out to depend on this third photoreceptor went well beyond setting the SCN's daily phase. ipRGCs also drive the pupillary light reflex — the very response Keeler had watched persist in his rodless mice decades before anyone could explain it — constricting the pupil in bright light on their own, without any contribution from rods or cones. They drive light-avoidance behavior in animals for whom sudden brightness signals exposure to a predator, a response that survives in mice with no functioning rods or cones at all as long as ipRGCs remain intact. And they make a smaller, harder-to-isolate contribution to ordinary conscious vision itself, feeding a slow, low-resolution sense of overall brightness into the same visual pathways that rods and cones use for the sharper picture. Researchers testing totally blind volunteers who had lost all rod and cone function but retained ipRGCs found that some of these volunteers, despite insisting they could see nothing whatsoever, could still correctly report whether a light in the room had just been switched on, and described a vague, wordless sense that something had changed nearby. They were not seeing in any ordinary meaning of the word. They were registering brightness through a channel that never routes through the parts of the brain that produce the experience of sight, and doing it anyway.

Confirming that ipRGCs were doing all of this on their own, rather than merely amplifying leftover rod and cone input, took a further round of experiments using mice engineered to lack rods, cones, and melanopsin simultaneously. These triple-knockout animals lost the pupillary reflex almost entirely and stopped entraining to light-dark cycles at all, confirming that ipRGCs were not simply one route among several but the last common pathway all of it depended on. Mice missing only rods and cones, with melanopsin intact, kept both functions close to normal. The comparison settled what Keeler's mice and the blind human volunteers had been suggesting all along: take away rods and cones and the SCN still gets its cue; take away the ipRGCs and no amount of intact rod and cone function further upstream will get a signal through.

It is worth sitting with how strange this is, because familiarity with eyes makes it easy to slide past. For roughly a century and a half, since the cellular structure of the retina was first resolved under a microscope, the working assumption had been that a vertebrate eye contained exactly two kinds of light-sensing cell, tuned to different intensities and wavelengths but performing the same underlying task: turning photons into the raw material of a picture. Ganglion cells were the part of the retina that took that finished material and shipped it out toward the brain. Nobody expected the shipping department to also be manufacturing a separate product on the side. But that is close to what a subset of them turned out to be doing — cells sitting at the very last stage of the visual relay, wired for an entirely different assignment, answering not to the brain's picture of the world but to the brain's clock.

The ipRGCs are not, it turned out, working entirely alone. They receive input from rods and cones in addition to generating a light response of their own, which is part of why the system is subtler than a clean wall between seeing and time-telling. In both mice and humans, most of what rods and cones detect reaches the SCN by first feeding into ipRGCs, which integrate that borrowed input with what they sense directly and pass a combined signal on. The picture, then, is less a partition than a funnel: several channels of retinal information converging on one small population of cells that happens to hold the only route out of the eye toward the master clock. That convergence is precisely what makes ipRGCs load-bearing in a way that rods and cones, for all their importance to vision, are not. Damage rods and cones and a person loses image-forming sight but can, if the ipRGCs survive, go on entraining to light exactly as before. Damage the ipRGCs, or lose the eye that contains them, and no amount of intact rod and cone function further upstream will get a light signal to the clock at all, because the one wire out of the building has been cut.

This is what had happened, unevenly, to the mice and the people in the original puzzle. Many of the most common causes of profound visual blindness — retinitis pigmentosa prominent among them — destroy rods and cones gradually while leaving the inner retina, where ipRGCs sit, comparatively untouched. A person with this kind of blindness can be legally and functionally blind, unable to read a letter on a chart or make out a face across a room, while still carrying a working population of ipRGCs quietly reporting each day's brightness to the SCN. That was the group, mouse and human both, whose circadian rhythms kept entraining to light they had no conscious awareness of seeing at all. Keeler's rodless mice had never lost their pupillary reflex for exactly the same underlying reason: ipRGCs drive that reflex too, on their own, entirely independent of the rods that had degenerated within weeks of birth, decades before anyone knew ipRGCs existed to explain it.

The other group — the mice and the people whose rhythms drifted freely, unanchored to the world outside — had lost something further in, or lost more of it. Total absence of the eye. Optic nerve damage severe enough to take the ganglion cell axons with it. Whatever the specific cause, when the ipRGCs themselves were gone, or their connection to the brain was severed, no light signal reached the SCN at all, regardless of how bright the room was or what remained functional elsewhere in the visual system. The clock kept running. It simply had nothing left to synchronize itself to.

That distinction — blind to seeing, as opposed to blind to time — is not a technicality, and it is close to the center of what this chapter is actually about. It means that total blindness, treated as a natural experiment, is not one condition but at least two, and only one of them shows what happens when synchronization itself fails completely rather than merely narrowing to a single quiet channel most people never notice they have.

Total blindness of that second kind — no light perception of any sort, conscious or otherwise, because the eyes or the pathway running from them into the brain no longer function at all — produces a condition with an almost clinically flat name: non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder, usually shortened in the sleep-medicine literature to Non-24. Researchers who have followed circadian function in totally blind populations for decades, among them Steven Lockley, who has spent much of his career at Harvard Medical School and Brigham and Women's Hospital studying exactly this group, estimate that somewhere between 55 and 70 percent of people with no conscious light perception at all go on to develop it. That is not a rare complication of blindness. For a majority of people who lose all light perception, it appears to be closer to the default outcome, unless something intervenes.

To see why, it helps to recall what an earlier chapter's account of the bunker under Andechs already established: a human circadian clock left alone, with no light cues to correct it, does not keep a period of exactly twenty-four hours. It runs close to that, but rarely exactly — in most people, some number of minutes longer. In an ordinary life this small daily error is invisible, because morning light resets the clock before the error has any chance to accumulate into something a person would notice. Take away every trace of that reset, as happens when the eye's entire light-sensing apparatus, ipRGCs included, no longer functions, and the small daily error has nothing left to correct it. It accumulates instead, the way an unadjusted clock loses a few minutes a day and, given enough days, loses hours. A person's internal sleep and wake times shift later by that same small margin, night after night, carrying the sleep window slowly around the full twenty-four-hour day. For a stretch, the drifting window lands somewhere near night, where the rest of the household and the rest of civil time expect it, and sleep goes reasonably well. Then it keeps sliding, later and later, until it lands in the middle of what everyone else calls the workday, or the middle of a school afternoon, and a person is trying to be alert during what their own body insists, with total conviction, is the deepest part of the night — or trying to fall asleep through what it insists, with equal conviction, is early afternoon.

The lived pattern this produces is not steady insomnia so much as a slow, involuntary tour through every possible relationship between a person's body and the clock on the wall. Clinical descriptions of Non-24 report exactly this cycling: stretches of days or weeks when sleep is close to normal, because the drifting internal night happens to overlap the external one, followed by stretches when it does not, and a person who has done nothing differently, changed no habit and broken no discipline, finds themselves wide awake at 3 a.m. and desperate for sleep at 3 p.m., for reasons that have nothing to do with willpower and everything to do with a clock that is keeping excellent time relative to itself and no time at all relative to everyone else. It is jet lag with no destination and no arrival, repeating on a loop that the person did not create and cannot simply decide to break.

The disorder is sometimes confused, at a glance, with the milder chronotype variation the previous chapter described — the tendency of some people to run persistently later than the clock on the wall would like. It is not the same thing. A late chronotype is a clock that has settled, stubbornly, on the wrong phase and stays there, still capable of being anchored by light and social schedule even if it resists the anchoring. Non-24 is a clock with no anchor to resist or accept, moving continuously rather than settling anywhere, which is why the people who have it describe the experience less as being a night owl than as living inside a schedule nobody else is on, one that keeps making and breaking appointments with the rest of the world on a timetable of its own. A person with the disorder can hold a job, raise a family, keep commitments, but doing any of it during the weeks when their internal night has drifted into the middle of everyone else's day takes a kind of sustained effort that a person whose clock is simply anchored late never has to spend, because there is no light cue arriving each morning to make the effort easier the way it does for nearly everyone else.

Two broad approaches exist for holding that drift in place, and they map directly onto the distinction this chapter has been drawing between the two kinds of blindness. For the many totally blind people who have lost image-forming vision to something like retinitis pigmentosa but have, without knowing it, kept a functioning population of ipRGCs, carefully timed light exposure can still work. The person reports seeing nothing. Consciously, functionally, that is true. But a light bright enough and delivered at the right hour can still land on ipRGCs that never stopped responding, and nudge the SCN back into phase with the outside day — the same logic clinicians use to treat jet lag or shift-work disruption in people who can see perfectly well, applied here to a population whose access to that light is entirely non-conscious. For the smaller group who have lost the eye altogether, or whose optic nerve damage took the ganglion cell layer with it, there is no residual receptor left for any amount of light to reach, and the entrainment has to arrive by a different channel. Orally administered melatonin, taken at a consistent time each day, can do this. So can a melatonin-receptor agonist called tasimelteon, developed specifically for this population and approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2014. Both work by giving the SCN a chemical timing signal that substitutes for the light signal it no longer receives — a proxy for the message an intact eye would otherwise deliver on its own, every day, without anyone involved having to think about it. Taken consistently enough, at the same clock hour, that proxy can catch the drifting rhythm and hold it against the outside day, and clinical follow-up on patients using it has shown measurable improvement in nighttime sleep and daytime alertness. The improvement holds only as well as the regimen is kept; miss enough doses, at the wrong hour often enough, and the entrainment it was providing slips, and the drift resumes.

There is something clarifying in how modest that intervention actually is. Melatonin at a fixed hour, or a drug built to mimic it, does not restore anything like vision. It does not give a totally blind person back a sense of light, or color, or shape. What it restores, when it works, is a single unambiguous cue, delivered on a reliable schedule, telling one small nucleus behind the eyes roughly what time it is in the world outside the skull. That, and apparently only that, is what a functioning circadian system actually needs from its environment in order to stay lined up with the day everyone else is living in. Not sight. A signal.

Neither approach is a cure in the sense that word usually implies, and both share the same underlying limitation as the clock they are trying to correct: they only work if someone keeps administering them, at the same hour, day after day, indefinitely. Stop the light therapy, or stop the melatonin, and the drift a person has been holding in place resumes exactly as before, because nothing about the underlying eye has changed. What has been engineered, in both cases, is a workaround for a missing wire, not a repair of it. That is a modest thing to have built, and also, for the roughly six in ten totally blind people it can help stabilize, not a modest thing at all.

Which is, in the end, the real subject of this chapter, and the reason the puzzle Clyde Keeler stumbled into with his rodless mice, and researchers rediscovered decades later in blind mice and blind volunteers, turned out to matter well beyond ophthalmology. Every account of the body's clock this book has offered so far — the genes ticking in individual cells, the master pacemaker behind the eyes, the fact that people vary in when their own clock prefers to run — has left open the question of how any of that machinery knows, at all, what time it actually is outside. The ipRGCs are the answer. They are the eye's third photoreceptor, and unlike the other two, their job has nothing to do with seeing. They do not care about edges, faces, color, or motion. They register brightness, average it over time, and hand the result to the SCN, which uses it to keep the rest of the body's clocks in step with a sun most of those clocks will never directly perceive. Rods and cones let a person read this page. The ipRGCs let the body know, without anyone reading anything, what part of the day it currently is.

Total blindness, when it takes that channel along with everything else, is the cleanest natural demonstration available of what happens when the signal stops arriving altogether: not a broken clock, but an accurate one running in permanent isolation from the world it is supposed to be tracking, drifting through its own private day at its own private pace. Most of what the rest of this book describes is a milder version of the same disconnect — a clock that is working correctly, receiving a signal, and still being asked to run against a civil schedule it was never built to keep. Sighted people experience a faint echo of the same thing every time they cross several time zones in a day or rotate onto a night shift, which is a large part of why this chapter's detour through blindness turns out to matter for a book that is not, in the end, mostly about blindness.

What it is mostly about is what a clock does once it has been set, and here the necessary next question presents itself. The ipRGCs and the SCN exist to keep one particular process running on schedule above nearly everything else the body does, a process that consumes roughly a third of a human life and that most people, most of the time, describe with a single flat word, as though it were one uniform state rather than an engineered sequence with its own architecture. In sleep clinics that treat Non-24 today, physicians still plot a patient's drifting nights the same simple way clinicians were plotting them decades before anyone had heard of melanopsin: a grid of days running down the page, a mark for sleep and a mark for waking on each one, until thirty or sixty rows later a diagonal becomes visible, sleep sliding a little later across the page night after night, a clock keeping faithful time with itself regardless of what the sun outside the window happens to be doing. It is worth asking, now that it is clear how that clock gets set in everyone whose eyes still let it, what it is actually setting out to accomplish once the lights go off.
