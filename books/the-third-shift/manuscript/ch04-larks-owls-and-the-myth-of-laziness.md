# Larks, Owls, and the Myth of Laziness

She is good at her job, and everyone who works with her knows it, and almost nobody who works with her has seen it happen. The 8 a.m. status meeting finds her three coffees in, answering in short sentences, missing the joke that lands with everyone else in the room. Her manager has learned to route anything that actually matters to the afternoon. By four o'clock she is a different employee — faster, funnier, able to hold six moving pieces of a problem in her head at once — and by nine at night, alone at her kitchen table with a laptop nobody is monitoring, she is doing what is quietly the best work of her week. No one sees that part. The organization has already gone home. What the organization sees is a woman who cannot seem to get it together before lunch, and the word that tends to attach itself to that observation, in performance reviews and in the private judgments of coworkers, is some version of undisciplined.

Sitting two desks away is a colleague who has the opposite problem in reverse, though nobody frames it as a problem for him. He is in the office before anyone else, answers email at 6:40 a.m. with a clarity that makes him look like the most conscientious person on the floor, and is visibly fading by mid-afternoon, nursing the same cup of coffee for two hours because a second one this late would keep him up. By eight in the evening he is asleep on the couch in front of a television he is not watching. Nobody calls this laziness, because it does not look like laziness — it looks, if anything, like virtue front-loaded into the part of the day everyone agrees matters most. The two of them are doing something closer to the same thing, at opposite ends of the same twenty-four hours, and only one of them is getting credit for it.

This is an old story, and it usually gets told as a character flaw. Some people are morning people; some people are night people; the culture has had folk names for both — lark and owl — for about as long as it has had clocks worth setting. The judgment embedded in those names has never been evenly distributed. The lark gets the aphorisms. Benjamin Franklin's line about early to bed and early to rise has outlived nearly everything else he ever wrote, precisely because it flatters a listener into believing that virtue and a five a.m. wakeup are the same purchase. The owl gets no equivalent proverb, only the accusation that trails a late riser through every stage of institutional life, from the high school attendance office to the annual review: that the person could get up earlier, and simply chooses not to. The moral weighting is not evenly old, either. Factory bells, school bells, and the whole apparatus of industrial timekeeping were built around the assumption that a disciplined worker rises with or before the sun, and a culture organized that way had every incentive to treat early rising as a sign of character rather than as one biological setting among several. The night owl, by contrast, inherited centuries of suspicion that had nothing to do with sleep timing as such — late hours were where drinking happened, where gambling happened, where the respectable day's business had already been concluded — and some of that suspicion has simply outlived the reasons for it, attaching itself instead to anyone whose clock runs late regardless of what they are doing with the hours.

What almost nobody asked, for most of the history of that accusation, was whether it was true — whether the difference between the woman finishing her best work at nine p.m. and the colleague who is sharpest at seven a.m. is a matter of willpower at all, or something closer to a fact about her physiology that happens to be very inconvenient for a calendar built around office hours. Answering that question required, first, a way to measure the thing being argued about, because there had never been one. People could report whether they thought of themselves as a morning type or an evening type, and researchers built questionnaires around exactly that kind of self-image for years. But self-image is a slippery instrument. A person who has spent a decade forcing herself out of bed at six can start to describe herself as a morning person purely out of habit, having lost track of what her body would do on its own.

The person who did the most to fix that problem was Till Roenneberg, a professor of chronobiology at the Institute of Medical Psychology at LMU Munich, working with his colleague Martha Merrow. Together they built something called the Munich ChronoType Questionnaire, now usually shortened to the MCTQ, and the design decision at its center is almost embarrassingly simple once it is stated out loud. Instead of asking people what kind of person they believe themselves to be, it asks them what time they actually fall asleep and what time they actually wake up — and it asks the question twice, once for the days they have to be somewhere and once for the days they do not.

That second category, the days with no alarm and no obligation, is where the real information lives. On a workday, sleep timing is a compromise between what the body wants and what the job demands, and it tells you mostly about the job. On a free day — a Saturday with nothing scheduled, a vacation day, any stretch of time a person is allowed to sleep until they simply stop being asleep — the compromise falls away. What is left, roughly, is the schedule the body would choose if nobody were watching. It is not a perfect measurement; a few days off are not the same as the total isolation of a bunker with no clock and no daylight, of the kind an earlier chapter described happening outside the Bavarian village of Andechs, where volunteers spent weeks with no way to know what time it was and their sleep and waking still settled into a rhythm of their own. But it captures something close to the same signal using nothing more elaborate than a two-page questionnaire, which is part of why it has been filled out, at this point, by more than twenty-five thousand people.

There is one wrinkle the questionnaire has to correct for, and it is worth understanding because it is the difference between a crude measurement and a careful one. Most people carrying a week's worth of insufficient sleep will use a free day partly to catch up, sleeping later than their body would otherwise choose simply to repay a debt accumulated on workdays. Left uncorrected, that catch-up sleep would make a chronically sleep-deprived early type look artificially late, since the questionnaire would be picking up recovery sleep rather than a true preference. Roenneberg and Merrow built a correction into the MCTQ for exactly this reason, adjusting the midpoint of sleep on free days by the amount of debt a person is likely to be carrying from the workweek before treating the result as a stand-in for underlying chronotype. The adjustment is a small piece of statistical housekeeping, but it is the kind of housekeeping that separates a questionnaire built to measure something real from one that merely sounds scientific.

The quantity the MCTQ is really after has a slightly forbidding name — the phase angle of entrainment — but the idea underneath it is intuitive once you sit with it. Nobody's internal clock runs at exactly twenty-four hours. Chapter Three described the molecular gears that keep it running at something close to that length inside nearly every cell in the body, but close is not identical, and the small departure from a perfect twenty-four-hour period differs from one person to the next. Left alone, a clock like that would drift — falling further out of step with the sun by some number of minutes every day, the way a cheap watch gains or loses time. It does not drift, in practice, because it gets reset, mainly by light, on a daily basis; the technical term for that resetting is entrainment, and the phase angle is essentially where in the reset a given person's clock lands. Some people's internal rhythm sits close enough to the external day that very little correction is needed, and they wake early and alert with only a gentle nudge from the morning sun. Others carry a clock that wants to run later, and holding it in sync with a job that starts at eight requires forcing it backward against its own tendency, day after day, indefinitely.

Laid out across the more than twenty-five thousand people who have taken the MCTQ, that variation does not sort itself into two tidy bins of lark and owl. It forms something closer to a bell curve, a continuous spread with a large cluster somewhere in the middle and long tails reaching toward both extremes — a scattering of people whose bodies want to be asleep by nine at night and up before dawn without effort, and, at the other end, people whose midpoint of sleep on a free day does not arrive until well past what most calendars would call morning. Chronotype, in other words, is not a personality quiz with two possible outcomes. It is a measured trait with a distribution, more like height than like a category on a form, and the woman falling asleep for real at nine p.m. after a workday's worth of morning meetings she could barely follow sits somewhere out along that same curve as everyone else, at a point she did not choose and cannot simply relocate through effort.

What makes that distribution more than an academic curiosity is where the standard workday sits relative to it. A schedule that begins at eight or nine in the morning is not centered on the population's actual midpoint; it sits closer to the early half of the curve, a legacy of factory shifts and school bells set generations ago around whichever chronotype happened to hold institutional power at the time. Someone sitting at the true center of the distribution is already mildly inconvenienced by a schedule like that. Someone out toward the late tail is not inconvenienced so much as structurally at odds with it, in the same way a left-handed child was once structurally at odds with a classroom built entirely around right-handed desks — not broken, just measured against a design that never had them in mind.

Once chronotype could be measured this way rather than merely reported, a second and more consequential measurement became possible, because it is one thing to know where a person's biological clock sits and another to know how far the world has dragged them from it. In a 2006 paper, Roenneberg and his colleagues gave that gap a name: social jetlag. The definition is almost clinically plain — the discrepancy between a person's social schedule, meaning the days governed by work or school, and their biological schedule, indicated by what they do on free days — but the comparison the term invites is not plain at all. It is jetlag, the same disorientation and fatigue produced by flying across several time zones, except that nobody has gone anywhere. The body is not adjusting to Denver after a flight from New York. It is adjusting, every single week, to the workweek itself, and then adjusting back on the weekend, and then adjusting forward again on Monday, in a cycle that repeats for as long as the job does.

Picture it as travel, because that is functionally what it is. A person whose internal clock wants to fall asleep at one in the morning and wake at nine, but who has to be at a desk by eight-thirty five days a week, is not merely tired. She is living, Monday through Friday, several time zones east of where her body actually resides, forcing herself into alignment with a sun that has not yet risen as far as her circadian system is concerned, and then on Saturday she flies home — sleeping in, drifting back toward her own zone — only to fly back out again on Sunday night in preparation for Monday. Ordinary jetlag earns sympathy precisely because everyone recognizes it as a temporary cost of a trip, something the body will recover from once the traveler stops moving. Social jetlag gets none of that sympathy, largely because it looks, from the outside, like nothing more than an ordinary Tuesday. But the underlying physiology does not know the difference between a plane and a school bell. It only knows that the schedule it has been handed and the schedule it would set for itself do not agree, and that the disagreement is chronic rather than a one-time event a person can simply sleep off.

Something like this is already familiar to almost everyone under a different, less precise name. The particular heaviness of a Monday morning — worse, most people would say, than a Tuesday or a Wednesday even when the total hours of sleep are the same — is usually blamed on the abstract unpleasantness of the workweek beginning again. Social jetlag offers a more literal explanation. Monday is not simply the first day back; it is the day the body is dragged out of the time zone it drifted into over the weekend and back into the one the job requires, which is a description of jet lag arriving on schedule, every week, for anyone whose weekend sleep timing differs meaningfully from their weekday sleep timing. The feeling has a folk name already. What the MCTQ data supplied was a mechanism.

That chronic quality is what separates social jetlag from an isolated bad week. A traveler crossing time zones has an endpoint; the body adjusts and stays adjusted until the next trip. A person carrying social jetlag has no endpoint, because the two schedules in conflict are both permanent fixtures of their life — the job is not going away, and neither is the biology. Roenneberg's MCTQ data made visible just how many people are living inside that permanent conflict rather than at its edges, and it did so by giving researchers something they had never really had before: a large, cross-national dataset in which a person's true chronotype and the size of their weekly misalignment could both be read off the same simple set of bedtime and wake-time questions, rather than inferred from how tired someone said they felt or how virtuous they believed themselves to be.

What made social jetlag worth taking seriously as more than an evocative metaphor was the discovery that it correlates with things that have nothing to do with feeling sleepy. A 2023 study by Neda Ghotbi, Andrea Rabenstein, Luisa K. Pilz, Tobias Rüther, and Till Roenneberg looked at how chronotype and social jetlag related to health behavior across the MCTQ dataset, and one finding stood out for how concrete and checkable it was: people with a later chronotype, and people carrying a larger burden of social jetlag, showed a markedly higher prevalence of smoking. This is not, on its own, proof that circadian misalignment causes anyone to pick up a cigarette; a great many things travel together with chronotype, including work schedule, income, and the general stresses of a life built around irregular hours, and untangling which of those is doing the causal work is exactly the kind of problem chronobiology is still working through. But the association held up as one of the more consistent ones in the data, and it matters for a simple reason: it moves the conversation about social jetlag out of the realm of self-report and mood — how tired do you feel, how well do you say you slept — and into something a public health researcher can count. A cigarette either gets smoked or it does not. Its statistical association with the gap between a person's biological clock and their social one is a fact about the world, not a fact about how sympathetically that person describes their own exhaustion.

Researchers looking at that finding have wondered, without yet settling the matter, whether nicotine is doing some of the same work for a chronically misaligned late chronotype that a fourth cup of coffee does for the colleague at the next desk — a way of borrowing alertness during the hours the body would rather not be conscious for, paid back later in whatever currency a stimulant habit eventually demands. That is a hypothesis, not a finding the 2023 study itself proves, and it sits alongside more mundane possibilities: smoking correlates with plenty of other social and economic circumstances that also happen to correlate with late chronotype and heavier social jetlag, and any of those could be doing real work underneath the association. What the data rule out is the easier story — that late risers simply have less regard for their own health across the board, as a kind of general character trait. If that were the whole explanation, it would be strange for the effect to track so specifically with the size of the gap between a person's social schedule and their biological one, rather than with chronotype alone. The gap itself appears to be carrying some of the weight.

It is worth sitting with what that finding does to the laziness story. The stereotype has always assumed that the late riser is choosing something — staying up too late on purpose, sleeping in out of indulgence, failing to exercise the same discipline that gets the early riser to the gym before sunrise. What the MCTQ data describe instead is a population distributed along a biological trait that predates any decision the person in question ever made about their own habits, plus a civil schedule that was never built with that distribution in mind. Smoking prevalence does not track with willpower in any way anyone has ever demonstrated. It tracks, in this data, with chronotype and with the size of the gap between a person's clock and their calendar — which suggests that whatever is driving the association, it is closer to a downstream cost of chronic misalignment than to a character trait shared by people who happen to prefer the evening.

None of this means an evening person is powerless to change her habits; a late chronotype can still choose to smoke or not smoke, can still negotiate a later start time or protect an evening for exercise instead of a screen. The point is narrower and, for anyone who has spent years being told to simply try harder, more exonerating than a call to further effort would be. The starting position from which she is negotiating is not the same starting position as her early-rising colleague's, and pretending otherwise — treating identical demands as though they land on identical biology — is where the accusation of laziness quietly does its damage. It compares two people running on different schedules as though they were running on the same one, and then blames the one whose schedule the comparison was never built for.

The mechanism behind that variation, it is worth remembering, is not a separate mystery from the one the last chapter described. The transcription-translation feedback loop running inside a liver cell or a neuron in the suprachiasmatic nucleus does not tick at a fixed, universal rate; it ticks at a rate that differs, in small ways, from one person's cells to the next, and that difference compounds. A period that runs a few minutes longer than twenty-four hours, repeated across enough cycles, requires more correction to stay pinned to the sun than a period that runs a few minutes shorter — and more correction, absorbed day after day, is a plausible piece of what produces an owl rather than a lark. The same molecular clockwork that Chapter Three described as universal, present in nearly identical form in nearly every cell of nearly every person, turns out to run at slightly different speeds in different bodies, and that slight difference in speed is enough, compounded over a lifetime, to put one person's natural wake time three or four hours ahead of another's.

This is, in its way, the necessary complication to everything the last chapter established. It would be tidy to learn that human beings share one clock mechanism and conclude that the mechanism produces one human schedule, give or take a bit of noise. It does not. The same gears, assembled the same way in every cell of every body, run at slightly different speeds depending on whose cells they happen to be in, and a difference too small to notice on any single day becomes, added up across a childhood and an adolescence and an adult working life, the entire distance between the colleague who is fluent by seven in the morning and the one who does not really arrive, in any sense that matters, until well after the office has emptied out.

None of this is symmetrical in its consequences, because the built world is not neutral between larks and owls. The standard workday, the standard school day, the standard shift schedule for anyone not explicitly assigned to nights — all of it was set by institutions that had no way of knowing, and mostly still act as though they do not know, that a meaningful share of the population they are scheduling has a biological clock running later than the hour on the wall. A lark shows up for an eight a.m. shift more or less in sync with her own physiology. An owl shows up for the same shift already jetlagged, by the very definition Roenneberg's group gave the term, and stays that way for as long as the job requires it — not because she failed to try hard enough to be a morning person, but because the trait in question does not respond to trying in the way the culture around it assumes.

There is one more piece of the picture that keeps this from being a fixed sorting of the population into two permanent camps, and it matters enough that this book will return to it directly later on. Chronotype is not stamped onto a person for life the way eye color is. It moves, over the course of a lifetime, in a fairly well-established pattern: it tends to run earlier in childhood, drifts later through the teenage years, reaches its latest point somewhere around the late teens or early twenties, and then gradually swings earlier again through adulthood and into old age. The shift during adolescence is the steepest part of that curve, and it is not a phase teenagers are choosing any more than the woman in the opening pages of this chapter chose to do her best thinking at nine at night. A fifteen-year-old whose biological clock has drifted noticeably later than it ran at age ten is not becoming lazier. He is becoming, briefly, a more extreme version of exactly the kind of misalignment this chapter has been describing — and he is still being asked to sit for a first-period class at an hour his own institutions once reserved for people twice his age. What that collision looks like in practice, and what a number of school districts have eventually done about it, is a story for later in this book.

For now, the more immediate question is a narrower one, and it is the question this chapter has been circling without quite asking directly: if every person's clock runs at a slightly different pace, and if that pace produces the whole range from extreme lark to extreme owl, something still has to explain how any of those clocks stay pinned to a twenty-four-hour day at all rather than drifting off indefinitely into a private, ever-shifting rhythm of their own. Entrainment was the word used earlier for that resetting, but a word is not a mechanism. Something outside the body is doing the resetting, on a schedule precise enough to hold a population of slightly mismatched internal clocks approximately in step with the sun, year after year, without most of the people being reset ever noticing it happen. Whatever that something is, it does not announce itself. It does its work quietly, every morning, through an organ most people would not think to associate with timekeeping at all.
