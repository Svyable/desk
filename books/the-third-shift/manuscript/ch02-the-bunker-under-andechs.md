# The Bunker Under Andechs

The volunteer walked down a short flight of concrete stairs, through a steel door, and into two small rooms that would be the entire visible world for the next four weeks. There was a bed, a desk, a chair, a hot plate, a supply of food chosen and stocked in advance, and a bathroom. There was no window. There was no clock, no radio, no television, no telephone, and no way to receive a letter or a visitor except through a hatch that delivered meals and collected urine samples without any words attached to the exchange. Above ground, in the low hills west of Munich near the Bavarian village of Andechs, monks at a working monastery brewed beer and rang bells on a schedule the volunteer, one floor below, had no way of hearing. The point of the bunker was to remove every one of those cues on purpose, and then watch what the volunteer's body did without them.

That was the experiment. It sounds almost too simple to have needed a bunker, a research institute, and twenty-five years to run — lock someone away from every hint of what time it is, and see whether their body still behaves as though it knows. But in the mid-1960s that question was not settled, and the case for building an actual underground facility rather than just asking people to guess was stronger than it looks in hindsight.

People had tried versions of this before, above ground and with far less control. Researchers curious about sleep had occasionally taken volunteers into caves or basements for a few days at a stretch, hoping that darkness and quiet alone would be enough to reveal whatever rhythm lay underneath ordinary life. Those attempts were suggestive but never conclusive, because a few days is not long enough to distinguish a genuine internal period from the noise of a person still adjusting to strange surroundings, and because a cave, however dark, rarely blocks every incidental cue — a draft that changes with the weather above, a researcher's footsteps that happen to follow a routine, a stretch of tunnel warm enough in one direction to hint, without meaning to, at which way was toward the entrance. Andechs was conceived as a corrective to that vagueness: not a few days but weeks, not a borrowed space but a facility purpose-built to keep every possible cue out, and not a single case study but, eventually, a running program large enough to generate patterns rather than anecdotes.

By then it was well established that plants, insects, and other animals showed daily rhythms that persisted even in constant conditions — a fact biologists had documented since well before the Second World War, in bean plants that kept raising and lowering their leaves in a steady rhythm inside a darkened room, in fruit flies that kept eclosing from their pupal cases at roughly the same time of day for generations raised without any light-dark cue at all. Whether the same was true of a full-grown human being living an unstructured daily life was a different question, and a harder one to answer cleanly, because a person carries so many implicit clocks around with them that separating the intrinsic one from all the external ones is not straightforward. A subject who knows, or even half-suspects, what time it probably is — because a shift change happens at a certain hour, because meals arrive on a schedule, because a research assistant's voice sounds tired in a particular way late in the day — has not really been isolated from time cues at all. Proving that the human body runs its own clock meant closing off every channel by which a person might infer the hour, and then trusting the data enough to believe what it showed.

Jürgen Aschoff, who ran the Max Planck Institute for Behavioral Physiology in the town of Erling-Andechs, had already spent years studying free-running rhythms in birds and other animals, using isolation chambers that removed the normal cycle of light and dark. Starting in 1966, he and a younger colleague at the institute, Rutger Wever, extended that work to people, building a proper underground bunker specifically for the purpose — thick enough to block outside light and sound, isolated enough that a volunteer could not overhear the monastery bells or catch a glimpse of daylight through a gap in a door, self-contained enough that a person could live there for weeks without ever needing to leave. Wever, an engineer as much as a biologist, was the one who worked out much of the instrumentation: the sensors that recorded when the volunteer was moving around the rooms and when they were still, the rectal thermometer that logged body temperature continuously, the arrangements for collecting urine so that hormone levels could be measured later. Aschoff supplied the underlying question and the institutional weight to keep the unusual facility running for more than two decades.

The design asked something specific of the volunteers, and it is worth pausing on what that actually meant day to day. They were not told to sleep at any particular time, or to eat at any particular time, or to follow any imposed schedule at all. They were simply told: live as you like, sleep when you're tired, eat when you're hungry, work or read or do nothing as you please, for the length of the study. In that sense the bunker was less a prison than an oddly appointed hotel room with the clocks removed — comfortable enough, stocked with what a person needed, and free of any instruction except the absence of information. Some volunteers brought books. Some brought hobbies. A person who wanted to write letters could write them, though they would not be sent until the study ended, and a light switch let them turn the lights on and off as they saw fit, since even that had to be left to the volunteer rather than imposed by researchers who could otherwise have accidentally handed back a lighting cue disguised as convenience. What could not be smuggled in was any reliable signal of the hour. No sunlight reached the rooms. No clock hung on a wall. No radio program, with its predictable morning show or evening news, gave away what time of day it probably was. Researchers who needed to enter to service equipment did so in a way that avoided giving verbal or behavioral hints about the outside world's clock. The volunteer's job, in effect, was to just live, and the bunker's job was to make sure that living carried no information about what o'clock it happened to be.

The volunteers themselves were not a uniform group, and their motives for spending weeks alone underground varied about as much as motives for anything do — students earning a stipend, people curious about their own habits, a handful drawn back for a second or third stay because they had found something in the experience worth repeating. What almost all of them reported afterward, when asked, was some version of surprise at how quickly the absence of clocks stopped feeling strange. The first day or two carried the discomfort of not knowing; a volunteer would catch themselves reaching for a watch that had been left at the surface, or wondering, with real uncertainty, whether it was worth trying to sleep yet. By the second week, most had simply stopped asking the question, going to bed when their body pressed the matter and getting up again when it stopped, the not-knowing having quietly become the normal condition of the day rather than a puzzle to keep solving.

What Aschoff and Wever were measuring, mostly, was deceptively ordinary: when the volunteer moved and when they lay still, which stood in for waking and sleeping; core body temperature, taken continuously through the rectal probe, which rises and falls across a healthy day in a rhythm largely independent of a person's willpower; the volume and timing of urine excretion, whose rate also varies rhythmically across the day in ways tied to underlying physiology rather than conscious choice; and, at intervals, a simple test of time perception, in which the volunteer was asked to estimate when a minute had passed without being able to count using any external reference. None of these measures required the volunteer to introspect about what time they thought it was — which was useful, because a person cut off from every clock is a notoriously unreliable judge of elapsed time, and Aschoff and Wever were not interested in what the volunteers believed. They wanted to know what the body did on its own.

Between 1964 and 1989 — earlier studies at the institute predating the bunker's own construction fed into the same broader program — the facility hosted 418 separate studies involving 447 volunteers in total, an unusually large body of controlled human isolation data for a single research site, and one that has never really been matched since, partly because isolating volunteers for weeks at a time is expensive, slow, and increasingly difficult to justify to an ethics board accustomed to shorter and less demanding protocols. Most of the volunteers who came through Andechs stayed for two to four weeks, long enough for the rhythms of interest to establish themselves clearly across many repeated cycles rather than showing up as a single ambiguous swing that might have been coincidence.

What they found was that the volunteers did not drift into chaos. Cut off from every cue of time, a healthy adult did not fall into a random or disorganized pattern of activity and rest. Instead, sleep and waking kept recurring in a cycle nearly as regular as an ordinary day — just not exactly an ordinary day. Across the pooled data, the free-running period, meaning the length of one full cycle of activity and rest with no outside timing cue to anchor it, worked out to something close to twenty-five hours rather than twenty-four. A volunteer who went to bed at what their body treated as midnight on the first night of the study would, over the following weeks, be going to bed a little later by the wall clock each night — roughly an hour later every day, day after day, with nothing in the environment nudging it in that direction — so that within two or three weeks their subjective midnight had drifted most of the way around an ordinary twenty-four-hour clock, had there been one to check it against. Body temperature showed a comparable rhythm, rising and falling on a cycle that, left alone, also ran a bit longer than twenty-four hours in most volunteers.

That finding alone would have been enough to answer the original question. The human body, it turned out, did not need daylight, mealtimes, clocks, or company to organize itself into a roughly day-length cycle of rest and activity; something inside kept time on its own, imperfectly matched to the twenty-four-hour rotation of the planet but clearly not just following it passively either. If the body were merely responding moment to moment to the accumulated fatigue of being awake, or to some simple physiological pressure that built up and discharged with no reference to time of day at all, there would be no particular reason for that cycle to land anywhere close to twenty-four hours, or to repeat with the regularity it did. A roughly day-length rhythm that persists without any day-length input pointed to something structured — an internal oscillator, not just an internal hourglass.

But the more striking and, in some ways, more useful discovery to come out of Andechs was what happened when two of the body's rhythms stopped agreeing with each other. In the majority of the bunker studies, the volunteer's rest-activity cycle and their core-temperature cycle drifted together, both settling into that same roughly twenty-five-hour period and staying loosely in step, the way two clocks running slightly fast will still tell similar times as long as neither is reset. But in a meaningful minority of cases, the two rhythms came apart. The temperature rhythm would hold close to its own period — often nearer twenty-five hours, sometimes closer to the low twenty-fours — while the sleep-wake rhythm adopted an entirely different one, occasionally stretching out to thirty-three or even nearly fifty hours per cycle, with the volunteer reporting what felt to them like a single ordinary day and night despite having, by the temperature record, been awake far longer than any one stretch should have lasted. Aschoff and Wever called this internal desynchronization, and it was arguably the more theoretically important of the two findings, because it implied that what had looked like one clock was actually more than one — separate oscillators, capable under the right conditions of running at different speeds and drifting apart from each other, rather than a single master rhythm dictating both temperature and behavior in lockstep. A person in that state did not necessarily feel unwell or disoriented in any dramatic way; they simply lived through days that, by an outside clock, had stretched or compressed, while insisting, when asked, that everything felt roughly normal. It is a strange thing to sit with — a body keeping two different kinds of time at once, and a mind unaware that it was happening.

It also raised a question the free-running data alone had not: if temperature and behavior could come apart, which one was closer to the "real" clock, and which was simply following along most of the time out of habit rather than necessity? The temperature rhythm, holding more stubbornly near its own period even while the sleep-wake cycle wandered off toward thirty-three or fifty hours, looked like the steadier of the two — evidence, though not yet proof, that somewhere beneath the more visible rhythm of getting up and lying down sat something more fundamental, less negotiable, less willing to be talked out of its own pace by boredom or restlessness or the simple appeal of staying up to finish a book. Behavior, it seemed, could drift. Whatever kept the temperature rhythm in place drifted more reluctantly. That distinction — between a clock a person can override for a night and one that keeps its own counsel regardless — would turn out to matter well beyond the bunker, in every later argument about whether a worker can simply decide to feel alert at four in the morning if the job requires it.

Aschoff described the whole research program himself in an address he gave in the early 1980s, later published in 1984 in the Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences under the title "Circadian Timing." By then the Andechs bunker had been running for the better part of two decades, and Aschoff's own account of it carries the tone of someone summarizing a body of work rather than announcing a single discovery — the internal desynchronization findings alongside the free-running period, the methodological refinements the team had made along the way, the questions the data still left open. He was careful, in that address and elsewhere, not to claim more precision than the bunker protocol could support. The roughly twenty-five-hour figure was an average drawn from imperfect conditions: volunteers who could turn lights on and off as they pleased, who could nap or stay up according to impulse, who were, in other words, still making behavioral choices that might themselves be shaping the very rhythm the experiment was trying to measure in its pure form. Aschoff and Wever both understood this limitation. It is a difficult thing to fully separate a person's spontaneous behavior — when they decide to turn out the light, when they decide they are tired enough to lie down — from the biological pacemaker sitting underneath that behavior and, in an ordinary environment, at least partly driving it. The bunker had shown, about as convincingly as an experiment could at the time, that the pacemaker existed. It had not fully isolated the pacemaker from the volunteer's own choices about what to do with the information, imperfect as that information was, that the pacemaker was quietly supplying.

That gap is where the story does not end so much as sharpen. Fifteen years after Aschoff's address, a team led by Charles Czeisler, working primarily out of Harvard, published a study in the journal Science in 1999 titled "Stability, Precision, and Near-24-Hour Period of the Human Circadian Pacemaker." Czeisler's group used a different method entirely, one built specifically to solve the problem Aschoff and Wever had run into: how to separate a person's voluntary sleep-wake behavior from the biological rhythm running beneath it. The technique is called forced desynchrony. Instead of letting subjects sleep and wake whenever they liked, as the Andechs protocol had, Czeisler's team put them on an imposed schedule deliberately set well outside the range a human circadian system could plausibly follow on its own — a "day" stretched to twenty-eight hours in the original version of the protocol, later work using other lengths, always chosen so that no internal oscillator with anything close to a normal period could entrain to it. Under carefully controlled, very dim lighting that was kept too low to reset the body's internal clock, subjects were instructed to attempt sleep and stay awake according to that artificial schedule, while researchers tracked the melatonin, core body temperature, and cortisol rhythms that continued, underneath the imposed routine, to run on whatever period the body's own pacemaker actually kept. Because the imposed day was so far from any period the internal clock could match, the two rhythms — the artificial behavioral one the subject was following and the true biological one their body was quietly maintaining regardless — necessarily pulled apart, and pulled apart in a way the researchers could track precisely, since they knew exactly what schedule they had imposed and could measure the hormonal and temperature rhythms drifting against it.

The result was a number markedly closer to twenty-four hours than the bunker-era estimate had suggested: an average intrinsic circadian period of 24.18 hours, with a margin of error of about two and a half minutes either way — 24.18 hours plus or minus 0.04. That is a striking figure on its own terms, both for how close it sits to the planet's actual rotation and for how little it appeared to vary from one person to the next once measured this precisely, a stability that surprised even researchers who had expected the clock to be fairly consistent.

The dim lighting in Czeisler's protocol deserves a word of its own, because it is easy to read past as a mere procedural footnote when it was in fact the hinge the whole method turned on. Ordinary room light, even light most people would describe as soft or low, is bright enough to reset a circadian pacemaker if it falls at the wrong point in the body's cycle — a fact that later chapters return to at length. If the light in a forced-desynchrony study were even modestly brighter than the threshold the protocol required, the subject's internal clock would simply follow the imposed twenty-eight-hour day instead of ignoring it, and the whole point of the experiment would collapse: there would be nothing left to measure except the schedule the researchers had imposed in the first place. Keeping the light dim enough, consistently enough, across weeks of a study, was as much the discovery as the number the study eventually produced. It is also, unmistakably, a different number from the "roughly twenty-five hours" that had circulated as the headline finding out of Andechs for over two decades by that point, repeated in textbooks and popular accounts as though it were simply the answer.

It would be easy, and wrong, to read that difference as evidence that Aschoff and Wever had gotten it wrong the first time, or worse, that the bunker work had been sloppy. It had not been. Given the tools and the ethical constraints available in the 1960s and 1970s — asking volunteers to spend weeks in an underground facility with no daylight was already about as demanding a protocol as any review board of the era was likely to approve, and stretching that isolation out for months at a time in service of an even more tightly controlled schedule was simply not on the table — the bunker studies represented about as clean an experiment as could have been run. What Czeisler's team had that Aschoff and Wever did not was a way of pulling behavior and biology apart that did not depend on isolation alone, and light-monitoring and hormonal-assay technology precise enough to track melatonin and cortisol rhythms in the kind of fine-grained detail forced desynchrony required. The gap between roughly twenty-five hours and 24.18 hours turned out to trace largely to that same problem Aschoff himself had already flagged as a limitation in his 1984 address: in the bunker, a volunteer's own choices about when to turn off the light and lie down were subtly pulling their measured period longer than the underlying pacemaker's true period actually ran, because staying awake a little later and sleeping a little later is, after all, an easy and unremarkable thing for a bored, comfortable person with nothing else to do to drift into, cue or no cue. The 1999 study did not knock down the Andechs findings so much as complete the sentence they had left open. The clock was real. It was just running a little closer to true than the first, necessarily rougher measurement of it had been able to show.

That correction is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is a more honest picture of how this kind of science actually proceeds than the version that tends to survive in summary. Aschoff and Wever proved something that had not been proven before — that an isolated human body organizes itself into a rhythm close to but distinct from twenty-four hours, entirely on its own, without any cue from the outside world telling it to. That was the load-bearing discovery, and it held up. The exact number attached to it moved, over three and a half decades and one considerably more sophisticated protocol, from an estimate of roughly twenty-five hours down to a tightly bounded 24.18. Both figures came from real data, gathered by careful researchers doing the best work the instruments of their era allowed. Neither was a guess. The later number is simply a sharper photograph of the same object, not a different object.

What both experiments agreed on, and what neither ever seriously doubted, is the part that matters for everything that follows in this book: left alone, with no sun, no clock, and no one to tell them otherwise, the human body keeps something close to a day's rhythm anyway. It does this without being taught, without conscious effort, and — as the internal desynchronization findings showed — sometimes even without full agreement between the parts of the body doing the keeping. Sleep and waking, body temperature, hormone release, the rate at which the kidneys process fluid: all of it moves to a beat that a person carries into a windowless room and does not leave behind. Whatever that beat is made of, it was clearly not being received from outside. It had to be coming from somewhere inside the volunteer, running quietly under the skin whether anyone above ground in Andechs happened to be paying attention or not, and by the late 1960s an entire research institute had built itself a bunker specifically to prove exactly that.

Neither Aschoff's data nor Czeisler's told anyone, yet, what that internal mechanism actually was. A rhythm reliably close to twenty-four hours, persisting for weeks in total isolation, distinct enough from ordinary behavior to survive an artificial twenty-eight-hour day without budging — that is the signature of a clock, but a signature is not the same as the mechanism itself. Somewhere in the volunteer's body, something had to be doing the actual counting. The bunker under Andechs, and the sleep laboratory that later refined its number, had answered the question of whether. The next question was where, and out of what.
