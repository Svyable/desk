# A World That Never Gets Dark

There is a photograph that most people have seen without ever quite looking at it: Earth from orbit, at night, continents traced not in coastline but in light. The eastern half of the United States resolves into a single dense mesh from Boston to Washington, with fainter threads running west along the interstates. Europe is a bright, almost continuous shelf from Lisbon to Warsaw. The Nile is visible as a green-lit line where a river is not supposed to be visible at all, because from three hundred miles up a river carries no light of its own; it is the cities strung along its banks, mile after mile, that make the water's path legible from space. Satellites carrying instruments built to record exactly this — the day-night band sensor flown on the American Suomi NPP satellite is the one most of the familiar composites come from — have been assembling versions of this image for years now, and successive editions of it tend to show the same thing: the lit area grows, and in most places it grows brighter, not dimmer, decade over decade.

Looked at from that altitude, the image reads as a kind of civic achievement, and in a sense it is one — light is one of the oldest and most literal proxies for human presence, for electrification, for a settlement's ability to keep functioning after the sun goes down. But the interesting part of this story does not happen three hundred miles up. It happens roughly two centimeters behind a human cornea, at the back of an eye that was built, across several hundred million years of vertebrate evolution, to expect near-total darkness for something close to half of every day. The photograph from orbit shows where the light is. It says nothing about what a landscape lit like that does, night after night, to the small population of cells behind the eyes of everyone living inside it.

That question is not photography. It is photometry, and it belongs to a different kind of fieldwork entirely — the kind done not from orbit but from a sidewalk, with a handheld instrument the size of a garage-door opener, pointed straight up. Researchers who study what is generally called artificial light at night, ALAN in the shorthand the field has settled on, have spent the past two decades doing exactly this in cities around the world: standing on a corner after dark with a sky-quality meter, logging how bright the sky itself has become as a byproduct of everything shining upward from streetlamps, storefronts, stadiums, and windows below it. The physicist Christopher Kyba has been one of the more visible figures doing this kind of work, and in 2019 a press account summarizing research associated with him laid out a finding that reframes the entire subject, because it is not really about how bright a city looks from a rooftop or a satellite. It is about how little light a human circadian system actually needs before it notices.

The number that account reported was startling mainly for how small it was. Melatonin production — the nightly signal, discussed earlier in this book, by which the pineal gland tells the rest of the body that it is now night — can be measurably suppressed in sensitive individuals by ordinary, broad-spectrum light at illuminance levels as low as roughly six lux. Six lux is not a number most people carry around, so it helps to put it beside a few others. Direct sunlight at midday runs to something on the order of a hundred thousand lux. An ordinary well-lit office sits around five hundred. A living room with a couple of lamps on might be somewhere in the range of fifty to a hundred. Full moonlight, by contrast, is a fraction of a single lux — dim enough that reading by it without effort is close to impossible. Six lux sits well below a lit living room and well above moonlight: roughly the brightness of a hallway night-light, or the wash of an unshaded streetlamp coming through a bedroom window from the far side of the street, or a partially lit room glimpsed through a doorway left ajar. It is not darkness. It is also nowhere close to what anyone picturing "the lights are on" would imagine. The body's own definition of "on" turns out to sit much lower than the wattage most people associate with a room actually being lit.

Wavelength complicates the picture further, and here the story folds back into ground this book has already covered. Not all light is equally good at telling the circadian system what time it is. The same 2019 account reported that light in the blue part of the visible spectrum — roughly the 460-to-480-nanometer range — is considerably more potent at suppressing melatonin than light of other colors at the same overall brightness, and that in one study cited in that reporting, the strongest suppressive effect of all was found at a wavelength around 424 nanometers, deep in the blue. Even two hours of evening exposure to blue light at that wavelength was enough, in that study, to measurably suppress melatonin. This is not an incidental detail. It is close to the whole mechanism, restated from the other end of a channel this book has already spent a full chapter tracing.

That channel is the one built from melanopsin and the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells — the ipRGCs — described several chapters back, the third class of photoreceptor discovered only within the last few decades, distinct from the rods and cones that produce ordinary sight and wired instead almost directly into the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain's master clock. That earlier chapter told the story of what happens when that channel goes dark: total blindness of a certain kind, drift, a circadian rhythm cut loose from the day it is supposed to be tracking. This chapter is the same mechanism run in reverse. Melanopsin's own sensitivity peaks in the blue part of the spectrum, which is exactly why blue-enriched light is so unusually effective at doing what daylight has always done to the ipRGCs: telling the SCN, with more conviction than dimmer or warmer light can manage, that it is daytime. In a world lit mostly by candles, oil lamps, and low-wattage incandescent bulbs — light sources that are comparatively dim and skew toward the warmer, redder end of the spectrum — this distinction barely mattered, because almost nothing available after dark was bright enough or blue enough to move the needle much. The light-emitting diode changed that arithmetic. Modern white LED lighting, prized by cities and manufacturers alike for its efficiency and its low cost per lumen, tends to produce a spectrum with a pronounced blue peak, precisely the part of the spectrum the ipRGCs are most sensitive to. Streetlights retrofitted from sodium-vapor bulbs, which glowed a warm orange and did comparatively little to the circadian system, to white LEDs, which are whiter, cheaper to run, and considerably richer in exactly the wavelengths melanopsin responds to, did not simply make a city look different at night. They changed, in a small but real way, what that city's light was doing to the people sleeping inside it.

It is worth pausing on the strangeness of this, the same way the earlier chapter on the eye's third photoreceptor asked readers to pause on the strangeness of a retinal ganglion cell turning out to sense light on its own rather than merely relaying it. The mechanism connecting artificial light to melatonin suppression is not speculative or contested in the way some of the population-level claims later in this chapter will be. It is about as settled as circadian science gets: light of sufficient intensity and the right wavelength, striking the ipRGCs, sends a signal to the SCN that overrides the pineal gland's nightly instruction to produce melatonin, and it does this at illuminance levels far below what casual intuition would predict and with a wavelength sensitivity that maps directly onto a physiological pathway this book has already described in detail. What varies, and what the rest of this chapter has to handle far more carefully, is what that suppression adds up to across a lifetime, across a population, and across the built environment as a whole.

Start with what chronic suppression looks like at the level of an individual body, because even here the honest answer involves several separate and only partly related effects rather than one clean story. A 2015 review published in the Royal Society journal Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, focused on artificial light at night and melatonin's role as a kind of mediator between the environment and the genome, laid out the general shape of the mechanism this way: nighttime light exposure disturbs the normal secretion of melatonin, and that disturbance has been linked, across a range of separate studies the review draws together, to several distinct downstream effects. Sleep disorders are the most direct of these, following almost mechanically from a melatonin signal that arrives late, arrives weakly, or barely arrives at all. Metabolic dysregulation is a second and less obvious thread — the review describes links between disrupted melatonin rhythms and insulin resistance, along with altered patterns of cortisol release that in turn affect appetite regulation, changes that researchers in this area connect to elevated risk of metabolic syndrome more broadly. A third thread runs toward the immune system, where studies cited in the review associate disrupted nighttime melatonin with measurable immune dysfunction. None of these three threads is presented in that review as a single simple causal chain running cleanly from a lit bedroom to a diagnosis. They are presented as converging lines of evidence, gathered from different research groups working with different methods, all pointing toward the same underlying disturbance: a hormone that is supposed to rise predictably each evening and fall each morning, doing neither reliably, in a body that is being asked, night after night, to sleep inside an environment its endocrine system does not fully register as dark.

The fourth thread the same review describes deserves to be handled with more deliberate care than the other three, because it sits at a different level of evidence entirely, and this book has already tried, in its account of shift work and cancer risk, to hold that distinction firmly rather than let a genuine mechanism and a population-level correlation blur into a single, tidier-sounding claim than the evidence actually supports. The 2015 review notes that at a population level, epidemiological studies have found that increases in artificial light at night co-distribute geographically with breast cancer incidence — that is, researchers mapping how much outdoor light a given region emits after dark, often using the same kind of satellite imagery described at the start of this chapter, have found that regions with more nighttime light tend, in various studies, to also show higher rates of breast cancer. That is a real finding, and it is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as noise. It is also, on its own, a claim about where two things happen to be more common in the same places, not a claim about what causes what in any individual body. Regions with more artificial light at night differ from darker regions in dozens of other ways that plausibly bear on cancer risk — population density, urbanization, air quality, diet, reproductive patterns, access to screening and diagnosis, wealth — and a co-distribution across a map cannot, by itself, separate the contribution of light exposure from the contribution of everything else riding along with it. Researchers in this field have proposed a specific candidate mechanism worth taking seriously: melatonin has been studied for antioxidant and tumor-suppressive properties in laboratory and animal work, and suppressing it chronically, the reasoning goes, could plausibly remove some part of that protection in ways that matter over years or decades. A plausible mechanism and a geographic correlation are both real pieces of evidence. Neither one, nor the two together, currently amounts to proof that artificial light at night causes cancer in any individual person exposed to it, and the responsible way to describe the finding is exactly the way this book has tried to describe every comparably uncertain claim before it: as something under active, serious investigation, worth acting on with reasonable caution, and not yet something anyone can honestly call settled.

That distinction — a mechanism this book can state almost as confidently as a fact, sitting beside a set of downstream health associations that range from fairly well supported to genuinely uncertain — is not a flaw in the science. It is simply what the science currently is, and pretending otherwise in either direction would be a disservice to the reader in different ways. Understating the melatonin-suppression mechanism would ignore some of the most carefully replicated findings in circadian biology. Overstating the cancer association would borrow the same kind of confidence this book has already criticized elsewhere, in an account of a popular sleep book that pushed further past its evidence than the underlying research actually supported. The honest version sits in between: a light-sensing channel, built from cells this book has already met, doing exactly what it evolved to do — reporting brightness to a clock that assumes brightness means daytime — inside an environment that has, within the span of a single human lifetime, made that assumption wrong more often than right for a large share of the people living in it.

Widen the frame further, past the individual body and the epidemiological map, and the same basic story turns out not to be a human story at all — humans just happen to be the species writing the book about it. The ipRGC-melanopsin pathway, or something functionally close to it, exists across a wide range of vertebrates, and other organisms have their own light-sensing systems tuned, across evolutionary time scales far longer than the century or so artificial lighting has existed, to a night sky that used to reliably go dark. Sea turtle hatchlings emerging from beach nests at night have historically oriented toward the brightest visible horizon, which for millions of years meant the open sky reflecting off the ocean; on beaches lined with hotels, streetlights, and parking-lot lighting, that same orientation reflex now reliably sends a share of hatchlings inland instead, away from the water, into roads and gardens and the paths of predators, a well-documented behavioral effect that conservation programs along developed coastlines now spend real money and real staff hours trying to counteract, mostly by shielding or dimming beachfront lighting during nesting season. Migratory birds, many species of which travel at night and appear to use celestial cues for navigation, are drawn off course by brightly lit buildings and towers; ornithologists have documented mass collision events at illuminated skyscrapers and communication towers during migration season for decades, and the pattern is consistent enough that a number of cities now run voluntary or mandatory programs asking building owners to dim exterior lighting during peak migration weeks. Insects circling a porch light on a summer evening are performing a version of the same disoriented navigation on a smaller and more familiar scale, and a growing body of research, still actively contested on the size and mechanism of the effect, has raised the question of whether the sheer proliferation of outdoor lighting is a meaningful contributor to the broader declines in insect populations that entomologists have documented across parts of the industrialized world — a question that, like the cancer association above it, remains a live and serious research problem rather than an established conclusion. None of these organisms evolved a melanopsin system, or its equivalent, in anticipation of a night sky with a floor under how dark it could get. Very little living tissue did.

Set beside that ecological picture, the human case looks less like an isolated medical curiosity and more like one instance of a general pattern: a planet's worth of organisms whose light-sensing machinery was built against an assumption — reliable, total, nightly darkness — that a fairly narrow slice of one species has spent roughly a century and a half quietly revoking, first with gas lighting, then incandescent bulbs, then sodium-vapor streetlamps, and now, in a final turn that happens to be more circadian-active than nearly everything that came before it, blue-rich LEDs. Cities did not set out to run a large uncontrolled experiment on nocturnal physiology. They set out to be safer to walk through, easier to do business in after dark, and more pleasant to look at from a hotel window, and the light pouring upward into the sky and sideways through ten thousand bedroom windows was, for most of that history, treated as a side effect too diffuse and too slow-acting for anyone to feel responsible for managing.

That is beginning, unevenly, to change, in the same halting, after-the-fact way this book has already traced in hospitals, cockpits, and school boards. Some cities have begun specifying warmer-spectrum, lower-blue-content LEDs for street lighting, motivated as much by concerns about ecological impact and human sleep as by the aesthetics of a warmer glow. A number of jurisdictions have adopted so-called dark-sky ordinances, restricting upward-facing light, mandating shielded fixtures that direct illumination downward at the ground where it is needed rather than outward into the sky where it is not, and setting curfews on decorative or advertising lighting after a certain hour. Some hospitals, aware of exactly the mechanism this chapter has described, have begun redesigning nighttime lighting on inpatient wards to minimize blue content during overnight hours, a small, specific echo of the same insight already discussed in this book's account of how light itself can function as a kind of drug, dosed by color and timing as much as by simple brightness. None of these interventions has undone what a century of expanding, brightening, increasingly blue-shifted artificial light has already done to the skies over most of the world's cities. Nearly all of them are recent, local, and adopted by a minority of the places that could plausibly use them. But they share a common premise with every other reform this book has documented: that once the mechanism is well enough understood, and the cost of ignoring it well enough documented, an institution eventually has to decide whether it is willing to keep treating a known effect as an acceptable side effect indefinitely.

There is a way of narrating this chapter that would end here, with dark-sky ordinances and warmer streetlights as a hopeful coda — evidence accumulating, policy slowly catching up, a story that bends, eventually, toward correction. That would not be dishonest, exactly. It would also be incomplete, because it treats the built environment as though its main task, going forward, were simply to get darker again, and that is not actually the direction most of the built environment is heading. The chapters before this one in this book have been mostly about specific institutions — a nuclear plant, a chemical factory, a hospital residency program, an airline, a school district — each keeping its own particular, bounded version of the third shift, staffed by people asked to be awake and competent at hours their bodies would rather not be. Those institutions have edges. A hospital has a night shift and a day shift, however imperfectly staffed and lit. A factory eventually closes a line for maintenance. Even a twenty-four-hour trading floor, in the era before this book's final subject, still answered to a market that opened and closed at scheduled hours somewhere in the world, with a recognizable gap, however brief, when almost nothing was trading anywhere.

What is emerging now, and what the final chapter of this book takes up directly, is something the built environment described in this chapter turns out to have been quietly rehearsing for decades: institutions with no edges at all. A cryptocurrency market does not close for the night, or the weekend, or a holiday, anywhere on Earth; there is no hour when trading simply stops the way it stops on a stock exchange floor. Software systems monitoring infrastructure, processing payments, and running customer-facing services increasingly run continuously by design, with human beings on call to intervene at whatever hour something breaks, because the system itself recognizes no distinction between three in the afternoon and three in the morning. A distributed team spanning a dozen time zones can, in principle, hand off work around the clock with no natural pause anywhere in the cycle, the way a lit city never actually goes dark from any single vantage point even as individual windows switch off one by one. The light pollution traced across this chapter — a signal that used to arrive only from the sun, now arriving from streetlamps and screens and skyglow at every hour a person might look up — turns out to have been a preview of something larger: a civilization built, gradually and mostly without deciding to, on the premise that neither the sky above it nor the systems running beneath it are ever required to go dark. The next and final chapter asks what happens to a species carrying a several-hundred-million-year-old clock behind its eyes when the institutions demanding its presence are no longer just the hospitals, factories, and control rooms this book has already visited, but markets and machines that, for the first time in human history, genuinely never close at all.
