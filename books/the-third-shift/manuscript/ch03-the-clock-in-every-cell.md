# The Clock in Every Cell

In 1971, in a windowless room in Seymour Benzer's lab at Caltech, a graduate student named Ronald Konopka spent his days checking vials of fruit flies under red light. Drosophila cannot see red wavelengths, so a researcher can work under it without the flies knowing whether it is day or night — a small trick that let Konopka watch thousands of them without ever tipping off their internal sense of time. What he was watching for was arithmetic: the hour at which each vial's pupae broke open and released their flies into the world. Left alone in constant darkness, a healthy fly population still emerges in a narrow band around the hour that would have been dawn, day after day, with no sun to tell it so. That fact alone had puzzled biologists for decades, and one detail of it made the puzzle sharper still. The length of the rhythm barely changed whether the flies were kept at a cool sixty degrees Fahrenheit or a warm eighty. Ordinary chemical reactions do not behave that way — heat speeds them up, sometimes drastically, because warmer molecules collide more often and more forcefully. A biological clock built from ordinary chemistry should have run measurably faster in a warm room than in a cool one, the way a pendulum clock left near a radiator can drift out of true. It did not. Whatever was generating the rhythm seemed to compensate for temperature almost perfectly, holding close to twenty-four hours across a range in which nearly every other biochemical process in the fly sped up or slowed down. Nobody in 1971 could explain that stability. It simply sat there, an odd and stubborn fact, waiting for someone to find the part of the fly responsible for it. Konopka's question was blunter and stranger. If a fly's body could keep time without a clock on the wall, was there something in its genes doing the counting — and could you break it on purpose and watch what happened?

Benzer had already made a name for himself treating behavior as something you could take apart the way a mechanic takes apart an engine, gene by gene, using chemicals that scramble DNA at random and then breeding the damaged flies to see what changed. Most of the mutations he and his students created did nothing detectable, or killed the fly outright, or produced flies that behaved normally in every way anyone had thought to test. Konopka's version of the search meant feeding male flies a mutagen, breeding them, and then checking each new lineage's vials for a pupal-emergence pattern that drifted from the dawn-centered norm. It was slow, repetitive work — count the flies, note the hour, discard the vial, start the next one — with long stretches in which nothing distinguished itself from the background rhythm everyone already expected.

Then, within the same set of screens, three lines did distinguish themselves. One strain of flies kept emerging and moving on a cycle close to nineteen hours, noticeably short of a day. Another ran long, closer to twenty-eight hours. A third showed no discernible cycle at all — its flies emerged and moved at scattered, unpredictable hours, as if the mechanism that should have been keeping time had simply stopped keeping it. Konopka and Benzer mapped all three mutations to roughly the same small stretch of the fly's X chromosome. Different mistakes, in other words, in the same address. They called the gene period, and in 1971 they published the finding that a single gene, altered in different ways, could speed up a fly's internal clock, slow it down, or erase it.

It was a strange kind of proof. The experiment did not show what period actually did inside a cell, or how a stretch of DNA could produce something as abstract as timekeeping. It showed only that such a gene existed and that breaking it broke the clock in three distinct, reproducible ways — evidence of a mechanism you could locate before you understood it, the genetic equivalent of finding a light switch on a wall in a house whose wiring you have never seen. Some biologists at the time were openly skeptical that a rhythm as elaborate as a twenty-four-hour cycle, coordinating metabolism, movement, and development, could really trace back to one gene rather than some diffuse property of a whole nervous system. The skepticism was not unreasonable. It would take thirteen years, and laboratories on opposite sides of the American Northeast working independently, to find out what period actually was.

In 1984, Jeffrey Hall and Michael Rosbash, working together at Brandeis University in Boston, and Michael Young, working at Rockefeller University in New York, isolated and molecularly characterized the period gene — identified its physical location in the fly's DNA closely enough to read what it coded for. What it coded for was a protein, which came to be called PER. And when Hall, Rosbash, and Young tracked how much PER protein was present in fly cells at different hours, they found something that must have felt, in the moment, like watching a gene confess to a crime it had been suspected of for over a decade. The protein was not present in some constant, static quantity. It accumulated inside the cells through the night, reached a peak, and was then broken down and cleared during the day, only to begin accumulating again after dark. The behavioral clock Konopka had found by breaking flies in three different ways now had a molecular clock underneath it, rising and falling on the same schedule as the flies whose pupae Konopka had once counted under red light.

A rising and falling protein is not yet an explanation. It raises an obvious question: what makes PER rise, and what makes it fall, and why does the whole cycle keep repeating instead of running down like a wound clock left ticking toward zero. The answer that emerged from Hall and Rosbash's and Young's subsequent work, and from the identification of a second gene called timeless, turned out to be a loop rather than a line. The protein made by period does not act alone. It pairs with a protein called TIM, made by timeless, and the two together perform a peculiar act of self-sabotage: they travel back into the cell's nucleus and shut down the very gene that made them. While period and timeless are being read and translated into protein, PER and TIM accumulate. Once enough of the paired proteins have built up, they suppress their own genes' activity, and production stops. With no fresh protein being made, the existing supply of PER and TIM begins to degrade, as proteins in a cell constantly do. As the level of the paired proteins falls, the suppression they were exerting on their own genes falls with it, and eventually there is too little PER and TIM left to keep the genes silent. Transcription starts again. The whole sequence — production, accumulation, self-suppression, decay, release — takes about a day to complete, and then it starts over, inside the same cell, with no need for a signal from outside to restart it.

That is the essential trick of the thing: a chemical process with a built-in delay, wired so that its own output eventually switches it off, and its own decay eventually switches it back on. Nothing about the loop as Hall, Rosbash, and Young described it required light, temperature, or any cue from the environment to keep running. A single fly cell, isolated and left alone, could in principle keep counting toward twenty-four hours indefinitely, using nothing but the rise and fall of two proteins acting on the genes that made them. The clock Konopka had located by breaking it was not a metaphor. It was a mechanism, self-contained enough to run inside one cell with the door shut.

In 2017, Jeffrey C. Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael W. Young were jointly awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, cited by the Nobel Assembly at the Karolinska Institutet for their discoveries of molecular mechanisms controlling the circadian rhythm. Forty-six years had passed since Konopka's vials. Konopka himself, whose original screen had located the gene and effectively started the whole inquiry, had died in 2015; the Nobel committee does not make posthumous awards, and his name is not among the three attached to the prize, though it appears in nearly every historical account of how the prize's discoveries began. It is a small, unresolved asymmetry in an otherwise tidy story — the person who first proved a clock gene existed did not live to see what his own screening work in a Caltech basement eventually earned. It was the first Nobel Prize awarded specifically for circadian research, closing out, at least in the eyes of the committee that hands out the prize, a question that had opened with a stubborn puzzle about temperature and a red-lit room: not merely that a fly's body could count something close to a day without outside help, but exactly which genes and which proteins were doing the counting.

None of this, on its own, explains where in a human body such a mechanism might live, or whether it has any bearing on a person rather than an insect bred for laboratory convenience. That question was being answered by an entirely separate line of research, using different animals and a different set of tools, in the same rough period that Konopka was counting fly vials.

In 1972, two research groups working independently — Robert Moore and Victor Eichler in one lab, Fred Stephan and Irving Zucker in another — turned to a much blunter instrument than gene mapping. They used lesions: small, deliberate injuries to specific tissue in the brains of laboratory rodents, made to see what stopped working once that tissue was gone. Both groups focused on a tiny paired structure sitting deep in the hypothalamus, just above the point where the two optic nerves cross — a location so precise that the structure takes its name directly from it: the suprachiasmatic nucleus, or SCN, "above the chiasm." When Moore and Eichler, and separately Stephan and Zucker, destroyed this structure in rats and hamsters, the animals did not become merely sluggish or erratic in their daily habits. Their rhythms of activity, feeding, and body temperature — the rise and fall that had previously tracked a reliable near-twenty-four-hour cycle even in constant laboratory lighting — flattened into something close to randomness. The animals kept eating, moving, and sleeping. They simply did it without any discernible pattern tied to time of day. Destroy this one small structure, and the rest of the rhythm-generating capacity of the brain and body, whatever it consisted of, no longer expressed itself as a coherent daily cycle.

The suprachiasmatic nucleus is easy to underestimate on sight. Each of its two lobes contains something on the order of twenty thousand neurons, a minuscule fraction of the roughly eighty-six billion neurons in a human brain — smaller, by volume, than many structures whose job is comparatively narrow, like a single relay station in the visual system. Most of what the brain does is distributed. Memory recruits the hippocampus and wide stretches of cortex working in concert; language draws on several interconnected regions whose boundaries blur under close study; almost nothing a neuroscientist studies turns out, on inspection, to live in exactly one place and nowhere else. The SCN is one of the rare exceptions — one of the clearest cases known of a brain structure built to do a single, specific job and, when it is missing, to leave that job undone rather than picked up by some other region. Destroy it, and an animal does not develop a slightly worse sense of time. It loses the sense of time entirely, while everything else it is capable of doing continues unimpaired. That degree of specificity, in a brain otherwise defined by overlap and redundancy, is what earned the SCN its description, in the decades of work that followed the 1972 lesion studies, as the body's master circadian pacemaker — the structure other clocks answer to. Researchers who went on to record directly from individual SCN neurons found a further signature of the same underlying rhythm: the cells fire electrical impulses at a noticeably higher rate through the subjective day than through the subjective night, a pattern that persists even when the tissue is kept alive in a dish, disconnected from eyes, muscles, and the rest of the animal it came from. The neurons, in effect, are loud during the day and quiet at night, on a schedule that needs no instruction from outside the dish to keep repeating.

For a long time, that phrase — master pacemaker — suggested a fairly simple picture: one small brain structure generating the body's sense of time, and every organ downstream simply obeying signals sent out from it, the way a factory floor might run off a single central clock mounted on the wall. It is a tidy picture, and for years it was more or less the working assumption. It also turned out to be significantly less complete than researchers first supposed. As molecular tools that had been developed to study the fly's period and timeless genes were turned on mammalian tissue, and as those tools grew sensitive enough to look at cells not just in the brain but throughout the rest of the body, something unexpected began turning up in laboratory after laboratory: the same core clock architecture — a feedback loop of transcription and protein accumulation directly analogous to the one Hall, Rosbash, and Young had described in flies — was not confined to the suprachiasmatic nucleus at all. It was present, and functioning, in the liver. In the heart. In skeletal muscle. In the lungs. In, as the accumulated research came to show by the time a widely cited 2013 peer-reviewed review titled "Molecular Architecture of the Mammalian Circadian Clock" summarized the field, most other organs in the body.

This was not a case of the SCN's signal simply reaching further than anyone had realized, the way a radio broadcast might carry farther than expected. The clocks found in the liver and the heart and the other peripheral organs were self-sustained and cell-autonomous — meaning a liver cell, isolated in a laboratory dish with no connection whatsoever to a brain, a spinal cord, or another cell, will keep its own feedback loop of clock-gene transcription running on a period close to twenty-four hours, entirely on its own. It does not need the suprachiasmatic nucleus to generate the rhythm. It needs the suprachiasmatic nucleus, it turned out, mainly to stay synchronized — to keep its clock set to roughly the same hour as the clock running in the neighboring heart cell, and the muscle cell, and the lung cell, and the thousands of other clocks ticking away independently throughout the rest of the body. Left alone long enough, without any coordinating signal from the brain, these peripheral clocks tend to drift out of phase with one another, each still oscillating close to twenty-four hours but no longer agreeing on what hour it currently is. The SCN's role, on this revised picture, is not to generate the body's sense of time from a single source and broadcast it outward. It is to act as a conductor keeping an orchestra of independently capable musicians playing in time with each other, using neural and hormonal signals rather than a literal beat.

The SCN itself resets quickly when the light-dark schedule it depends on changes — it takes its cues almost directly from the eyes. The peripheral clocks in the liver, the heart, and the rest of the body do not have that same direct line to daylight, and they do not necessarily reset at the same pace. A liver cell's clock is nudged toward realignment less by light than by the timing of meals, hormone levels, and body temperature, all of which the SCN itself only indirectly controls. The practical consequence, for a body that has just crossed several time zones or rotated onto an overnight shift, is that the brain's clock and the organs' clocks do not necessarily arrive at the new hour together. For some days, a traveler's suprachiasmatic nucleus can register morning while the liver, still running on the old schedule, is behaving as though it is the middle of the night. The felt sense of jet lag — alert at the wrong hour, hungry at the wrong hour, digestion and appetite and alertness all pulling in different directions — is, at the tissue level, several independent clocks disagreeing with each other about what time it currently is, each of them correct according to its own count and none of them yet caught up to the others.

The stranger finding, and the one that took the picture further from the factory-clock metaphor than most people expect, concerned what all these organ-level clocks were actually doing with their independence. If every peripheral clock ran on the same core molecular machinery as the SCN, a reasonable guess might have been that they were all driving more or less the same downstream processes — a kind of redundant backup system, useful mainly for keeping the whole body in sync if the master signal from the brain were ever interrupted. That is not what researchers found. When investigators cataloged which genes actually rise and fall on a daily rhythm in different tissues — which genes each local clock switches on and off as it completes its cycle — the overlap between organs turned out to be remarkably small. Somewhere in the range of one to three percent of the genes that oscillate on a daily rhythm in the liver also oscillate on a daily rhythm in the heart, or in the suprachiasmatic nucleus itself. The clock machinery is shared. What each organ does with it is not. The liver appears to use its clock to run a genetic program built around metabolism and the processing of nutrients on a schedule tied to when an animal is likely to be eating. The heart uses essentially the same underlying mechanism to run a largely different program, tuned to its own functional demands. The SCN runs a different program still. Nearly every cell in the body, in other words, is not simply checking in with a central authority to find out what time it is and then falling in line behind a single, uniform command. It is running its own local calendar, built from the identical feedback loop first worked out in a fly's period and timeless genes, deciding for itself which of its own genes to activate and which to quiet at each point in that calendar, and merely checking against the SCN's signal often enough to keep its particular clock reasonably close to everyone else's.

It is worth sitting with how strange this arrangement actually is, described plainly rather than through the metaphor of clocks and conductors. A muscle cell in a person's leg, a liver cell processing what that person ate for lunch, a heart muscle cell contracting roughly once a second, and a cluster of twenty thousand neurons sitting just above the crossing point of the optic nerves are all, right now, running the same molecular loop that Ronald Konopka located by breaking it in fruit flies in 1971 and that Hall, Rosbash, and Young spent the following decades pulling apart down to its molecular parts. None of these tissues needs to be told, from outside itself, that a day has a beginning and an end. Each one already knows, in the most literal biochemical sense available to a cell: a gene is switched on, a protein accumulates, the protein switches its own gene back off, the protein decays, and the gene switches back on, in a loop that takes about twenty-four hours to complete whether or not the person carrying that cell has any idea what time it is. The version of the mechanism found in fruit flies and the version found in humans are close enough in their basic architecture that the underlying logic — self-suppressing feedback, protein accumulation and decay, a period close to a full day — is recognizably the same machine, refined by hundreds of millions of years of shared evolutionary history rather than invented twice from scratch.

This is the piece of the argument that the earlier bunker experiments outside Andechs could only gesture toward from the outside. Watching a person's core body temperature and sleep timing drift on their own schedule inside a room with no clock and no daylight proves that something inside a person is generating that rhythm without help from the environment. It does not show what that something is made of. What Konopka's mutant flies, and Hall's and Rosbash's and Young's molecular follow-up, and Moore's and Eichler's and Stephan's and Zucker's lesioned rodents, and the researchers who went on to find clocks quietly running in liver and heart and lung tissue all did, across four decades and several unrelated fields of biology, was locate the actual hardware. Not a folk intuition about morning people and night people, not a behavioral tendency that might have a dozen competing explanations, but a specific gene, a specific protein, a specific feedback loop, and a specific, anatomically locatable structure in the brain, coordinating a specific set of near-identical loops running independently in nearly every other organ in the body. It is about as close as biology gets to being able to point to a mechanism and say: this is the thing itself, not a shadow of it.

And it is, as far as the evidence accumulated since 1971 currently indicates, close to universal. Barring the kind of rare mutation that gave Konopka his original three fly strains — mutations that exist in humans too, and produce their own unusual sleep timing in the small number of families that carry them — every person's cells run this loop. The period and timeless genes have human counterparts; the suprachiasmatic nucleus sits above the optic chiasm of every human brain in roughly the same location it occupies in a rat's or a hamster's; the liver and heart and lungs of every reader of this book are, at this moment, running clocks built from the same feedback architecture, largely independent of one another, loosely synchronized by signals from a cluster of neurons smaller than a grain of rice. It is not a cultural habit. It is not a preference that firm enough instruction could override. It is closer to the fixed dimensions of a room that every later chapter in this book will have to fit inside — the hospital ward at three in the morning, the reactor control room, the cockpit on the descent into a fogged-in airport, the trading desk that never technically closes. Whatever those institutions decide is convenient, the loop keeps running on its own schedule, in liver cells and heart cells and the small brain structure coordinating them, indifferent to the shift roster taped to the break-room wall.

What the loop does not explain, on its own, is why some people seem to run noticeably ahead of that twenty-four-hour schedule and others noticeably behind it — why the version of a person awake and clear-headed at six in the morning can be a genuinely different person, biologically, from the version awake and clear-headed at eleven at night, even though both of them are built from the same period and timeless genes, the same feedback loop, the same tiny nucleus above the same optic chiasm. The machinery, it turns out, is close to universal. What people do with it is not.
