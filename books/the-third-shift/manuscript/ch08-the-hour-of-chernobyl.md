# The Hour of Chernobyl

The test that destroyed Reactor No. 4 at the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant was supposed to have happened in daylight. It had been written up months in advance, reviewed on paper, scheduled for the afternoon of April 25, 1986, timed to coincide with a planned shutdown of the reactor for routine maintenance. The idea behind it was narrow and, on its face, unglamorous: to find out whether the momentum of a spinning turbine, cut off from steam but still coasting on its own inertia, could generate enough electricity to run the reactor's emergency cooling pumps for the minute or so it would take backup diesel generators to kick in during a total loss of outside power. It was a question worth answering. A reactor that loses power to its own cooling system is a reactor that can begin, within minutes, to damage itself, and the engineers who designed the test wanted proof that the plant had a bridge across that gap. Similar tests had reportedly been attempted at Chernobyl before, without producing a clean result, which is part of why this particular run of the experiment had been rewritten and rescheduled in the first place — one more piece of unfinished business layered onto a plant that was, by the spring of 1986, still treated by its own management as a going concern with routine engineering questions left to answer, not as a site already carrying the seeds of catastrophe. None of what happened later erased the reasonableness of wanting to know.

What erased the afternoon was a phone call from outside the plant. A regional grid controller, responsible for keeping electricity flowing to the surrounding area, asked the station to keep Unit 4 supplying power a while longer; a demand had come up elsewhere on the grid, and taking the reactor offline right on schedule would leave a gap the controller did not want to explain. The plant complied, as plants generally did in a system where the grid's needs sat above a single station's test schedule, and the shutdown that the experiment depended on kept sliding backward, hour by hour, through the afternoon and into the evening. By the time authorization to proceed finally came, the reactor's day shift — the engineers who had drafted the test procedure, argued over its details, and understood, as well as anyone at the plant did, what they were about to ask the reactor to do — had gone home for the night. A chronology maintained by the Laka Foundation, a Dutch archive devoted to nuclear documentation, lays out this sequence in some detail: the delay at the grid controller's request, and the fact that a different, less experienced shift had taken over operation of the reactor partway through preparation for the test, inheriting a procedure they had not written and had had little time to absorb before they were expected to carry it out. David Holloway, writing in the New York Review of Books in 1990 in an essay titled "The Catastrophe and After," corroborates the same general picture from a vantage close enough to the events, in time, that the Soviet investigation itself was still being argued over and revised as he wrote.

The night shift that took the reactor over was led by Aleksandr Akimov, the unit's shift foreman, with Leonid Toptunov at the control panel responsible for regulating the reactor's power output — a post he had held for only a few months. Overseeing the test on behalf of plant management was Anatoly Dyatlov, the deputy chief engineer, a more senior figure with long experience at the plant but not, by most accounts, deep familiarity with the specific procedure now sitting in front of him, revised and re-timed by people who were no longer there to walk him through it. This was the crew handed a delayed experiment, at night, on a machine whose behavior at the low power levels the test required was less familiar to nearly everyone in the control room than its behavior at full output would have been. None of that, by itself, guaranteed disaster. Delayed tests get run by night shifts routinely, at nuclear plants and everywhere else, without incident. What it meant was that the margin for confusion — for a step misread, a warning sign discounted, a deviation from procedure that seemed reasonable in the moment — was wider than it had been designed to be, on a night when the people best equipped to notice that the margin had widened were no longer in the building.

There was, underneath the specific decisions made in that control room, a broader institutional habit worth naming before the physics of the test takes over the story, because it shaped what everyone in the room that night did and did not know. Soviet civilian nuclear power operated inside a culture that treated safety incidents as information to be contained rather than disseminated. Other RBMK reactors elsewhere in the Soviet Union had experienced their own power instabilities in earlier years, episodes that were not widely publicized even within the industry, let alone to the public, on the theory that admitting a design weakness reflected poorly on the program rather than protecting the people who ran it. Production targets, plan fulfillment, the political costs of missing a scheduled deadline — all of it weighed on plant managers in ways that had nothing to do with reactor physics and everything to do with the system the reactor sat inside. None of that excuses what happened at 1:23 in the morning. It does help explain why a delayed test, run by a shift that had not designed it, proceeded as far as it did before anyone with the authority to stop it decided that stopping it was worth the cost of admitting the schedule had failed.

The test itself required the reactor to be brought down to a specific, fairly low power level and held there steadily before the turbine trip that was the actual point of the experiment could be triggered. Getting a reactor to sit calmly at low power and stay there is, in an RBMK-type reactor like Chernobyl's, a genuinely difficult thing to do, for reasons rooted in the physics of the fuel itself rather than in anything the crew that night did or failed to do. As a reactor runs, it accumulates a byproduct, xenon, that absorbs the neutrons needed to sustain the nuclear reaction; at high power this xenon burns off about as fast as it forms, but when power is cut sharply, the xenon briefly piles up faster than it decays, smothering the reaction and dragging power down further than intended — a well-understood phenomenon by 1986, but not one nuclear operators of the era could correct for with anything more precise than judgment and the slow, imprecise business of withdrawing control rods to compensate. On the night of the test, power fell substantially below the level the procedure called for, deep enough into that unstable low-power territory that the plant's own operating rules treated it as a condition to recover from cautiously or abandon, not one to push through in order to salvage a test already hours behind its own delayed schedule. The crew chose, by most reconstructions of that night, to keep going rather than start over — withdrawing rods to coax the reactor back up toward a workable power level, holding it there by hand, and pressing ahead with the turbine test in a state the reactor had not been designed to be run in for long. It is a decision that is easy to condemn in hindsight and harder to fully explain from inside the control room that night, where a delayed test, a shift that had not designed it, and a plant culture in which questioning a superior's decision to proceed carried its own real risk were all pressing on the same handful of people at once.

What makes an RBMK reactor unforgiving of exactly this kind of low-power maneuvering is a design feature that would become, after the accident, one of the most scrutinized facts in the history of civilian nuclear power: a positive void coefficient. Put plainly, when water inside this particular reactor design turns to steam — forms a "void" — the reaction inside the fuel does not slow down the way it does in most Western reactor designs. It speeds up. Steam is a weaker absorber of neutrons than liquid water, and in an RBMK, removing that absorption lets the chain reaction intensify rather than settle, so that a reactor already unstable at low power can, under the wrong conditions, begin feeding a runaway acceleration rather than damping one down on its own. Western reactor designs of the same era were built, deliberately, with the opposite property, so that a loss of coolant slowed the reaction rather than fed it — a difference in philosophy, not merely in engineering detail, between reactor traditions that had evolved somewhat separately on either side of the Cold War. Soviet nuclear engineers understood the void coefficient problem in principle; what is less certain, and became a matter of real dispute in the years after the accident, is how well that danger had been communicated down to the operators actually running the machine, on a given night, at a given power level, with a delayed test in front of them and a shift that had not planned it.

At 1:23 a.m. on April 26, 1986, with the reactor held near the low power level the crew had fought to reach and hold, the turbine test finally began. Steam to the turbine was cut, and the turbine's coasting rotation was left to supply what power it could to the reactor's own pumps, exactly as the experiment intended. What happened over the next roughly forty seconds is the part of the story that nuclear engineers have spent the decades since arguing over in the greatest technical detail, because the reactor's response did not match anything the test's designers had anticipated. Power began to rise, not fall — a consequence, later analysis concluded, of the combined effect of the reduced water flow through the core as the coasting pumps slowed, the residual xenon instability, and the reactor's inherent tendency, at that power level and configuration, to accelerate rather than self-correct once a rise began. Toptunov and Akimov, reading the signs of a dangerous power excursion building in front of them, moved to shut the reactor down using the emergency scram button, AZ-5, designed to drive every control rod back into the core at once and kill the reaction within seconds. It was, by every account of the operators' own understanding that night, the correct response to what they were seeing. It did not work the way it was supposed to. The RBMK's control rods were tipped with graphite, a material that, in the first instant of insertion, displaced water at the very bottom of the control-rod channels rather than immediately absorbing neutrons the way the rod's boron-carbide body would once it traveled further in — a design detail unrelated to anything the night crew had done, which meant that pressing the button meant to stop the reaction produced, for a brief and fatal window, a further localized surge in reactivity at the bottom of the core rather than the shutdown everyone in the room expected. Within seconds, two explosions tore through the reactor building, destroying the core, blowing the massive concrete lid off the reactor vessel, and igniting fires, including a graphite fire that would burn for days and carry radioactive material high into the atmosphere, eventually depositing fallout across a wide swath of the European continent.

The scale of what followed is, by now, part of the general historical record rather than a discovery this chapter needs to make. Pripyat, the purpose-built town that housed the plant's workers and their families a couple of miles from the reactor, was not evacuated until roughly a day and a half after the explosion, its residents told at first only that the evacuation was precautionary and temporary — a delay attributable less to any single decision made at 1:23 in the morning than to a Soviet governmental and administrative culture built around controlling information first and responding to emergencies second. Firefighters and plant workers who responded in the first hours, many without any real understanding of what they were being exposed to, suffered acute radiation sickness in the following days and weeks; a number of them died. The broader toll — how many additional deaths, over decades, should be attributed to the radiation released that night, among cleanup workers, evacuees, and residents of contaminated regions — has never been fully agreed upon, and estimates offered by different bodies over the years have varied enormously, a genuine scientific and epidemiological uncertainty rather than a dispute this chapter is equipped to resolve. Chernobyl remains, alongside the 2011 accident at Fukushima, one of only two nuclear power accidents ever rated at the maximum severity level on the scale used internationally to measure them. The ruined reactor was eventually enclosed, first in a hastily built concrete and steel structure known as the sarcophagus, and decades later in a far larger arched containment shell moved into place over the entire site, engineering projects that would themselves occupy years and cost billions, standing as a kind of permanent monument to how long a single night can go on costing money and labor after the fires are out. The exclusion zone drawn around the plant, tens of kilometers across, remains largely uninhabited by people to this day, even as it has become, unexpectedly, a de facto wildlife refuge for species that do not read radiation warnings. Nuclear regulators outside the Soviet Union, watching the disaster unfold from a distance, absorbed lessons about reactor design, containment, and emergency communication that shaped safety reviews and public confidence in nuclear power for a generation, in countries that had never operated an RBMK and never would.

In the aftermath, several plant officials and engineers, Dyatlov among them, were tried by Soviet courts and sentenced to prison terms for their role in the disaster, a legal outcome that tracked closely with the first official investigation's emphasis on operator misconduct rather than reactor design. It is worth sitting, briefly, with the discomfort of that fact alongside everything the later international reassessment established. Individual operators that night did make decisions — to keep the test going rather than abort it, to hold the reactor at an unstable power level rather than start over — that a more cautious crew, or the same crew on a different night, might have made differently. Those decisions had consequences, and the men who made them were not blameless bystanders to what followed. But a legal system that needed defendants, operating inside a state that badly wanted the story to end with individual culpability rather than an admission that the reactor itself, and the secrecy surrounding its known flaws, bore comparable or greater responsibility, produced a verdict that later technical reassessment did not so much overturn as complicate. Both things can be true at once: that specific people made specific, consequential choices in that control room, and that the deck those choices were dealt from had already been stacked, by designers and by a government, in ways no operator on any shift, tired or rested, senior or junior, could have fully compensated for on their own.

The official reckoning with why it happened did not stay still for long, and its own instability is part of what makes the disaster hard to summarize in a single sentence without doing violence to the truth. The first Soviet account, presented to the International Atomic Energy Agency later in 1986, placed the weight of causation heavily on the operators — on a crew that had, in this telling, violated procedure, disabled safety systems that would otherwise have prevented the test from proceeding under such unstable conditions, and pressed forward with an experiment they should have abandoned. It was a version of events congenial to a Soviet state eager to locate the failure in individual misconduct rather than in a reactor design approved, built, and operated under its own authority, and it was not, in the years that followed, allowed to stand unchallenged. A later reassessment by international nuclear-safety reviewers, drawing on more complete information about the RBMK's design that Soviet secrecy had kept even from the reactor's own operators, shifted the balance of responsibility substantially — toward the positive void coefficient, toward the flawed control-rod design that turned an emergency shutdown into a momentary accelerant, and toward a culture of institutional secrecy in which operators were, by design, not told the full extent of the instabilities their reactor could exhibit at low power, because that information was itself treated as a state secret rather than as operational knowledge the people running the plant needed. Holloway's 1990 essay, written in the gap between those two accounts, reflects a moment when the full picture was still coming into focus — when it was already clear that the initial Soviet narrative of operator recklessness was incomplete, but before the extent of the reactor's own design culpability, and the government's culpability in withholding it, had been as fully documented as it later would be.

It is inside that more complicated, more honestly contested account of causation that the hour on the clock and the shift on duty belong — not as the explanation, but as one thread among several that investigators and later chroniclers of the disaster have kept returning to, because it kept showing up alongside the others rather than instead of them. A 2023 industry safety research summary published by Optalert, a company that develops fatigue-monitoring technology and reviews major industrial accidents as part of its own safety research, states that Chernobyl investigators identified operator fatigue, following a pattern of thirteen-hour shifts, as a leading contributor to the human error involved in the accident. It is worth being precise about what that claim is and is not. It is not the verdict of the primary Soviet investigation, which in its earliest form was more interested in blaming operators for insubordination than for exhaustion, nor is it the centerpiece of the later international reassessment, which directed most of its corrective weight at the reactor's design and at the institutional secrecy that had concealed that design's flaws from the people operating it. It is a claim made by an industry-safety source looking back at the disaster decades later, situating fatigue and long shifts as one contributing thread in a story whose two heaviest threads — a reactor built with a known, poorly communicated instability, and a state apparatus that had made keeping such instabilities quiet a matter of policy — sit elsewhere. Treated that way, as one strand rather than the whole rope, the fatigue claim fits comfortably alongside everything else known about that night: a crew working long hours on a test that had already run late into what should have been their rest, absorbing a procedure they had not designed, at an hour when the human capacity for exactly the kind of fine-grained, high-stakes judgment the situation demanded is, for almost anyone, at its weakest point in the day. A thirteen-hour shift beginning in the afternoon, of the kind Optalert's summary describes, runs its final hours precisely into the stretch of night this book keeps circling back to — the hours in which core body temperature sits near its daily low and the kind of fast, adaptive reasoning a deteriorating reactor test demands is, for physiological reasons that have nothing to do with an individual operator's competence or diligence, harder to summon than it would be earlier in the same shift, let alone earlier in the day. That does not make the crew's decisions inevitable. Other operators, on other nights, held reactors at unstable power levels and lived to explain the choice at a hearing rather than in an investigation. But it means the crew at Chernobyl that night was not simply unlucky in the ordinary sense of the word; it was doing difficult, unfamiliar work at the one part of the twenty-four-hour clock when difficult, unfamiliar work is hardest to do well, on top of every other disadvantage already stacked against it.

That is why the timing keeps recurring in the retellings, even among writers and investigators who know perfectly well that no honest account of Chernobyl can rest the disaster's weight on a single hour or a single tired mind. A test conceived for the daylight, delayed by a grid controller's ordinary demand, executed instead after midnight by a crew that had not built it, on a reactor already carrying a design flaw few outside a narrow circle of Soviet nuclear specialists were permitted to know about — every piece of that sentence would have been true regardless of the clock. But the clock is the piece that turns a bad situation into an unforgiving one. A more experienced crew, working at ten in the morning rather than one in the morning, might have caught the xenon instability sooner, might have pushed back harder against continuing a test that was clearly not behaving as planned, might have brought a sharper kind of attention to a control panel that, at that hour, on that crew, was being read by people whose bodies were, by the plain physiology of the hour, working against the very alertness the moment required. None of that is provable in the way the void coefficient is provable. It is a counterfactual, and counterfactuals about a single catastrophic night can never be run twice to check. What can be said, and said honestly, is that the investigators who came after — Soviet and international both, working from different assumptions and reaching different conclusions about almost everything else — kept finding their way back to the same two facts recorded plainly in the Laka Foundation's chronology of that night: that the test began at 1:23 in the morning, and that the shift running it was not the shift that had planned it. Whether a different hour would have changed the outcome is, in the end, a question the record cannot answer and this book will not pretend to. What the record does show, repeatedly, is that when something has already gone wrong with the design of a machine and with the institutions responsible for operating it honestly, the middle of the night is where the last remaining margin for catching the error tends to run thinnest.

Seven years before Chernobyl, on the other side of a divided world, in a reactor built to a very different design and operated under a very different set of institutional habits, another crew had faced its own unfamiliar instrument readings during its own least-alert hours of the night — not at 1:23 in the morning but at four, not amid a delayed test but amid an ordinary shift gone suddenly, silently wrong.
