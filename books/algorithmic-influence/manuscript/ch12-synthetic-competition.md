# Synthetic Competition

The cheapest way to change a selection system is to change what it has to select from.

For most of the history of media, production constrained volume.

A newspaper page cost money to print. A television program required cameras, crews, editing, distribution, and a schedule. A record required studio time and physical manufacturing. Even the early web demanded enough human effort to write, design, photograph, record, or code something worth publishing.

The costs fell for decades.

Generative AI pushed them lower again.

A person can now produce ten headlines where one existed, fifty product descriptions, hundreds of image variants, translated versions of a campaign message, summaries of a long report, synthetic voices, video clips, background music, advertising concepts, replies, posts, and entire websites at a cost that would have seemed absurdly small a few years earlier.

The obvious consequence is more content.

The less obvious consequence is more competition for machines.

Human attention did not expand when generation became cheap.

Every feed, search engine, marketplace, inbox, app store, music service, video platform, and answer system still has to decide what deserves a scarce position in front of a person. When supply expands faster than attention, selection becomes more valuable.

That gives content producers an incentive to optimize production around the selector.

The old creator made a piece and hoped it traveled.

The synthetic creator can make variants and treat distribution as an experiment.

Change the opening. Change the thumbnail. Change the voice. Change the duration. Translate it. Recut it. Generate ten versions. Publish across accounts. Keep what survives.

Evolutionary language is difficult to avoid because the mechanism genuinely resembles selection among variants.

The important difference is that the variants can now be created intentionally at machine speed.

This turns algorithmic influence from craft into something closer to search.

A human creator may hold a theory about what the recommender rewards and test one hypothesis a day. An automated system can generate many candidates, measure outcomes, and update much faster. The production process begins to meet the distribution system on its own computational terms.

This does not require fully autonomous agents.

A marketing team using generative tools to produce dozens of ad variants is already doing it. An e-commerce seller generating title and image alternatives is doing it. A political organization creating localized clips is doing it. A publisher producing multiple summaries for different channels is doing it.

Humans remain in the loop.

The loop has become much wider.

The popular label “AI slop” captures genuine frustration with low-effort synthetic material flooding digital spaces. It is not a useful analytical category because it begins by assuming quality. Some synthetic content is useless. Some is fraudulent. Some is strange. Some is excellent. Some is indistinguishable in purpose from ordinary automation: resizing images, translating text, creating captions, summarizing documentation, generating a first draft that a human improves.

The important variable is not whether a machine touched the artifact.

It is the economics of producing another candidate.

When the marginal candidate becomes cheap, strategies that were previously too expensive become rational.

A publisher can cover the long tail of queries. A seller can create a page for every product variation. A music operation can release more tracks. An anonymous social account can maintain continuous output. A spammer can personalize. A legitimate educational organization can create explanations for different reading levels and languages.

Abundance is morally neutral at the point of generation.

The selection system has to decide what to do with it.

That creates pressure on recommenders to become better at quality, originality, authenticity, satisfaction, and provenance rather than relying on crude activity signals that synthetic producers can manufacture cheaply.

A signal valuable yesterday can become worthless tomorrow if generation makes it abundant.

Consider polished prose.

For years, basic fluency acted as a weak quality filter on the web. Producing thousands of grammatical pages required writers, editors, or expensive automation. Large language models reduced that cost dramatically. Fluency now tells a ranking system less about effort, expertise, or truth than it once did.

The system needs different evidence.

Source reputation matters more. Direct experience matters more. Provenance may matter more. User satisfaction over time matters more. Original reporting, verified transactions, demonstrated expertise, durable relationships, and signals costly to fake can become relatively valuable.

Synthetic abundance does not necessarily destroy quality.

It can make quality harder to infer from surface appearance.

This is the same problem employers face with AI-generated applications. The cover letter once carried some evidence that a person invested time in a particular role. If anyone can produce a tailored letter in seconds, tailoring no longer signals the same level of interest. Employers may stop valuing the letter or demand different evidence.

A signal dies when its production cost collapses.

Recommendation systems will experience this repeatedly.

A platform that once treated posting consistency as a weak signal of serious creators may encounter automated accounts capable of publishing constantly. A marketplace that once treated detailed product descriptions as evidence of a careful seller may find perfect descriptions generated for low-quality goods. A news system that once associated polished formatting with professional organizations may encounter synthetic sites mimicking the style at scale.

The selector has to move deeper.

This is where provenance becomes strategically important.

Who made this? Under what process? Was the photograph captured by a camera, generated, edited, or assembled? Is the seller connected to a verified business? Did a reviewer complete a real transaction? Does a publisher have a history? Can claims be traced to primary evidence?

Standards such as C2PA attempt to make some provenance information machine-readable through cryptographically signed Content Credentials. The technology cannot tell whether a claim is true. It can help establish parts of the artifact's history.

That distinction matters.

A photograph can have perfect provenance and depict something misleading. A synthetic image can be clearly labeled and entirely legitimate. Provenance is evidence about origin, not a universal quality score.

Yet in an environment of cheap generation, origin becomes economically valuable information.

Recommendation systems can use it.

Creators will then optimize around provenance too.

The loop continues.

A legitimate publisher adopts credentials because platforms or agents trust them. Competitors adopt them. Fraudsters look for ways around them. Systems learn which signals remain hard to fake. Standards evolve.

The same arms race that search experienced around links can appear around authenticity.

This is one reason it is too simple to imagine a future divided into authentic human content and synthetic machine content.

Real production will be hybrid.

A human reporter records an interview, uses AI to transcribe it, uses software to identify themes, writes the article, generates alternate headlines, edits a photograph, attaches provenance metadata, and publishes into a system that uses AI to rank the result. Which part is synthetic?

The more useful question is what claims of authorship, evidence, and process matter for the context.

The recommendation system does not need a metaphysical answer.

It needs enough trustworthy information to make a useful selection.

Synthetic competition also changes the labor of creators.

When rivals can produce more, the individual creator feels pressure to produce more too.

This can lead to a miserable equilibrium.

Everyone publishes at machine pace. Audiences become overwhelmed. Recommendation becomes harsher. Creators use more automation to survive the harsher selection. The volume rises again.

A 2026 game-theoretic paper on the “enshittification of algorithmic recommendation” explored one version of this co-adaptation between strategic creators and recommendation systems. The paper is theory, not a verdict on every platform, and the loaded title should not substitute for evidence. Its underlying problem is useful: optimizing creators can alter the distribution of available content in ways that force the recommender to change, and the combined dynamics may not produce the outcome either side would choose in isolation.

This is a coordination problem.

Each creator has an individual incentive to produce another variant if the marginal cost is low and the probability of breakout reach is positive.

Collectively, the extra volume can make discovery harder and reduce the value of each additional item.

The recommender responds by filtering more aggressively.

Filtering more aggressively makes creators optimize harder.

No villain is required.

The system can become worse through individually rational behavior.

Platforms have several defenses.

They can limit spam. They can reduce distribution for duplicative or low-satisfaction material. They can increase the value of account history or verified identity. They can diversify results. They can detect coordinated networks. They can impose rate limits. They can measure downstream satisfaction rather than immediate interaction. They can privilege original sources. They can use provenance.

Every defense creates new selection criteria.

Every selection criterion becomes a new surface for algorithmic influence.

This is why generative AI will not eliminate platform optimization.

It will industrialize it.

A person used to ask, What should I make?

An optimization system can ask, Which of these hundred candidates should survive?

The human role shifts upward toward objectives, taste, constraints, and judgment—unless the human delegates those too.

That delegation is already visible in advertising, where automated systems can generate creative variations, choose audiences, adjust bids, and allocate budgets toward predicted performance. The advertiser sets goals and constraints; the platform performs much of the tactical search.

Creator tools can move in the same direction.

A musician may generate cover concepts, preview clips, descriptions, and short videos automatically. A retailer may let software generate listing variations. A publication may automatically test headlines. The optimization loop shortens until generation and selection are nearly continuous.

This can produce efficiency.

It can also produce a world in which cultural artifacts are optimized before any human has cared about them.

That sentence needs qualification.

There is no reason machine-assisted optimization must remove human care. A novelist can test jacket copy without changing the novel. A museum can translate descriptions. A nonprofit can produce accessible formats. A small business can compete with large marketing departments.

Generative tools can democratize production just as recommendation democratized distribution.

The danger appears when the measurable performance of the representation becomes detached from the value of the thing represented.

An AI-generated product photograph can improve conversion while making the product look better than it is. A synthetic testimonial can manufacture social proof. A fabricated news clip can trigger engagement before correction arrives. A machine-generated review can inflate reputation. A generated résumé can imply polish not reflected in the applicant's work.

These are old deceptions made cheap.

The economic change is scale.

Detection becomes necessary because trust cues that once depended on human effort are no longer costly.

This is why the future of algorithmic influence will be partly a contest over costly signals.

What remains expensive to fake?

A long history of verified transactions.

A direct relationship.

A physical credential.

A cryptographic signature tied to a trusted process.

A live performance.

A product that survives years of use.

A reputation accumulated across independent systems.

A piece of evidence that can be checked against the world.

None is perfect. All can be manipulated under some conditions. Their value lies in imposing friction on mass imitation.

The strange consequence of synthetic abundance may therefore be renewed interest in friction.

For decades, digital systems treated friction as the enemy. Remove steps. Reduce cost. Make publishing instant. Make sharing instant. Make signup instant. Make purchase instant.

When generation itself becomes instant, friction can become evidence.

A system may value the fact that a claim passed through a costly verification process. A consumer may value a human recommendation because the recommender's reputation is at stake. A reader may subscribe to a publication not because information is scarce but because trusted filtering is scarce.

The abundance problem returns at a higher level.

More content creates demand for selection.

More machine-generated content creates demand for trustworthy selection.

Trustworthy selection creates incentives to influence the trust machinery.

That machinery becomes another audience.

This is why a simple ban on synthetic content would not solve the structural problem even if such a ban were desirable or enforceable.

Human creators optimize too. Human spam predates AI. Human propaganda predates feeds. Human advertising predates tracking. The new technology changes the production frontier; it does not invent strategic behavior.

The challenge is designing selection systems whose proxies remain aligned with what people actually want when the population being selected is intelligent, adaptive, and increasingly automated.

That is an adversarial problem in the broad sense.

Not everyone is malicious.

Everyone has an objective.

The creator wants distribution. The platform wants satisfied users and a sustainable business. Advertisers want conversion. Users want some mixture of entertainment, information, connection, status, and escape. Regulators want legal compliance and public protections. Synthetic agents may pursue delegated goals.

The system has to mediate among objectives that do not line up perfectly.

Generative AI increases the number of actors capable of playing at scale.

One research line in 2025 began documenting “agentic” or highly automated AI-generated accounts and the dynamics of synthetic content in social-media discovery. The evidence is still early, and platform detection makes measurement difficult. That uncertainty should not be hidden.

We do not yet know the equilibrium.

Platforms may become extremely good at suppressing low-value automation. Users may embrace synthetic creators in some genres and reject them in others. Provenance may become routine. Human authenticity may command a premium. Synthetic media may blend into ordinary production until the category itself stops being useful.

The one thing we can say with confidence is that cheaper production does not make selection less important.

It makes selection more important.

That puts more power in the intermediary.

And more power in the intermediary increases the value of learning how to influence it.

This is the recursive engine running through the entire book.

Now the intermediary itself is changing form.

Search and social feeds present lists of possibilities. A person still clicks, scrolls, compares, and assembles an answer.

Generative systems can do something more aggressive.

They can read the possibilities and speak back in one voice.

The producer is no longer competing only to appear near the top.

The producer is competing to become part of the answer.
