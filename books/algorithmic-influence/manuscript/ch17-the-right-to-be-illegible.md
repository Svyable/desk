# The Right to Be Illegible

Privacy is often described as hiding information.

That is too narrow for algorithmic life.

Sometimes the important freedom is not to hide a fact but to prevent one representation of the fact from becoming the only way an institution can see you.

A person can be accurately measured and still be misunderstood.

The worker completed fewer tickets because she solved the hardest ones. The applicant lacks the conventional title because his career crossed industries. The customer watches a series of videos about grief because a friend died, not because grief has become a durable preference. The merchant has few reviews because the business is new, not because it is untrustworthy.

The data can be correct.

The inference can still be wrong.

This is why autonomy in an algorithmic society requires more than data protection.

It requires room for context, correction, exception, and sometimes refusal of the model itself.

I call this the right to be illegible.

Not a literal universal legal right. Not a demand that institutions stop measuring. Not a romantic defense of opacity.

It is a design principle: consequential systems should preserve meaningful ways for people to avoid being trapped by one machine-readable representation of themselves.

The principle begins with a fact we have seen throughout the book.

Legibility is useful.

Search works because pages can be indexed. Commerce works because products have identifiers and prices. Fraud systems work because transactions leave patterns. Hiring works at scale because applications can be organized. Recommendation works because behavior supplies evidence. Logistics works because packages can be tracked.

A world without machine legibility would be less convenient, less accessible, and in many cases less fair.

The question is where legibility should stop.

One answer is user choice.

The European Union's Digital Services Act created important obligations around recommender-system transparency for very large online platforms and search engines and, in applicable settings, options for recommendation not based on profiling. The exact implementation varies by service, but the underlying principle is significant.

Personalization should not always be the only doorway.

A chronological or non-profiled feed may be less relevant. That is the tradeoff. The user receives less prediction in exchange for more control over the allocation rule.

Choice makes the cost visible.

This is healthier than pretending personalization is pure benefit or pure manipulation.

Some people want a feed that knows them. Others want to see what they explicitly chose. The same person may want both at different moments.

Plural interfaces preserve agency because no single model becomes mandatory reality.

The principle can extend beyond feeds.

A job applicant should sometimes be able to request human review of an automated conclusion, especially when the decision is consequential and the representation may be incomplete.

A worker should be able to contest a productivity signal with context.

A consumer should be able to correct factual information used in a consequential profile.

A merchant should be able to repair incorrect product identity or account data.

A citizen should be able to know when a significant decision is being made through automation rather than guessing.

These are not demands for humans to redo every machine decision.

That would destroy the scale benefits that made automation useful.

They are escape hatches.

An escape hatch matters because models fail unevenly.

Most routine cases may be handled extremely well. A small fraction contain unusual context. If the system offers no route for the unusual case, efficiency for the majority becomes rigidity for the minority.

Human review is one way to buy context.

It is not automatically superior.

Humans are biased. Humans are inconsistent. Humans get tired. Humans can rubber-stamp machine recommendations. A human reviewer who sees the model's score first may anchor on it and provide the appearance of review without independent judgment.

The goal is not to insert a ceremonial person.

It is to create genuine contestability.

Can new evidence change the outcome?

Can the reviewer see why the representation may be incomplete?

Does the institution have authority to override the automated result?

Is there a record of the challenge so recurring errors can improve the system?

Contestability turns exception handling into organizational learning.

This is one of the strongest arguments for appeals. An appeal is not merely kindness to the individual. It is a sensor for model failure.

If hundreds of workers successfully challenge the same productivity flag, the metric is wrong or badly applied. If applicants repeatedly correct the same parsing error, the extraction system needs repair. If merchants constantly appeal category assignments, the ontology is failing.

The institution learns where legibility breaks.

A system without appeals can look cleaner because contradictory evidence never enters the data.

That cleanliness is dangerous.

The model sees only the world it successfully represented.

The right to be illegible also includes the right not to optimize every part of oneself for selection.

This is harder to formalize.

A creator should be able to make a slow video even if fast videos usually perform better. The platform does not owe it equal reach. An applicant should be able to write an unusual résumé. The employer does not owe an interview. A merchant should be able to sell a product that resists standardization. An agent does not owe a recommendation.

Autonomy cannot mean immunity from other people's choices.

The meaningful question is whether alternative routes remain viable.

Can the creator reach subscribers directly?

Can the applicant provide a portfolio or explanation?

Can the merchant be found through direct search or a different agent?

Can the worker talk to a manager?

Can the citizen choose a different feed?

Plurality protects illegibility better than any single transparency report.

When one intermediary controls the only practical route to opportunity, its representation becomes compulsory.

Competition among intermediaries can create different ways of being seen.

This is one reason open protocols matter. A merchant can expose product data once and allow several agents to interpret it differently. A creator can publish on an open web and be discovered through several systems. A worker can carry portable credentials rather than being trapped in one platform's rating history.

Portability converts reputation from dependency into asset.

Closed reputation systems do the opposite.

A driver may spend years building excellent standing on one platform and start from zero elsewhere. A seller's marketplace history may not transfer. A creator's follower relationship may be difficult to export. The intermediary owns the accumulated legibility.

This raises a property-like question.

Who owns the evidence of your successful participation?

Not every datum should be portable. A platform has legitimate proprietary information, anti-fraud signals, and other users' privacy to protect. But verified credentials, transaction histories, qualifications, and reputation summaries can sometimes travel without exposing sensitive internal systems.

Portability reduces the cost of exit.

Exit is the ultimate appeal.

A system behaves differently when participants can leave without erasing their history.

The right to be illegible therefore has an economic foundation.

People need alternatives.

Without alternatives, refusing optimization becomes self-exclusion.

This is where discussions of “digital detox” can become unserious. A worker cannot detox from the scheduling system that controls shifts. A merchant cannot simply ignore the marketplace containing most customers. An applicant cannot refuse online applications if employers require them.

Individual discipline cannot solve institutional dependency.

Design and competition have to preserve meaningful routes.

There is also a temporal dimension.

People should be allowed to change.

Machine systems love history because history predicts. Human dignity includes the possibility that history stops predicting.

A teenager's interests should not define the adult. A worker's bad month should not become permanent identity. A merchant's early failures should decay after years of good service. A person's past location should not always shape future offers. A political curiosity should not become an ideological label that follows indefinitely.

Forgetting is a form of illegibility.

Not total erasure. Calibrated decay.

Good systems ask how long evidence remains relevant.

Credit reporting already contains versions of this idea through time limits and recency. Recommendation systems often weight recent behavior because preferences change. Reputation systems can emphasize current performance. Risk models can distinguish temporary events from stable traits.

The principle is statistical and moral at once.

Old evidence becomes less predictive and people deserve room to become different.

These goals often align.

The hardest form of illegibility is ambiguity.

Machines are built to reduce uncertainty. People often contain it.

A person can hold inconsistent political views. A consumer can want cheap and luxurious. An employee can be brilliant and unreliable. A work of art can resist categorization. A community can use language outsiders misunderstand. A medical symptom can fit several diagnoses.

A model eventually has to choose a representation to act.

The right to be illegible asks the system to preserve uncertainty when certainty is not justified.

This is an argument against false precision.

A score of 72.4 can look exact while resting on weak evidence. A recommendation can sound confident while sources disagree. A risk classification can hide a wide confidence interval. A ranking can imply meaningful separation between items whose predicted values are nearly identical.

Uncertainty should sometimes be visible.

“Insufficient information” is a legitimate machine output.

So is “several options are similarly good.”

So is “human judgment required.”

Systems optimized for smooth interfaces may resist these answers because certainty feels helpful.

False certainty is a user-experience bug with social consequences.

It encourages institutions to act as if the representation is the person.

The right to be illegible asks for epistemic humility in the interface.

There are limits.

A bank cannot let every applicant opt out of fraud detection. An airport cannot make security screening purely voluntary. A marketplace cannot allow sellers to hide information necessary for consumer safety. An employer cannot ignore legally required records. A hospital needs patient identity and medication history.

Illegibility is not a trump card against legitimate coordination.

It is a presumption against unnecessary totalization.

Collect what the decision needs. Explain what matters. Preserve alternatives where feasible. Let people correct factual errors. Keep consequential rules contestable. Do not turn one proxy into a permanent identity. Avoid requiring people to expose more than the task demands.

This is data minimization expanded into institutional design.

The strongest systems may eventually make strategic self-presentation less necessary.

If an applicant can see and correct what the parser extracted, there is less need to guess how to format a résumé. If a merchant has a documented protocol for product data, there is less need to reverse engineer agent preference. If a worker knows which metrics affect discipline and can challenge errors, there is less need for rumor. If a creator can choose direct distribution to subscribers, recommendation becomes an opportunity rather than an existential dependency.

Good governance reduces folk physics.

It does not remove algorithms.

It makes the relationship less superstitious.

That may be the most practical test for whether a machine-mediated institution respects autonomy.

Do participants spend their time doing the underlying work, or performing rituals for an invisible judge?

Some optimization is unavoidable. A restaurant should publish its hours accurately. A worker should document completed work. A writer should communicate clearly. A merchant should maintain inventory data.

The problem is not adaptation.

The problem is adaptation detached from purpose.

When people spend enormous effort manipulating representations because they cannot trust the process behind them, the institution is imposing an algorithmic tax.

The tax appears as SEO busywork, résumé rituals, dashboard theater, review anxiety, feed superstition, schema maintenance, prompt testing, and endless attempts to infer what the machine wants.

Some of that work creates real interoperability.

Some is waste created by opacity and dependency.

The right to be illegible is partly a right not to pay unnecessary algorithmic tax merely to remain visible.

This brings the argument to its end.

Machines are not going away. Selection is not going away. Representation is not going away. Generative agents will make mediation deeper in some domains and barely touch others.

The question is what influence becomes once everyone knows the machine is in the room.

Do we spend our lives flattering statistical intermediaries?

Or do we build systems in which the best way to influence the machine is increasingly to produce evidence of something genuinely valuable to the person beyond it?

That is the difference between an algorithmic society organized around gaming and one organized around alignment.

The final chapter is about what survives either way.
