# Legibility Drift

A machine does not need to misunderstand you to change you.

It only needs to see some parts of you more clearly than others.

Imagine two employees. One prevents problems. The other resolves them quickly after they occur. A dashboard can count resolved tickets with little ambiguity. Prevention is harder. It appears as an absence: the outage that never happened, the customer who never became angry, the defect caught before it entered production, the colleague quietly taught enough to avoid a future mistake.

If the organization rewards the countable work, the second employee has an advantage even when the first creates more value.

Nothing dishonest has happened. Nobody manipulated the metric. The system measured what it could see.

That is enough.

Over time, people shift effort toward the visible work. Managers hire people whose records contain the visible work. Training emphasizes it. Promotions validate it. New software is designed around it. The organization gradually becomes better at producing evidence of the thing it can measure.

I call this legibility drift.

The term is less a law than a recurring pressure. When a selection system matters, the population being selected has reason to move toward characteristics the system can observe, compare, and reward.

The drift can be beneficial.

Hospitals that record medication accurately are safer. Products with standardized dimensions are easier to compare. Code with tests is easier to trust. Buildings with inspectable safety systems are easier to regulate. Financial statements create a common language for outsiders who cannot personally inspect every factory and invoice. Civilization scales by making important facts legible to strangers.

The alternative to measurement is not perfect human understanding.

It is often favoritism, memory, inconsistency, rumor, and power exercised without records.

A hiring manager who says “I know talent when I see it” may be less fair than a structured process. A lender who relies on personal familiarity excludes people outside the lender's network. A restaurant recommendation based only on who knows the critic privileges established institutions. A supervisor who evaluates by impression can overlook quiet performers.

Legibility can widen access.

The problem is not that systems use representations.

The problem is that representations become environments.

Once people know that a representation affects selection, they adapt to the representation. The adaptation can slowly alter the underlying population until qualities that are easy to encode become more common than qualities that are valuable but resistant to encoding.

Search provides a simple example. Pages acquire titles, headings, structured data, descriptive links, and clear language partly because machines need them. Much of this improves the web. But search-oriented publishing can also produce uncanny sameness: the same questions answered in the same order, the same phrases repeated because query data shows demand, the same length because competitors appear to rank at that length.

The web becomes more legible and, in some corners, less particular.

Social media accelerates the process because the feedback is faster. A format that survives recommendation is copied. The copied format becomes familiar. Familiarity lowers comprehension cost. The system learns from the new audience behavior. The original innovation becomes a convention.

Workplaces make the stakes less voluntary.

When a creator dislikes a platform's dominant style, she may leave if she has another audience. An employee often cannot leave the measurement system without leaving the job.

This is why algorithmic management deserves attention beyond the gig economy. Software can assign work, measure output, forecast staffing, schedule shifts, detect anomalies, recommend performance actions, or optimize routes. The OECD's 2025 work on algorithmic management found such tools used across conventional workplaces, not only app-mediated labor, while also documenting concerns about accountability, worker consultation, and the quality of managerial decisions.

The important point is not that every workplace has become an automated factory.

It is that software increasingly participates in deciding what counts as visible performance.

Once visibility matters to pay, scheduling, promotion, or discipline, workers rationally learn the representation.

A customer-service representative learns which statuses stop the clock. A warehouse worker learns which scan closes the task. A salesperson learns which activities count toward the dashboard. A driver learns which app behaviors preserve access to bonuses or preferred work. A software engineer learns that shipping visible features is easier to narrate during performance review than preventing a future architectural problem.

The last example may contain no algorithmic management at all. A human manager can create legibility drift with a spreadsheet.

That continuity is useful.

The machine is not magical. It amplifies an old organizational tendency by making measurement cheaper, more detailed, more frequent, and easier to connect to allocation.

The question is what disappears when a representation becomes operational.

In education, test scores can miss curiosity, courage, kindness, persistence, or the ability to recognize a badly framed problem. In medicine, coded activity can miss reassurance, diagnostic patience, or the decision not to perform an unnecessary procedure. In software, tickets closed can miss system understanding. In creative work, views can miss the book that changes one reader's career.

These examples sound sentimental until a system needs those invisible qualities.

Then the loss becomes operational.

An airline can optimize turn times until a rare maintenance concern requires someone willing to delay a flight. A hospital can optimize throughput until an ambiguous patient needs more time. A company can optimize sales activity until the important account requires months of relationship-building that looks unproductive in weekly numbers.

Robust institutions preserve space for judgment partly because the future contains situations their metrics cannot anticipate.

Algorithmic influence pressures that space from two directions.

First, systems favor information that can enter their models. Second, people learn to produce that information because it affects outcomes.

The loop can make the missing information increasingly invisible.

Suppose an employer adopts a screening process that values recent experience described in standardized job-family terms. Applicants adapt their résumés to those terms. Career advisers teach the terms. People choose titles that travel better across employers. Training programs align curricula with the vocabulary. Over time the applicant pool itself becomes more standardized.

The system appears to be getting better data.

It is also helping produce the data it expects.

This is not necessarily bad. Common occupational language makes labor markets easier to navigate. Standardization can improve matching. The concern is that unusual but valuable trajectories may become harder to recognize because the system encounters fewer of them and people learn not to present themselves that way.

The population narrows without anyone banning difference.

This is one reason algorithmic power often feels softer than bureaucratic power.

A bureaucracy says fill out this form.

An algorithmic environment says people who look like this tend to receive better outcomes.

The first feels like a rule. The second feels like advice.

Repeated often enough, advice can standardize more effectively than a rule because people experience it as self-interest.

Creators are not ordered to make thumbnails a certain way. They see what travels. Applicants are not always ordered to write in machine-readable language. They fear disappearing before human review. Sellers are not necessarily ordered to provide every structured attribute. Incomplete data may reduce eligibility, placement, or the ability of an agent to compare the product.

The institution can truthfully say participation is optional.

The opportunity may not be.

This is where legibility becomes political.

James C. Scott's work on state simplification made famous the danger of governments reducing complex social worlds into administrative representations: maps, surnames, standardized measures, cadastral surveys, categories that made populations easier to govern while often erasing local knowledge. The digital version differs in important ways. It is not only the state. Firms, marketplaces, platforms, employers, and users all participate. The representations are often personalized and adaptive rather than fixed. People themselves generate much of the data voluntarily because the systems are useful.

But the underlying tension remains recognizable.

A large institution cannot act on what it cannot see.

People who depend on the institution learn to become visible in the institution's language.

Modern machine learning adds a complication Scott's bureaucrats did not face at the same speed: the categories can be learned rather than declared.

A traditional form tells you which boxes exist.

A model can infer patterns across hundreds or thousands of features. The individual affected may not know which representation mattered. The institution may not be able to summarize the model as a stable list of boxes. The pathway to legibility becomes probabilistic.

That makes adaptation more speculative.

People infer from outcomes, creating the folk physics of the previous chapter.

The pressure to conform can therefore exist without a clear standard to conform to.

This sounds paradoxical. It is common in culture. A young professional may know there is a way successful people in an industry dress without a written dress code. A student may sense which essay voice receives praise without a rubric naming the voice. An entrepreneur may learn the style of pitch investors reward without any investor intending to produce a monoculture.

Algorithmic systems add scale to this implicit norm formation.

Millions of people can receive individually varied judgments from systems built on shared data.

The result can be convergence in some dimensions and hyper-personalization in others.

A creator may adopt the same opening convention as thousands of peers while being recommended to a highly specific audience. A merchant may use standardized product fields while targeting narrow customer segments. An applicant may write a standardized résumé for a highly personalized job match.

Sameness and personalization are not opposites.

They can reinforce each other.

The intermediary benefits from standardized inputs because standardized inputs are easier to compare. It then uses those inputs to produce individualized outputs.

Think of music streaming. Tracks arrive with standardized metadata, audio features, rights information, and histories of interaction. The service can use common representations to produce playlists that feel personal. The more standardized the catalog becomes at the machine layer, the more individualized the surface can become.

The same logic is moving into commerce. Products acquire structured identifiers, prices, availability, sizes, colors, shipping information, review data, and increasingly provenance. An AI shopping system can compare thousands of products more effectively when sellers expose these attributes consistently.

This is useful.

It also creates an incentive for products to be describable along comparable dimensions.

What happens to value that does not fit the schema?

A chair may be unusually comfortable for reasons no product field captures. A local restaurant may create an atmosphere a rating cannot represent. A teacher may be transformative in ways standardized outcomes miss. A manager may prevent organizational failures nobody knows were possible.

The answer cannot be to abandon measurement. Large systems need it.

The answer begins by remembering that legibility is lossy.

Every representation leaves something out.

A number is a compression. A category is a compression. An embedding is a compression. A résumé is a compression. A rating is a compression. A profile is a compression. A model necessarily transforms a richer world into a form usable for a particular decision.

Good system design asks whether the omitted information matters for the decision.

Bad system design forgets there was omitted information.

People adapting to the system can accelerate that forgetting because they begin organizing the world around the representation.

Consider reviews.

A five-star score starts as a compressed record of customer experience. Once the score affects seller visibility or worker standing, the service interaction changes. Drivers may ask for ratings. Hosts become strategic about guests. Restaurants respond defensively to criticism. Customers learn that anything below five can carry disproportionate consequences. Rating inflation changes what a 4.6 means. Platforms recalibrate thresholds.

The representation modifies the behavior it represents.

That is legibility drift in miniature.

Or consider school rankings. Families change behavior around the ranking. Schools change behavior around measurable inputs. Real estate prices incorporate the score. Student composition changes. The ranking now measures a population partly created by the ranking.

Performative prediction research gives this loop a formal language. A prediction can change decisions, those decisions can alter the distribution of future data, and the model can therefore change the environment in which its own accuracy is evaluated.

The mathematics is useful because it removes moral theater from the problem.

No villain is necessary.

A model can be well intentioned and statistically competent while changing the population it predicts.

People can respond rationally while degrading the signal.

Designers can update rationally while creating a moving target.

The system can become unstable through intelligence on every side.

This is one reason calls for “better algorithms” are necessary but incomplete.

A more accurate model can create stronger incentives to adapt because its decisions matter more. A more useful recommender can become a more important gate. A more efficient hiring system can cause more applicants to optimize their representation for it. A more capable shopping agent can make merchant data quality more consequential.

Competence can deepen dependence.

The danger is not that systems fail.

Sometimes it is that they work well enough to reorganize the environment around themselves.

There are several ways institutions can resist harmful drift without abandoning machine mediation.

One is plural measurement. If a job has several valuable dimensions, do not collapse them unnecessarily into one number. Another is delayed judgment. Some qualities require time. Another is human review for ambiguous cases. Another is random inspection, which makes it harder to optimize solely for predictable checks. Another is explicit room for exception, allowing people to explain why the representation is misleading.

None is free.

More dimensions create complexity. Human review creates cost and can reintroduce bias. Randomness frustrates people who want predictable rules. Exceptions can become favoritism. Slower judgment can be unbearable at scale.

There is no frictionless way to represent a complicated world.

The point is to treat the friction as real rather than as a temporary software bug.

This matters because the next generation of systems will appear more understanding than the scorecards they replace.

A language model can read prose. A multimodal model can inspect images and video. An agent can gather information across sources. The machine can become legible to more of the world, reducing the need to compress everything into a few rigid fields.

That could reduce some forms of legibility drift.

It could create others.

People will learn how the richer model interprets narrative, evidence, reputation, provenance, and style. Instead of optimizing for ten fields, they may optimize for a learned representation too complex to inspect directly. The form of the pressure changes from bureaucratic standardization to probabilistic impression management.

We know that social behavior already.

It is what people do around powerful human judges.

The difference is scale. One model can become the judge for millions of interactions.

That possibility returns us to work.

For creators, optimization can feel optional because the work can sometimes travel elsewhere. For employees and contractors, the machine can sit closer to the paycheck.

A dashboard does not need to understand a person completely.

It only needs to decide what happens on the next shift.
