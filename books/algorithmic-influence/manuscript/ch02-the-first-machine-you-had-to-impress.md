# The First Machine You Had to Impress

In the early web, a page could exist without being found.

That sounds obvious now, but it changed the meaning of publishing. Printing had always required distribution. A book needed a store, a library, a catalog, a review, a recommendation, a table near the front, a person who knew its title. The web appeared to abolish much of that machinery. Anyone could put a page online. The cost of making a copy approached zero. Distance barely mattered.

Then the number of pages became the problem.

The web did not eliminate distribution. It turned distribution into retrieval.

By the late 1990s, search engines were trying to answer a question that would become one of the governing questions of digital life: when there are far more possible things than a person can inspect, which few should appear first?

Sergey Brin and Larry Page's 1998 paper on Google described a large-scale search engine built around the insight that links could carry information about importance. PageRank treated the web's linking structure as evidence. A link from one page to another was not merely a path; in aggregate, it could function as a kind of vote, weighted by the importance of the page casting it. Google also used text, anchors, proximity, and other signals. The system was never just PageRank, and modern search is vastly more complicated. But the central design move mattered culturally as much as technically.

A machine began evaluating not only what a page said but how the rest of the web related to it.

Publishers adapted.

Some adaptations were almost embarrassingly wholesome. Give a page a descriptive title. Use language that tells readers what the page is about. Make navigation understandable. Avoid hiding important information inside formats a crawler cannot interpret. Link sensibly. Keep the site available. These are not tricks. They are ways of making a human publication legible to software that helps humans find it.

Other adaptations were less dignified.

If a search engine appeared to reward a signal, people manufactured the signal. Keyword stuffing turned prose into incantation. Invisible text was hidden on pages. Link farms manufactured relationships that looked, to a ranking system, like evidence of authority. Doorway pages were built for machines and discarded by humans. Entire businesses formed around discovering where the ranking system's simplified representation of value could be made to diverge from value itself.

The search engine responded. Publishers responded to the response.

This was one of the first mass encounters with the recursive structure of algorithmic influence.

A ranking rule was built to identify useful pages. People learned enough about the rule to change pages. The changed population reduced the usefulness of some signals. The ranking system changed. A new optimization industry studied the new system. The vocabulary evolved from crude tricks toward a broad profession called search engine optimization, a term capable of describing both careful publishing practice and industrialized manipulation depending on who was using it and how.

The ambiguity remains important.

There is a habit in discussions of algorithmic systems to place purity on one side and gaming on the other. A page should simply be good, the argument goes, and any attempt to improve how a machine evaluates it contaminates the work.

That position collapses under ordinary experience.

A librarian needs metadata. A catalog needs a title and author. A newspaper needs a headline. A database needs fields. A restaurant needs an address that a map can parse. A screen reader needs structure. A search engine cannot directly absorb the total meaning of a business, a medical practice, a recipe, a legal opinion, or a poem. Information has to acquire a form through which systems can handle it.

Making something legible is not the same as making it fraudulent.

The trouble begins because legibility affects selection.

Once a publisher knows that a field, phrase, link, structure, speed, or markup can influence discovery, the field becomes part of the publishing decision. It may still serve the human reader. It may serve both audiences at once. Or it may begin to exist principally because the machine expects it.

The easiest examples are visible in language.

Imagine a local carpenter whose customers have always called a particular service “built-ins.” Search data suggests that prospective customers more often type “custom bookshelves.” The carpenter's website changes. It does not become false. The business really does make custom bookshelves. But a machine-mediated vocabulary begins to pull the public description of the work toward the vocabulary people use when querying the machine.

Scale that effect across travel, health, finance, recipes, consumer products, news, education, entertainment, and local services. The search box does not merely retrieve culture. It sends demand signals backward into how culture names itself.

Publishers learned to ask not only what a reader would understand, but what a query might be.

This is influence in both directions. Search systems learn from the web and from users. The web learns from search systems. Users learn how to formulate queries based on what search seems able to answer. Businesses learn which terms bring people. Journalists learn which questions are being asked. Entire genres of pages appear because query volume reveals an addressable audience.

The machine becomes an instrument for observing demand and a gate through which that demand is reached.

It is difficult to overstate how normal this became. Search optimization no longer feels like a philosophical event. It feels like work.

That normality is useful because it strips away some of the drama surrounding newer systems. People did not need generative AI or addictive feeds to begin adapting to algorithmic intermediaries. They needed a machine whose ranking decisions mattered to income, attention, or access.

Search supplied one.

The relationship also exposed a structural problem that will recur throughout this book: the difference between the objective a system is designed to pursue and the signals it can actually observe.

A search engine wants, in some broad product sense, to return useful results. “Useful” cannot be read directly from the page. It has to be inferred. Links can indicate authority. Words can indicate relevance. User behavior can supply evidence. Site quality can be approximated through many features. Spam systems can identify patterns associated with manipulation. Human raters can help evaluate results and train or assess systems.

Every observable signal is incomplete.

That incompleteness creates room for legitimate representation and strategic distortion at the same time.

A physician who writes a clear page answering a common question is helping a search engine understand useful information. A content farm that produces hundreds of thin pages around every variation of the same query may be exploiting the same retrieval logic without improving anyone's understanding. Both are responding to demand expressed through the machine.

The search engine cannot resolve the distinction once and keep it resolved forever because publishers change.

This is why modern search companies publish extensive documentation while refusing to reveal a complete ranking recipe. Google describes broad ranking systems, quality principles, spam policies, structured data, and technical requirements. It also warns against practices intended primarily to manipulate rankings. A fully public, stable formula would invite people to optimize directly against the formula. A fully opaque system would make it harder for legitimate publishers to build compatible sites.

The platform lives between those pressures.

So does the publisher.

The result is a peculiar social contract. The search company says, in effect, make useful things, make them accessible to our systems, and do not confuse accessibility with a guarantee of placement. The publisher says, in effect, tell me enough about your system that I can participate without telling everyone so much that participation becomes meaningless.

Neither side gets everything it wants.

This compromise is older than the web. Standardized tests reveal enough of their structure for students to prepare but try not to reveal exact future questions. Tax authorities publish rules while guarding against schemes that technically satisfy one representation of economic activity while defeating the purpose of the law. Financial markets publish listing standards and punish manipulation. Bureaucracies provide forms because applicants need to know how to become legible.

Search accelerated the loop because the population was enormous, the stakes were commercial, and the system could be revised continuously.

It also created a new profession: people whose expertise consisted partly of understanding what a machine could see.

That expertise was never purely technical. The best practitioners had to understand information architecture, language, audience intent, reputation, publishing, business economics, and the limits of platform documentation. A technically perfect page for a question nobody cares about is not valuable. A wonderful page that cannot be crawled may be invisible. A page that ranks but disappoints readers can fail commercially even if the machine initially approves.

The optimization problem therefore became triangular.

The publisher had to satisfy the human, the machine, and the underlying business or institutional purpose.

The easiest way to fail was to let one corner consume the others.

A site built only for readers but invisible to retrieval systems could remain unknown. A site built only for rankings could attract traffic that did not trust, buy, learn, or return. A site built only for immediate conversion could degrade into a maze of interruptions that neither readers nor search systems considered useful.

This triangular problem is more durable than any specific SEO tactic.

The tactics change because the systems change. The shape remains.

Consider the history of links. In the early PageRank intuition, links carried information because making a link was a meaningful act. As publishers learned that links affected ranking, a market emerged for producing links. Once the signal could be purchased or manufactured at scale, the meaning of a link became noisier. Search systems developed methods to discount manipulative patterns. Publishers found new ones. The arms race was not a side effect of ranking. It followed from the fact that ranking distributed something valuable.

Any system that turns a measurable signal into access creates an incentive to produce the signal.

That sentence can sound cynical, but it is not universally bad.

If a marketplace ranks sellers partly on reliable shipping, sellers have an incentive to ship reliably. If a search engine rewards fast, secure, accessible sites, publishers may improve them. If a hiring system values demonstrable experience, applicants may make experience clearer. If a recommender demotes obvious spam, creators may produce less spam.

The problem is not that incentives exist.

The problem is that the signal and the thing we actually value can separate.

A search engine can measure the presence of useful-seeming structure more easily than whether the reader's life improved. A platform can count a click more easily than regret. A manager can count completed tasks more easily than quiet prevention. A hiring model can parse a credential more easily than practical judgment. A shopping agent can compare a return window more easily than the feeling of using a well-designed object every day.

When selection matters, people gradually orient toward what selection can observe.

Search gave the web decades to practice this.

It also demonstrated that the effect runs deeper than individual pages. Industries restructure around discoverability.

Travel publishers create pages for specific routes, seasons, neighborhoods, and questions because search demand is visible. Retailers create category structures that align with query language. Newsrooms watch search trends. Recipe sites standardize formats. Service businesses solicit reviews because local ranking and consumer trust reinforce each other. Product makers learn that the words used in a listing can affect whether a shopper finds the product at all.

Some of this is efficient matching. A person asks a question; a publisher notices; useful information appears.

Some of it produces a strange sameness. Search a commercial question and encounter ten pages whose headings seem to have been assembled from the same set of expected subquestions. The pages are not identical because writers lack imagination. They have been produced under a shared selection environment.

People blame “the algorithm,” but the algorithm did not write the pages.

People wrote them after observing what the system appeared to reward.

That distinction will matter later when generative systems produce even more of the material competing for selection. The machine can influence culture without generating a single sentence itself. It only has to make some sentences easier to distribute than others.

Search also taught people a second lesson: machine audiences can be valuable even when they are not end users.

No one writes a product page because a crawler deserves to be entertained. The crawler matters because of the human on the other side. This makes the machine an instrumental audience. Its approval is not the final objective. It is a condition that changes the probability of reaching the final objective.

That is the simplest definition of the phenomenon this book is trying to isolate.

Algorithmic influence is influence aimed through a machine.

The target can be a ranking system, classifier, recommender, score, auction, parser, agent, or model. The hoped-for consequence is downstream: attention, work, trust, price, access, purchase, citation, approval, or some other human outcome.

Search engine optimization fits the definition almost perfectly. The publisher changes a page partly to change how a search system evaluates it, because the search system changes which people arrive.

The word optimization can make the process sound cleaner than it is. Real publishers face conflicting goals and incomplete information. They do not maximize a known function. They make guesses under constraint.

A useful page may lose traffic after an update. A manipulative page may rank for months. A technical problem may make a strong site invisible. A new competitor may simply be better. Search demand itself may shift. A query may be absorbed into the interface so that fewer people click any result. A publisher can do everything recommended and still fail because ranking is relative.

This last point is crucial.

Algorithmic systems often create competitions without fixed passing scores.

There is no universal number of good links that guarantees first position. There is no amount of watch time that guarantees distribution. There is no résumé format that guarantees an interview. There is no seller rating that guarantees the buy box. You are evaluated in a changing field containing other people who are also adapting.

The machine is not merely an audience. It is an audience that is comparing you with a population being transformed by the same audience.

This is why optimization spreads.

If one restaurant improves its listing, nothing forces another to respond. If better listings increase discovery, competitors notice. Soon what was once a differentiator becomes ordinary hygiene. The baseline moves. The same thing happens with site speed, metadata, production quality, thumbnails, response times, résumé conventions, seller policies, and any other observable characteristic connected to distribution.

The result is not always a race to the bottom. Sometimes the baseline improvement is real.

The web became easier to navigate partly because machines demanded structure. Websites became faster partly because speed affected users and increasingly affected discovery. Security practices improved under pressure from browsers, search engines, payment systems, and consumers. Structured data made products, events, recipes, and organizations easier for software to understand.

Machine legibility can create public goods.

But it can also become compulsory.

A small publisher that declines to think about search is not making a philosophical choice in a vacuum. If most discoverability in its field runs through search, refusing machine legibility can mean refusing the audience. The freedom to ignore the intermediary depends on having another route.

This is where the old gatekeeper returns in a new form.

The early web celebrated disintermediation. You no longer needed permission to publish. That was true. It remains one of the web's greatest achievements.

Permission to exist, however, is not the same as a reasonable chance of being found.

Search engines became powerful because they solved the abundance problem created by open publishing. Their usefulness made them central. Their centrality made their ranking decisions economically consequential. That consequence made publishers adapt.

The machine became a gatekeeper because it was good at opening gates.

This paradox is worth keeping in mind. Many algorithmic intermediaries gain power by solving a real problem. The recommender rescues us from too many videos. The marketplace rescues us from too many products. The hiring system rescues an employer from too many applications. The dispatch system coordinates too many workers and requests. The answer engine rescues a user from too many documents.

Abundance produces selection. Selection produces incentives. Incentives reshape supply.

Search was the training ground.

By the time the social feed became the center of digital culture, millions of people already understood the basic bargain even if they had never called it algorithmic influence. Make something. Make it legible. Watch the ranking. Change what you made. Try again.

Social recommendation would remove one remaining comfort.

A search publisher at least knew when the audience was looking for something.

The feed would learn to decide what the audience wanted before the audience asked.
