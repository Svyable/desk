# Work for the Score

The driver sees a map before seeing a customer.

The map glows in places. A price may appear. A destination may be partially visible, fully visible, or withheld depending on the platform, market, product, and moment. There may be a timer. There may be an incentive for completing a certain number of trips. There may be a bonus attached to one zone and not another. Somewhere behind the screen, software is matching requests with available drivers while accounting for geography, estimated arrival time, demand, product rules, and other constraints.

No dispatcher speaks.

The driver still has a boss-shaped problem.

This was one of the earliest reasons scholars became interested in algorithmic management. In their 2016 study of Uber drivers, Alex Rosenblat and Luke Stark described an employment-like environment organized through information asymmetries and algorithmic systems even though drivers were formally independent contractors. A year earlier, Min Kyung Lee and colleagues had studied how drivers for Uber and Lyft made sense of algorithmic assignment and pricing, finding that people attributed agency to systems they could only partially understand and developed strategies around them.

The details of ride-hailing platforms have changed repeatedly since those studies. The underlying problem has spread.

Software increasingly participates in work that used to belong to supervisors: assigning tasks, forecasting demand, measuring activity, recommending schedules, tracking performance, flagging anomalies, setting incentives, evaluating quality, and sometimes triggering discipline.

The phrase algorithmic management can sound as if a robot has replaced the manager.

That is usually wrong.

Human beings choose the business model, define policies, configure software, set goals, respond to regulations, negotiate with labor, design incentive programs, and decide which systems are deployed. Managers still make judgments. Customers supply ratings. Workers coordinate with one another. The algorithm is embedded in an organization.

Yet from the worker's side, the interface can become the most immediate expression of managerial power.

The app tells you where the work is.

The dashboard tells you how you are doing.

The score tells you whether you are safe.

The schedule tells you when you are needed.

The notification tells you that your behavior has been noticed.

This changes influence because the worker begins managing not only the job but the representation of the job.

A human supervisor can observe context. Not perfectly, and not always fairly, but context is available in principle. The employee can explain that the shipment was delayed because a machine failed, that the customer changed the requirements, that the patient needed unusual care, that the task prevented a larger problem, that training a new colleague reduced output this morning and improved the team this afternoon.

A dashboard sees what has been instrumented.

When the dashboard affects consequences, the employee has reason to make work visible to it.

This can be as harmless as closing tickets correctly.

It can become stranger.

Workers learn which states stop a timer. They learn when to scan. They learn which tasks are counted separately and which disappear inside another task. They learn how breaks are represented. They learn what the system interprets as idle. They learn whether rejecting an assignment carries a cost. They learn the difference between work that improves the customer's outcome and work that improves the metric through which the company observes that outcome.

The distinction is not evidence of laziness.

It is evidence of intelligence under measurement.

People have always learned what supervisors care about. The difference is that digital systems can turn that adaptation into continuous microbehavior.

A salesperson once knew the quarterly target. Now the CRM can expose calls, emails, meetings, pipeline stages, response times, forecast confidence, and activity by day. A warehouse once knew units shipped. Now scanners can associate actions with individuals and minutes. A call center once knew average call duration. Modern systems can combine handling time, hold time, customer sentiment, resolution, schedule adherence, and quality monitoring.

More information can produce better management.

It can also produce more surfaces to manage.

The OECD's 2025 work on algorithmic management surveyed managers across thousands of establishments in several countries and found that tools for instructing, monitoring, and evaluating workers had moved well beyond platform labor. Managers reported benefits, including improved decision-making in some contexts, alongside concerns about accountability, explainability, and effects on workers.

That mixed picture is more believable than either extreme.

A software scheduler can be better than a manager who rewards friends with desirable shifts. A routing system can reduce wasted miles. A matching system can connect a worker to demand without a dispatcher maintaining personal relationships. A structured performance process can expose a manager whose evaluations are wildly inconsistent.

Automation can remove discretion that was never benevolent.

The question is what replaces it.

A worker dealing with a bad human manager can at least identify the source of the judgment. A worker dealing with an opaque system may experience consequences without knowing which signal mattered, whether the result was automated, or whom to persuade that the representation is wrong.

The old workplace skill of managing up becomes managing sideways into the system.

Sometimes workers do this collectively.

Ride-share drivers have long exchanged observations about airport queues, surge zones, destination filters, bonuses, acceptance behavior, and platform changes. Warehouse workers compare rates. Delivery workers compare dispatch patterns. Freelancers on labor platforms learn how profiles, completion records, response times, and ratings affect access to work.

These communities perform the same folk physics seen among creators.

They are reverse engineering consequence.

The stakes are different because visibility is not applause. It is income.

A creator can interpret a low-view post as a disappointment. A worker can interpret a low allocation of work as a missing shift.

This economic immediacy changes the ethics of opacity.

Platforms have legitimate reasons not to disclose every anti-fraud rule or matching detail. Employers cannot make every scheduling model fully manipulable. A system that publishes a simple rule may invite people to produce the rule rather than the underlying value.

Workers have an equally legitimate interest in understanding how decisions affecting livelihoods are made.

The conflict cannot be solved by saying the algorithm is proprietary.

Nor can it be solved by publishing source code that few affected workers could interpret and that may not explain a personalized decision anyway.

The useful unit is often not transparency but contestability.

Can the worker identify that a consequential decision occurred? Can the worker know the broad reasons? Can obvious errors be corrected? Is there a human route for unusual cases? Can workers or their representatives participate when monitoring changes? Is the metric stable enough to plan around? Are sanctions proportional to the confidence of the system?

These questions sound procedural because procedural rights matter most when substantive rules are complicated.

A worker rarely needs to know every parameter of a dispatch model.

The worker needs to know whether declining an unsafe assignment will threaten access to future work.

This is where algorithmic influence becomes easy to confuse with obedience.

A worker who changes behavior around a score may simply be complying with a known standard. If the company says deliveries must be attempted within a window and the worker meets the window, nothing mysterious has happened.

Algorithmic influence appears when the worker goes beyond explicit rules to shape how the system interprets performance.

The driver waits in one location because the app seems to favor it.

The courier accepts an unattractive task because rejecting it appears risky.

The customer-service representative chooses a status that better represents active work to the monitoring system.

The warehouse worker sequences tasks to preserve a rate.

The salesperson logs activity in a way that keeps a dashboard healthy.

The employee learns the machine's language.

Some adaptations make the organization more accurate. If workers consistently use statuses badly because the taxonomy is confusing, changing behavior improves the data. Some adaptations reveal a badly designed metric. If employees need to create artificial activity to avoid appearing idle while doing real work, the system is measuring the representation rather than the job.

This distinction can be difficult for management to detect because the metric looks better after adaptation.

A team may become excellent at appearing productive.

Organizations have always faced this problem, but algorithmic management can hide it behind technical sophistication. A predictive model built from hundreds of variables can still learn from proxies that employees know how to produce. More features do not eliminate strategic response. They can make the response harder to diagnose.

The worker is part of the data-generating process.

That fact is obvious and surprisingly easy to forget.

A productivity system is not measuring a passive natural phenomenon. It is measuring people who know, at least approximately, that they are being measured.

The Hawthorne effect is often invoked too casually, but the broader point is sturdy: observation can change behavior. When observation carries rewards or penalties, the incentive to change is stronger.

Digital systems make observation persistent.

The old supervisor eventually went home.

The software may not.

This persistence changes how workers experience time. If every period can become evidence, downtime can feel suspicious even when downtime is part of good work. A maintenance technician waiting for a failure is valuable because the technician is available. A security team with no incident may be succeeding. A nurse sitting with a frightened family may be doing work that a task system poorly records.

Utilization is not the same as value.

Yet utilization is easier to measure.

The drift toward visible activity can therefore produce brittle organizations. Everyone looks busy. Nobody has slack. Every minute is allocated. Then an unusual event arrives and there is no capacity to absorb it.

This is not an argument against monitoring.

It is an argument for remembering what work contains that monitoring cannot see.

The strongest algorithmic-management systems are likely to be those that treat measurement as a sensor rather than a verdict. A sensor can detect a pattern worth investigating. It should not automatically collapse context where context changes the meaning of the signal.

This becomes harder at platform scale.

A ride-hailing company cannot personally investigate every ordinary matching decision. A marketplace cannot manually review every routine quality signal. Automation is the only way the service can operate at its size.

Scale creates the need for machine judgment and then makes human exception handling expensive.

That economic fact sits underneath many debates about algorithmic fairness. People ask for human review because humans can hear context. Firms automate because context is costly to hear millions of times.

There is no moral slogan that makes the cost disappear.

The design question is where context is worth buying.

High-stakes sanctions deserve more. Routine low-stakes assignments can tolerate more automation. Systems should be calibrated not only for predictive accuracy but for the cost of being wrong and the reversibility of the decision.

Workers understand this intuitively.

A strange dispatch is annoying.

A mistaken deactivation is existential.

When the same interface produces both, the worker's relationship to the system changes. Every metric can feel like a threat because the boundary between ordinary optimization and account standing is unclear.

This is one reason ratings carry so much power in platform work.

A rating looks like customer feedback. It can also become an input to access.

The customer may believe four stars means good. The worker may know that repeated fours can be dangerous in a system where the average is compressed near the top. The same interaction is therefore experienced under two rating scales: the consumer's intuitive scale and the platform's institutional scale.

Workers adapt by trying to influence the rating.

They offer water, change music, adjust conversation, send messages, avoid certain customers, ask explicitly for five stars, or absorb mistreatment they might otherwise challenge. The exact behaviors vary by platform and occupation. The logic is stable.

The worker is performing for the customer and the score produced from the customer.

The score then influences the system that influences future customers.

Three parties become four.

Customer, worker, rating mechanism, allocation system.

This is why algorithmic influence is not simply “people gaming algorithms.”

The worker is often trying to produce a faithful representation of good work inside a system that compresses the work aggressively.

If a customer gives a low rating because the restaurant forgot an item sealed before the courier arrived, the courier may bear a signal unrelated to courier performance. If a rider dislikes surge pricing the driver did not set, the driver can receive the emotional residue. If a buyer misunderstands a product description written by a merchant, a fulfillment worker may still encounter the complaint downstream.

Machine systems inherit noisy human judgments.

Then people optimize around the noise because the institution treats it as data.

This is not new either. Performance reviews have always contained noise. Customer complaints have always been unfair sometimes. The machine changes the persistence and portability of the judgment.

A bad interaction used to end when the customer left.

A rating follows.

That persistence can protect future customers. It can also turn every encounter into a small reputational negotiation.

There is another asymmetry worth noticing.

Employers and platforms can often observe workers more deeply than workers can observe the system.

The company sees large populations, experiments, demand patterns, acceptance behavior, performance distributions, fraud signals, and economic outcomes. The individual sees a phone.

This information asymmetry means the two sides learn at different speeds.

A platform can discover that a particular incentive changes driver supply across a city. A driver can only discover what happened to one household's income.

The company can run randomized experiments. The worker lives inside them.

This does not make experimentation illegitimate. Digital services improve through testing. But it creates a governance question when the subject of the experiment depends economically on the system.

What degree of notice, participation, or protection is appropriate when the interface is also a workplace?

Traditional employment law was built around identifiable employers, workplaces, supervisors, hours, and contractual relationships. Platform labor complicated those categories. Algorithmic management inside ordinary firms complicates them again because the software layer can spread without changing employment status at all.

The future of the issue is therefore not confined to gig workers.

It is the ordinary employee opening a dashboard on Monday morning.

The dashboard may be genuinely helpful. It may show a problem before a supervisor notices. It may give the employee evidence to argue for a promotion. It may make expectations clearer and reduce office politics.

It may also teach the employee that invisible work does not count.

People learn that lesson quickly.

Once learned, it travels into what they choose to do.

A senior employee stops mentoring because mentorship hurts measured throughput. A nurse documents defensively because the record has become as important as the encounter. An engineer picks tasks that generate visible artifacts. A salesperson overfills the CRM. A driver works the incentive map instead of the city.

The organization then looks at the data and sees the workforce it helped create.

This is the recursive heart of algorithmic management.

The model is not outside work.

It becomes part of work.

Workers act in response to it. Their response becomes data. Management changes the model. Workers adapt again.

A good system can use this loop for learning. If a metric creates perverse behavior, designers can change it. Workers can identify edge cases. Managers can add guardrails. The organization can become more intelligent through feedback.

A bad system mistakes adaptation for proof that the metric was right.

Everyone is hitting the number, therefore the number must capture performance.

Sometimes the number is simply what people learned to hit.

The difference is hard to see from the dashboard.

It becomes even harder when the score is supplied by customers, because customer judgment carries moral authority. If people rate the experience highly, who can argue?

The worker can.

The customer thought five stars meant delight.

The worker knew five stars meant continued access to work.

A reputation system sits between those meanings and turns a tiny gesture on a screen into an input for the future.

To understand algorithmic influence at its most intimate, we need to look at what happens when ordinary people are recruited as evaluators for the machine.
