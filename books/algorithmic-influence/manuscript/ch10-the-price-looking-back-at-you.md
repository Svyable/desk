# The Price Looking Back at You

A price used to sit on the object.

That is not literally true. Haggling is ancient. Airlines have changed fares by time, route, seat inventory, and demand for decades. Hotels vary rates. Insurance prices risk. Financial markets reprice continuously. Coupons have always divided customers into groups according to who notices them and who bothers to use them.

But the sticker carried a powerful social intuition: the seller names a price, and buyers decide whether to accept it.

Digital markets loosen that intuition.

The seller can observe more about the buyer before deciding what to show.

A website may know the referring page, approximate location, device, account history, browsing behavior, previous purchases, loyalty status, time of day, and whether the person arrived through an advertisement or direct search. Advertising systems can infer interests and conversion probabilities from enormous data sets. Retailers can segment offers. Platforms can auction attention in milliseconds. Travel systems can respond to changing inventory and demand.

The price begins to look back.

This phrase should not be mistaken for a claim that every person receives a secret individual price calculated from intimate personal data. That would be a better thriller than description of most commerce.

Many markets still use common posted prices. Many forms of personalization affect which promotion, advertisement, ranking, or product is shown rather than changing the nominal price itself. Even when prices vary, the cause may be ordinary dynamic pricing based on supply and demand rather than a model of one individual customer's willingness to pay.

The distinction matters because personalized pricing sits at the intersection of two anxieties that are easy to exaggerate: surveillance and discrimination.

The evidence is already concrete enough without exaggeration.

In January 2025, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission released initial staff findings from what it called a surveillance pricing study. The agency had used compulsory orders to examine firms providing pricing products and services and reported that retailers could use a wide range of personal information, including location, demographics, browsing, shopping history, and other behavioral signals, in pricing and promotional strategies. On August 31, 2026, the FTC sought comment on a draft enforcement policy statement addressing personalized pricing practices.

Those documents do not establish that every firm uses every type of data or that individualized prices dominate retail.

They establish something more modest and more important for this book: the technical and commercial machinery for turning behavioral data into differentiated offers is real enough to require regulatory attention.

Once behavior can affect the offer, consumers enter the same feedback loop as creators and workers.

They may not know they are in it.

A creator deliberately studies analytics. A driver deliberately watches incentives. A shopper may simply browse.

That browsing becomes evidence.

The system learns what attracts attention, what converts, how long the person waits, which categories are revisited, whether a discount triggers purchase, whether a basket is abandoned, which advertisement produces a return, and perhaps how the person resembles others.

The consumer influences the machine by behaving.

The machine influences the consumer by changing the environment built from that behavior.

This is algorithmic influence without conscious strategy.

It raises an important distinction for the rest of the book: influence can be intentional on one side and incidental on the other.

A retailer intentionally uses a model to decide which offer to display. The consumer may unintentionally supply the data that shapes the model. Once consumers become aware of personalization, some begin acting strategically too.

People clear cookies. They browse in private windows. They compare prices across devices. They abandon carts hoping for a coupon. They wait for sales. They use loyalty accounts selectively. They change location settings. They ask friends what price appeared. Entire travel rituals formed around the belief that repeated flight searches raise fares, a belief often repeated more confidently than the evidence warrants.

The folklore follows the stakes.

If a consumer thinks the system is estimating willingness to pay, the rational response is to look less willing.

This is bargaining without a human negotiator.

Traditional bargaining is explicit. The seller asks one hundred. The buyer offers seventy. Each side reads the other. A personalized digital market can move some of that inference into software. The buyer's history becomes part of the negotiation even if no one labels it negotiation.

This possibility unsettles people because a posted-price market carries a norm of equal treatment.

We accept some variation readily. A student discount is public. A loyalty reward is expected. A matinee costs less than a Saturday night. An airline seat can cost more the day before departure because scarcity changed. A coupon rewards search effort.

We react differently when two similar people are shown different economic opportunities for reasons neither can inspect.

The discomfort is partly about fairness and partly about information asymmetry.

The seller may know far more about the buyer than the buyer knows about the seller's decision process.

This echoes the worker-platform relationship from Chapter 7. One side observes a population; the other experiences a single outcome.

The retailer can test discounts across millions of sessions and estimate response. The individual shopper sees one price.

The platform can learn elasticity.

The shopper has an anecdote.

Algorithmic markets therefore create a new form of bargaining power from statistical scale.

That power is not absolute. Consumers compare across retailers. Regulators intervene. Competitors undercut. Price transparency tools appear. Reputation matters. A seller that extracts too aggressively can lose trust.

Markets push back.

But the asymmetry changes what firms can try.

Advertising provides a mature example.

Online ad auctions already decide which message to show a particular user under a particular context based on predicted value. Advertisers bid for outcomes, platforms estimate relevance and conversion, budgets pace across time, and users generate the behavior that updates future predictions.

The advertiser is not simply persuading the customer.

The advertiser is persuading the auction that the customer is worth reaching.

This is algorithmic influence in industrial form.

A marketer changes creative, targeting, bids, landing pages, conversion events, catalog data, and campaign structure partly to alter how the platform's delivery system allocates impressions.

The machine is not a passive pipe.

It is an active market maker for attention.

The advertiser's optimization changes the population of messages users see. Users react. The reaction becomes data. The auction learns. Advertisers adapt again.

This is so normal in digital advertising that the conceptual novelty disappears inside professional vocabulary: cost per acquisition, return on ad spend, lookalike audiences, conversion optimization, bid strategies.

The language makes it sound like bookkeeping.

It is a social allocation system deciding which persuasion attempt gets access to which person.

The economic significance is enormous because advertising finances much of the consumer internet.

It also demonstrates why machine intermediaries do not need to be explicitly “persuaded” to become objects of strategy.

An advertiser does not flatter the ad auction. It supplies signals the auction can use.

A strong conversion history may cause a campaign to receive different delivery. Better structured product data may improve matching. A new creative may attract a population the system predicts will respond. The advertiser shapes inputs in order to shape allocation.

That is persuasion translated into machine terms.

Pricing systems extend the logic from attention to value extraction.

Suppose a retailer estimates that one customer is highly price sensitive and another is less so. The retailer could respond in several ways. It might offer the first customer a discount. It might advertise a cheaper product. It might withhold a promotion from the second. It might simply change which products are ranked most prominently. These mechanisms have different legal, ethical, and economic implications, but from the consumer's perspective they share one feature: the environment is no longer independent of the profile.

The person sees a market partially built for that person.

This can improve welfare.

A discount targeted to someone who otherwise would not buy can create a transaction beneficial to both sides. Personalization can help people discover cheaper alternatives. Dynamic pricing can allocate scarce capacity and smooth demand. Airlines would waste seats if every fare stayed fixed regardless of departure date and load. Electricity systems increasingly need prices that reflect time-dependent scarcity. Ride-hailing surge prices can attract supply when demand spikes.

Variable price is not exploitation by definition.

The hard questions are about which variables are used, who understands them, whether protected or sensitive characteristics enter directly or by proxy, whether consumers can compare offers, and whether the system exploits behavioral vulnerabilities rather than ordinary willingness to pay.

A person desperately searching for emergency plumbing at two in the morning is willing to pay more for reasons different from a luxury shopper. Should a system infer urgency and charge accordingly? Markets have always done some version of this through scarcity and after-hours fees. Personal data can make the inference more precise and less visible.

Precision changes the moral texture.

The seller no longer needs to create a public category called emergency service. The model can identify people who behave like urgent buyers.

This is where privacy and pricing become inseparable.

Data collected for one purpose can become economically useful for another. Browsing patterns collected to improve a site can help predict conversion. Location can indicate delivery cost and socioeconomic context. Device characteristics can correlate with purchasing power. Loyalty data can reveal habits. Demographics can aid segmentation.

A model does not need to “know” a sensitive fact in human terms to use correlated signals.

The distinction between knowing and inferring becomes thin when the output changes the offer.

Regulation often responds by focusing on disclosures, unfair or deceptive practices, discrimination, data minimization, and sector-specific rules. The policy landscape is still developing. It should be.

The technology makes it easy to confuse capability with legitimacy.

A firm may be technically able to infer a person's urgency, income, health concern, or life event. That does not answer whether the inference should affect price.

The most interesting effect for this book appears before law resolves the boundary.

People who believe they are being profiled begin managing the profile.

That behavior can make data less truthful.

A shopper who abandons carts strategically looks like a shopper who genuinely lost interest. A user who searches anonymously looks like a new user. A customer who manipulates location complicates logistics. A business traveler shopping from a personal device can look like a leisure traveler. The system then adapts to a population containing strategic signals.

Performative prediction arrives again.

A model changes behavior. The changed behavior changes the model's future evidence.

Markets have always contained this recursion. A price changes demand; changed demand changes price. A credit score changes access to credit; access changes future credit history. An insurance premium changes behavior; behavior changes risk.

Algorithmic systems make the recursion more individualized and data-rich.

That creates opportunities for what might be called machine-facing negotiation.

A consumer can use an agent to search across sellers, delay purchase, compare offers, bundle demand, or hide unnecessary personal information. If sellers gain automated pricing intelligence, buyers can gain automated purchasing intelligence.

The balance of power may shift again.

This is one reason agentic commerce is more interesting than a faster checkout button. A shopping agent acting for the consumer can change the amount of strategic attention the consumer brings to every transaction. It can compare total cost, return policy, delivery time, quality, and price across many sellers without becoming tired.

The seller then has a new audience.

Not just the person.

The person's agent.

Before reaching that future, we should notice what pricing has already taught us.

Machine mediation changes influence even when no one is trying to create a viral post or game a score.

A person can influence a model simply by generating behavior.

The model can then change the choices presented to the person.

This makes ordinary consumption part of a learning system.

Every click can become evidence. Every purchase can become evidence. Every refusal can become evidence. The evidence is valuable because it improves prediction.

Prediction is valuable because it improves allocation.

Allocation changes the next behavior.

The loop is economically productive. It is also difficult to escape.

A consumer can refuse one platform. The broader economy still contains loyalty systems, recommendation engines, credit models, advertising auctions, fraud systems, and dynamic prices. Machine interpretation increasingly sits underneath the transaction even when the front end looks like an ordinary store.

This raises a question of agency.

If people do not know which behavior is consequential, can they meaningfully influence the system on their own terms?

The answer may require reducing the need for strategic behavior rather than teaching consumers how to perform invisibility.

Clear disclosure can help. Limits on sensitive data can help. Competition helps. Auditable practices help. Public categories are easier to contest than hidden inference. Agents acting for consumers may help restore bargaining symmetry.

None creates a world without differentiated prices.

The goal is not to freeze markets.

It is to prevent the information advantage from becoming so large that the consumer enters every transaction as the least informed participant about the price being offered to them.

There is a political analogy.

A voter sees one message.

A campaign and platform can see populations.

The campaign learns which message travels through which channel. The platform learns which users interact with which political material. Journalists and activists watch the results. Everyone adapts.

The difference is that the thing being allocated is not a discount.

It is political attention.
