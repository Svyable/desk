# Folk Physics

Nobody in the room has seen the machine.

They talk about it anyway.

A creator says posting twice in one day suppresses the second video. Someone else says the opposite. A third person says the platform rewards consistency but punishes predictability. A fourth says deleting a weak post harms the account. Screenshots arrive. A graph is interpreted. Somebody knows somebody whose representative at the platform said the real issue is completion rate. Another creator with a larger account insists completion is overrated. An agency posts a thread. A consultant makes a video explaining why everyone else is wrong.

The machine itself remains elsewhere.

This is not stupidity.

It is what people do when an invisible system has visible consequences.

Sophie Bishop's phrase “algorithmic gossip” captured one version of the practice among YouTube creators. Taina Bucher's work on the “algorithmic imaginary” captured another: people form ideas about what algorithms are, how they work, and what they want from lived encounters with their effects. Kelley Cotter described influencers “playing the visibility game,” developing tactics in an environment where platform rules, technological systems, and professional aspirations intersect.

The terminology differs. The human behavior is old.

Sailors inferred weather before meteorology. Farmers read soil, clouds, insects, and seasons. Traders invent stories about markets. Office workers infer what a boss really rewards from who gets promoted. Children learn a teacher's grading style. Citizens learn how a bureaucracy treats forms by listening to people who survived it.

People build local theories of systems they cannot fully observe.

A local theory does not need to be elegant. It needs to help decide what to do next.

That is folk physics.

The phrase is not meant to insult the people doing it. Folk physics is what allows a child to catch a ball without solving differential equations. Human beings are remarkably good at acting inside complicated systems using compressed, approximate models. We learn that wet pavement is slippery, that heavy doors carry momentum, that a pan stays hot after the burner is turned off. The rules are incomplete. They often work.

Algorithmic systems create the same practical demand under stranger conditions.

The object being inferred can change while it is being inferred.

A creator tests a posting strategy in March. The recommendation system is updated in April. Audience behavior changes in May. A new format arrives in June. Competitors adapt in July. By August, the March rule may still sound authoritative because the tutorial explaining it has accumulated a million views.

The advice has become content inside the environment it claims to decode.

This is one reason algorithmic folklore spreads so efficiently. Advice about algorithms is itself rewarded or suppressed by algorithms.

The subject has built-in drama. People want an explanation for volatile outcomes. “Your work met a changing audience through a complex stochastic system with many unobserved variables” is accurate but emotionally useless. “The algorithm hates this” gives the uncertainty a face.

A face is easier to negotiate with.

Creators often speak about platforms as if the system has moods. It wants consistency. It notices a hiatus. It is confused by a niche change. It rewards loyalty. It punishes links. The anthropomorphism can sound naive from outside the work, especially to engineers who know that no module in the stack contains a variable labeled jealousy.

But the creator is describing an institutional relationship, not writing technical documentation.

Employees do the same thing when they say “finance won't let us” or “legal hates that” or “the market wants growth.” A corporation does not possess one mind called finance. The shorthand refers to recurring consequences produced by a collection of people, rules, incentives, and processes.

“The algorithm” functions similarly.

The practical question is not whether the metaphor is technically pure.

It is what the metaphor causes people to do.

Suppose a false belief spreads that a platform rewards a particular video length. Enough creators may begin producing that length for the belief to affect supply. Audiences become more accustomed to the format. Editing tools add templates. Agencies recommend the standard. Advertisers request it. The platform observes increased production and user response around the length.

A false description can become a real cultural force.

This possibility makes algorithmic folklore unusually consequential. Beliefs do not merely describe the selection environment. They can alter it.

The effect is not guaranteed. Millions of creators can follow bad advice and fail. Platforms can deliberately counter manipulation. Audiences can reject homogenized material. A system's ranking logic may be insensitive to the supposed trick. But when participation is large enough, collective adaptation becomes part of the environment the model later sees.

The folk theory enters the data.

This is where ordinary superstition becomes performative.

Economists have long known that expectations can alter outcomes. If enough depositors believe a bank will fail and withdraw at once, their belief can help produce the failure. Financial markets react to beliefs about other people's beliefs. Sociologists have studied self-fulfilling prophecies. The newer machine-learning literature on performative prediction formalizes a related problem: deploying a predictive model can change the population on which future predictions are made.

The creator economy supplies a messy cultural version.

A system ranks. Creators infer the rule. Creators change. The system sees the changed population. Designers and models adapt. New creator theories form around the new consequences.

Nobody is standing still long enough to be measured cleanly.

This makes the promise of “cracking the algorithm” suspect on its face.

A crack implies a fixed object and a hidden code. Many modern ranking systems are neither. They can contain learned representations, multiple models, hand-built rules, experiments, safety layers, personalized predictions, freshness constraints, diversity interventions, and business policies. Two users can receive different results because the system is supposed to treat them differently. A technique that appears powerful on one account may be irrelevant on another because the audience, content, and competition differ.

Even insiders may not possess the simple answer outsiders imagine exists.

A machine-learning engineer can understand a model architecture without knowing why one creator's video succeeded yesterday. A product manager can know an objective without knowing the weights learned for a particular item-user pair. An executive can know the business goals without being able to predict the next ranking decision.

Opacity is not always secrecy.

Sometimes the system is genuinely too complicated to compress into a rule a person could use.

This matters for governance. Demanding that a platform “tell us the algorithm” can be like asking a weather service to tell us the atmosphere. Source code, training data, objective functions, model weights, experiments, and operational rules are all forms of information, but none alone is equivalent to a comprehensible explanation of outcomes.

People affected by the system still need something better than mystery.

That creates a difficult design problem: what explanation is both truthful and useful?

Platforms often respond with broad guidance. Make original material. Avoid spam. Use accurate metadata. Follow community rules. Publish for people. Create high-quality content. These principles may be sensible and sincere. They are also frustrating for someone trying to explain a forty-percent decline in distribution this week.

The creator wants a local causal answer.

The platform can usually offer a general behavioral one.

The gap is filled by folk physics.

There are several recurring sources.

The first is personal experimentation. A creator changes a variable and observes what happens. This is the most respected form because it feels empirical. It is also easy to overinterpret. The experiment may not be randomized. The content differs in ways the creator did not notice. The audience changed. The sample is small. Success is noisy. The platform may be running its own experiment simultaneously.

The second is peer comparison. Creators exchange results. A pattern seen across many accounts feels stronger. Peer evidence can identify real shifts quickly, especially when an obvious product change occurs. It can also suffer from selection bias. People with surprising outcomes post. People whose accounts behaved normally remain silent.

The third is platform communication. Documentation, creator representatives, engineering posts, product announcements, and policy pages provide authoritative information. But they usually describe what the platform wants users to know, not a complete mechanistic account. A representative may know policy well and ranking details poorly. Documentation can lag deployment.

The fourth is commercial interpretation. Agencies, consultants, analytics companies, and tool vendors aggregate information and sell advice. Some develop genuine expertise. Others turn uncertainty into a business model. The customer often cannot distinguish them until after paying.

The fifth is reverse engineering from success. People inspect viral or highly ranked material and infer shared characteristics. This can be useful for learning form. It is terrible at revealing the invisible failures that had the same characteristics.

Survivorship bias acquires an interface.

The sixth is rumor.

Rumor deserves more respect than it gets. In opaque systems, rumor can be an early-warning network. Drivers learn about changes to dispatch before formal notices arrive. Sellers notice enforcement shifts. creators observe a sudden pattern. Workers share screenshots of dashboards. What institutions dismiss as gossip can contain distributed observation.

The problem is that rumor and evidence travel through the same channel.

A community can detect a real change and invent three imaginary ones around it.

This creates a peculiar epistemic burden for people whose livelihoods depend on platforms. They have to remain skeptical without becoming passive. If they wait for proof, the opportunity may pass. If they chase every rumor, they can destroy the thing that made their work distinct.

The best practitioners develop a kind of calibrated superstition.

They hold rules lightly. They distinguish platform facts from working hypotheses. They look for repeated patterns rather than one success. They care about the human audience because human response is one of the few stable anchors in a shifting system. They understand that tactics decay.

This is not very satisfying to sell as a course.

It is closer to reality.

Folk physics appears outside creator culture whenever people experience machine allocation without complete explanation.

Ride-share drivers learn where the app seems to send work. They infer how acceptance, cancellation, location, timing, bonuses, and demand interact. Warehouse workers learn which actions move the productivity number. Sellers learn which customer complaints matter most to account standing. Job applicants exchange advice about parsers and automated assessments. Landlords learn which listing features increase ranking. Restaurants learn how reservation systems react to cancellations and availability.

In every case, the affected person forms a behavioral model from feedback.

The model may be accurate enough to matter without being accurate enough to explain.

This distinction is important because institutions often defend opaque systems by pointing out that users' folk theories are wrong.

Sometimes they are.

That does not resolve the institutional problem.

If a driver incorrectly believes declining a trip lowers future assignment quality, the platform can say the belief is false. If the driver continues to experience assignment as unpredictable and economically consequential, the underlying need for a workable model remains. Correcting one rumor does not create legibility.

The same is true for creators. A platform can deny a specific myth while leaving the broader distribution system opaque. The denial is useful. The uncertainty survives.

There is a symmetry here worth noticing.

Platforms build statistical models of users.

Users build folk models of platforms.

The platform's model may predict that a viewer will watch a video. The creator's model predicts that the platform will predict that.

This is second-order behavior.

A merchant does not merely ask what the customer wants. The merchant asks what the recommendation system believes the customer wants. An advertiser asks what the auction will infer about conversion. A political communicator asks what the feed is likely to distribute to which group. An applicant asks what the screen will treat as relevant to the employer.

The person acts through a model of a model.

Once generative agents become intermediaries, the chain can lengthen again. A seller may ask what a shopping agent will infer from structured product data about what a user wants. The seller may never interact with the user directly.

This layered prediction changes the social meaning of expertise.

Traditionally, expertise meant understanding the underlying domain: medicine, law, music, woodworking, retail, logistics, journalism. Digital expertise increasingly includes understanding the representational layer through which the domain is evaluated.

A brilliant physician can be difficult to find online. A wonderful restaurant can be badly represented in a map system. A skilled worker can look mediocre on a dashboard. An experienced applicant can be poorly parsed. A durable product can have incomplete attributes.

The gap creates work for intermediaries who know how to translate value into machine-legible evidence.

Some of that work is useful translation.

Some is theater for the machine.

The boundary can move as the system learns.

Consider search once again. Early keyword manipulation worked because simple lexical signals were useful and exploitable. Search systems improved. Crude manipulation became less effective. Publishers developed more sophisticated strategies around authority, structure, intent, and quality. Spam systems improved again. The optimization industry did not disappear. It evolved alongside the selector.

The same pattern is likely wherever the economic prize is large enough.

A static rule invites gaming. An adaptive model invites adaptive gaming. A model trained on adaptations can make yesterday's strategy obsolete.

This does not mean the platform always wins. Strategic behavior can expose blind spots. Users can coordinate. Competitors can build alternatives. Regulators can force disclosures. Researchers can audit. Workers can reverse engineer enough of a system to reclaim some autonomy.

Influence remains contested.

The most interesting struggle is often not over the secret formula but over what counts as legitimate adaptation.

A creator who changes a title to describe a video accurately is adapting. A creator who fabricates controversy to trigger engagement is also adapting. A seller who provides complete product data is adapting. A seller who manufactures reviews is gaming or fraud. An applicant who rewrites experience in the employer's vocabulary is adapting. An applicant who invents credentials is lying.

Machine mediation does not erase moral distinctions.

It creates new surfaces on which old distinctions have to be redrawn.

The platform participates by defining which behaviors it treats as spam, manipulation, abuse, quality, authenticity, or ordinary optimization. Those definitions are not neutral because they determine whose adaptations remain viable.

A system can therefore govern culture partly by governing optimization.

It decides which attempts to influence it are allowed.

This is especially visible when platforms punish coordinated manipulation, fake engagement, deceptive metadata, review fraud, or automation. The platform is defending the usefulness of its signals from people trying to manufacture them.

The people being ranked are defending their access to opportunity from a system they cannot fully see.

Both sides have legitimate interests.

The conflict is structural.

As soon as a proxy distributes value, producing the proxy becomes valuable. As soon as producing the proxy becomes widespread, the institution has to distinguish genuine evidence from manufactured evidence. As soon as the institution changes its methods, participants need new theories.

Folk physics returns.

This recursive dance has an effect that is easier to see at population scale than individual scale.

Over time, people become more like what they believe the machine can recognize.

They write clearer titles, shorter openings, more standardized résumés, more complete listings, faster responses, more frequent updates, more measurable work. Some changes improve the underlying thing. Others merely improve the representation. Most contain a mixture.

The population drifts toward legibility.

And once legibility affects who gets selected, being difficult for the machine to understand stops being a neutral characteristic.

It becomes a disadvantage.
