# Campaigning for the Recommender

A campaign speech used to have an obvious audience.

The people in the room mattered. The cameras mattered because television would carry parts of the speech elsewhere. Reporters mattered because tomorrow's stories might decide which lines survived. Party officials mattered. Donors mattered. Opponents mattered.

The speaker was already addressing several audiences at once.

Social media added another.

A sentence can now be written partly for the voter who hears it and partly for the distribution system that decides whether millions of other voters will encounter it at all.

This does not make modern politics uniquely artificial. Political communication has always adapted to media. Radio rewarded the voice. Television rewarded the face. The thirty-second advertisement created its own grammar. Cable news rewarded confrontation. Campaigns learned the evening news cutoff, the newspaper deadline, the direct-mail envelope, the fundraising subject line.

Media shape messages because messages have to survive media.

Algorithmic feeds intensify the old relationship by making distribution personalized, measurable, and adaptive.

A campaign can publish a clip and watch where it travels. Supporters can recut it. Opponents can quote it. Influencers can react. The platform can recommend any of those versions to people who do not follow the original account. Comments, shares, watch behavior, searches, and social connections generate more evidence about where the material resonates.

The political message becomes an input to a learning system.

The campaign learns too.

A clip that spreads widely becomes evidence about form and topic. Staff make more clips. Consultants inspect retention and engagement. Candidates learn which lines reliably produce shareable moments. Journalists learn what generates discussion. Activists learn which frames travel. Opponents learn which statements can be amplified against the speaker.

Everyone begins campaigning for the recommender even when no one can fully describe the recommender.

This is a dangerous subject because claims about political algorithms can become political claims themselves.

Partisans have incentives to believe a platform suppresses their side and amplifies the other. Platforms have incentives to describe systems as neutral or user-centered. Researchers face difficult access problems. Recommendation systems change faster than academic publication. Political content is hard to categorize without judgment. Exposure is not the same as persuasion, persuasion is not the same as voting, and voting is not the same as an election result.

Any serious account has to keep those layers separate.

One of the largest research collaborations on social-media feeds did exactly that during the 2020 U.S. election.

Researchers working with Meta studied what happened when consenting Facebook and Instagram users were assigned to reverse chronological feeds rather than the ordinary algorithmically ranked experience. The intervention produced meaningful changes in exposure and in some aspects of platform use. Users saw different mixes of content. They spent less time on the platforms under chronological feeds.

Yet the study did not find statistically significant changes in several measured political attitudes, including political knowledge, polarization, and related outcomes examined by the researchers.

This result frustrated anyone hoping for a simple story.

The feed mattered.

The measured beliefs largely did not move with it.

A later field experiment on X produced a different result. In work published in Nature in 2026, researchers studied users during a seven-week period in 2023 and found that enabling X's algorithmic feed changed political content exposure and shifted some measured political opinions in a conservative direction relative to a chronological condition. The same study did not show that every attitude moved or that the effect should be generalized indefinitely across platforms and elections.

Both findings matter.

They make it harder to say “algorithms don't affect politics.”

They also make it harder to say “algorithms program voters.”

A recommendation system can have strong allocation power and variable persuasion power.

That distinction should be written above every argument about algorithmic politics.

Allocation power is already consequential.

Politics depends on attention before it depends on agreement. A candidate who never reaches a voter cannot persuade that voter. An issue that never enters a person's information environment cannot become salient through that channel. A rumor repeatedly surfaced may matter even if most people reject it because journalists, officials, and opponents are forced to respond.

The feed shapes the agenda of possible persuasion.

That is enough to influence campaigns.

Political professionals do not need proof that a recommender changes ideology to care how it distributes content. They care about reach, fundraising, volunteer recruitment, media coverage, candidate recognition, and which messages supporters repeat.

The recommender can affect all of those intermediate outcomes.

This is where political communication begins to acquire the same dual audience as creator culture.

The candidate speaks to a voter.

The clip speaks to the machine.

The machine does not understand policy in the way a voter does. It estimates relevance, interest, safety, quality, or other objectives from available representations and learned patterns. The campaign therefore has an incentive to package political material in forms that produce observable response.

A long policy explanation can become six short clips.

A speechwriter may place a memorable sentence where it can be separated cleanly. A campaign may film vertical video even for events designed around a physical stage. A candidate may answer a question with the awareness that the first ten seconds can circulate independently from the next two minutes.

The platform does not order this.

The distribution environment rewards portability.

This can improve politics.

A citizen who would never watch a forty-minute town hall may encounter a useful explanation in a sixty-second clip. Small campaigns can reach people without buying television. Candidates can speak without every sentence being filtered through journalists. Local organizers can find one another. Citizens can witness events directly.

The old gatekeepers were not democratically perfect.

Algorithmic distribution can broaden participation.

It can also reward political material for qualities orthogonal to public value.

Outrage is memorable. Conflict invites response. Moral certainty is easier to compress than institutional nuance. A claim that something terrible has happened can be more immediately legible than an explanation of why a complex system produced a mixed outcome.

This does not prove a platform optimizes for outrage.

The stronger claim is that political actors observe which forms attract attention and adapt.

If anger travels, more messages will be packaged in anger.

If humor travels, politicians become comedians.

If authenticity travels, professional campaigns manufacture informality.

If confrontation travels, clips are cut around confrontation.

The machine audience becomes a constituency with no vote.

The phrase is intentionally uncomfortable.

A political candidate is supposed to respond to citizens. A recommender is not a citizen. Yet its distribution decisions can change which citizens the candidate is able to reach at low cost. That makes the intermediary strategically important whether the candidate approves of it or not.

The same is true for journalists covering politics.

News organizations publish into recommendation systems. A headline is written for readers and feeds. A clip from an interview may receive more attention than the full interview. Reporters can see which stories draw traffic. Opinion writers see engagement. Editors face economic pressure from advertising and subscriptions.

Political information is therefore shaped not only by campaigns adapting to algorithms but by media adapting to algorithms.

Citizens then respond to the changed supply.

The loop contains several optimizing institutions at once.

This makes causal claims extraordinarily difficult.

Suppose political video becomes shorter and more confrontational over a decade. The recommender may contribute. So may smartphone hardware, platform competition, changes in journalism, polarization that began elsewhere, candidate strategy, economic pressures on media, audience demand, and generational changes in communication.

A confident monocausal explanation would be bad history.

The useful question is narrower: does algorithmic mediation create incentives that political actors must account for?

The answer is plainly yes once distribution through those systems becomes important.

Research on political amplification adds evidence while preserving complexity. Studies have found that ranking systems can amplify political content unevenly, but the direction and magnitude vary by platform, country, period, political group, and design. A 2026 Nature audit of TikTok during the 2024 U.S. election reported asymmetric partisan exposure in its study design. Other research on Twitter has documented algorithmic amplification patterns across political content.

None justifies a permanent label on a platform's ideology.

Algorithms change.

Content supply changes.

User populations change.

Strategic actors adapt once they discover patterns.

The safest conclusion is not that a machine belongs to one political side.

It is that opacity plus political stakes makes the machine itself an object of political contest.

Parties complain about ranking. Governments pressure platforms. Platforms alter political-content policies. Researchers audit. Users accuse one another of gaming. Coordinated networks attempt to manufacture signals. Platforms respond with integrity systems. Campaigns adjust again.

The distribution system becomes part of politics.

This recursive conflict resembles financial markets more than broadcasting.

Everyone is trying to anticipate everyone else's response.

A campaign asks what supporters will share. The platform predicts what users will engage with. Opponents predict which clip can be reframed. Journalists predict what will become news. Activists predict which controversy will force an institutional response. Users learn which kinds of political posts provoke their own networks.

The original policy issue can become secondary to the competition for distribution.

This is not always bad.

Politics is partly a competition for attention because democracy requires persuasion. Movements historically ignored by dominant media have used new channels to force issues into public view. Algorithmic distribution can let an unexpected message escape the old hierarchy.

The question is what happens once everyone learns the channel.

Early participants in a medium often look unusually authentic because they have not yet professionalized around its incentives. A politician posting a casual video feels direct when opponents are still issuing press releases. Soon every campaign hires staff who understand the format. The aesthetic of authenticity becomes a production style.

The system selects a form.

The profession learns it.

The form stops signaling what it once signaled.

This is a recurring theme in algorithmic influence. Signals decay after strategic adoption.

The casual video begins as evidence that a candidate is speaking directly. Once consultants produce casual videos deliberately, casualness is no longer strong evidence of spontaneity. The audience becomes more skeptical. Creators develop new authenticity cues. The platform sees new behavior. The cycle continues.

Generative AI accelerates this signal decay.

A campaign can produce more versions of a message, translate faster, generate captions, test language, summarize policy, and repurpose long events into short formats at low cost. Opponents can do the same. Citizens can do the same. Anonymous accounts can do the same.

The volume of political material can rise faster than human attention.

That makes recommendation more necessary.

More content increases the power of selection.

Selection increases the incentive to optimize.

Optimization increases content.

The loop tightens.

This is why the problem cannot be solved simply by demanding chronological feeds everywhere.

Chronology is a legitimate option and an important form of user control. Under the European Union's Digital Services Act, very large platforms face recommender transparency obligations and requirements related to offering options not based on profiling in applicable contexts.

But chronology does not remove selection from politics.

People still choose whom to follow. Popular accounts still dominate. Coordinated actors can flood timelines. Posting frequency becomes a strategy. The most recent item receives structural advantage. Human networks carry their own biases.

Every feed is an allocation rule.

The governance question is which allocation rules users can understand, choose, and contest.

Transparency helps but has limits.

A platform can say that recommendations depend on interaction signals, relationships, content information, and user preferences. That does not tell a campaign how one clip will be treated for one voter. A full technical disclosure might make manipulation easier without making the system more understandable to citizens.

Auditing becomes important because behavior can be measured from outside.

Researchers can create controlled accounts, recruit consenting users, compare feed conditions, inspect exposures, and test hypotheses. Platform access can make this easier or harder. Regulation can create data-access obligations. Independent audits can provide evidence that neither partisan anecdotes nor company statements can supply alone.

The objective should not be a politically neutral ranking in the simplistic sense of producing equal outcomes for every party.

Politics is dynamic. Parties produce different content. Users follow different accounts. Events differ. A system that forced identical exposure could itself be manipulative.

The better goal is accountable process: rules that do not secretly discriminate in prohibited ways, meaningful user choice, research access, clear enforcement against coordinated manipulation, and honest evidence about effects.

Democracy can tolerate disagreement about ranking.

It has more trouble when nobody can know whether the ranking itself is being used as political power.

There is also a cultural response available to campaigns and citizens: build channels that do not depend entirely on recommendation.

Email lists, membership organizations, local meetings, subscriptions, direct websites, and durable communities look almost old-fashioned next to viral distribution. Their weakness is slower growth. Their strength is that the relationship belongs more directly to the participants.

The more volatile algorithmic reach becomes, the more valuable direct connection can become.

This is not a retreat from technology.

It is diversification of distribution risk.

A campaign dependent on one feed has the same structural problem as a business dependent on one marketplace or a creator dependent on one recommender. The intermediary can change for reasons unrelated to the participant.

Owning some route to the audience preserves autonomy.

That principle will matter even more as synthetic content floods the channels.

Politics has already shown us the shape of the next problem.

When producing a convincing message is expensive, distribution is one bottleneck among many. When producing thousands of competent messages becomes cheap, distribution becomes the scarce resource.

The machine audience becomes more important precisely because there is more human-looking material for it to judge.

The next contest is not merely over what people believe.

It is over which human or synthetic voice gets selected for the chance to speak.
