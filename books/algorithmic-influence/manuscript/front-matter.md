# Algorithmic Influence

*How We Learned to Persuade Machines to Persuade People*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a person you are trying to reach.

There is also something standing between you and that person.

For a century, the most obvious intermediaries were human. Editors decided which stories ran. Buyers decided which products reached shelves. Hiring managers decided which résumés reached the next room. Producers decided which songs received airplay. Publishers decided which books deserved printing presses and warehouse space. Party organizations decided which candidates received money, staff, and institutional legitimacy.

The gatekeeper might have been fair or corrupt, brilliant or lazy, but it could usually be pictured as a person with a desk.

That picture is becoming incomplete.

A musician can now release a song without a label and still discover that distribution depends on a recommender. A merchant can open an online store without a retail buyer and still discover that discovery depends on ranking. A job applicant can apply without knowing anybody inside the company and still discover that the first audience may be a parser, score, screen, or workflow. A driver can work without a supervisor in the passenger seat and still spend the day responding to dispatch logic, incentives, acceptance metrics, ratings, and automated warnings. A political candidate can speak directly to millions and still have no direct control over which few seconds of that speech are shown to whom.

The old gatekeepers did not disappear. A new one joined them.

We usually discuss this new intermediary from the receiving end. What do algorithms do to us? Do recommendation systems make people angry? Do personalized feeds polarize politics? Do ranking systems discriminate? Do automated managers reduce autonomy? Do AI systems manipulate attention?

Those are important questions. They have produced a substantial literature, and the evidence is more complicated than the slogans. Some ranking systems improve discovery. Some experiments find measurable effects on attitudes; others find large changes in what people see without corresponding changes in political beliefs. Recommenders can narrow a horizon or widen it. Ratings can create trust among strangers while also becoming instruments of pressure. Automated systems can remove arbitrary human discretion while creating new forms of opacity.

This book begins from the other side of the glass.

What happens when we know the machine is watching?

A search engine ranks pages, so publishers learn search engine optimization. A video feed rewards signals associated with watch time and continued engagement, so creators form theories about hooks, pacing, captions, upload times, thumbnails, sounds, topics, and retention. An online marketplace displays ratings, so sellers manage for the rating as well as the underlying transaction. A performance dashboard measures a worker, so the worker learns what the dashboard can see. An automated hiring workflow looks for structured evidence, so applicants learn to make experience machine-readable. A shopping agent compares products, so merchants begin thinking about the fields, reviews, availability, provenance, and structured data an agent can reliably inspect.

At that point the algorithm is not only acting on us.

We are acting on it.

That is the subject of this book.

I use **algorithmic influence** for a specific mechanism: the attempt to shape an algorithmic intermediary in order to affect the human opportunities, attention, prices, recommendations, work assignments, rankings, or decisions that come out the other side.

The phrase itself is not new, and I am not claiming otherwise. Researchers have used related language for years to describe algorithmic power, algorithmic persuasion, algorithmic visibility, algorithmic management, performative prediction, strategic classification, recommender effects, and the ways creators negotiate opaque ranking systems. What is less commonly treated as one coherent object is the reverse path that now connects all of those domains.

The applicant is writing for the recruiter and the parser.

The restaurant is serving the diner and the rating system.

The politician is speaking to the voter and the feed.

The merchant is describing the product to the shopper and the agent.

The worker is doing the job and producing the trace by which the job will be judged.

The creator is entertaining the viewer and generating signals for the recommender deciding whether another viewer will ever see the work.

There are now two audiences in the room, even when only one of them is human.

The second audience does not need to possess consciousness, intention, or a theory of mind for this to matter. A thermostat can shape behavior without wanting anything. A toll can reroute traffic without understanding a driver. An algorithmic system can alter incentives, visibility, and access because people know—or believe—that certain actions change its outputs.

Sometimes those beliefs are correct. Sometimes they are folklore. The distinction matters less than it first appears. If millions of people believe a platform rewards a particular behavior and reorganize their work around that belief, the belief can reshape the ecosystem even when the underlying theory of the algorithm is wrong.

That is one of the peculiar features of algorithmic influence. The target can be opaque, adaptive, probabilistic, personalized, and constantly changing. You may be trying to influence a system whose rules you cannot inspect, whose inputs you only partly understand, and whose objective is itself being updated from the behavior of people adapting to yesterday’s version.

A human persuader normally wants to understand the audience.

An algorithmic persuader often has to infer the audience from consequences.

The result is a new form of social physics built from experiments, gossip, dashboards, folk theories, consultants, optimization tools, A/B tests, rumors, metrics, screenshots, and survivor stories. Creators compare notes about what the feed wants. Sellers compare notes about what the marketplace rewards. applicants compare notes about résumé parsers. Drivers compare notes about dispatch systems. Publishers compare notes about search updates. Merchants now compare notes about how their products appear in generative answers and AI shopping systems.

This is not merely another chapter in advertising.

Advertising assumes you can buy exposure to a human audience. Algorithmic influence is often about influencing the machinery that allocates exposure, sometimes without paying for it and sometimes without knowing the exact rule by which exposure is allocated.

It is not merely manipulation either.

A hospital that formats information so a clinical system can process it is making itself legible to a machine. A small business that marks up prices and inventory accurately is doing the same. A worker who learns how an automated schedule is generated may be protecting time with her family. A candidate who makes a résumé readable by a parser may simply be preventing relevant experience from disappearing before a human sees it.

Optimization can be adaptive, sensible, and even necessary.

The danger appears when the proxy becomes the product.

If a system can measure speed more easily than judgment, people may become faster and less thoughtful. If a recommender can observe watch time more easily than whether something made a life better, creators may become better at holding attention than deserving it. If a reputation score compresses a complicated encounter into five stars, workers may learn to avoid any behavior that risks a four. If an AI shopping agent favors products with complete, machine-readable attributes, manufacturers may improve their data—and eventually their products may drift toward attributes the agent can compare.

The world begins to move toward what its intermediaries can see.

This is the deeper argument of the book.

Algorithms do not only select among the things people make. Over time, selection changes what people choose to make. The ranking system becomes part of the creative environment. The score becomes part of the workplace. The classifier becomes part of the applicant pool. The recommendation becomes part of politics. The agent becomes part of product design.

This is an old evolutionary pattern wearing computational clothes. Selection changes populations. The unusual part is the speed. Digital systems can measure millions of interactions, update continuously, and distribute incentives across enormous populations of people who are themselves watching the system and adapting in return.

Influence becomes recursive.

The machine ranks us. We change. The changed behavior becomes data. The machine updates. We change again.

That loop can produce extraordinary coordination. It can also produce monoculture, superstition, gaming, hidden power, and a society increasingly optimized for proxies that nobody remembers choosing.

The chapters that follow move through the places where this loop is easiest to see: search, feeds, metrics, creator culture, work, ratings, hiring, pricing, politics, synthetic media, generative answers, commerce, and autonomous agents. The final chapters ask what can be done when transparency alone is not enough—when the system is too complex to summarize as a rule and the people affected by it still need meaningful ways to understand, contest, refuse, or route around its decisions.

The point is not to teach anyone how to game a particular platform. Those instructions age badly and usually become part of the game they claim to explain.

The point is to name a change in the structure of influence.

For a long time, the basic unit of persuasion was one person trying to change another.

Now there is often a third participant.

It does not need to be persuaded in the human sense. It needs to be measured, predicted, satisfied, triggered, classified, ranked, parsed, recommended, or trusted.

And because so much human attention and opportunity now sits behind that machinery, learning how to affect the machine has become one of the most consequential forms of influence in the world.
