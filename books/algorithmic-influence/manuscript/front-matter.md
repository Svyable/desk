# Algorithmic Influence

*How We Learned to Persuade Machines to Persuade People*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is a person you are trying to reach. Increasingly, something is standing between you and that person.

For a century, the obvious intermediaries were human. Editors chose which stories ran. Buyers chose which products reached shelves. Hiring managers chose which résumés reached the next room. Producers chose which songs received airplay. Publishers chose which books deserved printing presses and warehouse space. Party organizations chose which candidates received money, staff, and legitimacy. The gatekeeper might have been fair or corrupt, brilliant or lazy, but you could usually picture a person with a desk.

That picture is now incomplete.

A musician can release a song without a label and still depend on a recommender for distribution. A merchant can open a store without a retail buyer and still depend on ranking for discovery. A job applicant can apply without knowing anyone inside the company and find that the first audience is a parser, score, screen, or workflow. A driver can work without a supervisor in the passenger seat and spend the day answering to dispatch logic, incentives, acceptance metrics, ratings, and automated warnings. A political candidate can speak directly to millions and still have almost no control over which few seconds reach whom.

The old gatekeepers did not disappear. A new one joined them.

We usually discuss this intermediary from the receiving end. What do algorithms do to us? Do recommendation systems make people angry? Do personalized feeds polarize politics? Do ranking systems discriminate? Do automated managers reduce autonomy? Do AI systems manipulate attention?

Those questions matter, and the evidence is messier than the slogans. Some ranking systems improve discovery. Some experiments find measurable effects on attitudes; others find large changes in what people see without corresponding changes in political beliefs. Recommenders can narrow a horizon or widen it. Ratings can create trust among strangers and become instruments of pressure. Automated systems can remove arbitrary human discretion while creating new forms of opacity.

This book begins from the other side of the glass:

**What happens when we know the machine is watching?**

A search engine ranks pages, so publishers learn search engine optimization. A video feed rewards signals associated with watch time and continued engagement, so creators form theories about hooks, pacing, captions, thumbnails, sounds, topics, upload times, and retention. A marketplace displays ratings, so sellers manage for the rating as well as the transaction. A dashboard measures a worker, so the worker learns what the dashboard can see. An automated hiring workflow looks for structured evidence, so applicants learn to make experience machine-readable. A shopping agent compares products, so merchants begin thinking about the fields, reviews, availability, provenance, and structured data the agent can reliably inspect.

The algorithm acts on us while we act on it.

I use **algorithmic influence** for a specific mechanism: the attempt to shape an algorithmic intermediary in order to affect the human opportunities, attention, prices, recommendations, work assignments, rankings, or decisions that emerge on the other side.

The phrase itself is not new, and I am not claiming otherwise. Researchers have used related language for years: algorithmic power, algorithmic persuasion, algorithmic visibility, algorithmic management, performative prediction, strategic classification, recommender effects, and the ways creators negotiate opaque ranking systems. What is less often treated as one coherent object is the reverse path connecting all of those domains.

The applicant writes for the recruiter and the parser. The restaurant serves the diner and the rating system. The politician speaks to the voter and the feed. The worker does the job while producing the trace by which the job will be judged. The creator entertains one viewer while generating signals for the recommender deciding whether another viewer will ever see the work.

There are now two audiences in the room, even when only one is human.

The second audience does not need consciousness, intention, or a theory of mind. A thermostat can shape behavior without wanting anything. A toll can reroute traffic without understanding a driver. An algorithmic system can alter incentives, visibility, and access because people know—or believe—that certain actions change its outputs.

Sometimes those beliefs are correct. Sometimes they are folklore. If millions of people believe a platform rewards a particular behavior and reorganize their work around that belief, the belief can reshape the ecosystem even when the theory of the algorithm is wrong.

That is one of the peculiar features of algorithmic influence. The target can be opaque, adaptive, probabilistic, personalized, and constantly changing. You may be trying to influence a system whose rules you cannot inspect, whose inputs you only partly understand, and whose objective is being updated from the behavior of people adapting to yesterday's version. A human persuader wants to understand the audience. An algorithmic persuader often has to reconstruct the audience from consequences.

The result is a new social physics assembled from experiments, gossip, dashboards, folk theories, consultants, optimization tools, A/B tests, rumors, metrics, screenshots, and survivor stories. Creators compare notes about what the feed wants. Sellers compare notes about what the marketplace rewards. Applicants compare notes about résumé parsers. Drivers compare notes about dispatch systems. Publishers compare notes about search updates. Merchants now compare notes about how their products appear in generative answers and AI shopping systems.

This is more than advertising. Advertising assumes you can buy exposure to a human audience. Algorithmic influence is often about changing the machinery that allocates exposure—sometimes without paying for it, sometimes without knowing the rule by which exposure is allocated.

Nor is every attempt manipulation. A hospital that formats information so a clinical system can process it is making itself legible to a machine. A small business that marks up prices and inventory accurately is doing the same. A worker who learns how an automated schedule is generated may be protecting time with her family. A candidate who makes a résumé readable by a parser may simply be preventing relevant experience from disappearing before a human sees it.

Optimization can be adaptive, sensible, and necessary. The trouble begins when the proxy becomes the product.

If a system can measure speed more easily than judgment, people may become faster and less thoughtful. If a recommender can observe watch time more easily than whether something made a life better, creators may become better at holding attention than deserving it. If a reputation score compresses a complicated encounter into five stars, workers may learn to avoid anything that risks a four. If an AI shopping agent favors products with complete, machine-readable attributes, manufacturers may improve their data—and eventually their products may drift toward attributes the agent can compare.

The world begins to move toward what its intermediaries can see.

Algorithms do not merely select among the things people make. Selection changes what people choose to make. The ranking system becomes part of the creative environment. The score becomes part of the workplace. The classifier becomes part of the applicant pool. The recommendation becomes part of politics. The agent becomes part of product design.

This is an old evolutionary pattern wearing computational clothes: selection changes populations. The unusual part is the speed. Digital systems can measure millions of interactions, update continuously, and distribute incentives across enormous populations of people who are watching the system and adapting in return.

The machine ranks us. We change. The changed behavior becomes data. The machine updates. We change again.

That loop can produce extraordinary coordination. It can also produce monoculture, superstition, gaming, hidden power, and a society increasingly optimized for proxies nobody remembers choosing.

The chapters that follow move through search, feeds, metrics, creator culture, work, ratings, hiring, pricing, politics, synthetic media, generative answers, commerce, and autonomous agents. The final chapters ask what can be done when transparency alone is not enough—when the system is too complex to summarize as a rule, but the people affected by it still need meaningful ways to understand, contest, refuse, or route around its decisions.

This is not a manual for gaming a particular platform. Those instructions age badly and usually become part of the game they claim to explain. The subject is the changing structure of influence.

For a long time, the basic unit of persuasion was one person trying to change another. Now there is often a third participant. It does not need to be persuaded in the human sense. It needs to be measured, predicted, satisfied, triggered, classified, ranked, parsed, recommended, or trusted.

So much human attention and opportunity now sits behind that machinery that learning how to affect the machine has become consequential in its own right.

The rest of the book is about what happens after people notice.
