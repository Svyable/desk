# Your Customer Has an Agent

The salesperson has spent centuries learning how to read a customer.

Is the customer rushed? Price sensitive? Confused? Loyal to a brand? Buying for someone else? Does she care about durability or appearance? Does he need reassurance? Is the hesitation about money, risk, status, or simply too many choices?

Commerce developed an enormous vocabulary around this human encounter.

Then the customer began sending software.

At first the software was simple. Price-comparison sites gathered offers. Travel aggregators searched flights. Shopping engines matched product feeds. Browser extensions found coupons. Consumers set alerts for price drops.

The customer was already delegating parts of shopping.

Generative agents expand the delegation from retrieval toward judgment and action.

A person can describe a need in ordinary language: find a carry-on that fits these airline dimensions, weighs less than this, arrives before Friday, has a strong warranty, and costs under two hundred dollars. An agent can search, compare, summarize tradeoffs, inspect reviews, and under some systems proceed toward purchase with the user's supervision or authorization.

This is still an early market.

The phrase “agentic commerce” currently describes a collection of emerging products, protocols, merchant integrations, and future ambitions rather than one settled infrastructure. User supervision remains common. Retailers still sell overwhelmingly through familiar websites, apps, marketplaces, stores, and human-driven checkout flows.

But the strategic change is already visible.

The merchant has acquired a new customer surface.

The human says what she wants.

The agent decides which evidence matters.

The merchant now has to persuade both.

Persuade is again metaphorical. A shopping agent does not need to feel desire. It needs to infer that an option satisfies the user's objective better than alternatives.

That changes what counts as good selling.

Human retail has always used atmosphere. Packaging matters. Photography matters. Brand story matters. Store design matters. A luxury shop can make a customer feel that a product belongs to a particular life.

An agent can be less susceptible to some of those surfaces and more sensitive to others.

Price. Availability. Dimensions. Return policy. Delivery date. Warranty. Compatibility. Verified reviews. Seller reliability. Product identifiers. Materials. Provenance. The exact constraint the user stated.

The merchant's beautiful page may matter less than whether the machine can extract the facts.

This is not the death of branding.

The user can instruct the agent to prefer a brand. Brand reputation can enter reviews, prior purchases, external sources, and the user's own preferences. A person buying a gift may care deeply about presentation. A fashion purchase may remain aesthetic and emotional. A restaurant choice may depend on atmosphere that structured data captures poorly.

Agents do not remove human preference.

They operationalize it.

That distinction matters.

A good consumer agent should not replace the user's values with the agent's values. It should translate a messy human request into a search over available options while preserving uncertainty where the request is ambiguous.

The challenge is that merchants can optimize for the translation.

Suppose an agent consistently values free returns because users often regret purchases. Merchants have reason to offer free returns or represent return terms clearly. That can improve commerce.

Suppose the agent gives weight to a product's review count. Sellers have reason to accumulate reviews. That can improve evidence and intensify review manipulation.

Suppose the agent prefers products with complete structured attributes. Merchants have reason to fill every field. That improves interoperability and disadvantages unusual products that do not fit the schema.

The agent becomes an allocator.

Merchants begin performing for it.

This is the same mechanism that shaped search pages and social videos, now attached directly to transactions.

The stakes are larger because an agent can reduce the number of moments in which a merchant can recover from poor machine representation.

In traditional e-commerce, a shopper might search broadly, see a product image, click through, read a description, inspect reviews, browse alternatives, encounter a promotion, and change her mind. The merchant has several opportunities to communicate.

An agent can compress the funnel.

It may compare fifty products and present three.

Forty-seven merchants never enter the human conversation.

This is recommendation with purchasing power.

The economics therefore favor machine-readable completeness.

Product feeds are not new. Google Merchant Center and other commerce systems have long required structured product information such as identifiers, price, availability, condition, shipping information, and attributes. Marketplaces have long normalized catalogs into schemas. Retailers already live inside machine-readable commerce.

Agentic systems make the consequence more intuitive.

The feed is no longer merely for an ad or shopping result.

It may become the evidence from which a delegated buyer decides.

That is why emerging commerce protocols matter even before they dominate transaction volume. They represent an attempt to standardize how agents, merchants, payment systems, and users exchange the information and authorization required to transact safely.

Protocols sound boring because they are supposed to be boring.

Their social effect can be enormous.

HTTP made documents interoperable. SMTP made email interoperable. Payment-card networks standardized enough of commerce for merchants and banks to coordinate globally. Product identifiers let supply chains refer to the same item.

An agentic-commerce protocol can define which facts a machine buyer expects to receive and which actions it can take.

Those definitions create legibility.

Legibility creates influence.

If a merchant wants to be considered, it exposes the expected data. If the agent needs reliable inventory, inventory systems improve. If the protocol needs clear refund terms, merchants formalize them. If agents compare total delivered cost, hidden fees become strategically dangerous because the machine can surface them.

Machine mediation can therefore discipline merchants in ways consumers benefit from.

A human shopper gets tired.

An agent does not care that the cheaper option is buried on page seven.

A human shopper may forget to compare warranty terms.

An agent can compare them every time.

A human shopper may be influenced by a countdown timer.

An agent can ignore it unless the deadline is real and relevant.

Delegation can shift bargaining power toward the consumer by making comparison cheap.

This is the optimistic case for agentic commerce.

The seller's response is predictable.

If agents become important buyers, sellers will learn how agents compare.

They will optimize prices, bundles, attributes, policies, descriptions, and availability around the selection process. Vendors will offer “agent readiness” audits. Analytics companies will measure inclusion in agent recommendations. Merchants will test how different assistants interpret product data. Brands will publish agent-readable buying guides. Fraudsters will search for features agents overweight.

The folk physics will begin before the protocols stabilize.

A new optimization industry will emerge because the economic prize is direct.

The difference from human persuasion is that the merchant may be able to influence the agent by improving objective terms.

If the agent is instructed to minimize total cost subject to quality constraints, the merchant can win by lowering cost, improving quality, or supplying stronger evidence. Manipulative copy has less leverage if the agent can compare the claim against independent data.

This creates the possibility of a healthier kind of algorithmic influence.

Make the thing better so the machine has better reasons to select it.

That principle sounds obvious. Markets do not always reward it because information is incomplete. Advertising can overcome weak evidence. Brand can substitute for comparison. Search costs can keep consumers from finding superior alternatives.

Agents can reduce those information frictions.

If they do, competition can move closer to underlying value.

But only if the agent's objective is aligned with the user.

That is the central governance problem.

Who is the agent working for?

A shopping assistant can be funded by subscriptions, transaction fees, merchant commissions, advertising, platform economics, or some combination. Each model creates incentives around which options are surfaced and how recommendations are explained.

Humans already navigate this problem with travel agents, brokers, financial advisers, marketplaces, comparison sites, and retail salespeople. We distinguish fiduciaries from commissioned sellers because economic alignment matters.

AI agents will need an equally legible theory of loyalty.

A user should know whether a recommendation is the agent's best judgment under the user's stated preferences, a sponsored placement, an eligible merchant subset, or an option influenced by commercial relationships.

Otherwise delegated shopping becomes hidden advertising.

The agent's apparent neutrality makes the conflict more dangerous, not less.

A banner ad announces persuasion.

A synthesized recommendation can sound like judgment.

That is why provenance of recommendation matters alongside provenance of product.

The user needs to know not only what the agent selected but under what economic rules the selection occurred.

This returns us to the political chapter. Allocation power does not require persuasion power to matter. An agent that excludes a merchant from consideration may determine the transaction without changing the user's underlying preference.

The user wanted a durable suitcase.

The agent chose which durable suitcases existed for practical purposes.

Selection is the market.

Merchants will therefore compete for eligibility before competing for preference.

This is already familiar on marketplaces. A seller can have an excellent product and lose visibility because inventory is unavailable, shipping is slow, data is incomplete, policy compliance fails, or account standing deteriorates. The agentic layer can add another eligibility surface above the marketplace layer.

A merchant might be optimized for humans and invisible to agents.

This creates a new class of intermediary business.

Someone will translate.

Small businesses will need tools that synchronize inventory, product identity, policies, availability, and reputation across agent-accessible systems. Large retailers will build direct integrations. Platforms will offer merchant APIs. Standards bodies will fight over schemas. Payment providers will compete to become the trusted execution layer.

The technical architecture will shape economic concentration.

If only large merchants can expose high-quality machine-readable data and support agent transactions safely, agents could strengthen incumbents.

If open protocols let small merchants become equally legible, agents could weaken marketplaces by allowing buyers to search the wider web directly.

Both futures are plausible.

The architecture matters more than the marketing language.

Open protocols can reduce dependency on a single gatekeeper. Closed agent ecosystems can create a new gatekeeper more powerful than the old search page because discovery and transaction occur inside one system.

A merchant that once worried about search ranking may now worry about whether the dominant agent can discover, evaluate, and transact with it at all.

This is why interoperability is a competition policy issue as much as a developer issue.

The more consumers delegate, the more consequential machine access becomes.

There is also a privacy inversion.

Personalized pricing gave sellers more information about buyers. Consumer agents can give buyers more strategic power while protecting some personal information from sellers.

The agent may know the user's preferences without revealing all of them.

A merchant does not necessarily need to know that the customer is buying a gift for a recently divorced brother who is moving to Chicago. The merchant may only need to know the constraints relevant to the transaction.

An agent can act as a privacy firewall.

Or it can become the most concentrated holder of consumer intention ever built.

Again, architecture decides.

An agent that knows purchases, messages, calendar, location, family preferences, travel plans, and budget can make extraordinary recommendations. It also occupies an extraordinary position of informational power.

The user is delegating not only clicks but context.

Trust becomes central.

A human buyer can inspect a store and decide whether it feels legitimate. An agent needs machine-readable evidence of legitimacy. A merchant can inspect a customer at checkout for fraud. An agent needs to prove it is authorized to act. Payment systems need to know which actions the user approved. Returns and disputes need accountability.

Commerce becomes a network of machines establishing trust about humans and organizations.

The sale is no longer merely buyer and seller.

It can include buyer, agent, merchant, payment provider, identity system, marketplace, fulfillment network, and provenance services.

Each layer can become an audience for strategic representation.

That sounds exhausting.

The purpose of standards is to make it less so.

A merchant should not have to learn the private language of every agent. A consumer should not have to reauthorize basic preferences for every transaction. Interoperable credentials and protocols can turn repeated persuasion into verifiable facts.

This may be the deepest transformation agentic commerce offers.

Persuasion can move from rhetoric toward proof.

Instead of saying “fast shipping,” expose a delivery commitment.

Instead of saying “easy returns,” expose the policy in a form the agent can compare.

Instead of saying “compatible,” provide verified compatibility data.

Instead of saying “trusted seller,” provide a history the agent can inspect.

The merchant's best argument becomes executable evidence.

This is not a world without marketing. Human desire remains messy. New products need stories because people cannot ask for what they do not know exists. Fashion, art, food, travel, entertainment, luxury, and identity will remain deeply rhetorical.

But once a person delegates a bounded decision, the machine can demand a different kind of proof.

The customer has an agent.

The seller therefore needs something new.

Not merely a reputation humans recognize.

A reputation machines can verify.
