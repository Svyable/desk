# The Third Person in the Room

A woman records a forty-two-second video in her kitchen.

She does not know who will see it.

That would once have been an odd way to describe publishing. A newspaper knew roughly who bought the paper. A television station knew which market carried the signal. A magazine had a subscription list. Even the early social web seemed to preserve the old intuition that an audience was a collection of people who had chosen, in one way or another, to receive what you made. You accumulated friends, followers, subscribers, readers. Then you spoke to them.

The woman with the phone is doing something different. She may have a few hundred followers or a few million. Either number can be a poor description of the audience for the next forty-two seconds. The video may disappear almost immediately. It may be shown to people who have never heard of her. It may travel through a population selected less by relationship than by a system's estimate of who is likely to watch, linger, respond, share, search, or return.

She is still speaking to people.

But before most of those people hear her, something else has to decide that she is worth hearing.

This is the third person in the room.

Calling it a person is obviously imprecise. A recommendation system does not sit on a chair, become bored, feel flattered, or decide that it likes the speaker. It has no need to understand the video as a human viewer understands it. The phrase is useful for another reason. It captures the practical fact that communication increasingly has two audiences: the human being one hopes to reach and the machine that decides whether, when, where, and in what context that human being encounters the message.

The distinction matters because the two audiences are not persuaded in the same way.

A human may care that a story is true, funny, useful, generous, beautiful, outrageous, familiar, or surprising. A ranking system has access to representations and signals. It can use features of the content, information about the account, prior behavior, relationships, context, feedback, and patterns learned from enormous numbers of earlier interactions. Different systems use different inputs and objectives. Their designers change them. The systems change as new data arrives. There is no universal lever called engagement, and there is no secret list of weights shared by every feed.

What matters is simpler: the machine has to turn a vast set of possibilities into a smaller set of exposures.

That act of selection changes the environment in which people make things.

In 2016, three Google researchers described the architecture then used for YouTube recommendations as two connected problems. One system generated a manageable set of candidate videos from a much larger corpus. Another ranked those candidates. The paper is now historical; YouTube's production systems have evolved enormously since then. But the division remains conceptually useful. Before a viewer can choose among videos, a machine has already chosen which videos can become choices.

TikTok later made the practical consequence unusually vivid. Its For You feed did not require a user to construct a mature network before recommendation became useful. The company has publicly described a mixture of signals that can include interactions, information about videos, and account or device settings, while stressing that stronger indicators of interest may receive more weight than weaker ones. The precise implementation is proprietary and changes over time. Yet the cultural result has been visible for years: an unknown creator can reach strangers very quickly, and an established creator can publish something that barely travels.

This is a genuine expansion of opportunity.

It is easy to become nostalgic for the gatekeepers that algorithms displaced because we remember the best editors and forget the closed doors. A young comedian once needed a club owner, talent booker, television producer, or record executive somewhere in the chain. A musician needed radio, retail, label distribution, or a scene sufficiently concentrated to carry the work. A writer needed an editor or enough money to print. A small merchant needed shelf space. A local expert needed a publisher, a journalist, a conference, a credential, or a mailing list.

Recommendation systems did not abolish those institutions, but they created another path. A machine with no social embarrassment about showing an unknown account beside a famous one can occasionally be more open than an industry built on relationships.

That openness is exactly why people begin studying the machine.

If the old editor says no, a writer can tell a story about the editor. Perhaps the pitch was wrong. Perhaps the magazine had already commissioned something similar. Perhaps the editor did not like the work. Perhaps the editor was mistaken. The reasons may be unfair, but they are imaginable as human reasons.

When distribution falls off inside an algorithmic system, the explanation is harder to locate. The creator sees outcomes: impressions, views, completion, likes, shares, follows, search traffic, suggested traffic, audience retention. She sees that one video reached strangers and another did not. She may see a graph showing exactly when viewers left. What she usually cannot see is the complete decision process that placed those videos into competition with millions of alternatives for millions of different people.

So she experiments.

She changes the opening second. She moves the conclusion forward. She adds captions. She removes them. She posts at another hour. She changes the thumbnail, title, topic, duration, music, framing, frequency, or first line. She watches other creators. She joins private groups. She listens to consultants. She reads platform documentation. She distinguishes advice that seems sensible from advice that sounds like superstition, while knowing that the difference can be difficult to prove from her own small sample.

In 2019, Sophie Bishop called one part of this process “algorithmic gossip” in her study of YouTube creators. The phrase is better than it first appears. Gossip is what develops when consequential knowledge is unevenly distributed and official explanations are incomplete. People compare outcomes. A rumor acquires examples. An example becomes a rule. The rule circulates until someone with a large enough counterexample weakens it. The system may have changed in the meantime.

Crystal Abidin, Brooke Erin Duffy, Kelley Cotter, Taina Bucher, Robert Gehl, and other researchers across adjacent traditions have documented how people imagine, negotiate, and labor around platform visibility. The details differ by site and occupation, but a recognizable condition recurs: a system is powerful enough that people must account for it and opaque enough that accounting for it requires inference.

That condition produces a new kind of performance.

The creator is not merely making a video for a viewer. She is making a video that must survive a selection environment before the viewer exists as an audience.

We should be careful here. It is tempting to jump from selection to control. The algorithm chose the audience; therefore the algorithm caused the response; therefore the platform controls the culture. The evidence does not support such a clean chain.

Ranking can have strong effects on what people encounter and weaker effects on what they ultimately think. Political-feed experiments make this distinction especially clear. During the 2020 U.S. election, researchers working with Meta recruited users who agreed to have their Facebook or Instagram feeds switched from algorithmic ranking to reverse chronological order. The intervention materially changed what people saw and some aspects of how they used the platforms. Yet the researchers did not find corresponding changes in several measured political attitudes.

A different field experiment on X, published later in Nature, found that enabling the platform's algorithmic feed during a seven-week period in 2023 altered political exposure and shifted some measured opinions in a conservative direction. The studies involved different platforms, periods, designs, content environments, and participants. They are more useful together than either one is alone.

The lesson is not that algorithms persuade or that they do not.

The lesson is that allocation and persuasion are different powers.

A recommender may have substantial control over who gets a chance to persuade without having deterministic control over what the person then believes. That is enough to change the incentives of anyone trying to reach an audience.

Imagine two speakers in a hall. One stands onstage. The other controls a movable wall between the stage and the seats. The wall contains thousands of small windows. The second speaker cannot force anyone to applaud. It can decide which windows open.

You would care about the person controlling the wall even if every audience member remained stubbornly independent.

The modern version of that wall is more complicated because it is not one wall and there is not one operator. It may be a collection of models, rules, filters, auctions, safety systems, product decisions, and experiments. The windows can differ for every viewer. They can change between morning and evening. The criteria can react to behavior at a scale no human editor could manage.

From the creator's perspective, however, one fact survives the complexity: some characteristics of the work increase the probability of being shown, and others decrease it.

That probability is economically meaningful.

A video not shown does not earn an advertising share. A product not ranked may not sell. A post not distributed does not recruit. A restaurant buried in results receives fewer reservations. A job listing shown to the wrong applicants produces a different pool. A political clip recommended into one network and not another participates in a different conversation. A scientific paper surfaced by a search or recommendation engine enters a different intellectual path.

Selection is not the whole outcome, but it is often the doorway to the outcome.

Once that doorway becomes important enough, people begin designing for it.

This is where algorithmic influence reverses the familiar story.

We usually say that a platform influences a creator by rewarding certain kinds of content. That is true. The creator also tries to influence the platform by producing the signals she thinks will cause further distribution. The platform changes because creators adapt; the content available to train and evaluate future systems now contains the residue of earlier optimization. Viewers respond to that changed content. Their responses become new signals. Designers react to abuse, dissatisfaction, competition, regulation, and business goals. Another version is deployed.

The arrow bends into a loop.

The interesting part is that nobody in the loop needs to understand the entire loop.

A teenager can discover that a certain kind of opening retains viewers without knowing anything about deep learning. A recommender can learn that viewers with a particular history tend to watch a format without possessing any cultural theory of why the format exists. A product manager can observe rising complaints and alter a ranking objective without knowing how thousands of creators will reorganize around the change. A consultant can infer a useful tactic from data without being able to say whether the tactic works for the reason advertised.

The system learns behavior. People learn the system. Neither side learns perfectly.

This produces an environment that feels strangely animate.

Creators say the algorithm likes consistency, hates links, wants novelty, rewards speed, punishes absence, prefers one duration this month and another next month. Workers say the app wants them in a certain zone. Sellers say the marketplace wants faster shipping. Publishers say search wants authority. Applicants say the parser wants keywords. The language anthropomorphizes machinery because ordinary grammar is clumsy around distributed selection systems.

A person can know that “the algorithm wants” is technically wrong and still find it useful at nine in the morning when deciding what to upload.

Institutions have always shaped behavior this way. A tax code can make an accountant ask what the code wants. A university admissions process can make a teenager ask what the university wants even though the university is a bundle of committees, rules, budgets, histories, and people. A market can seem to want lower prices. Bureaucracies acquire personalities in speech because their effects are easier to experience than their internal structure.

Algorithmic systems intensify the condition through scale and feedback.

An editor might read a hundred submissions in a month. A recommender can participate in billions of ranking decisions. A manager might review quarterly performance. A dashboard can update each shift. A retail buyer may reset shelf space seasonally. An online marketplace can re-rank continuously. A human gatekeeper can be inconsistent from fatigue. A machine can be consistently inconsistent because personalization gives different people different versions of the gate.

The affected population also responds at digital speed. Advice can travel instantly. Creators can run experiments today and publish theories tonight. Entire industries appear around helping people become more visible to systems whose internal rules the advisers do not possess.

Search engine optimization was an early version. Social media made it personal and continuous. Generative systems and agents are beginning to extend it from visibility into representation and transaction.

But the mechanism is easier to see if we stay in the kitchen for another moment.

The woman records a second version of the video.

In the first, she takes twelve seconds to explain why the subject matters before arriving at the interesting object on the counter. In the second, she shows the object immediately.

Perhaps she prefers the first version. It sounds more like the way she would explain the idea to a friend. She publishes the second because she believes people decide too quickly whether to continue watching and because the system will interpret early abandonment as evidence that the video should be shown less often.

There are at least three possibilities.

She may be right. The faster opening could improve retention and distribution.

She may be wrong about the algorithm but right about people. Viewers themselves may simply prefer the faster opening, and the system only records that preference.

Or she may be wrong about both. The second video may perform better for reasons unrelated to the change she made, and she may carry a false rule into the next hundred videos.

From the outside these possibilities can look identical.

That ambiguity is central to the culture forming around algorithmic influence. A person observes a result in a system with many hidden variables and then acts as if a causal story has been discovered. The story changes behavior. Enough changed behavior can reshape the environment whether the original causal theory was correct or not.

Suppose thousands of creators become convinced that no one will tolerate a quiet opening. They cut faster. Suppose audiences adapt to faster cuts and become less patient with slow ones. Suppose the recommender observes that impatience and begins favoring the faster material because it now predicts better retention. The original theory may have been exaggerated, but the ecosystem can move toward it.

This is not unique to media. It is the pattern we will encounter again in work, hiring, ratings, pricing, politics, and commerce.

A worker learns that the dashboard counts units per hour and reorganizes the shift around countable units. An applicant learns that a hiring workflow parses structured experience and rewrites a career in the grammar of the parser. A restaurant learns that a small rating decline affects discovery and changes how it handles awkward customers. A merchant learns that an agent compares shipping speed, return policy, availability, and standardized attributes and begins treating those fields as part of the product.

The machine did not issue an order.

It allocated consequences.

People supplied the rest.

That makes algorithmic influence different from conventional persuasion in one important respect. Persuasion usually imagines an attempt to alter another mind. Algorithmic influence often aims to alter a probability inside a system: the probability of being selected, ranked, recommended, trusted, assigned, quoted, approved, or purchased.

The person doing the influencing may never know whether the attempt worked. There may be no stable individual on the receiving end. A slightly different representation can be evaluated differently for millions of people. The target can learn from the attempted influence. The criteria can move.

This is why the word influence is more useful than control.

Control is too strong. Most people cannot command the systems that mediate them, and the companies operating those systems cannot fully command the populations adapting in response. Influence leaves room for partial power, uncertainty, strategic response, and unintended effects.

It also leaves room for resistance.

People can build direct audiences. Workers can coordinate. Regulators can require choices or explanations. Platforms can offer chronological feeds, human review, appeals, or clearer rules. Merchants can refuse a channel. Audiences can seek out sources rather than accept recommendations. New protocols can make machine-readable representation more portable. Old forms of relationship can become more valuable precisely because algorithmic mediation is common.

None of that erases the third person.

It makes the third person contestable.

The most important shift is therefore not that algorithms have become persuasive geniuses. Many are clumsy. They misread. They overfit. They surface nonsense. They can be gamed, audited, bypassed, regulated, improved, or replaced.

The shift is that people increasingly behave as if machines are consequential audiences for their work.

Once that happens, the machine enters the creative brief, the résumé, the schedule, the product database, the campaign plan, the restaurant policy, the customer journey, and eventually the design of the thing itself.

A message no longer begins only with what should I say to this person?

It can begin one step earlier.

What must the machine see so the person gets the chance to hear me?

That question feels native to social media now. It did not begin there.

Before creators learned to study recommendation feeds, an earlier generation of publishers had already discovered what it meant to build for two readers at once. One reader was human. The other crawled the web.
