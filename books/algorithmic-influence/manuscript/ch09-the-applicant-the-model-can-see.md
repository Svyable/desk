# The Applicant the Model Can See

The résumé has always been a machine for reducing a life.

A person works for twenty years, changes cities, learns from bad bosses, becomes good at calming difficult customers, fixes mistakes nobody records, raises children, studies at night, survives layoffs, develops judgment, and eventually compresses the whole thing into two pages.

The compression is not new.

What is new is the possibility that the first reader of the compression is itself a machine.

This fact has generated more folklore than clarity.

Job applicants are told that automated systems reject résumés if they use the wrong template, that invisible keyword scores decide whether a human ever looks, that artificial intelligence silently ranks everyone, that graphics confuse parsers, that certain phrases unlock the gate, that a résumé can be “optimized for ATS” as if every applicant-tracking system were one machine with one appetite.

Some of this advice contains practical truth.

Much of it collapses several different technologies into a single imaginary robot recruiter.

Applicant-tracking systems can store applications, parse documents, manage workflow, search candidate records, schedule interviews, and help recruiters organize large pools. Employers may also use assessments, matching tools, ranking systems, chatbots, video tools, or products that fall under legal definitions of automated employment decision systems. The degree of automation varies enormously. A system used by one employer may simply help a recruiter search. Another may score candidates. Another may automate scheduling but leave substantive evaluation to people.

There is no universal machine standing at the entrance to employment.

There is, however, a growing machine-readable layer between applicant and employer.

That layer is enough to alter behavior.

A candidate who believes a résumé will be parsed has reason to use recognizable headings. A candidate who believes recruiters search by skill terms has reason to describe experience in the vocabulary used by the role. A candidate applying through a structured form has to fit experience into the provided fields. A candidate taking an automated assessment learns what kinds of evidence the assessment collects.

This can be good discipline.

People often describe their own experience badly. A software engineer writes “worked on platform.” A recruiter cannot infer scale, language, system, responsibility, or result. An experienced operations manager uses a company-specific title no outsider recognizes. A military veteran carries skills hidden behind institutional vocabulary. Making those experiences legible can improve matching.

The machine audience may force clarity that helps the human audience too.

The risk appears when legibility becomes confused with ability.

A parser can recognize a title more easily than an unusual career story. A structured assessment can compare standardized responses more easily than tacit judgment acquired over years. A matching model trained on historical success can recognize patterns resembling the people who succeeded before.

These are not necessarily flaws. They are consequences of representation.

Every hiring process has them.

A human recruiter scanning quickly may rely on school, employer, title, or formatting as crude signals. A referral system privileges network position. An interview rewards verbal performance. A work sample rewards skills under a specific task design. There is no unmediated view of talent.

The important question is what kind of mediation an institution chooses and how applicants respond to it.

Automation can reduce some forms of arbitrary human judgment.

A structured process can make criteria more consistent. A search tool can surface candidates outside a recruiter's immediate memory. A well-designed assessment can identify skill without relying on pedigree. Scheduling automation can remove delays that have nothing to do with merit. Blind review can suppress information that triggers bias.

The case against automated hiring cannot rest on nostalgia for human hiring.

Human hiring is full of bias, inconsistency, overconfidence, credential worship, affinity effects, and interviews whose predictive value is often weaker than managers believe.

The machine enters an already imperfect institution.

The danger is giving the machine's imperfection a scientific-looking authority.

A score feels less subjective than a recruiter's impression. It may be more reliable. It may also encode assumptions the applicant cannot see.

New York City's Local Law 144 became one of the most visible attempts to create procedural obligations around certain automated employment decision tools. The law requires, among other things, a recent bias audit and notices before covered tools are used in employment decisions. The exact scope, enforcement, and practical effect are legal questions rather than slogans, but the basic governance instinct is important.

When automation participates materially in access to work, the institution should not be able to hide behind the fact that software made the recommendation.

The employer remains responsible for the process it chose.

That sounds obvious. Technology has a way of making responsibility diffuse.

A recruiter says the vendor supplied the score. The vendor says the employer configured the tool. The employer says the model only assists human judgment. The manager says candidates reaching the interview stage had already been filtered. Everyone can be correct about one step while no one owns the whole path.

Applicants experience only the outcome.

Rejected.

No interview.

No explanation.

That uncertainty creates the hiring version of algorithmic gossip.

People share templates. Career sites publish lists of keywords. Coaches advise matching the language of the job description. Online tools claim to score a résumé against an applicant-tracking system. Forums contain stories about applications that failed until a phrase changed.

The advice is difficult to validate because employers rarely disclose enough detail for an applicant to know which mechanism mattered.

A rejected application might have failed because the role was filled, because a recruiter preferred another candidate, because an assessment score was low, because experience did not match, because the résumé was poorly parsed, because a referral jumped the queue, because compensation expectations differed, or because the employer received eight hundred applications and never looked at most of them.

The applicant sees one bit of data.

No.

A whole optimization industry grows around interpreting that bit.

This is a particularly pure example of algorithmic influence because the applicant's desired human audience is clear.

The applicant wants a hiring manager to understand the value of the work.

The applicant first has to survive the systems that organize the path to the hiring manager.

That changes the résumé from a letter to a dual-use document.

It must communicate to a human and remain intelligible to software.

The two goals often align. Clear headings help both. Specific skills help both. Simple chronology helps both. Concrete outcomes help both. A résumé with text embedded in decorative images may genuinely be harder for systems and humans to process.

The conflict appears when the applicant starts writing for an imagined score rather than a reader.

A sentence becomes a bag of keywords. Every tool ever touched is listed. The language of the job description is copied mechanically. Nuance disappears. People with unconventional titles rename themselves into standard categories. Gaps become awkward because the machine-readable career is expected to be continuous.

The résumé begins to look like data disguised as prose.

This can create a population effect.

If applicants believe certain words are required, more applicants use them. The words become less discriminating. Recruiters rely on stronger filters or different evidence. Applicants adapt again.

A signal that once separated experience from inexperience becomes cheap to manufacture.

The cycle resembles search optimization.

The difference is that a page can be rewritten infinitely. A career cannot.

People may therefore begin optimizing upstream.

A student chooses a credential because it appears in job filters. An employee asks for a title that travels better across platforms. A worker collects certificates whose value lies partly in being machine-readable. A contractor keeps a portfolio in forms that matching systems can ingest. Companies standardize job-family language because external systems recognize it.

Labor markets become more interoperable.

They can also become more standardized around visible evidence.

This is legibility drift applied to identity.

The danger is not that people become fake.

It is that they learn to narrate themselves through whatever categories travel.

That can flatten unusual combinations.

A journalist who became a product manager through years of community work may look less legible than someone with the standard title sequence. A mechanic who learned electronics informally may have deeper practical understanding than a credential suggests. A parent returning after years of caregiving may possess managerial ability that the employment record does not encode.

A sophisticated human can notice these stories.

A sophisticated model might notice them too, especially as language models become better at reading narrative rather than relying only on rigid fields.

That improvement will not end adaptation.

It may move adaptation into a subtler register.

If a model can interpret prose, applicants will learn what kinds of prose produce favorable interpretations. If it evaluates portfolios, candidates will optimize portfolios. If it conducts conversational screening, candidates will practice for the conversation. If it uses richer histories, people will manage richer histories.

Humans already do this around human interviewers.

We rehearse answers. We dress for the room. We research the company. We learn the interviewer's vocabulary. We choose which parts of ourselves to foreground.

Why should adaptation to a machine feel more troubling?

One reason is scale.

A peculiar preference held by one interviewer affects one interview. A learned model or standardized workflow can carry the same preference across thousands or millions of applicants.

Another is opacity.

A human interviewer can sometimes explain a concern. A model may produce a score from a representation no one can translate cleanly into a reason.

Another is feedback.

The system may be trained on historical hiring or performance data created by earlier processes. If those processes favored certain backgrounds, the model can learn patterns that preserve the shape of the institution even without explicitly using protected attributes.

And another is strategic response.

Once applicants learn the system, the data changes. The model is no longer evaluating the population on which it was initially validated.

The institution and applicant begin learning one another.

This is why technical validation at deployment is not enough.

A hiring tool can be accurate against historical data and still alter applicant behavior after people learn its existence. Coaching markets appear. Test preparation spreads. Candidates share prompts. Vendors update models. Employers change thresholds. The environment moves.

The testing industry has lived with this for decades. Standardized assessments remain useful despite preparation, but preparation changes score meaning at the margin and creates unequal access to optimization resources.

Algorithmic hiring inherits the same problem at software speed.

The people best able to optimize for the machine may not be the people best able to do the job.

They may simply possess better information about the gate.

This is an underappreciated fairness problem.

Debates often focus on whether the model treats groups differently. That is essential. There is also unequal access to model literacy.

A professional with money can hire a résumé consultant, buy practice assessments, use AI to tailor applications, maintain a polished online presence, and test language repeatedly. Another applicant may be equally capable at the underlying work and far less capable at representing that work to automated systems.

Machine mediation can therefore create a new kind of cultural capital: knowing how to become legible.

The skill is valuable precisely because the gate is not fully transparent.

This repeats the pattern from creator culture. People with experience learn the institution's folk physics. They know which fields matter, which formats travel, which signals are ignored, which credentials are respected, which errors break parsers, which stories need translation.

The machine audience rewards people who know the machine audience exists.

That is not necessarily unjust. Institutions have always rewarded procedural knowledge. The concern is when procedural knowledge becomes too large a part of substantive opportunity.

A hiring system should select people who can do the work.

If success increasingly depends on people who can do the application system, the proxy has drifted.

Employers have reason to care because excessive optimization hurts them too.

Keyword-heavy applications create noise. Generative AI makes tailored applications cheap. Candidates can produce fluent cover letters at enormous scale. Employers receive more polished evidence with less information about underlying effort. Screening systems respond with stronger filters or more direct assessments. Applicants respond with better tools.

The application arms race intensifies.

This is a version of synthetic competition we will return to later. When the cost of producing machine-legible representation falls, volume rises. Institutions become stricter about which evidence they trust.

The result may shift hiring away from narrative claims toward verified skills, work samples, reputation, structured credentials, and direct experience.

Or it may deepen reliance on networks because trusted referrals become scarce signals in a world of abundant applications.

That would be an ironic outcome.

Automation introduced partly to scale access could increase the value of old human trust.

There are design choices that can reduce this pressure.

Employers can tell applicants which stages are automated. They can provide usable instructions rather than inviting a guessing game. They can validate tools on the decisions they actually support. They can allow correction of parsed information. They can avoid false precision in scores. They can preserve human review where context matters. They can examine whether successful applicants are simply the ones best coached for the system.

Applicants can be given a way to know what the machine saw.

That last idea is more powerful than it appears.

Imagine submitting a résumé and receiving the structured representation extracted from it: dates, roles, skills, education, certifications. Errors could be corrected before evaluation. The institution would still control its criteria, but basic legibility would stop being a hidden test.

We do this in finance. Consumers can inspect credit reports because inaccurate data can have serious consequences. We do it with medical records. We do it with tax filings.

Employment systems could treat representational accuracy as a procedural right rather than an optimization trick.

That would not eliminate competition.

It would move competition closer to the underlying qualifications.

The alternative is a labor market in which people increasingly write themselves for machines they cannot see.

That pressure will only grow as applications become easier to generate.

A candidate can already ask an AI system to tailor a résumé to a posting, produce a cover letter, rehearse interviews, and translate experience into the language of a role. Employers can use AI to summarize, screen, rank, or search candidate pools. Both sides can increase throughput.

The scarce resource becomes trusted discrimination in the statistical sense: evidence that separates a good match from a merely polished representation.

Hiring begins to resemble every other algorithmic marketplace.

More supply.

More filtering.

More optimization around the filter.

The irony is that the original goal was to find a person.

A human employer has a human problem and a human applicant has a human need. They are separated by machinery built because there are too many possible matches to inspect manually.

Abundance creates selection again.

The applicant adapts to selection.

Then selection has to adapt to the applicant's adaptation.

The résumé was already a compression of a life.

Now it is becoming something else too.

It is a message to the machine deciding which life deserves to become a conversation.

There are markets in which the machine does not merely decide whether a conversation begins.

It decides what offer appears when it does.
