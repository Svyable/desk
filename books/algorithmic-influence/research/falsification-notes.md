# Falsification Notes

This book is an argument about a recurring mechanism, not a claim that every consequential decision is already algorithmic or that every person responds to machine mediation in the same way. These notes record evidence that should weaken, narrow, or overturn claims in the manuscript rather than being edited away.

## What would substantially weaken the central thesis

The thesis depends on three links.

First, algorithmic intermediaries must materially affect downstream exposure, opportunity, price, allocation, ranking, or access in at least some important domains. If independent audits and experiments consistently showed that ranking or recommendation changes barely alter what people encounter, the book would be overstating the intermediary's power.

Second, people must materially adapt to those systems or to their beliefs about them. If creator, worker, applicant, merchant, and organizational behavior proved largely unchanged by ranking, scoring, automated allocation, or machine-readable requirements, the idea of an invisible audience would be more metaphor than mechanism.

Third, adaptation must sometimes change the population of things the system later evaluates. If people adapt but the changes remain superficial and do not alter content, labor practices, applications, products, prices, or institutional behavior, the recursive claim should be narrowed.

The book should not quietly redefine any of those links to make the thesis impossible to falsify.

## Evidence that already narrows the argument

### Exposure does not equal persuasion

The 2023 Science collaboration studying Facebook and Instagram during the 2020 U.S. election found that replacing algorithmic feeds with reverse chronological feeds produced substantial changes in what participants saw and in some forms of platform use, while showing no statistically significant effects on several measured political attitudes. This is important counterevidence to any simple claim that changing a feed necessarily changes a mind.

A later Nature field experiment on X found that enabling its algorithmic feed during a seven-week period in 2023 changed political exposure and shifted some measured opinions in a conservative direction. These findings can coexist. They are evidence against universal claims in either direction. Platform, period, treatment, user population, and political context matter.

The political chapter should therefore distinguish allocation power from persuasion power. A system may strongly influence who encounters a message without reliably determining what that person believes.

### Recommendation can expand opportunity

A recommender can break the monopoly of the follower graph. An unknown creator can reach people who did not previously know the creator existed. Search engines can surface a small publisher without a distribution deal. Reputation systems can let strangers transact. Automated matching can allocate work faster and sometimes more consistently than a human dispatcher.

These benefits are not exceptions to be mentioned grudgingly. They help explain why people accept machine mediation and why optimization around it becomes rational.

### Reputation can reduce discrimination

Field research on online marketplaces has found cases in which reputation information reduces information asymmetry and can reduce discriminatory behavior. A rating system that creates pressure can also create evidence that allows a stranger to be trusted. The manuscript should preserve both effects instead of treating ratings as merely coercive scores.

### Machine legibility can be useful

Structured data is not inherently dehumanizing. A price field that can be parsed accurately may prevent a shopper from receiving stale information. Accessibility metadata helps machines serve people. Standardized employment fields can make comparisons more consistent. Provenance metadata can help distinguish authentic files from altered ones.

The critique is not legibility itself. It is the tendency of consequential selection systems to privilege what they can observe, especially when valuable but harder-to-measure qualities disappear from consideration.

### Optimization is not the same as gaming

People adapt to every institution. Writers learn what editors value. Employees learn what managers notice. Companies learn procurement rules. Candidates learn debate formats. There is nothing uniquely corrupt about learning the constraints of an algorithmic intermediary.

The book needs a sharper distinction among interoperability, legitimate adaptation, strategic optimization, manipulation, and fraud. Those categories overlap in practice but should not be collapsed for rhetorical convenience.

### Personalization is uneven

The FTC's surveillance-pricing work documents firms using granular personal and behavioral data to support pricing and promotional decisions. That does not mean every retailer charges every person a unique price. Many consumer markets still use common posted prices; many systems personalize offers, rankings, or promotions rather than the nominal price itself.

The pricing chapter should use documented examples and preserve this boundary.

### Automated hiring is heterogeneous

Applicant-tracking software, résumé parsers, assessments, ranking systems, and legally defined automated employment decision tools are not one technology. Some employers automate only workflow; others use scoring or recommendation; others keep substantial human review. The applicant chapter must resist the popular but weak claim that a universal AI gatekeeper rejects résumés by secret keyword rules.

### Agentic commerce is early

By 2026, browser-using agents, merchant feeds, agentic commerce protocols, and product-discovery systems are concrete products rather than laboratory thought experiments. They are still early. User supervision is common, coverage is uneven, and most retail commerce is not delegated to autonomous agents.

Chapters 14 and 15 should mark scenarios as scenarios. If agentic shopping adoption stalls, those chapters become a study of an emerging interface, not a prediction that failed because the book quietly changed its timeline.

## Claims the manuscript should never need

The argument does not require any of the following to be true:

- that algorithms are conscious;
- that platforms have one stable objective function;
- that creators know secret ranking weights;
- that engagement optimization always causes outrage;
- that political recommenders determine elections;
- that every worker is managed by software;
- that every job applicant is scored by AI;
- that every consumer receives a personalized price;
- that generative search eliminates the open web;
- that AI agents replace human buyers;
- that transparency reveals a simple rule anyone can follow;
- that all optimization is manipulation;
- that direct human gatekeepers disappear.

If a chapter starts depending on one of these claims, rewrite the chapter.

## Stronger alternative explanations to test

### Ordinary institutional adaptation

Perhaps algorithmic influence is not a new category at all. People have always adapted to gatekeepers and measurement systems. The counterargument is serious. The book must show what is distinctive about scale, opacity, personalization, continuous measurement, rapid updating, and feedback from millions of simultaneous adaptations rather than relying on the mere fact that people respond to incentives.

### Market selection rather than algorithmic selection

A creator changing style may simply be responding to audience taste. A driver changing behavior may be responding to customer demand. A merchant improving product information may be responding to competition. The book should identify what the intermediary contributes instead of assigning all adaptation to the algorithm.

### Human management hidden behind software

Some apparently automated decisions reflect rules, policies, or incentives chosen directly by people. Calling the software powerful can accidentally let the institution that configured it disappear. Whenever possible, identify the human organization, objective, and governance around the system.

### Correlation mistaken for strategic response

People who succeed on a platform may share traits that both audiences and algorithms favor without deliberately optimizing for the machine. Interviews about folk theories demonstrate belief and practice, not necessarily causal success. Do not infer that an adaptation works merely because successful people report using it.

## Future observations worth tracking

The manuscript's strongest future-facing claims should be revisited as evidence accumulates.

For generative answer engines: track whether citation and inclusion become durable sources of traffic, brand discovery, or authority; whether publisher optimization measurably changes answers; and whether systems become less sensitive to obvious GEO tactics.

For agentic commerce: track the share of shopping journeys delegated to agents, the degree of user supervision, the role of merchant feeds and protocols, and whether agents converge on a narrow set of machine-readable product attributes.

For provenance: track whether C2PA-style credentials become widely inspected by platforms, agents, publishers, and consumers rather than merely attached to files.

For work: track whether algorithmic management expands from platform labor into conventional firms and whether consultation, explanation, and appeal mechanisms change worker adaptation.

For politics: continue separating exposure, engagement, attitude, behavior, and electoral effects. A platform can have large effects on one and small effects on another.

## Closing discipline

The book should survive evidence that particular algorithms are less powerful than critics fear. Its strongest claim is not that machines always win. It is that once a machine becomes an important allocator of human attention or opportunity, people begin accounting for that machine in their own behavior. The magnitude, desirability, and durability of that adaptation are empirical questions in every domain.

If the evidence says the machine is a minor audience, call it minor. If humans route around it, show the route. If a system improves outcomes, say so. If a claimed effect disappears under experiment, keep the null result.

A thesis becomes more interesting when it has edges.
