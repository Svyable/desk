# Chapter 9 media enrichment — applicant compression before judgment

## Asset added

- **File:** `../assets/ch09-applicant-compression.svg`
- **Working title:** *The applicant gets compressed before the model judges anything.*
- **Type:** original explanatory process diagram
- **Purpose:** make Chapter 9's central representational problem visible: a hiring system does not encounter a whole person. It encounters material captured by an application process, transformed into model-readable signals, and then used to support or make an institutional action.

## Recommended manuscript placement

Place the figure immediately after the passage ending **“That layer is enough to alter behavior.”** The preceding paragraphs distinguish the many technologies collapsed into the imaginary “robot recruiter”; the figure should arrive before the manuscript turns to applicant adaptation. That sequence lets the visual do one precise job: show the compression pipeline without implying that every employer uses the same tools or that every stage is automated.

### Caption

*The applicant is larger than the representation. Résumés, structured fields, assessments, interview responses, and other submitted evidence can be parsed or transformed into signals used for search, matching, ranking, routing, review, or screen-out. Exact inputs and decision roles vary by employer and system. The consequential point is the handoff: context can be narrowed before evaluation begins, and applicants learn to adapt what they make legible.*

### Alt text

Four-stage diagram titled “The applicant gets compressed before the model judges anything.” A rich human history of jobs, projects, tacit judgment, gaps, informal learning, and context becomes a captured record such as résumé text, application fields, assessments, interview responses, and work evidence. That record can become model-readable signals such as parsed roles and dates, skills, assessment results, derived scores or features, and search or match signals. Those signals can support institutional actions such as search, ranking, shortlisting, routing to human review, or screen-out. A return arrow shows applicants adapting what they foreground or make legible after observing outcomes. A note says exact inputs and transformations vary by system.

## Research grounding

### EEOC — automated employment tools are not one technology

**Source:** U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, *Artificial Intelligence and the ADA*  
https://www.eeoc.gov/eeoc-disability-related-resources/artificial-intelligence-and-ada

The EEOC's current resource page preserves its 2022 technical-assistance materials on the use of software, algorithms, and artificial intelligence to assess job applicants and employees. Those materials are useful here because they reject the idea of a single universal “AI hiring” mechanism and instead address software and algorithmic tools used in assessment and employment decisions.

**Use:** supports the figure's system-neutral language. The diagram says “captured record,” “model-readable signals,” and “institutional action” rather than naming one applicant-tracking system or implying that every workflow performs every transformation.

**Boundary:** the figure is conceptual. It should not be read as a claim that every employer parses résumés, derives scores, uses interviews algorithmically, or automates rejection.

### EEOC — automated systems can make or inform selection decisions

**Source:** U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, *EEOC History: 2020–2024*  
https://www.eeoc.gov/history/eeoc-history-2020-2024

The EEOC describes its May 2023 technical assistance on adverse impact as clarifying that Title VII applies to employers' use of automated systems, including AI, **to make or inform selection decisions**.

**Use:** grounds the final panel's deliberately broad range from search/routing and human review to screen-out. The diagram does not collapse “used somewhere in hiring” into “fully autonomous hiring.”

**Boundary:** legal applicability and liability depend on the actual system, employer conduct, and facts. The figure is explanatory, not legal guidance.

### EEOC — representational error can have substantive consequences

**Source:** U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, *Visual Disabilities in the Workplace and the Americans with Disabilities Act*  
https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/visual-disabilities-workplace-and-americans-disabilities-act

The EEOC explains that algorithmic or AI decision tools can unintentionally screen out applicants with disabilities and gives the example of an AI assessment whose accuracy is reduced by an applicant's visual disability. It recommends information about how technology evaluates applicants and appropriate accommodation where necessary.

**Use:** supports the chapter's deeper distinction between a person's qualifications and the representation a system successfully captures. A bad representation is not merely a cosmetic parsing problem when it affects access to employment.

**Boundary:** do not generalize this disability-specific guidance into a claim that all automated assessments are inaccurate or unlawful.

### Copyright Office — factual grounding versus copied expression

**Source:** U.S. Copyright Office, *What Is Copyright?*  
https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/

The Copyright Office explains that facts and discoveries are not protected by copyright and that public-domain works can be used without permission.

**Use:** documents why factual policy and process statements can inform an original explanatory diagram without reproducing the source's expressive artwork or layout.

## Manuscript research upgrade

The chapter already makes the crucial point that there is **no universal machine standing at the entrance to employment**. The figure should strengthen that point, not undermine it. It therefore uses verbs that preserve different levels of automation: a system may search, retrieve, match, rank, route, support human review, or screen out.

The useful distinction is not “human hiring versus robot hiring.” It is:

1. what evidence the process captures;
2. what representation the system constructs from that evidence;
3. what the institution does with the representation; and
4. how applicants change behavior after learning, or guessing, what travels through the pipeline.

That final return path is what makes this an *Algorithmic Influence* figure rather than a generic recruiting-technology diagram.

## Reader-formatting guidance

- Keep all four stages on one page or screen. Splitting the flow across a page break destroys the main comparison.
- Use full text-column width in EPUB/web and preferably 85–95% of printable page width in print; the labels are designed for landscape-like horizontal reading.
- Keep the caption immediately attached to the figure rather than allowing body copy to intervene.
- Preserve the qualifier **“Exact inputs and decision roles vary by employer and system.”** It prevents a useful conceptual synthesis from becoming a false universal workflow.
- The SVG is grayscale-safe and does not rely on color to encode stages.
- If reduction makes the body labels too small in print, use a full-width figure page rather than shrinking the text below normal caption legibility.
- Do not add vendor logos, screenshots, résumé-scanner interfaces, or decorative AI imagery. They would date the figure, imply product-specific claims, and create needless rights questions.
- If figures are numbered later, number them in the manuscript/caption layer rather than inside the SVG so editions can renumber cleanly.
