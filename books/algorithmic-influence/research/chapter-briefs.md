# Chapter Briefs

These are research and argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should still discover its own narrative shape from the evidence available when it is written.

## Chapter 1 — The Third Person in the Room

**Job:** Establish the book's central change through a familiar act of communication in which a person appears to address another person but distribution is actually mediated by a ranking or recommendation system.

**Questions to answer:** What does it mean to call an algorithm an audience without pretending it is conscious? What decisions can an intermediary make before the intended human sees anything? When does optimization for the intermediary materially alter the message itself?

**Evidence to seek:** First-party recommender descriptions; controlled ranking experiments; creator research on visibility and adaptation; examples in which distribution beyond a follower graph depends on recommendation.

**Counterevidence:** Recommenders can expand reach, diversify discovery, and reduce dependence on old human gatekeepers. The chapter should make the intermediary useful before making it powerful.

**Avoid:** Opening with a dystopian claim that an algorithm secretly controls everyone. The important fact is mediation, not omnipotence.

**Handoff:** If the machine has become a real audience for human effort, ask where people first learned to write for one.

## Chapter 2 — The First Machine You Had to Impress

**Job:** Trace the prehistory of algorithmic influence through web search and search-engine optimization.

**Questions to answer:** How did PageRank and later ranking systems change discovery? Why did publishers begin structuring pages, links, metadata, and content for crawlers as well as readers? Where is the boundary between making information legible and gaming a ranking system?

**Evidence to seek:** Brin and Page's original search-engine paper; Google's ranking-system and spam documentation; historical SEO practices and major anti-manipulation changes; evidence on search traffic dependence.

**Counterevidence:** Search optimization often improves technical accessibility, information architecture, and clarity. Not every attempt to become legible to a search engine is manipulation.

**Avoid:** A chronology of Google updates or an SEO manual.

**Handoff:** Search taught publishers to address a machine, but social recommendation made machine-mediated discovery continuous and personal.

## Chapter 3 — A Feed Without Followers

**Job:** Explain the shift from social graphs toward recommendation systems that can distribute content to strangers based on predicted interest.

**Questions to answer:** How do modern video and social recommenders use candidate generation, ranking, interaction signals, and personalization? What changes for creators when reach is not bounded by the audience they have already accumulated?

**Evidence to seek:** YouTube and TikTok recommender descriptions; recommender-system research; evidence on out-of-network distribution; creator studies.

**Counterevidence:** Follower graphs and editorial choices still matter, and recommendation can help unknown creators reach audiences that old media would never have given them.

**Avoid:** Claiming to know hidden platform weights or presenting creator folklore as platform fact.

**Handoff:** Once distribution is predicted rather than merely subscribed to, every observable signal can become part of production.

## Chapter 4 — The Metric Before the Message

**Job:** Show how measurable proxies migrate upstream into creative decisions.

**Questions to answer:** What does a recommender actually observe? How do watch time, clicks, completion, reactions, and other signals differ from the human qualities they stand in for? When does optimizing a proxy improve the underlying work, and when does it deform it?

**Evidence to seek:** First-party recommender documentation; ranking and social-proof experiments; measurement literature; specific creator practices supported by field research.

**Counterevidence:** Metrics can reveal genuine audience preference and punish self-indulgent work that old gatekeepers protected. Proxy optimization is not automatically degradation.

**Avoid:** Treating engagement as a single universal objective shared by every platform.

**Handoff:** People rarely know the full objective function, which creates an economy of inference around the machine.

## Chapter 5 — Folk Physics

**Job:** Describe the practical theories people construct about opaque algorithms and why even wrong theories can alter an ecosystem.

**Questions to answer:** How do creators infer platform rules? Why do gossip, experiments, consultants, dashboards, and anecdotes become substitutes for access to the model? What happens when millions of people act on the same folk theory?

**Evidence to seek:** Research on algorithmic gossip, imaginaries, visibility labor, creator communities, and platform experimentation.

**Counterevidence:** Platforms do publish some useful guidance, experienced practitioners can discover stable regularities, and not every adaptation is superstition.

**Avoid:** Mocking creators for anthropomorphizing systems whose practical effects they actually have to manage.

**Handoff:** The important consequence is not whether every folk rule is correct but how adaptation changes what the system gets to select from.

## Chapter 6 — Legibility Drift

**Job:** Generalize the mechanism: populations gradually move toward traits, formats, and behaviors that an important intermediary can measure and reward.

**Questions to answer:** What becomes easier to select when it is structured and observable? How do proxies reshape the population they measure? When can legibility improve coordination, and when does it erase valuable qualities that are hard to encode?

**Evidence to seek:** Measurement and organizational research; strategic classification; performative prediction; concrete examples from media, labor, hiring, and commerce.

**Counterevidence:** Better measurement can surface neglected performance and reduce favoritism. Some forms of machine legibility are simple interoperability.

**Avoid:** Turning Goodhart's law into an all-purpose explanation or pretending every metric inevitably gets corrupted.

**Handoff:** Workplaces make the drift especially visible because the score can affect tomorrow's income.

## Chapter 7 — Work for the Score

**Job:** Examine algorithmic management as a form of influence running in both directions between workers and automated allocation systems.

**Questions to answer:** How do dispatch, scheduling, productivity metrics, incentives, and automated warnings affect worker behavior? How do workers learn to anticipate or route around these systems? Which managerial decisions remain human?

**Evidence to seek:** Uber/Lyft studies; OECD evidence on algorithmic management; workplace surveys; platform rules and regulatory records.

**Counterevidence:** Algorithmic tools can improve consistency, matching, logistics, and managerial decisions. Workers can also benefit from reduced arbitrary supervision.

**Avoid:** Calling every software tool an algorithmic boss.

**Handoff:** A particularly compact management signal is the rating supplied by another human but interpreted by a platform.

## Chapter 8 — Five Stars

**Job:** Study reputation systems as machines that turn interpersonal encounters into future access.

**Questions to answer:** How do ratings create trust among strangers? What happens when a review affects search position, account standing, or work availability? Why do compressed scores produce strategic politeness, avoidance, inflation, and pressure?

**Evidence to seek:** Airbnb and platform reputation studies; evidence on discrimination and trust; labor-platform rating systems; experiments on reputation effects.

**Counterevidence:** Reputation can reduce information asymmetry and discrimination, make remote exchange possible, and reward genuinely better service.

**Avoid:** Assuming a five-star scale means the same thing on every platform.

**Handoff:** In employment, the human evaluator can arrive only after a machine has decided which applicants are legible enough to inspect.

## Chapter 9 — The Applicant the Model Can See

**Job:** Explore machine legibility in hiring without exaggerating the prevalence or sophistication of AI screening.

**Questions to answer:** What roles do applicant-tracking systems, parsers, assessments, ranking tools, and automated employment decision tools actually play? How do applicants adapt résumé structure and language? What forms of experience become harder to represent?

**Evidence to seek:** Employer and vendor documentation; NYC Local Law 144; audits and hiring research; careful surveys of adoption.

**Counterevidence:** Structured screening can reduce arbitrary human filtering, widen applicant pools, and make requirements more consistent.

**Avoid:** Saying that a robot hires everyone or that keyword stuffing reliably defeats screening systems.

**Handoff:** Applicants intentionally make themselves machine-readable; consumers often influence pricing systems without realizing they are supplying the signal.

## Chapter 10 — The Price Looking Back at You

**Job:** Examine personalization and surveillance pricing as a case in which behavior becomes input to the offer a person later receives.

**Questions to answer:** What kinds of data are used for segmentation or individualized offers? How much personalization is actually documented? How do advertisers, sellers, and consumers strategically respond to pricing and auction systems?

**Evidence to seek:** FTC surveillance-pricing study and 2026 policy materials; auction and ad-market research; first-party platform descriptions; documented cases rather than speculation.

**Counterevidence:** Personalized offers can lower prices or improve matching, and many markets still use posted prices. Do not imply universal individualized pricing.

**Avoid:** Claiming a specific consumer was charged more because of a protected trait without evidence.

**Handoff:** Politics makes the same mediation problem unusually consequential because exposure itself can become contested power.

## Chapter 11 — Campaigning for the Recommender

**Job:** Investigate political communication as persuasion aimed simultaneously at voters and distribution systems.

**Questions to answer:** How much do recommenders alter political exposure? When does exposure change attitudes? How do campaigns, activists, and media organizations adapt messages to platform distribution?

**Evidence to seek:** Meta 2020-election feed experiments; 2026 X field experiment; TikTok election audit; political amplification studies; campaign practices only where documented.

**Counterevidence:** Large exposure changes do not always change political attitudes. Preserve the contrast between the Meta findings and later platform-specific evidence.

**Avoid:** Partisan advocacy, claims that one platform universally favors one ideology, or causal claims beyond the experiment studied.

**Handoff:** Cheap generative production changes the population competing for recommendation, regardless of ideology.

## Chapter 12 — Synthetic Competition

**Job:** Show what happens when content production becomes cheap enough that optimization can be industrialized.

**Questions to answer:** How does generative AI change the cost of producing variants? What evidence exists for synthetic or agentic accounts competing in recommenders? How might platforms respond when volume itself becomes an optimization strategy?

**Evidence to seek:** Studies of AI-generated content in feeds; platform integrity reports; research on creator-platform co-adaptation; documented automation tools.

**Counterevidence:** Synthetic content can be useful, creative, or high quality, and platforms can adapt ranking and provenance systems. Volume does not guarantee reach.

**Avoid:** Using “AI slop” as an analytical category or assuming all synthetic media is deceptive.

**Handoff:** The next intermediary may not send the user to a ranked list at all; it may synthesize the answer itself.

## Chapter 13 — The Answer Engine

**Job:** Describe the move from being ranked among links to being selected, cited, summarized, or omitted by a generative answer system.

**Questions to answer:** How do AI Overviews, AI search modes, and generative engines gather and present sources? What is generative-engine optimization, and what has actually been demonstrated? What changes when a publisher can influence an answer without receiving a click?

**Evidence to seek:** Google and OpenAI product documentation; GEO research; search and publisher data where credible; experiments distinguishing citation from traffic.

**Counterevidence:** Traditional search remains large, generative systems still depend on web sources, and tactics that work in one benchmark may not generalize.

**Avoid:** Declaring the death of search or presenting GEO benchmark gains as universal platform rules.

**Handoff:** Answer engines still advise people. Agents can begin to transact for them.

## Chapter 14 — Your Customer Has an Agent

**Job:** Explore the early but concrete emergence of AI agents as purchasing intermediaries.

**Questions to answer:** What can browser and shopping agents actually do today? How do merchant feeds, product data, reviews, price, availability, and protocols become inputs to agent choice? What happens when the customer delegates comparison or checkout?

**Evidence to seek:** OpenAI Operator/agent and commerce documentation; Agentic Commerce Protocol materials; merchant-feed requirements; other first-party agent systems where relevant.

**Counterevidence:** Agentic commerce is still early, user supervision remains common, and merchants still sell primarily to humans. Treat future scenarios explicitly as scenarios.

**Avoid:** Writing as though autonomous agents already control retail.

**Handoff:** If machines increasingly compare and transact, sellers need a reputation machines can inspect.

## Chapter 15 — Reputation for Robots

**Job:** Examine the machine-readable layer of trust: structured product data, availability, reviews, provenance, and authenticity metadata.

**Questions to answer:** What information can systems reliably parse? Why do standardized fields and provenance matter? How might organizations begin designing claims for machine verification rather than human persuasion alone?

**Evidence to seek:** Google merchant structured-data guidance; product-feed requirements; C2PA specification; commerce protocols; documented ranking or eligibility requirements where public.

**Counterevidence:** Structured data does not guarantee ranking or trust, and human brand, judgment, experience, and direct relationships remain important.

**Avoid:** Inventing a universal robot reputation score.

**Handoff:** Once people optimize for machine-readable trust, the system can learn from those adaptations and move the target again.

## Chapter 16 — When the Model Learns Your Strategy

**Job:** Make the recursion explicit using performative prediction, strategic classification, and creator-platform co-adaptation.

**Questions to answer:** What happens when predictions change the population being predicted? How do strategic responses degrade or alter models? Can a stable equilibrium exist when both selector and selected are learning?

**Evidence to seek:** Performative-prediction literature; strategic classification; recent game-theoretic work on recommender co-adaptation; empirical cases from previous chapters.

**Counterevidence:** Some systems stabilize, some adaptations are benign, and designers can model strategic response. Recursion does not imply endless chaos.

**Avoid:** Pretending a social feedback loop is a single mathematical law.

**Handoff:** If adaptation is inevitable, governance has to offer more than a demand for perfect transparency.

## Chapter 17 — The Right to Be Illegible

**Job:** Ask what meaningful autonomy looks like when participation increasingly requires machine readability.

**Questions to answer:** When should a person be able to choose a non-profiled feed, human review, appeal, or direct channel? What can transparency accomplish when the full model is too complex to explain as a rule? What tradeoffs accompany less personalization?

**Evidence to seek:** EU Digital Services Act recommender provisions; workplace governance; NYC hiring rules; audit and recourse literature; privacy and contestability research.

**Counterevidence:** Illegibility can make systems worse at matching, accessibility, fraud prevention, or personalization. Choice imposes real costs and may not be feasible everywhere.

**Avoid:** Treating opacity as inherently liberating or transparency as sufficient.

**Handoff:** The final chapter asks what influence becomes when machine audiences are ordinary rather than exceptional.

## Chapter 18 — Influence After the Audience

**Job:** Resolve the argument without converting it into a growth-hacking manual or an anti-algorithm manifesto.

**Questions to answer:** Which forms of influence move from rhetoric toward data quality, interface design, protocol participation, provenance, and system incentives? What remains irreducibly human? When does direct relationship become more valuable precisely because mediation is abundant?

**Evidence to seek:** Synthesis from prior chapters; current agent and commerce systems; examples of durable direct relationships or human override where supported.

**Counterevidence:** Machine mediation will not replace every gatekeeper or every human audience. Many domains will remain hybrid for a long time.

**Avoid:** A list of ten rules for winning the algorithm. End with a structural observation the reader can continue testing as systems change.

**Final note:** Influence has not become less human. It has acquired another surface. Increasingly, the person we hope to reach is preceded by a machine that decides whether, how, and in what form we arrive.
