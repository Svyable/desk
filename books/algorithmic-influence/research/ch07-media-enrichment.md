# Chapter 7 media enrichment — worker/system information asymmetry

## Asset added

- **File:** `../assets/ch07-worker-system-asymmetry.svg`
- **Working title:** *The worker and the system do not see the same workplace*
- **Type:** original explanatory diagram
- **Purpose:** make the chapter's information-asymmetry argument visible without reproducing a proprietary platform interface, commercial dashboard, or third-party figure.

## Recommended manuscript placement

Place the figure in the section beginning **“There is another asymmetry worth noticing.”** It should follow the paragraph contrasting the company's population-level visibility with the worker's view through a phone, before the discussion of experimentation and governance.

### Caption

*The asymmetry is not only that the platform or employer can collect more information. It can connect population-scale observation to allocation, incentives, evaluation, and sanctions, while each worker learns from a narrow stream of personal outcomes. The return arrow is the important part: consequences teach workers what the system appears to reward, and those adaptations become new data.*

### Alt text

Three-column diagram titled “The worker and the system do not see the same workplace.” The first column lists the worker's local view: one shift or route, offers shown, score or dashboard, bonuses and warnings, personal outcomes, and fragments of the rule. The second lists population-scale system signals: many workers, demand and location, timing and acceptance, ratings and outcomes, historical patterns, and experiments and cohorts. The third lists consequences the system can change: task allocation, scheduling, incentives, rankings and targets, access to future work, and review or sanctions. A return arrow shows observed consequences teaching workers what to make legible next.

## Research grounding

### OECD employer survey — algorithmic management is not confined to gig work

**Source:** OECD, *Algorithmic management in the workplace: New evidence from an OECD employer survey* (6 February 2025)  
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/algorithmic-management-in-the-workplace_287c13c4-en.html

The OECD defines algorithmic management as technological tools that fully or partially automate tasks traditionally carried out by human managers. Its survey covered more than 6,000 firms across France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the United States. The study distinguishes tools used to instruct, monitor, and evaluate workers and reports both perceived benefits and trustworthiness concerns.

**Useful manuscript boundary:** this is employer/manager-reported evidence. It is strong evidence about adoption and managers' perceptions, not a direct survey of worker experience.

### OECD 2025 policy brief — prevalence and intensity

**Source:** OECD, *How widespread is algorithmic management in workplaces?* (19 December 2025)  
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/how-widespread-is-algorithmic-management-in-workplaces_cda7a114-en/full-report.html

The policy brief reports that 90% of U.S. managers in the surveyed firms said their firms used at least one tool to instruct, monitor, or evaluate workers; the average across France, Germany, Italy, and Spain was 79%, and Japan was 40%. It also reports that more than three-quarters of U.S. managers said their firms used ten or more of the fifteen tool categories in the survey. Nearly two-thirds of managers using algorithmic-management tools reported at least one concern about their impact on workers, with unclear accountability and difficulty following decision logic among the leading concerns.

**Use:** this is a high-value specificity upgrade for Chapter 7 because it turns “the problem has spread” into a measured statement while preserving geographic limits. Do not generalize these percentages to all employers worldwide.

### EU Platform Work Directive — a concrete governance response to asymmetry

**Source:** Directive (EU) 2024/2831 of 23 October 2024 on improving working conditions in platform work  
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/2831/oj

Chapter III regulates algorithmic management for digital labour platforms. Among other provisions, it requires information about automated monitoring and decision-making systems, addresses human oversight, and provides for review/explanation of certain decisions. Article 9 requires information about categories of data/actions monitored and about categories of automated decisions, main parameters, and their relative importance. Articles 10 and 11 address human oversight and review.

**Use:** this source supports the chapter's claim that useful transparency is not equivalent to source-code publication; governance can instead focus on notice, reasons, oversight, and contestability.

**Boundary:** the Directive applies to digital labour platforms in the EU legal context. It should not be described as a general worldwide rule for all workplaces.

## Reader-formatting guidance

- Keep the SVG at full text-column width in digital editions; the labels are designed for a 1200-pixel-wide source.
- Do not split the three columns across pages. If the print layout cannot keep them together at readable size, move the whole figure to the next page.
- Preserve the return arrow and its label. Without it, the figure becomes a static surveillance diagram and loses the chapter's recursive argument about workers adapting to consequences.
- The asset is grayscale-safe and should not depend on color for meaning.
- Keep the caption immediately below the figure and set it consistently with other figure captions in the book.
- Do not add decorative platform logos. They would create unnecessary trademark and product-version dependencies without adding explanatory value.
