# Chapter 10 — FTC personalized-pricing chronology and evidentiary boundary

## Claim under test

Chapter 10 currently says that on August 31, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission sought comment on a draft enforcement policy statement addressing personalized pricing. The policy statement itself is dated August 19, 2026. The August 31 date on the FTC press release reflects a later correction to that release, not the date the proposal was issued.

For manuscript purposes, use **August 19, 2026** as the issuance date. As of September 7, 2026, the proposal remains open for public comment, and the FTC has extended the comment deadline from September 18 to **September 25, 2026**.

- Federal Trade Commission, “Federal Trade Commission’s Proposed Enforcement Policy Statement Regarding Personalized Pricing,” August 19, 2026: https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/federal-trade-commissions-proposed-enforcement-policy-statement-regarding-personalized-pricing
- Federal Trade Commission, “FTC Seeks Comment on Enforcement Policy Statement Regarding Personalized Pricing,” August 19, 2026; release updated August 31, 2026 to correct an earlier error: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-seeks-comment-enforcement-policy-statement-regarding-personalized-pricing

## What the 2025 study establishes — and what it does not

The FTC’s January 2025 surveillance-pricing materials are strong evidence that the commercial infrastructure exists to use granular consumer information in pricing, promotions, and product presentation. The agency said its initial staff review covered documents from intermediaries serving at least 250 clients and described inputs including precise location, demographics, browsing behavior, shopping history, mouse movements, and abandoned-cart behavior.

That evidence should not be inflated into a prevalence claim about retail as a whole. The FTC described an **initial** review of materials obtained from a limited set of intermediary firms, said the larger 6(b) study was ongoing, and noted that its public examples were hypothetical because confidential business material had to remain aggregated or anonymized.

Primary source:

- Federal Trade Commission, “FTC Surveillance Pricing Study Indicates Wide Range of Personal Data Used to Set Individualized Consumer Prices,” January 17, 2025: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-surveillance-pricing-study-indicates-wide-range-personal-data-used-set-individualized-consumer

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest internal counterevidence comes from then-Commissioners Andrew Ferguson and Melissa Holyoak, who dissented from releasing the January 2025 research summaries. Their objection was methodological and procedural: the summaries were issued less than six months into the study and, in their view, before the Commission had completed the comprehensive analysis that a 6(b) inquiry warranted.

That dissent does not show that the described tools or data practices were fictional. It does weaken any attempt to treat the January 2025 summaries as a settled estimate of market-wide prevalence or consumer impact.

## Editorial implication

The chapter’s central argument survives the correction and becomes stronger when narrowed. The defensible proposition is not that individualized pricing already dominates digital commerce. It is that firms can use personal and behavioral data to differentiate prices, promotions, or product presentation; the FTC has found the capability and practice concrete enough to investigate and regulate; and consumers may respond strategically when they believe their behavior affects what they are offered.

A future manuscript pass should therefore:

- change the proposal date from August 31 to **August 19, 2026**;
- describe the statement as **proposed** and still under public comment as of September 7, 2026;
- avoid using the 2025 staff summaries as a market-wide prevalence estimate;
- keep the chapter’s existing distinction between individualized prices, promotions, rankings, and ordinary dynamic pricing.
