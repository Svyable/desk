# Chapter 11 research note — feed design is a governance variable

## Why this belongs in the chapter

Chapter 11 correctly separates a recommender's allocation power from its persuasion power and warns against treating political ranking as a fixed ideological property. A 2026 registered-report experiment on Bluesky adds a useful third distinction: the relevant comparison is not always algorithmic ranking versus chronology. Different ranking objectives can produce meaningfully different political information environments.

That matters for the chapter's governance argument. If ranking design can reduce distorted social signals without obviously destroying user experience, then the policy problem is not merely whether to permit recommendation. It is what the recommender is built to amplify, how alternatives are evaluated, and what evidence users and regulators can obtain about those effects.

## Primary sources checked

William J. Brady et al., “Redesigning algorithms to intervene on social norm misperceptions during a national election,” *Nature* 655 (2026), 942–956. Published online May 27, 2026. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10536-1

The study randomly assigned 2,000 participants to custom feed-ranking algorithms for eight weeks around the 2024 U.S. presidential election. Its engagement-based feed amplified intergroup, moralized, emotional, and toxic content relative to reverse chronology, with especially large increases in moral outrage and political content. It also increased perceived partisan animosity and reduced accuracy on one measure of perceived prescriptive norms, although the direction of that norm error was not the one the researchers had expected.

The researchers also tested a “diversified extremity” ranking design intended to reduce the influence of extreme users. That feed reduced exposure to intergroup/moralized/emotional and toxic content and improved prescriptive-norm accuracy while maintaining comparable reported platform enjoyment. The study did not find significant changes in participants' own engagement behavior from the engagement-based treatment.

The study is unusually useful methodologically because its Stage 1 protocol was accepted in principle before data collection, limiting researchers' freedom to redesign confirmatory hypotheses after seeing the results.

For the adjacent X evidence already used by Chapter 11, the correct citation is Germain Gauthier, Roland Hodler, Philine Widmer, and Ekaterina Zhuravskaya, “The political effects of X’s feed algorithm,” *Nature* 652 (2026), 416–423, published February 18, 2026. ## Strongest counterargument / boundary

The Bluesky experiment does not establish a universal recipe for healthier political ranking. The platform and participant population are not representative of all social-media users; the intervention lasted about two months; the study examined one U.S. election period; and some preregistered outcomes did not move as expected. Comparable enjoyment is also not the same thing as proving equal long-run retention, revenue, creator incentives, or platform viability.

Nor does the result prove that engagement ranking always amplifies toxic politics. Ranking objectives interact with the content supply, user population, product design, and political moment. A design that works on Bluesky in 2024 may behave differently elsewhere.

The strongest use is therefore narrower: chronology is not the only meaningful counterfactual to engagement optimization, and feed objectives are design choices that can be experimentally compared rather than treated as natural properties of “the algorithm.”

## Editorial use

A later Chapter 11 revision can sharpen the governance section from “every feed is an allocation rule” to a more testable proposition: **ranking objectives are governable variables, and their effects should be compared experimentally rather than argued from labels such as algorithmic or chronological.**

This also improves the chapter's counterevidence. The X experiment shows that a particular algorithmic feed can shift some political attitudes; the Meta election experiments show large exposure changes without corresponding movement in several measured attitudes; and the Bluesky experiment shows that alternative algorithmic ranking can improve some properties of the information environment without a detected enjoyment penalty. Together they argue against both technological fatalism and technological innocence.
