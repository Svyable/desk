# Algorithmic Influence — Research Brief

## Working question

What changes when the first audience for human effort is increasingly a machine that allocates the second audience?

The manuscript uses **algorithmic influence** in a deliberately narrow sense: attempts by people, firms, workers, creators, candidates, merchants, and institutions to shape an algorithmic intermediary so that the intermediary changes human attention, opportunity, ranking, price, access, trust, or behavior.

The phrase itself predates this book. The novelty target is the synthesis and direction of analysis. Much existing work asks how algorithms influence people. This book asks how people adapt to, signal toward, optimize for, resist, and strategically shape the algorithms that influence other people—and how those adaptations become new data that alter the systems again.

## Evidence standard

The book should distinguish four different things that are often collapsed:

1. **Documented system behavior.** First-party descriptions, audits, experiments, or reproducible measurements showing how a system ranks, recommends, scores, allocates, or otherwise mediates outcomes.
2. **Human adaptation.** Ethnography, interviews, field evidence, policy records, or directly observable practices showing people changing behavior because of the system or their beliefs about it.
3. **Causal effect.** Randomized experiments, natural experiments, or unusually strong quasi-experimental evidence showing that changing the intermediary changes exposure, behavior, prices, attitudes, work, or other outcomes.
4. **Interpretation.** The book's synthesis about what these mechanisms mean across domains. Interpretation must not be smuggled in as measured fact.

Use first-party documentation for product mechanics where possible; peer-reviewed research for causal or behavioral claims; regulators and official policy sources for legal obligations; and contemporary reporting mainly to identify cases worth tracing back to primary records.

## Novelty discipline

Do not write that nobody has studied algorithmic influence. Closely related literatures include algorithmic power, algorithmic persuasion, algorithmic visibility, algorithmic management, algorithmic imaginaries, strategic classification, performative prediction, platform optimization, digital reputation, recommender-system audits, search-engine optimization, and generative-engine optimization.

The manuscript's claim is narrower: these literatures become one phenomenon when the machine is treated as an **invisible audience** standing between human effort and human consequence.

The key recursive sequence is:

- a system selects or ranks;
- people observe outcomes and infer what the system rewards;
- people change behavior to become more legible or attractive to the system;
- the changed behavior alters the pool the system sees;
- the system retrains, reweights, or is redesigned;
- people adapt again.

Do not turn this sequence into a pseudo-mathematical law. It is an organizing mechanism whose strength varies by domain.

## Scope

The book spans:

- web search and SEO;
- algorithmic feeds and recommendation;
- creator optimization and algorithmic folklore;
- measurement and proxy effects;
- algorithmic management and gig work;
- ratings and reputation systems;
- automated employment workflows;
- personalized pricing and digital auctions;
- political exposure and recommender effects;
- AI-generated content competing for recommendation;
- generative answer engines and GEO;
- AI shopping and browser agents;
- machine-readable product and provenance data;
- performative prediction and strategic feedback;
- transparency, opt-out, recourse, and design alternatives.

## Claims to avoid

- Algorithms universally manipulate users.
- Engagement optimization always increases polarization.
- Every applicant is screened by AI.
- Every consumer receives a personalized price.
- Creators know how recommendation algorithms work internally.
- A ranking metric directly represents the platform's full objective.
- Generative answer engines have replaced search.
- AI agents already conduct most commerce.
- Structured data guarantees inclusion or ranking.
- Transparency alone neutralizes power.

## Counterevidence to preserve

The manuscript must repeatedly preserve evidence that complicates its thesis.

Chronological-feed experiments on Facebook and Instagram changed exposure and platform use substantially without significantly changing several measured political attitudes during the 2020 U.S. election period. Reputation systems can reduce information asymmetry and enable exchange among strangers. Algorithmic management can improve consistency or managerial decision quality in some settings. Search optimization can improve technical accessibility and make good information easier to discover. Recommendation systems can broaden discovery beyond existing social networks. Structured product data can reduce errors for shoppers. Automated systems can sometimes reduce arbitrary human discretion.

The book is strongest if it shows why people rationally adapt to useful systems before asking what those adaptations do to the surrounding culture.

## Falsification target

The broad thesis would be weakened if, across domains, people did not materially change behavior in response to algorithmic selection; if those adaptations did not alter what is produced or how work is performed; if machine intermediaries did not affect downstream exposure or opportunity; or if direct human relationships consistently dominated algorithmic mediation in the domains where the book claims otherwise.

A second falsification target concerns the future-facing chapters. If AI answer systems and agents remain marginal interfaces and merchants do not need machine-readable, structured, or protocol-based representation to reach customers, the book should treat agentic commerce as an emerging edge case rather than an inevitable destination.

## Voice

Calm, evidence-led narrative nonfiction. Start concrete, widen slowly, and allow uncertainty to survive. Avoid platform-help-manual prose, viral-growth advice, moral panic, invented quotations, and named frameworks that exist only to make the argument look tidy.
