*How We Learned to Persuade Machines to Persuade People.*

The working thesis is that influence has acquired a new audience. For most of human history, persuasion meant changing another person’s mind, attention, trust, or behavior. In an algorithmically mediated world, an increasingly important step comes first: becoming legible, attractive, safe, valuable, or relevant to a machine that decides what other people will see and what options they will receive.

That machine may be a search engine, feed ranker, reputation score, hiring screen, dispatch system, pricing model, recommendation engine, generative answer system, or autonomous agent. The consequence is the same. People begin shaping themselves for systems that stand between them and other people. Creators learn what a recommender appears to reward. Workers learn what a dashboard can measure. Applicants write for parsers before managers. Merchants structure product data for ranking systems and AI shopping agents. Political messages are built not only for voters but for the distribution machinery that decides which voters encounter them.

The book calls this **algorithmic influence** in a deliberately narrower sense than the existing literature on algorithms influencing people. Its subject is the reverse and recursive path: people influencing machine intermediaries so those systems influence human outcomes, after which the resulting behavior becomes new training data and changes the intermediary again.

The novelty claim is therefore about the synthesis and direction of analysis, not about ownership of the phrase. The manuscript treats the algorithm as an invisible audience and asks what happens to culture, work, markets, politics, commerce, and individual identity when succeeding with humans increasingly requires first succeeding with machines.
