# The New Geography of Power

Every age has a map of power.

The map is not identical to the political borders printed in an atlas.

It is the map of what civilization cannot easily do without.

At one time, fertile land dominated that map. Later, navigable ports, coal fields, railways, oil reserves, factories and financial centers acquired disproportionate importance. In the digital age, semiconductor fabrication plants, undersea cables, cloud regions, satellite networks and data centers joined them.

Power accumulates around bottlenecks.

Exponentiality does not remove this rule.

It makes the bottlenecks move faster.

Artificial intelligence is often experienced as weightless. You type words into a box and words return. The interaction feels almost immaterial.

Underneath that box is an industrial system.

Chips were designed, fabricated, packaged and transported. Data centers were financed and constructed. Electricity was generated and delivered. Cooling systems moved heat. Networks carried data. Engineers maintained software. Supply chains supplied replacement parts. Land was permitted. Contracts were negotiated. Capital was committed years before the user typed the prompt.

The cloud has always been a misleading metaphor.

There is no cloud.

There are other people's computers, and increasingly those computers are attached to strategic quantities of energy, capital and political leverage.

As intelligence becomes more computational, the infrastructure that produces computation becomes part of the infrastructure of intelligence.

That changes geopolitics.

A nation that cannot manufacture the most advanced chips may still use them, just as a nation without oil can import fuel. But dependence matters when a resource becomes strategic. Trade routes matter. Export controls matter. Manufacturing concentration matters. Energy prices matter. Political alliances matter.

The more industries depend on synthetic intelligence, the more the supply chain beneath synthetic intelligence resembles a nervous system for the economy.

This does not mean chips become the new oil in any simple sense.

Analogies conceal as much as they reveal. Oil is consumed when used; a chip performs operations repeatedly. Oil deposits are geological; semiconductor capability is manufactured and depends on accumulated knowledge, equipment, materials and organizational competence. Software can sometimes reduce hardware requirements. New architectures can change the balance.

But both resources illustrate the same political fact.

When many valuable activities depend on a constrained input, control of that input creates leverage.

The Age of Exponentiality may therefore produce an unusual combination: capabilities that feel increasingly decentralized at the surface and infrastructure that remains concentrated underneath.

A teenager can access a model from a bedroom.

A tiny company can deploy software globally.

A researcher can rent computation that would once have required a national laboratory.

Yet the underlying systems may depend on a relatively small number of chip manufacturers, cloud providers, model developers, energy systems and network operators.

The individual becomes more powerful and more dependent at the same time.

This is not a contradiction.

It is a stack.

Power can decentralize at one layer while concentrating at another.

The internet already taught us this pattern. It allowed anyone to publish while producing enormous platforms around search, social distribution, commerce and infrastructure. Digital abundance lowered the cost of creation and raised the strategic value of attention, aggregation and control over interfaces.

AI can repeat the pattern at a larger scale.

If producing software, images, analysis and advice becomes cheaper, distribution may become more valuable. If models become widely available, proprietary data may become more valuable. If cognitive capability becomes abundant, reliable access to computation and energy may become more valuable.

The scarcity shadow appears again.

This time it is geopolitical.

Consider energy.

Digital technology encouraged the fantasy that economic growth could become detached from physical resources. Information appeared weightless. Software could scale to millions of users without building a million factories.

Artificial intelligence complicates that story.

Training and running large computational systems require electricity. Data centers require physical construction. Robotics requires materials, motors, batteries and maintenance. Biotechnology requires laboratories and manufacturing. Electrification requires grids, transformers, generation and storage.

The more powerful the digital layer becomes, the more consequential some parts of the physical layer become with it.

Bits do not abolish atoms.

They direct more atoms.

This is why energy abundance sits so close to the center of Exponentiality.

Cheap, reliable, clean energy does more than lower electricity bills. It changes what is economical to compute, manufacture, transport, heat, cool, desalinate and synthesize. It can alter the feasibility of data centers, industrial processes and automated systems.

Energy is not simply another technology in the stack.

It is the budget from which physical possibility is purchased.

Intelligence can optimize a system, but optimization cannot violate the energy balance.

A civilization with abundant intelligence and constrained energy will behave differently from one in which both become abundant.

This interaction is important because technological curves rarely advance independently.

AI can help design materials, operate grids and improve industrial processes. Better materials can improve batteries and electronics. Better energy systems can support more computation. More computation can support better models. Better models can accelerate design again.

The feedback loops are real even when each link contains friction.

The strategic question is who participates in them.

Countries differ enormously in their ability to convert invention into infrastructure.

Some can permit and build quickly. Others possess deep scientific capacity but struggle to construct transmission lines, housing, factories or laboratories. Some have abundant energy but limited capital. Some have capital but depend on imported energy. Some have young populations and weak institutions. Others have strong institutions and aging workforces.

Exponentiality will not flatten these differences automatically.

It may amplify them.

A society that can turn new capability into deployed infrastructure quickly gains not only the benefit of the technology but the learning generated by using it. Deployment creates experience. Experience improves processes. Improved processes reduce cost. Lower cost encourages more deployment.

The learning curve is partly institutional.

Nations learn by building.

This is easy to forget in economies that have become good at invention and slow at construction.

A scientific paper is not a power plant.

A battery breakthrough is not a grid.

A robot demonstration is not a factory full of maintained robots.

A medical discovery is not a treatment delivered to millions of patients.

The distance between invention and deployment is itself a source of national advantage.

Exponentiality compresses some parts of that distance while making the remaining bottlenecks more visible.

This creates a new geography of power inside countries as well.

Places with abundant electricity, fiber connectivity, land, technical labor and permissive infrastructure can become unusually valuable. So can regions containing research universities, manufacturing ecosystems, ports, mineral resources or specialized suppliers.

The digital economy did not make geography disappear.

It changed which geography mattered.

The same will be true again.

A data center cares about electricity and networks more than foot traffic. A semiconductor fab cares about water, power, logistics, specialized equipment and extraordinary process control. A biotechnology cluster benefits from dense networks of scientists, capital and laboratories. Robotics benefits from proximity to manufacturing and customers with physical workflows worth automating.

Different exponential technologies produce different maps.

Their overlap produces strategic terrain.

This matters for cities.

For decades, knowledge economies rewarded dense urban concentrations of skilled people because ideas, capital and opportunity moved through human networks. Remote work and AI can loosen some of those constraints. A small team can collaborate across continents. Expertise can be summoned digitally. Certain professional tasks no longer require everyone to occupy the same expensive geography.

But physical industries pull in the other direction.

Laboratories, factories, energy infrastructure and logistics remain stubbornly located somewhere.

The future may therefore be neither a complete return to industrial geography nor a frictionless digital world.

It may be a recombination.

Cognitive work becomes more geographically fluid while strategic physical infrastructure becomes more important.

This could redistribute opportunity.

A person in a smaller city with excellent connectivity and AI assistance may gain access to work previously concentrated in global capitals. A region with cheap clean energy may attract industries that once cared more about labor costs. A country with strong digital infrastructure may export services without building the traditional institutions through which expertise was once delivered.

But access to a network is not the same as equal power within it.

Platforms can extract rents. Infrastructure owners can set terms. Governments can restrict access. Models can encode languages and cultures unevenly. Capital can flow toward already dominant centers.

Exponentiality can widen opportunity while widening dependency.

That duality should make us suspicious of simple stories about democratization.

A tool can empower its user and strengthen its provider simultaneously.

The same dynamic appears in national security.

As intelligence, autonomy and biological capability become easier to access, forms of power once limited to states or large institutions may diffuse toward smaller groups.

The cost of certain capabilities can fall faster than the cost of defending against them.

This is not unique to AI. Cheap drones altered battlefields because guidance, cameras, batteries, communications and manufacturing had all improved enough to make capabilities available at scales and prices that changed tactics. Cybersecurity has long demonstrated how small groups can exploit systems built by much larger institutions.

The pattern is important.

When capability democratizes, defense cannot assume that dangerous power remains concentrated.

Yet concentration creates risks of its own.

A small number of systems can become single points of failure. A technical error, cyberattack, political decision or supply interruption can propagate through many dependent organizations.

Efficiency encourages consolidation because shared infrastructure is cheap.

Resilience often requires redundancy, which looks wasteful until something breaks.

Exponentiality therefore creates a tension between optimization and resilience.

The fastest system is not always the safest system.

The cheapest supply chain is not always the most robust.

The most centralized model may be easier to improve and harder to replace.

The most integrated platform may create extraordinary convenience and extraordinary dependency.

Civilizations must decide how much inefficiency they are willing to purchase in exchange for options.

This is a question engineers understand as redundancy.

Economists may call it resilience.

Strategists call it security.

Individuals experience it as whether the system still works when something goes wrong.

The Age of Exponentiality will reward speed, but survival still rewards slack.

This applies to companies, countries and people.

A company dependent on one platform may scale quickly and discover later that it has no bargaining power. A country dependent on one supplier may enjoy lower costs until geopolitics changes. A person dependent on one employer, one skill or one digital identity may be efficient in normal times and fragile in transition.

Optionality is expensive before it becomes priceless.

The new geography of power will therefore be drawn not only by who possesses the most advanced technology.

It will be drawn by who controls bottlenecks, who can build, who has energy, who can coordinate, who can attract talent, who can maintain trust and who can recover from failure.

Some of these advantages are technological.

Others are institutional.

That is why a small country with excellent governance can outperform a larger one in certain domains, and why a wealthy country can still discover that money cannot quickly purchase permitting competence, industrial ecosystems or public trust.

Capability is not capacity.

A society can possess a technology without possessing the ability to absorb it.

This distinction may become one of the defining differences among nations.

The winners of earlier industrial eras were not simply the places where inventions occurred. They were places able to finance, standardize, manufacture, deploy and improve systems at scale.

The same is likely to be true again.

Exponentiality makes invention more powerful.

It also raises the value of absorption.

A country that can integrate new technologies into energy, education, health, manufacturing and government may compound advantages across all of them. A country that cannot may watch globally available capabilities accumulate without converting them into local productivity or resilience.

The technology can be universal while the benefit remains uneven.

This is another reason the future will not be determined by model capability alone.

The most capable intelligence in the world is economically irrelevant to a factory without power, a school without connectivity, a hospital without functioning administration or a citizen who cannot afford access.

Infrastructure translates possibility into lived reality.

The map of Exponentiality is therefore physical and digital at once.

It contains chip fabs and classrooms, transmission lines and models, ports and protocols, mines and mathematics, laboratories and laws.

Its most important territories may not look dramatic.

A transformer factory.

A fiber route.

A permitting office that works.

A technical college producing electricians.

A standards body agreeing on an interface.

A research institution trusted enough to evaluate a new technology without every result becoming a tribal dispute.

These are not the images usually chosen to represent the future.

They are how the future becomes operational.

The geography of power has always followed the infrastructure beneath civilization's most valuable capabilities.

Now those capabilities are beginning to compound across domains.

The map will move accordingly.

The places that matter will be the places where curves can connect.
