## Reader promise

In 1963 the United States successfully put hundreds of millions of hair-thin copper dipoles into medium Earth orbit so radio signals could bounce from an artificial belt instead of depending on the natural ionosphere. The experiment worked. It also provoked astronomers, exposed the weakness of promises that orbital hardware would simply disappear, and helped force an argument the space age had barely learned how to phrase: when one nation changes the sky for its own security, who else gets a vote?

This book follows Project West Ford from nuclear-war communications logic through antenna physics, launch failure, the successful 1963 belt, astronomical opposition, the transition to active communications satellites, the debris that outlived the experiment, and the emerging legal idea of harmful interference. Its claim is not that West Ford single-handedly created modern space law. It is that the project is an unusually clean early case of a problem we still have: engineering can make a shared environment technically useful before institutions have decided what responsible alteration of that environment means.

## Distinct contribution

West Ford is usually remembered as a Cold War curiosity: America once scattered copper needles in space. That version misses the collision. The same design features that made the system militarily attractive—passivity, redundancy, distributed geometry, resistance to attack—made its environmental footprint difficult to control and its externalities international. The book treats the communications system, the astronomy dispute, the orbital-dynamics assumptions, and the legal response as one technical-political object rather than separate anecdotes.

## Strongest competing explanation

The strongest contrary reading is that West Ford was a tightly bounded experiment whose astronomers' feared observational damage did not materialize, whose useful dipoles mostly dispersed and decayed, and whose lasting debris was an engineering imperfection rather than proof of a larger governance failure. Modern space-law and debris regimes have many causes. The manuscript preserves that explanation and asks a narrower question: what did the episode reveal early about predicting, consenting to, and cleaning up environmental effects in a domain no state owns?

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified/declassified/documented:** the Air Force/Lincoln Laboratory program; the 1961 deployment failure; the May 1963 successful deployment; dipole dimensions and intended 8 GHz resonance; demonstrated coast-to-coast communications; organized astronomical objections; National Academy review; later cataloging of West Ford-associated clumps; Article IX's consultation language in the 1967 Outer Space Treaty.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** the exact influence of West Ford on specific treaty wording; the full internal decision record behind military continuation after scientific objections; the precise surviving population attributable to each launch where catalog provenance is uncertain.
3. **Disputed or not established:** claims that the belt caused major lasting astronomical damage; that the United States intended permanent unilateral occupation of orbital space; that every surviving high-altitude fragment attributed in popular accounts is conclusively West Ford material.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** West Ford can be read as an early prototype of an engineering pattern in which strategic resilience is purchased by distributing hardware into a shared environment while cleanup, consent, and long-tail monitoring remain secondary design variables.
