# Chronology

- **1951** — MIT Lincoln Laboratory is established in the U.S. air-defense R&D ecosystem.
- **1957** — Sputnik demonstrates artificial Earth orbit and accelerates U.S. military/civil space activity.
- **1958** — West Ford concept emerges at Lincoln Laboratory as a distributed passive orbital-scatter communications approach.
- **1960** — Echo 1 demonstrates large passive satellite reflection for communications experiments; active and passive satellite architectures are both under rapid development.
- **21 October 1961** — First West Ford launch reaches orbit, but the dipoles fail to disperse as intended.
- **1961–1962** — International astronomical opposition becomes organized; concern centers on optical/radio interference, unilateral environmental alteration, secrecy and precedent.
- **9 July 1962** — Starfish Prime high-altitude nuclear test creates artificial radiation belts and damages/degrades satellites, changing the political context for environmental reassurance in space.
- **10 July 1962** — Telstar 1 launches, demonstrating an increasingly attractive active-satellite communications path.
- **6 May 1963** — NASA contemporary chronology records U.S. Air Force announcement that another West Ford attempt is imminent.
- **May 1963** — Second West Ford deployment places a payload of roughly 480 million copper dipoles into a near-polar medium-Earth orbit around 3,650 km mean altitude.
- **May–June 1963** — Belt spreads and closes around Earth over roughly forty days; ground teams observe scatter and conduct communications experiments.
- **1963** — Coast-to-coast communications via the belt demonstrate the orbital-scatter concept; effectiveness falls as the belt disperses.
- **1963** — Syncom era and other active communications-satellite advances reduce the strategic uniqueness of a passive orbital belt.
- **1964** — Program technical results and historical perspectives appear in major engineering/scientific literature; a Space Science Board review reports no significant observed optical/radio astronomy interference from the belt.
- **1964** — Shapiro uses the unusual dynamics of West Ford dipoles to bound orbital effects associated with charged-particle drag.
- **1966 onward** — Space Surveillance Network catalogs small objects associated with the second West Ford experiment; aggregate remnants persist much longer than the nominal isolated-dipole clearing story.
- **19 December 1966** — UN General Assembly adopts the Outer Space Treaty.
- **27 January 1967** — Outer Space Treaty opens for signature.
- **10 October 1967** — Outer Space Treaty enters into force; Article IX includes due-regard and consultation language for potentially harmful interference.
- **Late 1960s onward** — Active communications satellites become the dominant architecture; West Ford remains an experimental dead end but continues as an orbital population.
- **2013** — NASA Orbital Debris Program Office reports that 144 small objects associated with the second experiment had been cataloged since 1966 and that 46 clumps remained in orbit at the time of publication; cites post-mission conclusion that only 25–45% of planned dipoles dispersed properly.
- **2025** — Modern historical scholarship revisits West Ford as an episode in astronomical mobilization and early space-environmental politics.

## Chronology cautions

The 2013 object count is date-specific, not a 2026 current count. The next research pass should reconstruct present catalog status rather than silently carrying the number forward. Treaty chronology does not establish treaty causation. Starfish is contextual and must not be merged technically with West Ford.
