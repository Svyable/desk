# The Cleanup Assumption

The phrase "it will come down" has done extraordinary work in spaceflight.

Sometimes it is exactly right. Atmospheric drag removes low-orbit objects. Solar radiation pressure and other perturbations can change the trajectories of high area-to-mass debris. Deliberately choosing materials, altitudes and disposal orbits can reduce persistence. Orbital mechanics is not merely the source of debris problems; it is one of the tools used to solve them.

Project West Ford depended on that fact before orbital-debris mitigation became a formal discipline.

The intended individual dipoles were tiny enough that solar radiation pressure mattered greatly. Once properly dispersed, they would not behave like compact satellites. The belt's usefulness would decline as the population spread and orbital parameters evolved. Much of the released material did in fact leave the useful configuration and eventually the orbital environment.

The trouble was that "the dipoles" were not one population.

NASA's later Orbital Debris Program Office summarized a post-mission conclusion that only about 25 to 45 percent of the planned 480 million dipoles dispersed properly. The remainder were believed to persist in clumps. Space surveillance cataloged many objects associated with the second experiment. Decades later, dozens of clumps remained in orbit.

The cleanup model had been attached to the intended state.

This is a specific technical failure mode, not a philosophical indictment. If one isolated needle has an orbital lifetime L under a given set of perturbations, a cluster of needles with different area-to-mass ratio, attitude and geometry does not inherit L by moral association. The object has changed.

The lesson is embarrassingly simple and repeatedly expensive: mitigation is part of the system and can fail with the system.

A spacecraft designed to deorbit itself after mission completion must still have functioning power, attitude control and propulsion at the moment disposal occurs. A passivated upper stage must actually vent or discharge stored energy. A drag sail must deploy. A controlled reentry must retain enough command authority to target the burn. A constellation replacement policy must account for vehicles that die before they can execute disposal.

West Ford's version was mechanical. The wires had to separate.

The distinction between an individual filament and a clump also shows why environmental claims should specify denominators. "Most needles eventually decayed" may be true in terms of individual wires if a large dispersed population cleared. "Some West Ford-associated objects remain after six decades" may also be true because a small number of clusters persisted. Neither statement alone tells us whether the mission's disposal concept performed acceptably.

Acceptability depends on what was promised, what risk the remainder creates, and what alternatives existed.

The long-lived clumps are easy to dramatize because they remain. Their actual collision risk is another matter. At roughly medium-Earth-orbit altitudes, the environment is large, and the objects are small relative to many spacecraft. West Ford is not responsible for the dominant modern debris hazard. Fragmentations, explosions, collisions, spent stages and inactive satellites contribute far more mass and dangerous trackable objects to crowded orbital regions.

Historical importance should not be confused with contemporary risk contribution.

The clumps matter because they make a promise testable across time. The experiment was defended partly on transience. Residue survived the era that made the experiment relevant, the Cold War itself, and multiple generations of communications satellites.

The artifact outlived the rationale.

That is a peculiar category of environmental consequence. It is not catastrophic enough to force immediate remediation and not transient enough to disappear. It becomes inherited infrastructure without function.

Modern orbital-debris policy is full of inherited objects. Old rocket bodies, dead satellites and fragments remain because earlier missions operated under different assumptions. Current operators maneuver around history. New standards are partly an attempt to stop today's missions from becoming tomorrow's inherited conjunction burden.

West Ford sits near the beginning of that genealogy, but we should be careful not to retrofit today's debris rules onto 1963. There was no twenty-five-year post-mission guideline. No globally mature tracking catalog existed in its modern form. The collision environment was sparse. The expected harm of a few long-lived clumps would have appeared small.

The more relevant historical question is whether the failure mode was foreseeable in kind. After the 1961 deployment failed to disperse, yes. The exact fraction, lifetime and later catalog population may not have been. But "the material can remain clumped" was no longer hypothetical.

The second mission improved the dispenser and succeeded enough to form the belt. It did not eliminate the branch.

This gives the West Ford story a harsher edge than a pure unknown-unknown narrative. The environmental tail emerged from a mechanism the program had already encountered.

Again, that does not settle whether proceeding was unreasonable. Experimental programs often continue after failures when mitigations are improved. Aviation would not exist if every failed prototype prohibited a second flight. The correct comparison is between the value of the knowledge, the severity and probability of the residual risk, the adequacy of mitigation, and the consent structure around whoever bears the downside.

West Ford's residual risk was probably modest. Its consent structure was primitive.

That combination explains why the case echoes. It offers no dramatic victims. No city was struck. No observatory was blinded. No astronaut was killed by a copper cluster. The experiment is useful precisely because governance had to emerge before disaster supplied moral clarity.

The clumps were not an apocalypse.

They were an invoice.
