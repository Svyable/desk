# Resilience Has a Shape

Military systems are often described by function: communications, warning, navigation, command. But resilience is physical before it is doctrinal. It has a shape.

A buried cable is resilient against radio jamming and vulnerable to cutting. A high-frequency link is geographically flexible and vulnerable to propagation. A centralized relay is efficient and vulnerable to single-point failure. A constellation distributes failure across spacecraft but creates coordination, launch and debris burdens. A West Ford belt distributed the relay function so widely that there was almost nothing in orbit worth attacking individually.

Every architecture chooses what kind of failure it would rather have.

West Ford's answer was radical distribution. Its designers accepted low efficiency, demanding ground equipment and a fading medium in exchange for survivability. The resulting system is a useful corrective to the sloppy idea that technological progress always moves toward greater intelligence in each component. Sometimes progress removes intelligence from the component and pushes capability into arrangement.

A copper dipole has no software. Half a billion dipoles can constitute infrastructure.

This is strategically attractive because arrangement can be difficult to target. It is environmentally difficult for the same reason. An opponent cannot switch off the belt by destroying one node. Neither can the owner.

This symmetry deserves more attention in security engineering. The words "decentralized," "distributed," "redundant" and "resilient" carry positive associations because they describe systems that survive failure or attack. They say nothing about recoverability.

Recoverability is a different axis.

A resilient system may be easy to recover if its components are controllable and replaceable. It may be hard to recover if resilience comes from uncontrolled abundance. The most extreme version is a self-propagating system: survival and removal become mirror problems. West Ford was not self-replicating, but it occupied the same conceptual corner in milder form. Its strength was that no individual element mattered.

That means cleanup could not prioritize individual elements either.

Modern cybersecurity provides an instructive but imperfect analogy. A distributed botnet is resilient because taking down one machine does little. Defenders attack command infrastructure, credentials or economic incentives instead. In orbit, there may be no analogous control layer once passive material is dispersed. Physics becomes the only shutdown protocol.

This is why the initial state matters so much.

Project West Ford placed termination into orbital dynamics. The design accepted loss of control because the expected natural evolution was favorable. That was rational only to the extent that the deployed material actually entered the modeled regime.

The clumps did not.

The broader lesson is not to distrust passive safety. Passive safety can be excellent precisely because it does not depend on commands or active systems. The lesson is to identify what passive safety assumes about configuration. A reactor may shut down under gravity if control rods fall correctly. A satellite may decay if its orbit is low enough. A pressure vessel may vent if a relief path remains open. A copper dipole may be cleared efficiently if it is a copper dipole rather than part of a larger aggregate.

Configuration is part of the safety case.

West Ford also reveals why strategic requirements can push systems toward environmental externalities. The military wanted a communications capability that an enemy could not easily disable. One route to that goal was to put more state into the shared domain: more scatterers, more distribution, less dependence on a single platform.

This pressure has not disappeared. Proliferated low-Earth-orbit architectures are attractive partly because they complicate attack and provide redundancy. Distributed sensing reduces dependence on exquisite satellites. Mesh networks survive node loss. The national-security logic is sound enough that it will continue driving orbital population upward.

The governance problem is therefore not caused by frivolous commercial expansion alone. Security itself rewards proliferation.

That makes restraint politically hard. A state asked to reduce orbital population may hear a request to accept vulnerability. A commercial operator asked to reduce constellation size may hear a request to accept worse coverage or economics. Astronomers and debris experts can describe externalities accurately and still fail if they do not engage the architecture's reason for existing.

West Ford's history suggests a better route: find a competing architecture that preserves the valued resilience with fewer external costs.

Active satellite networks eventually did that well enough to make permanent dipole belts unattractive. They did not eliminate all externalities. They changed their form and improved controllability.

This is the technological complement to governance. Rules matter, but alternative designs matter too. If regulation simply prohibits the architecture with the strongest strategic advantage, pressure to evade or weaken the rule will be intense. If engineering supplies another path to the same mission, environmental restraint becomes easier.

The inverse is also true. When a new architecture offers an overwhelming capability advantage, governance can lag because nobody with power wants to slow deployment.

West Ford happened at a moment when alternatives were racing fast enough to save policymakers from that fight.

Today some space systems may not offer the same convenience.

The problem is not whether resilience is good. It is what shape we choose to give it, and what remains when that shape stops serving us.
