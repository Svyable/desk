# Back Matter

Project West Ford sits in an unusually good public record for a Cold War military technology because the core physical experiment was openly described in technical literature and later institutional histories. That does not make every surrounding policy inference equally secure.

The manuscript uses four evidence classes throughout. Documented program facts include the 1961 and 1963 launches, the dipole dimensions and intended resonant scattering, the successful 1963 communications experiment, organized astronomical objections, subsequent scientific review, and the later tracking of West Ford-associated clumps. Credible but incomplete claims include the exact pathway by which the controversy influenced particular international legal wording and the attribution of some small debris populations to the first mission. Disputed claims include strong versions of lasting astronomical harm or an accomplished plan for permanent operational belts. Speculative synthesis includes the book's comparison between West Ford's distributed-resilience logic and modern population-scale orbital architectures.

## A note on quotations

The manuscript presently paraphrases rather than reproduces colorful historical quotations except where a publication-stage source audit can verify exact wording and context against primary documents. This is deliberate. West Ford has accumulated memorable lines in later retellings, and the book does not need them badly enough to accept uncertain attribution.

## What would change the thesis

The book's central synthesis should be narrowed if primary records show that long-lived aggregation was both quantitatively anticipated and accepted through a genuinely international consultation process before the 1963 launch, or if later catalog analysis shows that the supposed long-duration West Ford residue has been substantially misattributed. Conversely, stronger causal documentary evidence linking West Ford deliberations to Article IX or COSPAR mechanisms would justify strengthening the institutional-history chapters. Neither outcome changes the core engineering record that a successful distributed communications experiment produced off-nominal aggregates whose orbital lifetime differed sharply from the intended individual dipoles.
