# Due Regard

International law often arrives after engineers have already demonstrated the verb.

Launch. Orbit. Transmit. Detonate. Observe. Interfere.

The lawyers then have to decide who may do what to whom in a domain whose physical properties do not respect the metaphors inherited from land and sea.

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty is careful about ownership. Outer space is not subject to national appropriation by sovereignty, use, occupation or other means. States remain internationally responsible for national activities. They retain jurisdiction and control over registered space objects. Nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction may not be placed in orbit.

Then Article IX reaches for a softer phrase: due regard.

States are to conduct activities with due regard to the corresponding interests of other states. They are to avoid harmful contamination and adverse changes to Earth's environment from extraterrestrial matter. And if a state has reason to believe a planned activity would cause potentially harmful interference with another state's peaceful exploration or use of space, it is to undertake appropriate international consultations before proceeding.

The language is less satisfying than a numerical standard because it has to govern technologies that did not yet exist.

Project West Ford is frequently placed in the prehistory of this consultation norm. The connection is plausible and supported by historical scholarship describing the astronomy controversy as part of the political environment from which harmful-interference principles emerged. But causation should be stated carefully. The Outer Space Treaty was negotiated amid superpower nuclear concerns, reconnaissance disputes, lunar competition, scientific cooperation, United Nations diplomacy and multiple environmental questions. No single experiment wrote Article IX.

West Ford did something more modest and perhaps more durable: it supplied a vivid example of why consultation might be necessary.

The United States could argue that its belt would not materially harm astronomy. Astronomers could argue that they should not have to learn whether that prediction was right only after deployment. Article IX does not resolve the scientific disagreement. It creates a procedural response to uncertainty when another state's activities may be affected.

That is what consultation is for.

The provision is weak compared with domestic environmental law that developed later. It does not establish an international space environmental agency. It does not create a mandatory permit with a quantified debris budget. It does not define "potentially harmful" with engineering precision. It does not hand astronomers a veto.

Its power lies in admitting that unilateral technical confidence is not the only relevant fact.

This was not obvious in the first years of the space age. The launch state had the rocket, the payload and the telemetry. If its territory was not physically trespassed upon, what exactly could another country object to? West Ford supplied one answer: interference with use can occur without territorial intrusion.

Radio astronomy had already learned this on Earth. A transmitter beyond an observatory's fence could make a frequency unusable. Space generalized the problem. A state could alter the shared operating environment from thousands of kilometers away.

The treaty's framers did not create a property right in an unchanged sky. That distinction is critical. "Due regard" is relational. It assumes multiple legitimate uses. Communications, reconnaissance, science, navigation and exploration may coexist. The obligation is to consider corresponding interests and consult under conditions of potentially harmful interference.

This is why West Ford is more relevant to modern constellations than the easy analogy suggests.

The lesson is not "large populations in space are illegal." They are not. The lesson is that scale can produce effects outside the operator's service boundary, and those effects belong in the governance of the activity rather than being dismissed as somebody else's preference.

Modern examples are more complicated than copper needles. Optical brightness from satellites can affect ground-based astronomy. Radio transmissions can affect protected bands or create out-of-band concerns. Collision avoidance imposes coordination burdens. Reentries can create casualty and atmospheric questions. Launch cadence changes local and upper-atmospheric emissions. None of these effects has one universally accepted threshold that converts engineering into law.

The Article IX problem is therefore still alive because the treaty gave us verbs—consider, avoid, consult—more readily than metrics.

That is not necessarily a failure. International law often begins with norms that technical communities later operationalize through standards, coordination procedures and practice. Orbital-debris mitigation guidelines, frequency allocation, conjunction data exchange and licensing requirements can be understood as later machinery around the broader idea that space is not a domain in which technical capability alone establishes permission.

West Ford's historical importance is strongest when stated at this level. It helped make visible a category of conflict that later law would need to handle.

The project also shows the limits of law as memory. Article IX entered into force after the West Ford launches. It could shape future conduct; it could not recollect the clumps. Treaties govern decisions better than residues.

This temporal asymmetry is another reason environmental design matters. By the time a legal consensus forms, physical systems may already be operating for decades. Regulation is slow partly because evidence and diplomacy are slow. Orbital objects are patient.

The result is a recurring race between architecture and norm.

Engineers can often deploy a new space capability within a few development cycles. International consensus can take years. Commercial scaling can happen faster still. If governance waits until effects are undeniable, the installed base itself becomes part of the bargaining power.

West Ford never reached that stage because the architecture lost technologically. Imagine the opposite. Suppose active satellites had failed and passive dipole belts had become indispensable. By 1967, would the international community have negotiated strict limits on a communications infrastructure the United States already depended on? Perhaps. The politics would have been harder.

Obsolescence made restraint cheap.

That should make us suspicious of histories that credit law alone for good outcomes. Sometimes a disputed technology simply loses the market before its governance problem matures.

Article IX remains because the category outlived the hardware.

When one actor changes the operating conditions of a domain used by others, technical success does not close the discussion. It starts another one.
