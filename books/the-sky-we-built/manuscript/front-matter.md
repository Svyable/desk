# The Sky We Built

*Project West Ford and the First Argument Over Who Gets to Alter Orbit*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a species of Cold War project that becomes funny with distance. A nuclear-powered tank. A pigeon-guided missile. A plan to heat the Arctic. The joke is usually temporal: people once believed this? Project West Ford has suffered that treatment because its central object sounds like something invented for a satire about technocracy. In 1963 the United States put hundreds of millions of tiny copper wires into orbit so that radio signals could bounce off them.

That sentence is true. It is also inadequate.

The wires were dipole antennas, cut to a length chosen for microwave scattering. The project grew from a real military problem. Long-distance communications depended on submarine cables and on a natural ionosphere that could vary, fade, be disturbed by solar activity, and in wartime be jammed or otherwise rendered unreliable. A passive belt of resonant scatterers could not be switched off by destroying a conventional satellite because there would be no single satellite to destroy. It was redundancy made physical, distributed around a planet.

The first deployment failed. The second worked well enough to carry communications between ground stations. Astronomers had objected before success, not because they were afraid of copper as a mystical contaminant, but because a shared observational environment was being modified for one nation's strategic purpose with consequences nobody could yet measure confidently. Engineers replied, with some justification, that the belt had been designed to disperse and decay and that the feared interference was limited. Both sides had real arguments. The interesting part begins there.

West Ford arrived at the wrong time to become infrastructure and the perfect time to become precedent. Active communications satellites improved rapidly. Telstar had already demonstrated a different path. The artificial ionosphere became unnecessary almost as soon as it proved itself. But some of the copper did not behave as the clean design story required. Dipoles remained in clumps. Objects associated with the project were cataloged for decades. NASA's later orbital-debris history would still be discussing West Ford half a century after the communications experiment had become obsolete.

The project therefore sits at an awkward hinge. It is early enough that the legal and environmental vocabulary of space is still forming. It is late enough that the strategic logic is recognizably modern. Build resilience by distributing capability. Remove single points of failure. Assume the environment can absorb what you place in it. Treat predicted decay as a form of cleanup. Promise that the side effects will be below the threshold anyone else can reasonably object to.

This book is not an argument that Project West Ford caused the Outer Space Treaty, created orbital-debris law, or permanently injured astronomy. Those claims are too easy and, in the first case, historically unserious. The treaty had many parents: superpower rivalry, nuclear danger, legal scholarship, United Nations negotiation, the rapidly expanding set of national space activities. Article IX's language about due regard, harmful contamination, harmful interference, and consultation belongs to that larger history. West Ford belongs in the room because it made one of the abstract problems concrete before the law had settled around it.

Nor is the book a disguised polemic against large constellations, military spacecraft, or engineering in orbit. The analogy to the present is useful only if the differences survive. West Ford's dipoles were passive and almost absurdly simple. Modern satellites maneuver, transmit, coordinate, fail in more complicated ways, and can provide services of immense public value. The point is not that Starlink is copper needles with software. The point is that the political problem exposed by West Ford did not disappear when the hardware improved: one actor's architecture can alter the operating conditions of a domain used by everyone else.

The evidence posture here is deliberately uneven because the evidence itself is uneven. The existence, dimensions, launches, communications experiments, scientific objections, and much of the later debris record are documented. The degree to which any particular diplomatic sentence descends from West Ford is harder to establish and should not be asserted by narrative momentum. The motives of officials are not filled in when the record is silent. An astronomer's fear is not proof that the feared harm occurred. An engineer's prediction is not proof that the environment complied.

That last distinction is where the book lives.

A project can be scientifically competent and still produce a governance problem. A critic can be directionally right about a governance problem while materially wrong about a predicted physical effect. An experiment can work and still lose. A cleanup plan can be embedded in orbital mechanics and still leave residue. The sky can be nobody's property and still become somebody's infrastructure.

Project West Ford was a communications experiment. It was also an argument conducted in copper.
