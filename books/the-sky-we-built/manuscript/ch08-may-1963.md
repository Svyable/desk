# May 1963

The second Project West Ford launch took place in May 1963.

This time the dispenser did what the project needed it to do.

A payload containing roughly 480 million copper dipoles reached a nearly polar orbit at a mean altitude of about 3,650 kilometers. The wires began to separate. Over the following weeks, the cloud spread along the orbit until the belt effectively closed around Earth. Lincoln Laboratory later described a formation period of about forty days and an eventual density on the order of five dipoles per cubic kilometer.

The numbers make the belt sound more substantial than it looked. Five objects per cubic kilometer is sparse in ordinary experience. The point was not to construct a metal wall. It was to arrange enough resonant scatterers across a vast common volume that a high-gain transmitter and receiver could extract a usable path through collective weakness.

The experiment required patience because the useful medium did not exist at launch. It emerged as the orbital cloud elongated. The team tracked the distribution and measured radar echoes. Communications tests connected stations in Massachusetts and California. The West Ford ground infrastructure included powerful microwave equipment and large antennas; the space segment itself remained almost perversely simple.

The contrast is easy to miss. The belt was dumb. The intelligence lived on the ground.

A modern reader accustomed to spacecraft packed with processors can interpret passivity as primitiveness. In West Ford, passivity was the feature. The orbital hardware could not be hacked, powered down or commanded into the wrong mode because it could not do anything. It merely occupied space with the right electrical length.

The cost of that robustness was that the system could not be corrected in flight.

The second launch therefore represented a point of no return more literal than a conventional mission's. As the dipoles separated, the experiment became progressively less recoverable and more operationally useful. Control and function moved in opposite directions.

This is the sort of trade that systems engineers usually hide inside phrases such as "graceful degradation" or "distributed resilience." West Ford makes it visible. A network can be made harder to disable by making its elements less individually important. But a network built from individually unimportant elements can also become harder to remove. A system's attack surface and its cleanup surface are sometimes the same geometry viewed from different sides.

Contemporary reporting followed the deployment closely. Astronomers continued to object. The United States emphasized the experimental nature of the belt and the expectation that it would not persist as a permanent ring. National scientific review became part of the legitimacy story. The experiment proceeded anyway.

Then the signals arrived.

The communications performance established that the concept was not merely theoretically clever. Lincoln Laboratory's later institutional history says the dipole belt supported data rates up to about 20,000 bits per second when density was favorable. That rate is microscopic by modern standards and substantial for a passive experimental relay in 1963. The link was never going to become the internet. It did not need to. It had to show that engineered orbital scatter could carry information over continental distance.

The experimenters had built a second ionosphere in the narrowest operational sense that mattered.

Not a plasma. Not an atmosphere. Not a continuous shell. A manufactured scattering medium.

The success should have strengthened the case for further development. Instead, it arrived while the technological landscape was moving away from the problem West Ford had solved.

Active communications satellites were becoming credible at extraordinary speed. Telstar 1 had launched in July 1962 and relayed television and other communications across the Atlantic. Relay 1 followed. Syncom demonstrated the promise of near-geosynchronous communications. Active spacecraft had vulnerabilities West Ford's passive belt avoided, but they also had capabilities the copper cloud could never acquire: controlled frequencies, amplification, routing, evolving payloads, clearer ownership and, eventually, commercial scalability.

A passive belt is impressive when the alternative is an unreliable ionosphere. It is less attractive when the alternative is a purpose-built repeater in a predictable orbit.

This timing produced a peculiar historical outcome. West Ford became a successful demonstration of a technology that was already losing its strategic monopoly on plausibility.

That is common in technological transitions. A system is often perfected just as another architecture changes the cost curve. The mature steam locomotive appears after internal combustion has begun to rewrite transport. The best analog recorder arrives while digital storage becomes cheap. The engineering achievement remains real. The infrastructure decision moves elsewhere.

West Ford's obsolescence was therefore not evidence that the experiment had been foolish. It was evidence that the space communications problem had more than one solution and the design space was moving quickly.

The artificial belt also exposed one advantage of conventional satellites that had not been fully priced at the beginning: accountability attaches more naturally to discrete objects. A communications satellite has a known bus, operator, orbit and mission. If it fails, the failure can be tracked as an object. If it interferes, responsibility can at least be assigned in principle. A diffuse belt crosses the line between equipment and environment.

This difference would matter more after West Ford stopped mattering as communications technology.

The successful mission answered the headline engineering question in 1963. Yes, a belt of copper dipoles could be deployed. Yes, radio signals could be scattered from it. Yes, the effect would decline as the belt dispersed.

The remaining questions moved more slowly.

How much of the payload had actually dispersed as intended? How long would the malformed remainder stay aloft? Had the astronomers' feared interference occurred? What institutional changes followed the controversy? What did the United States owe other users of orbit for an experiment that had technically succeeded and strategically expired?

The belt was closing around Earth at roughly the same moment the case for ever building an operational one was closing too.
