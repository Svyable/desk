# The Clumps

Space surveillance turned West Ford from a finished experiment into an inventory problem.

The distinction matters. A scientific program ends when the measurements are taken, the papers are published and the apparatus is shut down. An orbital object ends when physics removes it.

Those clocks can differ by decades.

NASA's Orbital Debris Program Office later summarized the surviving West Ford population in unusually concrete terms. Since 1966, the U.S. Space Surveillance Network had cataloged more than a hundred small objects associated with the second experiment. A post-mission investigation concluded that only roughly a quarter to somewhat under half of the intended 480 million dipoles dispersed properly. The rest were believed to remain in clumps. In the debris office's 2013 account, forty-six clumps remained in Earth orbit, with only a minority having perigees below 2,000 kilometers.

The exact present-day population changes as objects decay and catalogs improve, so a manuscript should not freeze a 2013 count into an eternal fact. The historical point is stable: a nontrivial set of aggregate objects survived far longer than the intended dispersed belt.

The clumps are interesting because they expose a mismatch between human and orbital categories.

To the program, a clump was a deployment defect. To the surveillance network, it was an object. To later debris analysts, it was part of an environment. To the public, it became evidence that America had "left needles in space," a phrase that erases the distinction between free dipoles and aggregated material but captures the emotional truth of persistence.

The catalog does not care why an object is there.

This is one of the brutal equalities of orbital mechanics. A dead satellite from a celebrated science mission and a fragment from a failed military payload both become conjunction partners. Intent disappears. Mass, area, velocity and trajectory remain.

That is why debris governance eventually became less interested in the moral category of the mission and more interested in measurable post-mission states. Was the object passivated? What is its expected orbital lifetime? What is the probability of successful disposal? Can it maneuver? What happens if it fragments? Who tracks it?

West Ford predated the language but not the problem.

The clumps also complicate a famous defense of space environmental interventions: space is very large. It is. At 3,000-plus kilometers altitude, the volume of an orbital shell is enormous. The chance that a single small object causes a collision in a given year can be very low. That is why one must resist sensational claims about West Ford as a major present-day collision menace.

But "space is large" is not a complete policy because orbital use is cumulative and structured. Useful orbits are not uniformly distributed through infinite emptiness. Objects cross, cluster in inclinations and altitude regimes, share launch corridors and create conjunction burdens whose significance grows with population. An object with tiny annual risk can remain long enough for integrated risk to matter. Millions of objects with tiny individual risk can create a non-tiny environment.

West Ford itself did not create the modern debris crisis. It demonstrated the scaling logic early.

The surviving material also reveals a strange asymmetry in engineering evidence. Successful dipole dispersion was visible through the communications experiment. Failed dispersion became visible through long-term surveillance. The project therefore needed two observing systems separated by institutional purpose and time.

The first measured function.

The second measured residue.

This separation still haunts technology assessment. Product teams are funded to measure performance. Environmental agencies, researchers or future operators measure persistence. The people who receive praise when the system works are not always the people maintaining the ledger when it stops working.

The orbital catalog is a ledger of abandoned optimism.

That phrase should not be romanticized. Many old objects were launched under reasonable assumptions and with real public value. Some persist because no feasible disposal option existed. The point is that orbit externalizes memory. It keeps hardware after institutions have reorganized, contractors have changed, missions have been forgotten and budgets have moved on.

A surviving West Ford clump does not know that active satellites won the communications competition. It does not know the Cold War ended. It does not know that the laboratory that designed it went on to build far more sophisticated systems. It continues under gravity.

This makes orbital debris unusually resistant to political closure. A government can declassify a program, acknowledge a mistake, change a regulation and still leave the physical object untouched.

The contrast with information secrecy is useful. Classified knowledge can become public in a single decision. Classified hardware in orbit may become public and remain physically inaccessible.

West Ford's long tail therefore belongs to the history of state secrecy in an odd way. The program itself became well documented. Its physical remnants were less secret than merely remote.

The clumps are sometimes invoked as proof that the project "polluted space." That wording is defensible in a colloquial sense but insufficiently precise for this book. Pollution usually implies harmful introduction. The observed harm from these specific clumps is modest and may be mostly conjunction burden and environmental occupancy rather than recorded damage. The stronger claim is not that they caused catastrophe. It is that the experiment failed to leave orbit as completely as its transient design story implied.

That narrower claim is enough.

A society does not need every environmental mistake to become a disaster before it learns to design for failure states.

The question then moves from engineering to obligation. Once long-lived residue exists in a domain no state owns, what does "due regard" require?
