# The Reactor in the Rock

Radioactivity is usually introduced as a hazard. In the deep biosphere it can also be part of a food chain.

That sentence sounds more exotic than the chemistry is. Uranium, thorium and potassium isotopes decay inside rock. The emitted radiation transfers energy into nearby water. Water molecules split, producing hydrogen along with oxidizing products. The exact yields depend on radiation type, mineral surfaces, water chemistry and the structure of the host material. None of this requires biology.

Then biology arrives and behaves like a thief at an unattended power station.

Hydrogen is an electron donor. If an environment also supplies an electron acceptor—sulfate, carbon dioxide, ferric iron or another suitable compound—a microorganism may be able to harvest the free-energy drop between them. At Mponeng, radiolysis became interesting because the surrounding mineral chemistry could help maintain a coupled redox system. The organism did not eat radiation. It used chemical disequilibria that radiation helped generate.

The distinction matters. “Life powered by nuclear decay” is a useful shorthand and a bad mechanism.

The deep-Earth version became more consequential in 2021, when Justine Sauvage and colleagues measured radiolytic hydrogen production in marine sediments and showed that common sediment minerals can substantially amplify hydrogen yields compared with pure water. Their *Nature Communications* analysis estimated that radiolysis is likely to become especially important in old marine sediments after fresh organic matter from the surface has been consumed. The global power contribution is small compared with the Sun-fed organic flux arriving at the seafloor, but the geometry differs: buried organic carbon is spent mostly near the top, whereas radiolytic production continues through wet sediment.

A weak source distributed through a large dark volume can matter more than a strong source that never reaches it.

This is the first principle of sunless habitability. Energy density and energy persistence are separate properties.

Surface organisms live in an economy of pulses. Daylight, seasons, blooms, carcasses, storms and sedimentation produce large transfers. Deep organisms often inhabit an economy of drips. Radioactive decay is not generous, but it is dependable on geological timescales. Serpentinization can continue wherever fresh reduced rock and water are brought into contact. Diffusion can sustain chemical gradients long after any surface event is forgotten.

A human brain has trouble respecting small numbers multiplied by ten million years. Biology does not have that prejudice.

The challenge is maintenance. A cell is not a mineral. Proteins hydrolyze. DNA is damaged. Membranes leak. Molecular gradients relax. Even an organism that does not divide must spend energy to remain an organism. Deep-life research therefore forces a question biology can often ignore in rich environments: what is the minimum continuous power needed for cellular identity not to dissolve into chemistry?

There is no single accepted answer. Maintenance requirements differ across organisms and conditions, and laboratory cultures are poor analogues for cells adapted to geological starvation. Estimates derived from deep sediments suggest energy fluxes so low that conventional growth-based intuitions become misleading. Some cells may divide on timescales of years, centuries or longer. Others may spend prolonged periods in states we do not know how to categorize cleanly.

The epistemic trap appears quickly. If a calculation shows that radiolysis can supply enough hydrogen to meet an estimated basal power requirement, it does not prove a viable ecosystem. One must still provide the other half of the redox couple, nutrient elements, a solvent, tolerable temperature, access pathways and mechanisms for waste removal. Thermodynamic feasibility is a permission slip, not an organism.

Yet permission slips matter in planetary science because they remove disqualifications.

Mars offers the clearest example. The surface has spent billions of years losing atmosphere and water while accumulating radiation damage. It is an excellent place to conclude that present-day Mars is dead if one identifies the planet with the top few centimeters. But radioactive isotopes do not care about ultraviolet light. Several common Martian rock types contain uranium, thorium and potassium. Wherever liquid groundwater persists, radiolysis can operate behind meters or kilometers of radiation shielding.

In 2021 Jesse Tarnas and colleagues modeled this possibility using compositions of Martian meteorites as proxies for crustal materials. They calculated that radiolysis could generate redox resources sufficient, in favorable water-bearing settings, to support sulfate-reducing microbial densities within the broad range observed in portions of Earth's deep subsurface. The result was explicitly about energetic habitability. It did not establish groundwater. It did not establish microbes. It did not establish that the meteorite source regions are representative of the most accessible future landing sites.

But it changed what “dead Mars” has to mean.

A sterile surface cannot answer for a wet fracture several kilometers down.

This is frustrating from an engineering perspective. The environments most protected from radiation and desiccation are also the least accessible to a robotic mission. A one-meter drill is transformational compared with scooping dust, and trivial compared with a terrestrial mine. Mars exploration has therefore spent decades in a strange observational regime: increasingly sophisticated instruments interrogating a biologically hostile surface partly because that is the surface spacecraft can reach.

The resulting literature sometimes confuses a sampling limit with a planetary conclusion.

Radiolysis also expands the logic beyond Mars. Any rocky body containing long-lived radionuclides and water has at least the ingredients for the mechanism. Whether the resulting energy flux matters depends on composition, age, mineral catalysis and competing sinks. On an icy moon with a rocky core, tidal heating may dominate the geophysical story while radiolysis supplies only a background trickle. In old sedimentary worlds, the trickle may become decisive precisely because everything faster has run out.

This leads to a broader distinction between primary and secondary habitability.

Primary habitability asks whether the environment receives a major energy source: stellar photons, volcanic heat, tidal dissipation. Secondary habitability asks whether local geochemistry converts that source—or stored geological disequilibrium—into chemical work a cell can actually capture. The Sun can blaze overhead while a buried organism starves. A dark fracture can remain viable because radioactive decay keeps splitting water molecule by molecule.

The unit that matters to life is not planetary power. It is accessible free energy at the membrane.

Deep biosphere work has also exposed a mistake in how we narrate “resources.” Hydrogen is often presented as if finding it were equivalent to finding food. But hydrogen can accumulate when consumption is absent. Methane can be produced abiotically. Sulfate can be present without an electron donor. Carbon may be plentiful in forms that are chemically difficult to use. A habitat is not a bag of ingredients. It is a circuit.

The circuit requires flow.

That word becomes especially important on icy moons. Cassini's detection of molecular hydrogen in Enceladus's plume was significant because hydrogen can be produced when water reacts with reduced rock under hydrothermal conditions, including serpentinization-like processes. The moon appears to maintain contact between ocean water and a rocky interior. Hydrogen was therefore evidence of an ongoing geochemical engine, not merely an inventory item frozen from formation.

The biological temptation was immediate because terrestrial methanogens can consume hydrogen and carbon dioxide. But geochemical hydrogen does not imply methanogens any more than an electrical outlet implies a lamp. One has to resist letting the existence of an energy source silently populate the environment.

This book will resist it repeatedly.

There is another reason. The more we learn about abiotic energy generation, the harder some biosignatures become to interpret. Hydrogen, methane and small organics can emerge from water-rock reactions. Serpentinization can create reducing conditions favorable to life while simultaneously generating molecules that resemble products of life. The same geology that makes a habitat plausible can make evidence of inhabitants ambiguous.

Habitability and detectability can work against each other.

This is one of the deep biosphere's most useful lessons for astrobiology. Surface oxygen on Earth is powerful partly because its biological production overwhelmed relevant abiotic sources and because the atmosphere integrates enormous planetary fluxes. Deep ecosystems may never create such a global signature. Their chemistry stays close to the rock, and the rock can counterfeit much of it.

A future mission that detects methane beneath Mars will therefore inherit an argument, not end one. Isotopic ratios, accompanying gases, mineral context, spatial gradients and temporal behavior will matter. Even isotopic patterns traditionally associated with metabolism can sometimes emerge through nonbiological fractionation. The burden rises with the claim.

The strongest evidence would be convergent: complex molecular structures, repeating cell-like morphologies, strong isotopic disequilibria, metabolic turnover, genetic polymers or an independently replicated combination difficult to generate geologically. No single universal test exists because life uses chemistry rather than a magic substance unavailable to rocks.

The reactor in the rock is therefore double-edged. It teaches us how a biosphere can live where sunlight never arrives. It also teaches us that geology can perform enough biochemical-looking work to embarrass anyone searching for one miraculous molecule.

That is not pessimism. It is a better target.

Instead of asking whether a world contains “the ingredients for life,” ask whether it maintains a durable redox circuit, whether the circuit can support known or plausible metabolisms, and whether any observed chemistry departs from the abiotic circuit in a way that survives adversarial explanation.

That sequence is slower than a headline. It is also how one avoids mistaking the power supply for the tenant.

On Earth, we know the tenant exists because we can bring cells out of the fracture. On Mars, Europa and Enceladus, we are still largely inspecting wiring diagrams.

The diagrams have become much more interesting.
