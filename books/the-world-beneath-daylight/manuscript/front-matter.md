# The World Beneath Daylight

## A note on the title

This is a book about places where biology, if it exists, has no use for noon.

Earth taught us to associate life with surfaces because our own conspicuous biosphere occupies one. Forests, reefs, crops and oxygenated air dominate the visual record. The deep biosphere complicates that picture. Microbial life occupies sediments, fractures, aquifers and hydrothermal systems far beyond sunlight. Some of those communities still depend indirectly on surface products. Others can run local metabolic economies on chemical energy generated by rock, water and natural radioactivity.

That difference is the argument.

The book moves from the best terrestrial evidence outward: deep mines, ancient subseafloor sediments, radiolysis, serpentinization and low-power microbial persistence. Only after the terrestrial mechanisms are established does it ask what they permit on Mars, Europa and Enceladus.

Permit is not prove.

An energetically habitable Martian fracture is not a Martian bacterium. Hydrogen in Enceladus's plume is not methanogenesis. Europa's ocean is not an ecosystem. A phosphate-bearing ice grain is not evidence of a cell. Where the evidence changes class, the prose should change with it.

Throughout the manuscript, claims are treated under four working categories:

1. **Documented / directly established.** Peer-reviewed measurements, recovered terrestrial organisms, mission observations, established geophysical results and other evidence that does not depend on the book's synthesis.
2. **Credible but incomplete inference.** Model-supported interpretations, global extrapolations, likely source assignments and habitat calculations whose premises are serious but whose conclusions remain conditional.
3. **Disputed / unresolved.** Claims with material competing explanations, inconsistent measurements, uncertain provenance or inadequate replication.
4. **Speculative synthesis / open conjecture.** The book's larger implications: that hidden microbial biospheres may be systematically undercounted, that surface habitability can be much shorter than biological habitability, or that subsurface life could be common while technological life remains rare.

The fourth category is where the author is allowed to take intellectual risk. It is not allowed to impersonate the first.

## Scope

This is not a general book about extremophiles. It is about one specific collision of evidence: Earth possesses a large, persistent, sometimes geochemically self-sufficient deep biosphere at the same time that several of the Solar System's most interesting candidate habitats are hidden beneath hostile surfaces.

The question is what changes when those facts are treated as one scientific problem.

## Source discipline

Links and source notes are collected in `research/evidence-and-source-log.md`. The manuscript relies on peer-reviewed papers, NASA/JPL mission material, USGS publications and other primary or authoritative sources where available. Popular summaries are not used to upgrade a disputed claim.

The book does not imply possession of non-public mission data, proprietary drilling results or restricted material.
