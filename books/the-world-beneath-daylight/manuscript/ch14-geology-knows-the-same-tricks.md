# Geology Knows the Same Tricks

The best chemical evidence for life is produced by chemistry.

That is the problem.

Methane can be biological. It can also emerge from abiotic water-rock reactions. Amino acids are used by every known organism. They also occur in meteorites. Lipid-like molecules can form without cells. Carbon isotope fractionation can be created by metabolism and by nonbiological processes under some conditions. Mineral structures can resemble microfossils. Repeating organic patterns can arise through catalytic chemistry.

Life does not own its ingredients.

The search for deep or extraterrestrial biology is therefore less like finding a unique substance and more like distinguishing a crime scene from a chemistry set after both have been left running for a billion years.

This is especially severe in the environments that look most habitable. Serpentinization produces hydrogen and can produce methane and other reduced carbon compounds. Hydrothermal systems synthesize organics. Radiation creates oxidants and radicals. Mineral surfaces concentrate molecules. The same geological engine that supplies free energy to cells creates false positives for cells.

Habitability manufactures ambiguity.

Earth geobiology learned to cope by asking for context. A methane sample is more informative when its isotopes, associated gases, temperature, source rock and spatial gradients are known. A putative microfossil is stronger when morphology aligns with organic chemistry, mineral context and independent specimens. A lipid biomarker is persuasive when its molecular structure is difficult to generate abiotically and contamination is excluded.

The strongest cases are consilient.

This is why no future Mars headline should read “methane means life,” regardless of how clean the detection becomes. Methane is a target because biology is one possible source and because temporal or spatial variability may indicate active production. Geology retains standing.

Mars has made the lesson concrete. Telescopic, orbital and rover measurements of atmospheric methane have not always agreed. Curiosity has reported low background levels and episodic spikes at Gale crater, while the Trace Gas Orbiter has set stringent upper limits at higher atmospheric scales. Reconciling local production, destruction, transport and measurement remains difficult.

The discrepancy is scientifically interesting without needing biology.

A subsurface source could be geological or biological. Rapid destruction mechanisms might be required under some interpretations. Surface adsorption and release can create variability. The right response is not to choose the most exciting mechanism; it is to design measurements that discriminate among them.

Enceladus presents a richer version of the same problem. Methane in the plume, molecular hydrogen, silica particles and organics support hydrothermal activity. On Earth, methanogens consume hydrogen and carbon dioxide in analogous energy regimes. But abiotic methane synthesis is also possible. Statistical analyses of plume chemistry can compare biological and nonbiological models, yet their conclusions depend on assumptions about hydrothermal conditions and kinetic pathways.

Model preference is not microbial microscopy.

This distinction becomes even more important when researchers use words like “biological likelihood.” Bayesian frameworks can be useful because they force priors and likelihoods into the open. But priors for independent alien life are poorly constrained. A model can appear quantitative while hiding the largest uncertainty in an assumed probability.

Precision does not rescue unknown priors.

What, then, would count as strong evidence?

One useful approach is to look for multiple kinds of improbable organization at once. Life creates bounded structures, selective molecular inventories, homochirality or strong chiral biases, polymer sequences, coupled metabolic disequilibria and replication. No single feature is perfectly universal, but a correlated package can overwhelm separate abiotic explanations.

Imagine plume grains from Enceladus containing amino acids. That is interesting. If the amino acids show a strong consistent enantiomeric excess, interest rises, though abiotic chiral asymmetry is not impossible. If the same grains contain long polymers built from a restricted alphabet, with recurring motifs and isotope patterns indicating kinetic fractionation, the case strengthens sharply. If different flybys reproduce the pattern and blanks remain clean, contamination becomes harder to sustain.

Evidence compounds when failure modes are independent.

That last clause matters. Ten measurements produced by the same contaminated reagent do not make ten lines of evidence. Ten papers analyzing the same ambiguous sample are not independent replication. A biosignature matrix must track shared dependencies.

This is one place where controversial sciences provide a useful negative example. Claims can look multiply supported because testimony, media reports and analyses trace back to a single source. Physical biosignature science can make the same error if every “independent” signature derives from one extraction protocol.

Dependency is the hidden architecture of certainty.

Deep-Earth research also warns about contamination from modern organic matter. Drilling introduces lubricants, polymers, microbes and atmospheric gases. Laboratory plastics shed compounds. Reagents contain trace DNA. Human skin contributes cells. Modern sequencing is sensitive enough to find a contaminant that arrived in quantities invisible to older techniques.

Sensitivity without provenance can make instruments better at detecting ourselves.

The problem becomes philosophically strange on Mars. Terrestrial contamination could survive in protected niches. A future mission may encounter organisms related to known Earth bacteria because earlier spacecraft carried them, because natural panspermia connected the planets, or because contamination occurred in the laboratory. Phylogenetic similarity would therefore be powerful evidence of biology and weak evidence of alien origin.

Second genesis requires a second argument.

The cleanest proof would be biochemistry genuinely independent of terrestrial ancestry: a different genetic polymer, different coding architecture, opposite or mixed chirality in fundamental biomolecules, or another coherent information system. But life constrained by carbon chemistry may converge on some of Earth's solutions. Independent origin need not look maximally alien.

We therefore need both biological evidence and ancestry evidence.

Europa and Enceladus offer lower contamination histories from human missions, but future landers and penetrators will change that. Planetary protection is not only about protecting hypothetical ecosystems ethically; it protects the evidentiary value of discovery.

The false-positive problem extends to morphology. Mars exploration has repeatedly generated public excitement over shapes in rocks that resemble biological structures. Human perception is superb at finding familiar forms. On Earth, accepted microfossils require contextual petrography and chemistry because minerals can make cell-like shapes.

A circle is not a bacterium.

This seems obvious until the sample comes from another planet.

The same caution applies to “complexity.” Abiotic chemistry can be complex. Meteorites contain thousands of organic compounds. Hydrothermal systems create rich reaction networks. Complexity becomes biologically interesting when it is organized, selective and dynamically maintained away from equilibrium.

Life is not complicated dirt. It is constrained chemistry with history.

That suggests a different mission objective. Instead of looking for one biomarker, look for chemical selection.

Terrestrial life uses a restricted subset of possible amino acids, sugars and lipids. Enzymes produce narrow product distributions. Genetic systems use repeating backbones and finite monomer alphabets. Metabolism couples reactions into networks. Abiotic synthesis often produces broader distributions, though mineral catalysis can also be selective.

A life-detection instrument should therefore measure distributions, correlations and structures rather than binary presence.

This requires better mass resolution, separation chemistry and sample handling than many prior missions carried. It also requires enough material. A sparse biosphere may generate signals below detection thresholds. Sampling strategy becomes part of evidence quality.

The strongest terrestrial analogy is not a field geologist finding one odd molecule. It is a clinical laboratory receiving a sample with chain of custody, running independent assays, comparing controls and asking whether multiple signatures converge on one process.

Alien life deserves at least that standard.

There is an emotional cost to this rigor. Every exciting molecule becomes provisional. Every possible biosignature acquires an abiotic literature. Discovery appears to recede as instruments improve.

That is progress.

A biosignature capable of surviving geology's best counterfeits will mean more than one accepted because we were impatient.

The universe has had billions of years to make strange chemistry without life. We should assume it is good at the job.
