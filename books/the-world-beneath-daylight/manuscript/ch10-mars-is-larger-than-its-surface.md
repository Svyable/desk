# Mars Is Larger Than Its Surface

The first successful landers made Mars look sterile because they landed on Mars.

That sentence is deliberately unfair to Viking. The 1976 landers carried sophisticated biology experiments for their time, and the results remain historically important. The labeled-release experiment produced responses after nutrient solution was added to Martian soil, while other instruments failed to detect organic compounds at expected levels. The combined interpretation favored reactive soil chemistry rather than life. Later discoveries complicated parts of the chemical context—perchlorates can destroy or transform organics during heating, for example—but no consensus has emerged that Viking detected Martian organisms.

The deeper mistake happened culturally. Mars became the surface the landers touched.

The modern surface is an excellent place to kill an unprotected terrestrial microbe. Atmospheric pressure is low. Liquid water is generally unstable. Ultraviolet radiation reaches the ground. Galactic cosmic radiation penetrates farther. Strong oxidants and perchlorate salts complicate organic preservation. Temperature cycles are severe.

Move downward and each term changes at a different rate.

A few centimeters block ultraviolet light. Meters of soil and rock increasingly attenuate particle radiation. Pressure from overburden rises. Temperature becomes steadier. At sufficient depth, geothermal heat may bring ice toward melting conditions, though present-day Martian heat flow is low and local. Salts can depress freezing points. Fractures can concentrate fluids.

This is why the question “Is Mars habitable?” has become less useful than “Where, when and at what depth might Mars maintain liquid water and usable chemical energy?”

Earth's deep biosphere supplies mechanisms rather than answers.

Radiolysis is one. Tarnas and colleagues used Martian meteorite compositions to model radiolytic production of hydrogen and oxidants in water-bearing Martian crust. Their 2021 result showed that, from an energy perspective, some rock compositions could support sulfate-reducing microbial populations at densities comparable with portions of Earth's deep subsurface. The necessary clause was groundwater.

We do not know the distribution of present liquid groundwater on Mars.

Radar interpretations of possible subglacial liquid water beneath the south polar cap generated excitement and dispute. Some bright radar reflections initially interpreted as liquid have alternative explanations involving clays, saline ice or other materials. The controversy is useful because it demonstrates how badly we want a buried lake and how many physical models can inhabit the same remote signal.

A credible search must therefore combine methods.

Seismic measurements can constrain crustal structure and perhaps fluid-bearing layers. Electromagnetic sounding can detect conductive materials. Radar can image interfaces. Heat-flow data narrow thermal models. Mineral maps identify salts and alteration products. Surface geology reveals faults and ancient hydrothermal systems. None alone finds a microbe.

The InSight mission transformed Martian geophysics by measuring marsquakes and interior structure, but its heat-flow probe did not achieve its intended depth. The failure was mechanical rather than conceptual: the probe could not generate enough friction in unexpectedly cohesive soil. It is a small episode with a large lesson for subsurface astrobiology.

The obstacle is not imagining a drill. It is drilling.

On Earth, kilometer-scale boreholes depend on massive rigs, drilling muds, replacement bits, casing, pumps, crews and power systems. A Mars lander has a launch mass budget and no mechanic. Even reaching two meters with clean samples is difficult. Kilometer access is a different technological class.

That engineering gap shapes scientific rhetoric. Because deep extant life is inaccessible, Mars missions often emphasize ancient habitability and preserved biosignatures in rocks exposed at the surface. Perseverance explores Jezero crater, an ancient lake basin, collecting samples chosen partly for their potential to preserve evidence of past environments and perhaps ancient life. This is excellent science. It is not the same question as whether anything is alive under Mars today.

Past-life exploration and present-life exploration diverge geographically.

Ancient lake muds are good archives. Modern deep fractures are potential refuges. A mission optimized for one may never encounter the other.

This divergence matters because a negative result is easy to overgeneralize. Suppose future laboratories examine returned Jezero samples and find no convincing biosignature. That would weaken particular hypotheses about ancient life in the sampled environments. It would not establish that Mars was always sterile, much less that no subsurface biosphere exists elsewhere.

Planetary nulls have boundaries.

The same is true in reverse. Finding ancient organic structures would not prove a modern biosphere. Mars can preserve history after ecology ends.

A serious deep-Mars program would therefore need to choose candidate sites for present habitability rather than surface preservation. Regions with young volcanism or elevated heat flow become interesting. Deep faults can offer permeability. Mid-latitude ground ice may reduce drilling distance to water but can remain frozen. Salt deposits can lower melting points while creating osmotic stress. Hydrogen availability depends on rock composition and radiation chemistry.

The target is a conjunction, not a landmark.

There is another reason to go deep: preservation from radiation. Cosmic rays break complex organic molecules over geological time. Burial shields them. A sample recovered from sufficient depth could contain better-preserved evidence even if no organism remains alive. This creates a useful overlap between past-life and present-life goals at intermediate depths.

But burial introduces geological age ambiguity. Organic material can be indigenous, meteoritic, transported by groundwater or introduced by the spacecraft. Carbonaceous meteorites continuously deliver organics to Mars. Abiotic geochemistry creates more. Any detection must reconstruct provenance.

This is where the deep-Earth contamination literature becomes immediately relevant.

Terrestrial borehole microbiologists use tracers to detect drilling-fluid intrusion. They compare microbial communities in drilling fluids and cores. They sterilize equipment, take procedural blanks, sequence contaminant backgrounds and preserve pressure or anaerobic conditions when possible. Mars drilling will need similar discipline with higher stakes, because the contaminant is us.

Forward contamination can create the worst possible scientific outcome: a living terrestrial organism recovered from a Martian subsurface sample after our own drill placed it there.

The deeper the mission reaches, the more consequential sterilization becomes. Surface radiation can naturally kill many contaminants over time. A protected wet subsurface habitat might preserve them. Planetary-protection rules therefore are not ceremonial. They preserve interpretability.

Mars adds another complication. Terrestrial life and hypothetical Martian life could share biochemical features even if they originated independently, simply because carbon and water impose constraints. Or they could share actual ancestry if material has moved between Earth and Mars through impact ejecta over billions of years. Martian meteorites prove that rocks cross the gap.

A discovery of DNA-based life on Mars would be extraordinary and immediately raise a phylogenetic question: second genesis or cousin?

If sequences nested convincingly within terrestrial lineages, contamination would be the first explanation. If they formed a deep but recognizable relationship, panspermia would enter. If they used a different genetic polymer or coding system, independent origin would become easier to defend. The first alien microbe may produce more arguments about ancestry than about existence.

This is why the cleanest discovery would come with multiple orthogonal signatures and uncontested custody.

Imagine a future borehole intercepting a brine-bearing fracture two kilometers down. Instruments measure temperature, pH, redox potential, dissolved gases and isotopes in situ before opening the sample to terrestrial-contact surfaces. Microscopy identifies repeating compartmentalized structures. A mass spectrometer finds a restricted set of complex polymers with consistent handedness. Isotope measurements show fractionation linked to substrate consumption. Repeated samples upstream and downstream show a spatial metabolic gradient. Sequencing assays designed for terrestrial DNA return nothing.

That package would be stronger than any single biomarker.

It is also far beyond current landed capability.

The gap between the best target and the feasible mission should not be hidden. It should organize technology development.

For decades Mars exploration has moved from flybys to orbiters to landers to rovers to sample caching. A deep-biosphere program would be another generational shift: geophysical prospecting first, then sterile drilling, then in situ life detection or carefully governed sample return.

The expense will invite a reasonable question: why drill Mars when Enceladus throws ocean material into space for free?

Because Mars asks a different biological question.

Enceladus may have maintained an ocean continuously. Mars appears to have lost surface habitability. If life is found deep under Mars, it would show that a biosphere can retreat through planetary decline and persist on geology after climate fails. That is one of the most consequential survival experiments nature could offer.

If life is not found, the result will still be difficult to interpret unless the habitat itself is verified. A sterile dry borehole is not evidence against life in wet rock. A wet energetically favorable fracture with no biosignature is much more informative.

Astrobiology needs negative controls on a planetary scale.

Mars is larger than its surface because all planets are larger than the part light reaches. Mars simply makes the error visible. Its exterior is hostile enough that we cannot pretend surface habitability and planetary habitability are synonyms.

The next serious search for living Mars will probably begin by refusing to look where Mars is easiest to see.
