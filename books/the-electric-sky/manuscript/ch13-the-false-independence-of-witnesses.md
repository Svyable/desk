# Chapter 13 — The False Independence of Witnesses

Ten witnesses can watch one cue.

That sounds obvious. It matters enormously.

A radar operator announces a contact. Pilots look where instructed. One pilot sees a light. Another slews an infrared sensor to the same bearing. A controller watches a track symbol generated from the radar. Later, everyone remembers a multi-sensor event.

It is multi-sensor.

It is not fully independent.

The evidence graph has a common root.

This is not an argument against witness competence. It is an argument for causal bookkeeping.

Human observation is especially vulnerable to cueing because attention changes what gets noticed. Once a location is marked as anomalous, every glint in that region becomes evidence-bearing. Once the word "object" is used, later recollection inherits solidity. Group reports converge socially even when the initial sensory evidence differed.

The same logic applies to sensors.

Two cameras on the same platform share motion. Two systems using the same track file share upstream processing. Two radars may share atmospheric propagation. A visible camera and infrared camera behind the same window can share reflections or line-of-sight obstruction.

Independent confirmation therefore means independent failure modes.

A ground camera thirty kilometers away is more valuable than a second zoom mode on the aircraft's targeting pod.

A weather radar operating at another frequency and geometry may be more valuable than another display fed by the same primary sensor.

A satellite observation synchronized independently can be decisive.

This is why the ideal anomaly network should be deliberately heterogeneous.

Different optics. Different manufacturers. Different baselines. Different wavelengths. Different software. Separate clocks reconciled after collection. Raw data preserved before fusion.

Data fusion is operationally powerful because it produces one coherent picture.

Anomaly science sometimes needs to unfuse it.

The weird event may live in the disagreement.
