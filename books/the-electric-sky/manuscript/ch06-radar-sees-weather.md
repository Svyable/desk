# Chapter 6 — Radar Sees Weather

Radar is persuasive because it feels less human.

A witness can be mistaken. A camera can bloom. Radar gives a track, a range, a velocity. The screen looks like machine testimony.

But radar does not see objects.

Radar sees returned energy after propagation through a medium, reflection or scattering from targets, receiver processing, filtering, thresholding and track formation. By the time an operator sees a symbol, physics has already passed through software.

The atmosphere is part of the radar.

Temperature and humidity gradients change refractive index. Under some conditions, radar energy bends more than standard models expect. Ducting can trap propagation near the surface or in elevated layers. Ground or sea clutter can appear at unusual ranges. Weather can create strong returns. Birds and insects can populate airspace with distributed biological scatterers. Turbulence and precipitation complicate the field further.

None of this means radar evidence is weak.

It means radar evidence is technical.

A report that "radar confirmed it" is not enough. Which radar? What frequency? What waveform? What beam geometry? What signal-to-noise ratio? Primary or secondary? Was the target tracked by raw returns or by a tracker? Did the system have range ambiguity? Was there a known anomalous-propagation environment? What were the weather soundings? Did independent radars with different geometry see the same target?

Those questions separate a dramatic story from an analyzable event.

The most famous UFO cases often gain authority from multiple sensors. That is reasonable only if the sensors fail independently.

If two systems share the same environmental distortion, correlation can be counterfeit independence.

A radar and an infrared camera mounted on the same moving platform can share line-of-sight geometry errors. Two radars operating through the same ducting layer may both see anomalous propagation. A pilot's visual attention may be cued by the radar track, making the witness and sensor reports psychologically coupled rather than independent.

Independence is causal, not numerical.

Three correlated sensors do not automatically equal three witnesses.

This becomes critical when strange apparent velocity enters the story. A track-management system may associate detections into a moving target. If detections are intermittent or cluttered, the tracker can bridge gaps. In ordinary operations this is desirable. In anomaly analysis, it can turn a sequence of uncertain returns into a confident kinematic narrative.

A plotted line is an inference.

The proper response is not to distrust radar. It is to preserve lower-level data.

Raw or minimally processed returns, calibration state, weather, system mode and track-association logic matter enormously. Without them, later analysts inherit the display artifact rather than the measurement.

This is one reason older UFO cases are so resistant to final interpretation. The story survives longer than the engineering context.

Modern programs should not repeat that mistake.

AARO's own public case material increasingly makes sensor limitations explicit. In one Middle East case, the office described an apparent thermal contrast that might represent a physical source but lacked corroborating telemetry or multi-modal data sufficient to distinguish a sensor artifact from emission or reflection by a physical object. Its official imagery archive is at https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/ . That kind of language is not bureaucratic evasion. It is what disciplined uncertainty sounds like.

There is another reason radar belongs in a book about the electric sky. Atmospheric electrical events can alter local ionization and electromagnetic conditions, while thunderstorms are already radar-rich environments. A luminous event occurring near convection may therefore inhabit exactly the medium where propagation is least boring.

The hypothesis should still be bounded. Sprites themselves are generally high-altitude optical discharges associated with lightning; that does not mean they create aircraft-like radar targets. A proposed plasma body would need an electron density, size, lifetime and radar cross-section consistent with the observed return. Those are calculations, not adjectives.

"Plasma can reflect radar" is as incomplete as "metal can fly."

The material can participate in the mechanism. That does not establish the vehicle.

A mature UAP program should maintain an atmospheric-propagation twin for every radar event: radiosonde data, numerical weather profiles, precipitation, inversion layers, sea state, lightning data, nearby emitters and system diagnostics. Analysts should be able to ask how the same radar would have behaved in the measured atmosphere if no unusual object existed.

That is the null model.

Without a null model, every unexpected track begins life halfway to a craft.

Radar is powerful because it can destroy bad hypotheses.

It deserves to be used that way.
