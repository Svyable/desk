# Back Matter

## Chronology

**1950s–1970s** — Pilots and observers report unusual luminous events above thunderstorms; the phenomena later called transient luminous events are not yet a mature scientific category.

**1983–1984** — Project Hessdalen is organized in Norway; the 1984 field campaign records dozens of luminous anomalies with multiple instruments.

**1989** — A low-light camera accidentally captures what becomes one of the first widely recognized video records of a sprite, accelerating modern transient-luminous-event research.

**1990s** — Sprites, elves and jets become active subjects of atmospheric-electricity research using ground, aircraft and space-based instruments.

**1998** — The Hessdalen Automatic Measurement Station begins continuous operation.

**2000** — UK Defence Intelligence completes *Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in the UK Air Defence Region*, later known as Project Condign.

**2006** — The UK Ministry of Defence releases the Condign report under freedom-of-information procedures. Laboratory researchers also report stable atmospheric-pressure microwave fireballs relevant to some ball-lightning models.

**2010** — Paiva and Taft publish a hypothetical dusty-plasma model for Hessdalen lights.

**2020** — DRDC and collaborators publish laboratory work on confined plasma balls propagating along discharge channels. NASA reports likely transient luminous events in Jupiter's atmosphere from Juno observations.

**2022** — NASA commissions its independent UAP study.

**September 14, 2023** — NASA publishes the UAP Independent Study Team final report emphasizing better data collection, metadata and scientific analysis.

**November 14, 2024** — DoD releases AARO's FY2024 consolidated annual report, describing resolved prosaic cases, sensor effects and a large data-insufficient active archive.

**July 3, 2025** — Astronaut Nichole Ayers photographs a gigantic jet from the International Space Station; NASA later publicizes the identification.

**August 15, 2026** — AARO's public reporting-trends page shows closed cases dominated by balloons and satellites, with smaller categories including UAS, birds, aircraft, sensor artifacts and natural atmospherics.
