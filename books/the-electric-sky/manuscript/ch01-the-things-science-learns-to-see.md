# Chapter 1 — The Things Science Learns to See

On July 3, 2025, NASA astronaut Nichole Ayers photographed what at first looked like a sprite above a thunderstorm. It was not a sprite. NASA later identified it as a gigantic jet: a discharge climbing from the cloud top toward the ionosphere, making an electrical bridge across something like eighty kilometers of sky.

That sentence should bother anyone who thinks the atmosphere is a solved medium.

A gigantic jet is not a metaphor. It is not an eyewitness category. It is a real electrical event large enough to span the distance between a city and its airport vertically, bright enough to be photographed from orbit, brief enough to be missed by almost everybody below it, and rare enough that a working astronaut can accidentally capture one in 2025 and still make news. NASA's account is here: https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/heliophysics/a-gigantic-jet-caught-on-camera-a-spritacular-moment-for-nasa-astronaut-nicole-ayers/

The atmosphere contains things that were folklore until instrumentation improved.

That is the starting fact of this book. Not that every unidentified light is plasma. Not that every radar anomaly is weather. Not that the military has been hiding sprites in a hangar. The narrower claim is more interesting and harder to wave away: the inventory of physically real luminous, electrical and radar-visible atmospheric phenomena has expanded materially within living scientific memory. Some members of that inventory are huge. Some move strangely. Some occur above storms instead of inside them. Some are too fast for unaided human perception. Some are long-lived compared with ordinary lightning. Some can be luminous without resembling any aircraft lighting pattern. And some were reported by pilots for decades before the physics community was willing to treat them as a serious observational category.

The first unambiguous video image of a sprite was obtained accidentally in 1989 by researchers testing a low-light camera. Pilots had described strange flashes above thunderstorms long before that. Once the right cameras existed, the argument changed. The witness did not become smarter. The sky did not become stranger. The measurement chain got better.

This distinction matters because the modern UAP debate is dominated by nouns. Balloon. Drone. Satellite. Craft. Plasma. Alien. Sensor artifact. The noun arrives too early. A luminous dot has already become an object before we know whether there is an object in the ordinary mechanical sense. A track has already become motion before we know whether the sensor is tracking a physical body, an emitting region, a reflection, a propagation artifact or a processed estimate. A sphere has already acquired a boundary because the image-processing pipeline drew one.

Science is at its best when it delays the noun.

The old UFO literature is full of nouns. So is the debunking literature. This book will try to stay with verbs longer. Glowed. Accelerated in the image plane. Persisted. Split. Appeared on radar. Changed spectrum. Occurred near lightning. Recurred in a valley. Saturated a detector. Vanished when viewed from another angle. Correlated with no transponder. Those are observations or at least claims about observations. They can be tested separately.

The most useful modern control case is not Roswell. It is the transient luminous event.

Sprites, elves, blue jets and gigantic jets are now ordinary parts of atmospheric electricity, but ordinary is a retrospective privilege. A sprite can extend tens of kilometers, occur around fifty to ninety kilometers altitude, display branching structures, and last milliseconds. An elve can expand to hundreds of kilometers across as an electromagnetic pulse excites the lower ionosphere. A gigantic jet can propagate from a thundercloud toward the ionosphere. NASA and atmospheric-electricity groups now classify these events with a zoological confidence that would have sounded like pulp fiction in 1970.

The point is not that trained pilots mistook sprites for structured vehicles. The geometries, timescales and contexts often differ. The point is epistemic. A domain can contain rare, luminous, large-scale physical events that are real before its taxonomy is mature. That should change how both believers and skeptics behave around residual cases.

Believers should stop treating lack of identification as positive evidence for technology. The history of atmospheric electricity is an object lesson in how many surprising natural categories can be waiting behind an unknown.

Skeptics should stop treating natural explanation as synonymous with already familiar explanation. The atmosphere has repeatedly embarrassed that assumption.

NASA's 2023 UAP independent study made a similar methodological point from a different direction. Its report did not endorse an exotic hypothesis. It argued that the central problem is poor data, heterogeneous sensors, inconsistent metadata and the absence of a systematic collection architecture. NASA's UAP portal and final-report materials remain available at https://science.nasa.gov/uap/ . That is a boring conclusion only if one has never watched a scientific field become possible when data quality changes.

Consider what happened with sprites. For decades, anecdote was weak evidence. Then an accidental camera capture provided a repeatable target. Researchers developed observing strategies. Aircraft, ground stations and satellites measured optical emissions, altitude, duration and coupling to lightning. Theory improved. The category became less mysterious as the data became more multidimensional.

That is what a serious UAP program should want.

Not another witness taxonomy.

Not a larger folder of compressed clips.

A transition from story to event physics.

The atmosphere is an adversarial measurement environment even when nobody is trying to fool you. It refracts. Scatters. Ducts radio energy. Produces thermal gradients. Hosts ice crystals. Generates electrical discharges. Moves aerosols. Reflects sunlight from objects below naked-eye resolution. Places bright planets near horizons where turbulence distorts them. Carries satellites through geometries that create flares. Makes distant aircraft appear stationary. Turns birds into infrared blobs. Lets one sensor see what another does not.

AARO's current public statistics are instructive. As of August 15, 2026, its closed-case outcomes are dominated by balloons and satellites, with smaller numbers of UAS, birds, aircraft and other categories. Natural atmospherics account for only a few closed cases in the published table. That is not evidence that natural atmospherics are irrelevant. It is evidence that AARO's resolved-case population is so far overwhelmingly prosaic, while a large archive remains data-poor. The current trend table is public: https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/UAP-Reporting-Trends/ .

A statistic can tell you what has been resolved. It cannot tell you what the unresolved remainder is made of.

That remainder is where epistemology gets expensive.

The temptation is to fill the gap with the category one already favors. A physicist says plasma. An intelligence analyst says foreign platform. A pilot says object. A UFO advocate says craft. A debunker says artifact. The disciplined response is not to split the difference. It is to ask which measurements would force the categories apart.

Radar plus calibrated optical spectrum is different from radar plus witness recollection. Two optical cameras with known baseline are different from one zoomed phone video. A thermal signature with range data is different from an infrared blob with no telemetry. A luminous event coincident with lightning, local electric-field change and VLF disturbance belongs to a different inference tree from a silent metallic object resolved in daylight from multiple angles.

This book is about building those trees.

The title, *The Electric Sky*, is not an answer. It is a warning against a dead atmosphere. We live at the bottom of a chemically active, electrically coupled, optically deceptive fluid threaded by radiation, charged particles and human transmissions. Above the weather we ordinarily notice is another electrical weather whose major members were discovered late. Below the threshold of clean identification are luminous events, propagation effects and sensor interactions that are easy to overname.

And beyond all of that, perhaps, are cases that remain genuinely difficult.

The difficult cases deserve better than premature wonder and premature contempt.

They deserve instruments.
