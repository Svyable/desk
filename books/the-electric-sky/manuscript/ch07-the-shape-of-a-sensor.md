# Chapter 7 — The Shape of a Sensor

A military infrared video is not a window.

It is an instrument product.

That sounds pedantic until the object rotates because the camera rotates.

Electro-optical and infrared systems make the invisible useful by transforming radiance into imagery, then sharpening, stabilizing, compressing, enhancing and displaying it for an operator who has a job to do. Those steps are optimized for detection and engagement, not philosophical purity.

The result can be extraordinary operational vision and mediocre forensic evidence if the metadata disappears.

Glare enlarges hot sources. Saturation removes internal structure. Point-spread functions make unresolved sources adopt instrument-defined shapes. Digital zoom magnifies pixels rather than information. Compression creates edges and blocks. Automatic gain changes apparent contrast. Stabilization can make a moving background feel stationary or a stationary source feel dynamic. Gimbal geometry can introduce apparent rotation. Parallax can make slow objects appear fast when the observing platform moves quickly.

This is not a catalogue of excuses.

It is the physics of the measurement chain.

AARO's FY2024 report explicitly notes that birds can appear as amorphous blobs or orbs because of compression and pixelation, and that electro-optical/infrared glare can distort apparent shape. It also notes flickering signatures consistent with wing beats in full-motion video. The report is at https://media.defense.gov/2024/Nov/14/2003583603/-1/-1/0/FY24-CONSOLIDATED-ANNUAL-REPORT-ON-UAP-508.PDF .

This creates an uncomfortable inversion.

The better the sensor is at detecting a tiny distant target, the easier it is for the viewer to forget how unresolved that target remains.

A bright blob tracked continuously feels like an object you are seeing clearly. Often you are seeing location and contrast clearly, not morphology.

That matters for atmospheric hypotheses too. A diffuse luminous volume may collapse into a compact source after processing. Conversely, a compact hot source may bloom into a larger apparently structured object. Without raw radiometric data and optical calibration, visual size is treacherous.

The correct forensic object is therefore not the clip.

It is the sensor state.

Field of view. Zoom level. gain. exposure. wavelength band. stabilization mode. platform attitude. line-of-sight angles. range source. track source. image processing. time synchronization. atmospheric transmission. compression history.

A video without those variables can still be interesting.

It is not a complete measurement.

The public debate has often moved in the opposite direction. Clips get detached from the systems that produced them precisely because the system details may be classified. That is understandable operationally and disastrous scientifically. The public receives the most ambiguous layer while the calibration layer remains inaccessible.

Secrecy can therefore manufacture mystery without anyone lying.

The government may possess enough classified context to resolve a case prosaically but be unable to release it. Or it may possess classified imagery without the environmental data needed for scientific resolution. Or the sensor may be classified because of capability, causing analysts outside the program to speculate about an image whose distortions are well known to cleared engineers.

This is an information-architecture problem, not a conspiracy theory.

A serious public UAP program should design releasability upstream. It should create sanitized derivative products that preserve the variables necessary for scientific interpretation without exposing sensitive performance. Calibration ranges can be quantized. Platform identity can be masked while motion vectors are preserved. Timing can be shifted consistently. Sensor response curves can be released in normalized form.

The objective is not maximal disclosure.

It is sufficient disclosure for falsification.

Atmospheric science has learned this lesson because images alone are rarely enough. A sprite image becomes much more valuable when tied to lightning timing, altitude geometry, spectra and electromagnetic measurements. A glowing point in Hessdalen becomes more useful with synchronized radar and weather data.

UAP analysis needs the same discipline.

The shape on the screen belongs partly to the sky and partly to the sensor.

Before we infer a vehicle from the shape, we should know which half drew the outline.
