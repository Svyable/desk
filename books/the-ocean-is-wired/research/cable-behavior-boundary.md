# Cable-field behavior boundary — THE OCEAN IS WIRED

This note is a bounded research correction for the manuscript's move from established electroreception to anthropogenic subsea fields. It does not revise Chapter 1. Its purpose is to keep three different propositions from collapsing into one another: **the animal can sense a field; a cable changes individual behavior; the behavioral change matters ecologically.**

## Primary / authoritative anchors checked

### Kalmijn 1971: the biological use case is stronger than a threshold number

A. J. Kalmijn, “The Electric Sense of Sharks and Rays,” *Journal of Experimental Biology* 55 (1971): 371–383. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.55.2.371

The useful result is not merely that sharks and rays respond to weak fields. Kalmijn tested feeding behavior around flatfish hidden in sand, manipulated the availability of non-electrical cues, and simulated the prey's bioelectric field with buried electrodes. The animals produced comparable feeding responses to the artificial source. That is a direct bridge from receptor sensitivity to a biologically meaningful task under the experimental conditions.

Publication consequence: when Chapter 1 eventually expands, prefer this experiment to another generic “sixth sense” sentence. It gives the reader an object in the sand, a competing cue problem, and an intervention. Keep the species and geometry attached to the claim; do not turn one feeding experiment into a universal navigation threshold.

### BOEM 2018 / Hutchison et al. 2020: cable exposure can alter movement without becoming a barrier

Z. L. Hutchison et al., BOEM 2018-003, *Electromagnetic Field (EMF) Impacts on Elasmobranch (Sharks, Rays, and Skates) and American Lobster Movement and Migration from Direct Current Cables*. U.S. Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, 2018. Public project summary: https://web.uri.edu/offshore-renewable-energy/research/electromagnetic-field-emf-impacts-on-elasmobranch-sharks-and-rays-and-skates-and-american-lobster-movement-and-migration-from-direct-current-cables/

Z. L. Hutchison et al., “Anthropogenic electromagnetic fields (EMF) influence the behaviour of bottom-dwelling marine species,” *Scientific Reports* 10 (2020). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-60793-x

The project measured fields around two HVDC cables and ran an in-situ enclosure experiment with little skates and American lobsters. The authors report behavioral effects, but the BOEM-linked project summary explicitly says effects from a single DC cable were unlikely to translate into population-level impacts. The 2020 paper is therefore useful precisely because it occupies the middle rung: measured anthropogenic field → detectable behavioral change, without automatically yielding migration failure or population harm.

Publication consequence: “cable fields can enter the sensory world” is defensible. “Cables disrupt shark/ray migration” is not earned by this experiment.

### Hermans et al. 2024: the risk synthesis remains uncertainty-heavy

A. Hermans, H. V. Winter, A. B. Gill & A. J. Murk, “Do electromagnetic fields from subsea power cables effect benthic elasmobranch behaviour? A risk-based approach for the Dutch Continental Shelf,” *Environmental Pollution* 346 (2024): 123570. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envpol.2024.123570

This synthesis reports a mixed literature: attraction, disturbance and indifference vary with field characteristics, life stage, exposure and species. Its risk conclusion is explicitly uncertain. It is useful for designing the book's comparison table, not as a substitute for the underlying experiments.

Publication consequence: do not use a single generic `EMF effect` variable. Extract AC/DC, amplitude, gradient, cable/burial geometry, species, life stage, behavioral endpoint, exposure duration and field-vs-laboratory setting.

### Hermans et al. 2025: a strong null-ish control at field-relevant gradients

A. Hermans et al., “From subsea power cable to small-spotted catshark *Scyliorhinus canicula*: Behavioural effects of electromagnetic fields in tank experiments,” *Marine Environmental Research* 208 (2025): 107127. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marenvres.2025.107127

Fourteen small-spotted catsharks were exposed to field-relevant gradients in AC, DC and control trials. The sharks did not startle at field onset, did not move toward or away from the simulated cable, and crossed it as frequently as in controls. The analysis did find a treatment-associated behavioral-state difference: about 25% less time in a transiting state during DC trials compared with AC/control.

This is unusually useful counterevidence for the book. A field can be biologically detectable or behaviorally measurable without acting as a wall, waypoint or migration veto. The experiment is small and laboratory-bound, so it cannot settle population ecology; it does prevent `sensitivity` from doing that work for free.

## Strongest counterargument

The manuscript's `field ecology` idea may be conceptually true but scientifically trivial at the infrastructure scale that matters. Sharks and rays indisputably inhabit an electrical sensory world, yet modern cable fields may produce mostly local, species-specific and behaviorally modest perturbations. Habituation, route flexibility, field decay, cable burial and ordinary environmental variability may keep those perturbations from accumulating into meaningful fitness or population effects.

That model should be allowed to win.

The inverse overcorrection is also wrong: a null attraction/avoidance result in one catshark tank experiment does not establish ecological irrelevance for every elasmobranch, life stage, field geometry or repeated exposure. The right object is the transfer function from **field at the animal → receptor exposure → individual behavior → repeated movement/foraging effect → fitness → population consequence**. Evidence can stop at any rung.

## A sharper narrative object

Chapter 1 currently has a good abstract line: a field can be information. The research supports a more concrete progression for a later prose pass:

1. Kalmijn buries the source and shows that the electrical cue can guide feeding behavior.
2. Humans bury cables and create a different field source in the same medium.
3. Field experiments show some animals alter movement around real cable fields.
4. A later catshark experiment finds no attraction, avoidance or crossing barrier under tested field-relevant conditions, despite a subtler behavioral-state change.

The point is not `cables harm sharks` or `cables do nothing`. The interesting scientific problem is that **detection, behavioral salience and ecological consequence are different thresholds**.

That sequence is stronger than another general paragraph about invisible sensory worlds because the evidence itself changes what the reader is allowed to conclude.

## Quantitative extraction burden before manuscript claims

For every cable-effect study record:

- species, sex and life stage;
- sample size and repeated-measures structure;
- AC/DC and frequency;
- magnetic and induced-electric field magnitude, vector and spatial gradient at animal position;
- cable current, conductor configuration, burial depth and substrate where applicable;
- background geomagnetic/electric field and environmental noise;
- exposure duration and prior exposure/habituation;
- field, enclosure or tank geometry;
- preregistered/primary behavioral endpoint versus exploratory state classification;
- attraction/avoidance, crossing, speed, path tortuosity, transit state, foraging or other endpoint;
- effect size and uncertainty, not significance alone;
- whether the result was individual behavior, inferred ecological risk or measured population outcome.

## Publication holds

- Do not infer population-scale migration disruption from sensory thresholds or individual behavioral effects.
- Do not call a detectable cable field a behavioral barrier without crossing/route evidence.
- Do not use the 2025 catshark result as proof of ecological safety across species or field configurations.
- Do not convert modeled field reach into demonstrated biological effect distance.
- Keep electroreception, magnetic information, orientation, navigation and map-like position sensing as separate propositions.
- When a review supplies a threshold/effect range, recover the underlying study before attaching the number to a species-level claim.

## PALS boundary

DARPA's 28 September 2020 Phase 2 announcement is also worth keeping precise: Phase 1 teams had shown organism responses to underwater vehicles **or confounders** and measurable signals; Phase 2 was intended to build the man-made detector/interpretation layer and discriminate targets from other stimuli to limit false positives. https://www.darpa.mil/news/2020/pals-program-second-phase

This supports the manuscript's `translation` problem better than a generic program description. It does not establish an operational shark sensor, and the named Phase 2 performers used snapping shrimp, goliath grouper and a reef-ecosystem approach rather than sharks.

Publication consequence: keep sharks/rays as the sensory-physiology case and PALS as a separate engineering translation case unless primary program records establish a direct shark lineage. Their collision is the author's synthesis, not a documented DARPA development path.
