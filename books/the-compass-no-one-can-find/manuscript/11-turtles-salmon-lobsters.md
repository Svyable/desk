# 11. Turtles, Salmon, Lobsters

Birds have monopolized the romance of magnetoreception.

This is unfair to lobsters.

A useful antidote to avian tunnel vision is to examine animals whose magnetic behavior appears in entirely different bodies. Sea turtles, salmon, lobsters, insects, fishes, mammals, and bacteria have all supplied evidence for magnetic sensitivity, though not always with the same strength or mechanism.

The diversity is the point.

Sea turtles have become a model for magnetic navigation because hatchlings and juveniles can respond to experimentally simulated field parameters associated with different geographic regions. The work suggests that geomagnetic signatures can contribute to inherited or learned movement programs. Salmon appear capable of using geomagnetic information at large scales during oceanic migration. Caribbean spiny lobsters displaced from home have oriented in experimentally altered magnetic fields, implying a magnetic map-like sense in an invertebrate whose anatomy offers no reason to assume the avian mechanism.

Then there are magnetotactic bacteria, which make the word magnetoreception almost too grand. They biomineralize magnetic particles in ordered chains. Their magnetosomes interact with Earth’s field and bias swimming orientation. No quantum romance is needed. The magnetic hardware is visible.

The bacteria are important because they destroy a lazy argument: that Earth’s field is simply too weak for biology to exploit. Evolution has already solved the coupling problem in at least one brutally direct way.

But the bacterial solution does not tell us how a robin works.

Cross-species evidence should increase confidence in the general proposition that geomagnetic information is biologically useful while decreasing confidence that one mechanism explains every lineage.

Convergent evolution is likely. The field offers persistent directional and spatial structure. Any organism moving over sufficient distances could benefit from extracting it. Different evolutionary histories can arrive at different sensors.

This also changes how we should interpret molecular findings. If cryptochrome plays a causal role in a bird, that does not establish that turtles or lobsters use the same protein. If magnetite-based structures are found in fish, that does not demote radical-pair chemistry in birds.

The word “magnetoreception” may be taxonomically like “photoreception”—a functional category containing multiple hardware solutions.

There is a methodological lesson here too. The strongest experiments tend to manipulate the field rather than merely correlate behavior with it. Move the apparent magnetic signature while holding the animal’s physical location constant. Reverse a component. Change intensity and inclination within ecologically meaningful ranges. Use sham manipulations. Look for directional shifts predicted in advance.

Those experiments are the reason this field deserves to be taken seriously even while its receptor biology remains unsettled.

A lobster in a coil system is not impressed by our need for one grand explanation.
