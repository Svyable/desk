# 13. Humans in the Coil

Humans ruin good animal-sense stories by asking the obvious question.

Do we have it too?

For decades the answer around magnetoreception was a mixture of failed behavioral studies, suggestive anecdotes, and methodological frustration. People are terrible subjects for weak sensory effects because expectation, strategy, conscious guessing, room cues, and experimenter leakage can generate impressive nonsense.

In 2019, Connie Wang, Joseph Kirschvink and colleagues tried a different route. They placed human participants in a controlled magnetic-field chamber and measured EEG while rotating Earth-strength magnetic fields. They reported a repeatable decrease in alpha-band power after particular geomagnetically relevant rotations. The response depended on field geometry. The participants did not report a conscious magnetic sensation.

The paper is fascinating.

It is not closure.

The strongest version of its result is narrow: under the study’s conditions, some human brains showed an EEG response correlated with specific rotations of an Earth-strength magnetic field. The authors argued that control patterns favored a ferromagnetic transduction mechanism rather than induction or a simple radical-pair account.

A larger claim—that humans possess a functional magnetic sense—requires more.

Independent replication matters enormously here. So does the distinction between neural transduction and useful perception. A sensory pathway can be present without reaching consciousness or behavior. Evolution can leave traces. The brain can respond to stimuli that subjects cannot report.

The study therefore sits in evidence class two for this book: credible, technically serious, incomplete.

It also creates an interpretive hazard. Once “human magnetoreception” appears in a headline, it gets absorbed into stories about intuition, ancient navigation, lost abilities, electromagnetic hypersensitivity, ley lines, and generalized sensitivity to modern electronics. None of those claims follows.

A specific EEG response is not a diagnosis.

Nor does animal magnetoreception validate broad health claims about ambient EM fields. The relevant endpoints are different. In a bird, weak electromagnetic noise might disrupt an information-processing channel without causing injury. In a human, a laboratory EEG response might exist without producing a conscious percept, impairment, or disease.

This separation between sensation and pathology is essential.

The human study is valuable precisely because it can be tested. Repeat the apparatus in independent laboratories. Preregister the field rotations. Blind the analysis. Vary hemisphere-appropriate inclination. Test whether individual responders replicate across sessions. Ask whether the effect survives changes in coil configuration that preserve the intended field while altering apparatus artifacts. Attempt causal localization.

If the result survives that gauntlet, the textbook will have to change.

If it fails, magnetoreception in birds, turtles, salmon, lobsters, and bacteria will remain.

The human question is not the foundation of the field.

It is one of its sharpest open edges.
