# 5. The Radical Pair

The radical-pair mechanism sounds harder than it is because chemistry inherited some of physics’ least friendly vocabulary.

Start with two electrons.

When a light-driven chemical reaction transfers an electron, it can create two molecular radicals. Their unpaired electron spins may be born correlated. The pair can occupy singlet or triplet character, and magnetic interactions can alter the rate at which that character changes. Because different spin states can lead to different chemical products or reaction yields, a magnetic field can influence chemistry without providing enough mechanical force to swing anything like a compass needle.

This is the core of the radical-pair proposal for animal magnetoreception.

The scale is startling. Earth’s field is on the order of tens of microtesla. It is weak compared with the magnets in laboratory hardware or medical scanners. Yet weak-field magnetic effects on radical-pair reactions are physically legitimate. The challenge is not whether spin chemistry exists. It does. The challenge is whether a biological radical-pair system has the right lifetime, orientation, reaction partners, signal amplification, noise tolerance, and neural coupling to serve as a sensory receptor.

That is a much more demanding list.

P. J. Hore and Henrik Mouritsen’s 2016 review laid out why the mechanism remains attractive and what it must satisfy. A receptor should be directionally ordered rather than freely tumbling if it is to generate orientation information. The spin dynamics must remain sensitive in the geomagnetic regime. The chemistry must occur under biologically relevant illumination and temperature. The effect must connect to physiology.

Cryptochromes became leading candidates because they are flavoproteins capable of light-dependent electron-transfer reactions and already exist in animal photobiology. That is useful evolutionary real estate. Evolution often repurposes.

But candidate plausibility can create tunnel vision. Once a model explains several odd facts—light dependence, inclination-compass behavior, susceptibility to radiofrequency fields—it starts to feel inevitable. It is not.

Some behavioral findings resist simple versions. Some radiofrequency experiments have not produced the predicted effects. The relevant radical pair in vivo remains debated. The identity of the operative cryptochrome has shifted across the literature. A molecular effect measured in a purified protein is not yet a sensory organ.

This is not a weakness unique to magnetoreception. Mechanistic biology is full of ladders where each rung requires its own evidence: molecule, cell, circuit, behavior, ecology. The problem arrives when success at one rung is narrated as if the ladder has been climbed.

The 2021 CRY4 work is a beautiful example. Researchers showed that cryptochrome 4 from the European robin can undergo magnetically sensitive photochemistry in vitro, and that its response differed from homologues in non-migratory species tested in the study. That is important. It moves the discussion beyond “this molecule could maybe do it.” But it does not show that removing or altering CRY4 in a robin abolishes the compass while preserving other relevant functions.

The decisive experiment has to connect chemistry to the behaving animal.

Until then, the radical pair is not merely a hypothesis. It is a physically grounded mechanism with substantial supporting constraints. But it is still a mechanism under prosecution.

The jury should not leave just because the math is beautiful.
