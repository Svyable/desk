# 3. North Is Not a Place

The first error in thinking about animal navigation is imagining that “north” is enough.

A compass tells you which way you are pointed. A map tells you where you are. A migrating animal often needs both, plus some way to decide where it is trying to go.

This sounds obvious until magnetoreception enters the conversation. Then every magnetic response gets flattened into “internal compass.” That phrase is so sticky that it conceals the harder possibility: parts of the geomagnetic field may be used not only for direction but as components of a positional map.

Sea turtles make the problem vivid. Hatchlings entering the Atlantic encounter a moving world of currents and large-scale circulation. Experiments by Kenneth and Catherine Lohmann and collaborators have shown that turtles can respond to magnetic field parameters corresponding to geographically distinct regions along migratory routes. The interpretation is not that a turtle carries a labeled atlas. It is that inherited or learned responses to regional magnetic signatures can bias movement in adaptive directions.

Salmon add another scale. The magnetic field around Earth is not a perfect bar code. It changes over time. Secular variation shifts intensity and inclination contours. Yet work on salmon migration suggests geomagnetic information can contribute to large-scale return behavior. If true, that makes the sense less like GPS than like an old mariner’s map built from gradients, coastlines, odors, and updating cues.

The difference matters because mechanism depends on what information must be extracted. An inclination compass can in principle operate with a receptor that is insensitive to polarity. A magnetic map based partly on intensity requires a way to register magnitude with sufficient stability. A receptor useful for one job may be poor at the other.

This is where the two-mechanism idea earns its keep. Radical-pair chemistry is naturally attractive for a light-dependent directional compass because molecular orientation relative to the field can alter reaction yields under appropriate conditions. Magnetite-based structures, if coupled mechanically to cells, are attractive for intensity or polarity-sensitive channels. Attractive is not proven. But the functional split is plausible enough that experiments should stop assuming a single winner must explain every magnetic behavior.

There is another complication: the Earth itself is noisy.

The field varies by latitude, longitude, geology, time of day, solar activity, and local magnetic material. Human structures add distortions. If an animal extracts map information from this environment, the nervous system must solve a calibration problem. It may integrate magnetic cues with stars, sun, polarized light, odor, wave direction, infrasound, landmarks, or learned route features. A cue can be genuinely used and still be neither necessary nor sufficient in every context.

That makes navigation experiments vulnerable to overstatement. Knock out one cue under laboratory conditions and an animal fails; the result can look like the cue is the navigation system. Restore the animal to a richer environment and redundancy may reappear.

Mouritsen’s 2018 review emphasized this multisensory architecture. Long-distance navigation is not likely to be solved by one master sensor. That is almost disappointing if you want a magic molecule. It is much more interesting if you want the animal.

The bird does not need a theory of geomagnetism. The turtle does not need latitude printed in its brain. They need control policies that turn environmental regularities into useful movement.

North is not a place.

The real question is how a nervous system turns a field into a decision.
