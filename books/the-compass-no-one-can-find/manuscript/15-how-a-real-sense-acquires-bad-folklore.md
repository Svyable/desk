# 15. How a Real Sense Acquires Bad Folklore

Real anomalies are dangerous because they subsidize fake ones.

Magnetoreception is real enough in animals that almost any magnetic claim can borrow its halo. If robins respond to Earth’s field, why not humans sensing power lines? If cryptochrome reactions are quantum, why not magnetic healing bracelets? If radiofrequency noise can disrupt avian orientation, why not every diffuse symptom near a router?

The error is not skepticism versus openness.

It is transfer without mechanism.

Scientific facts are local before they are general. The robin result is about a migratory bird performing a magnetic orientation task under specified conditions. The human EEG result is about alpha-band changes during controlled field rotations. Magnetotactic bacteria use mineral chains. These facts do not fuse into a universal law that weak magnetic fields have large effects on every organism.

The same mistake runs in the other direction. Because magnets are marketed dishonestly, some people learn to dismiss weak-field biology altogether. The fraud contaminates the real phenomenon.

This social dynamic matters in frontier research. A subject can become epistemically toxic: serious investigators know that mentioning it invites association with weaker claims, so they narrow their language or leave. Then the public field is increasingly occupied by people least constrained by evidence.

Magnetoreception partly escaped that fate because the behavioral experiments became too strong and too repeatable in enough species. It acquired a professional literature deep enough to resist the surrounding folklore.

But the boundary still has to be defended.

One useful rule is to demand an evidence bridge for every extrapolation. Bird to human? Show homologous receptor machinery or direct human data. Orientation disruption to health effect? Show a physiological pathway to pathology. In-vitro protein sensitivity to whole-animal navigation? Show causal necessity in vivo. Quantum spin chemistry to cognition? Show the neural chain.

Without the bridge, the claim remains analogy.

There is also a media incentive problem. “Scientists still cannot find the receptor” sounds like uncertainty. “Birds use quantum physics to see Earth’s magnetic field” sounds like discovery. The second line is not entirely wrong, which makes it more dangerous. It converts a live mechanistic contest into an ontological headline.

The public then encounters later corrections as backtracking.

The better story is that science can know at different resolutions simultaneously. We can know that a bird uses geomagnetic information. We can have strong reason to favor a class of photochemical mechanisms for one compass behavior. We can have a leading protein candidate. We can still not possess the final receptor circuit.

That is not weakness.

It is what honest partial knowledge looks like.

A real sense does not need folklore to stay strange.
