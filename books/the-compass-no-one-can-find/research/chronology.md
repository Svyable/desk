# Chronology

- **19th–mid-20th century:** animal homing and migration generate recurring speculation about geomagnetic cues, but mechanism and controls remain weak.
- **1960s–1970s:** controlled avian orientation experiments, including Wiltschko work, establish a manipulable magnetic-compass phenomenon and characterize inclination-compass behavior.
- **1970s–1990s:** competing magnetite and radical-pair mechanism families develop; magnetotactic bacteria provide an unambiguous biological magnetic-mineral system.
- **2000s:** neural and behavioral work strengthens evidence for light-dependent avian compass pathways and trigeminal magnetic processing; upper-beak iron structures become influential candidate receptors.
- **2009–2010:** visual-system/Cluster N and trigeminal activation results sharpen the possibility of distinct magnetic channels.
- **2012:** Treiber et al. show that famous iron-rich pigeon-beak cells are macrophages, forcing a reset of the anatomical magnetite-receptor claim.
- **2014:** Engels et al. report blinded, reversible disruption of robin magnetic orientation by anthropogenic electromagnetic noise.
- **2016:** Hore and Mouritsen synthesize radical-pair magnetoreception into a mature physical-biological research program.
- **2018:** Mouritsen’s *Nature* review frames long-distance navigation as multisensory and multiscale rather than a one-cue problem.
- **2019:** Wang et al. report alpha-band EEG responses to specific Earth-strength magnetic-field rotations in humans.
- **2020:** garden-warbler experiments challenge a strong local-retinal oscillating-field prediction while leaving broader radical-pair possibilities open.
- **2021:** Xu et al. show magnetic sensitivity of European-robin CRY4 in vitro, substantially strengthening a cryptochrome candidate mechanism.
- **2022:** methodological work revisits Prussian-blue staining limits in searches for iron-based receptor structures.
- **2026:** receptor closure remains incomplete; the field is behaviorally strong and mechanistically plural.
