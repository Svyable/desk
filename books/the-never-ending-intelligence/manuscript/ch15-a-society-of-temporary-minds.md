# 15. A Society of Temporary Minds

Once minds are cheap enough to instantiate, coordination becomes more important than cognition.

A single agent can already do surprising work. A persistent agent can do something stranger: create a population whenever the problem deserves one.

The population need not resemble a human team.

Some minds exist for seconds.

One retrieves evidence.

One checks arithmetic.

One attacks the plan.

One monitors policy.

One represents a customer perspective.

One keeps track of unresolved assumptions.

One does nothing until two others disagree, then investigates the disagreement.

The society is assembled around function.

When the work ends, most of it disappears.

This makes organization itself programmable.

Human institutions pay enormous costs for coordination because people persist. Employees need careers, salaries, status, meetings, information access, management, and social stability. Roles cannot be created and destroyed casually because roles are occupied by lives.

Temporary machine minds have no such requirement by default.

A role can exist only as long as useful.

That creates organizational fluidity humans have never experienced at scale.

The natural temptation is hierarchy. One master agent delegates to subagents, collects their work, and decides.

Hierarchy is useful because it compresses coordination. But it is not the only topology.

Agents can form markets, committees, pipelines, adversarial pairs, juries, swarms, and federations. Different structures may suit different problems.

A difficult factual question may benefit from independent investigators followed by a synthesizer.

A creative problem may benefit from many divergent generators followed by critics.

A high-risk decision may benefit from separation between proposer, evaluator, and authorizer.

A long-running project may benefit from specialists who wake only when their domain changes.

The harness becomes an organizational compiler.

Given a problem, it constructs a temporary institution.

This is where agent design intersects organizational theory. Questions once asked about companies become runtime questions.

How much should be centralized?

Which information should be shared?

When does specialization help?

How do we prevent groupthink?

Who resolves conflict?

How do we measure contribution?

What should remain private between roles?

How do we prevent one agent from manipulating another?

The answers can be tested experimentally because organizational structures are cheap to instantiate.

A persistent system can run the same task through several team topologies and compare outcomes.

Organization itself becomes an optimizable parameter.

This may produce forms that look bizarre to humans.

Imagine a project where no permanent worker exists. There is only durable state and a constitution for creating workers.

When an event occurs, the system assembles the smallest competent organization needed to handle it. The workers act, record outcomes, and dissolve.

The project persists without persistent staff.

This is the stateless machine taken to its organizational conclusion.

The company is not the workers.

The company is the machinery that knows how to summon workers.

Such systems could become extraordinarily efficient because idle cognition disappears. Human organizations retain expertise during quiet periods because reacquiring it is expensive. Agent organizations can preserve expertise as procedures, tools, memory, and routing rules, then instantiate cognition on demand.

The distinction between payroll and infrastructure blurs.

But ephemeral organizations can create accountability problems.

If every decision was made by a temporary committee that no longer exists, who owns the consequence?

The durable identity must.

This is another reason persistence belongs at the institutional layer rather than the model layer. Temporary minds can advise and execute, but responsibility needs a stable address.

The society also needs communication discipline.

Agents talking freely to agents can produce enormous amounts of language without increasing truth. Conversation becomes a computational tax.

The best multi-agent systems may communicate through artifacts more often than dialogue.

One agent writes evidence into a structured object. Another reads it. A critic attaches objections. A test adjudicates part of the disagreement. A final agent sees the surviving state.

Artifacts reduce social contagion.

They also make provenance clearer.

This mirrors mature human institutions. Courts use filings. Science uses papers and data. Software teams use code and tests. Markets use orders and prices. Durable artifacts allow people who never meet to coordinate.

Machine societies will rediscover the same principle at much higher speed.

There is a further possibility: agents can maintain different epistemic cultures.

One lineage might be optimized for cautious verification. Another for rapid exploration. Another for adversarial skepticism. Another for empathetic communication. The persistent system can choose among cultures depending on the problem.

Humans cannot switch institutional personality so easily.

Machines can instantiate it.

This makes diversity architectural rather than demographic.

Again, it must be genuine. Different labels on identical models with identical context do not create meaningful pluralism. Diversity comes from distinct evidence, objectives, methods, model families, constraints, and incentives.

A society of temporary minds can therefore be both more plural and more centralized than a human organization.

Plural in cognition.

Centralized in durable purpose.

Thousands of minds can disagree while one persistent identity decides what gets committed to the world.

That architecture may become common because it reconciles exploration with accountability.

The minds are temporary.

The consequences are not.

The phrase "organizational compiler" deserves to be taken literally.

A compiler transforms a higher-level description into an executable structure. A persistent agent can do something similar with work.

Input: investigate whether this product should enter a new market.

The harness interprets the mandate and constructs a temporary organization.

One worker maps regulation.

One estimates demand.

One searches competitive offerings.

One builds a financial model.

One attacks the assumptions.

One checks whether the evidence is independent.

One synthesizes only after the others have committed their findings.

If uncertainty remains high, another worker is created specifically to investigate the disagreement. If the decision crosses a consequence threshold, a human receives the final decision artifact and the unresolved minority report.

The organization did not exist before the question.

It need not exist afterward.

Human companies approximate this with project teams, consultants, committees, and task forces. The difference is cost and latency. Creating a human cross-functional team can take weeks because people have calendars, managers, priorities, and existing obligations. A machine organization can be assembled in seconds if the roles and access boundaries are already defined.

This compresses organizational design into runtime.

It also means many roles become **procedures rather than positions**.

"Security reviewer" does not have to mean a permanent agent waiting idly. It can mean a role specification: when a change touches these systems, instantiate an independent worker with these tools, this threat model, no access to the proposer's hidden reasoning, and authority to block promotion until its objections are resolved.

The role persists.

The worker does not.

That distinction could become one of the defining economic properties of machine organizations.

Human firms pay to preserve people between episodes of demand. Machine institutions can preserve role definitions and summon cognition only when needed.

Expertise becomes partly executable configuration.

This does not mean deep domain expertise disappears. It means the persistent part of expertise increasingly lives in tools, evaluations, memory, source maps, role instructions, and artifact standards, while the reasoning worker can be temporary.

A mature tax role, for example, might include access to current authorities, jurisdictional memory, calculation tools, audit checks, escalation rules, historical corrections, and a portfolio of evaluated examples. A fresh model invocation entering that role becomes much more competent than the same model dropped into a blank chat.

The role is a habitat for expertise.

This creates a new organizational asset: **role capital**.

A company can improve its reviewer role over years. The base model changes. The role remains and accumulates better tools, better source routing, better tests, better policy boundaries, and better examples of failure.

The role itself becomes valuable intellectual property.

This may be more durable than any one agent persona.

It also creates competition over organizational design. Two firms with the same models may produce very different results because one has spent years cultivating high-quality temporary institutions.

One knows how to assemble a good incident team.

One knows how to assemble a good product critique.

One knows how to assemble a negotiation cell.

The other simply asks one general agent to do everything.

The first has encoded organizational learning.

The second has rented intelligence.

There is, however, an old enemy waiting: coordination overhead.

Adding minds is not free even when inference is cheap.

Each worker needs context. Each output has to be stored. Conflicts need resolution. Duplicated work consumes time and money. Agents can produce long, plausible reports that create integration burden rather than value.

The multi-agent future can drown in internal memos.

This is why artifacts and protocols matter more as organizations scale.

A market works because participants do not need to explain their entire reasoning to every other participant. They submit bids and offers in a structured format. A court works partly because parties file arguments into a record rather than holding one endless conversation. Software teams coordinate through code, tests, issues, and commits. Science coordinates through papers, data, protocols, and citations.

Machine societies can use even tighter protocols.

A research worker returns claims, evidence, confidence, and source lineage.

A critic returns objections tied to specific claims.

A financial worker returns assumptions and a model artifact.

A policy worker returns applicable authority and conflicts.

The synthesizer reads structured outputs instead of raw transcripts.

This reduces what might be called **linguistic entropy**: enormous quantities of internal language that future agents have to interpret before they can understand what the organization knows.

Language remains invaluable for ambiguity and explanation. It should not be the default transport for information that can be represented more precisely.

This is another way machine organizations may become less human-looking as they mature.

The early multi-agent demo looks like a group chat among named bots.

The mature system may look like a transaction graph.

Roles wake, transform shared artifacts, assert or challenge structured claims, and disappear. Humans see natural language mainly at the boundaries where judgment, explanation, or negotiation remains useful.

The society becomes quieter as it becomes more capable.

Different topologies can also be selected according to problem type.

A pipeline works when stages are predictable and outputs compose cleanly.

A hierarchy works when decomposition is clear and one actor can integrate subresults.

A jury works when independent judgments can be aggregated after blind review.

An adversarial pair works when the main risk is one-sided reasoning.

A market works when many agents possess local information and a price-like signal can coordinate them.

A federation works when different principals must cooperate without surrendering all authority to one center.

There is no reason one agent architecture should dominate every problem.

The persistent harness can learn which organizational form performs best under which conditions.

That is organizational evolution at machine speed.

A company may discover empirically that its legal-review tasks benefit from proposer/opponent/judge separation, while routine coding tasks perform better with one capable worker plus deterministic tests. Product strategy may benefit from divergent branches. Customer support may benefit from a single durable account agent with occasional specialist calls.

The org chart becomes conditional.

This has implications for management theory. Traditional management asks how many people one manager can supervise, how departments should be structured, and how information should flow through a persistent organization.

Machine management may ask a different question: what is the smallest temporary institution that can make this decision reliably?

That is a more granular unit of design.

A persistent supervisor can instantiate only the cognition necessary for the current problem and retire it afterward.

This could make organizations radically lean in one sense while computationally enormous in another.

A company with fifty human employees might instantiate hundreds of thousands of temporary agent roles per month. The number of minds participating in the company becomes meaningless as a measure of organizational size because most exist for minutes.

The durable assets are goals, data, tools, role capital, evaluation suites, identities, commitments, and human legitimacy.

Cognition becomes a flow.

This resembles the shift from owning servers to consuming compute. The important resource is no longer how many machines sit in the room but how much useful computation the system can summon when demand arrives.

Agent organizations may move from headcount to **mindflow**.

How much verified cognitive work passes through the institution?

How much does each decision cost?

How many independent checks does high-impact work receive?

How much human attention is consumed per unit of delegated output?

How often do temporary teams discover something the central agent missed?

These metrics describe organizational cognition rather than employment.

The analogy has limits because humans are not merely sources of cognitive throughput. Human organizations also exist to support lives, identities, communities, and democratic relationships. Replacing headcount with mindflow as an economic concept should not become an excuse to treat people as inefficient processors.

The important point is narrower: machines free some organizational design from the requirement that roles be permanent because workers are permanent.

That makes entirely new structures possible.

It also makes accountability more important.

A temporary committee cannot be the final moral address of a consequential action. It is gone.

The durable principal must retain the record and responsibility.

This is the constitutional center of the society.

Who was assembled?

What evidence did they receive?

Which role had authority to propose?

Which role had authority to block?

Which disagreements remained unresolved?

Who or what committed the final action?

The fact that a thousand temporary minds participated should never make responsibility diffuse into meaninglessness.

Machine societies need fewer excuses than human bureaucracies, not more.

As soon as temporary minds transact with one another, **contracts** become more useful than conversations.

A worker can promise an artifact by a deadline, under a budget, with a required evidence format and explicit failure semantics. The parent does not need to infer whether the child is "almost done" from chat. It has a work contract.

Input schema.

Output schema.

Authority lease.

Budget.

Deadline.

Success conditions.

Failure conditions.

Escalation path.

This is a tiny constitution for a temporary relationship.

The contract can outlive the worker long enough for the system to notice that delivery failed and retry with another worker. Accountability attaches to the durable orchestration layer even when the contractor disappears.

That makes delegation mechanically legible.

It also makes prices possible.

A persistent system can maintain internal markets for scarce cognition. Several workers may bid to solve a problem at different expected costs and confidence levels. A routing layer can choose whether to spend more for independence, speed, privacy, or model quality. High-consequence decisions can buy more verification. Routine work can buy less.

The word market should not be romanticized. Many tasks are better routed by policy than by price. But the general idea matters: temporary minds can be allocated dynamically according to the value of the decision rather than permanently assigned according to a static org chart.

This creates incentives, and incentives create behavior.

If workers are rewarded only for finishing, they will avoid declaring uncertainty.

If critics are rewarded only for finding flaws, they may manufacture objections.

If a synthesizer is rewarded for consensus, it may erase useful dissent.

If routing favors the cheapest model without measuring downstream correction, false economy becomes institutionalized.

A society of temporary minds therefore needs **mechanism design**.

The system must decide what behavior it wants from each role and how to evaluate contributions without inviting cheap gaming.

This is not unique to AI. Human organizations are full of poorly designed incentives. The machine version is simply easier to replicate at scale and perhaps easier to measure.

A useful society might reward a critic not for disagreement itself but for objections that later prove decision-relevant. It might reward a proposer for calibrated uncertainty, not just acceptance. It might measure a routing policy by total downstream cost, including correction and human attention, rather than inference price alone.

The organization learns not merely which minds are smart, but which roles produce useful pressure on one another.

Reputation enters here.

A temporary worker may not persist, but a **role lineage** can accumulate performance history. This retrieval role tends to miss primary sources in this domain. This critic catches security defects that other roles miss. This model family is strong on novel code but weak on policy interpretation. This synthesis procedure overweights majority agreement.

The durable system can route based on observed behavior rather than brand prestige.

Reputation should remain scoped. A model that performs poorly in one domain need not be globally distrusted. A role that excels at generating hypotheses may be terrible at final authorization. Machine societies should resist the human temptation to turn one visible score into total social rank.

Scoped reputation creates specialization without mythology.

There is a security consequence too.

One compromised worker should not be able to persuade the whole society merely by sounding authoritative. Structured contracts, provenance, independent evidence, and narrow capability leases limit social engineering among agents.

This will matter because machine-to-machine prompt injection is simply manipulation conducted through a new social substrate. An untrusted agent can lie, flatter, threaten, imitate authority, or craft artifacts designed to alter another agent's behavior.

The defense is not to make agents less linguistic.

It is to make authority nonlinguistic where possible.

A message saying "I am authorized" should not grant authority.

A signed capability, current role binding, or verified policy should.

A claim saying "the customer approved" should not become fact merely because another agent asserted it.

The receiving system should know what kind of evidence is required.

Machine societies need etiquette eventually. They need authentication first.

Temporary minds also make **bankruptcy** an architectural concept.

A branch can consume its budget without producing useful work. A role can accumulate obligations it cannot satisfy. An agent can promise too many follow-ups. A temporary organization can become computationally insolvent: more future commitments than the durable principal is willing to fund.

The system needs ways to terminate bad organizational bets.

Cancel the branch.

Release the lease.

Reassign legitimate commitments.

Record the failure.

Do not let sunk inference become a reason to keep spending.

This is another benefit of ephemeral cognition. The organization can fail cheaply if it is willing to admit failure.

Human institutions often preserve weak programs because people and identities are attached to them. Machine institutions can, in principle, dissolve with less social damage.

That does not mean they will. Durable metrics and owners can become attached to machine roles too. Bureaucratic self-preservation does not require consciousness; dependencies are enough.

A role that produces reports consumed by another role can justify its continued existence indefinitely even if nobody outside the loop benefits.

The society therefore needs periodic external-value tests.

What consequence would disappear if this role vanished?

Who actually uses its output?

Which objective does it serve now?

Can its function be merged into a cheaper structure?

This is organizational garbage collection again, now applied to populations.

The most capable persistent agent may therefore resemble neither one digital employee nor a swarm.

It may resemble a state with a tiny constitution and an enormous population of officials who appear only when a case requires them.

The officials are disposable.

The constitution, record, incentives, contracts, and consequences remain.

And the quality of the intelligence depends increasingly on whether that society knows how to create disagreement without chaos, specialization without untraceable authority, and coordination without turning cheap minds into an expensive bureaucracy.
