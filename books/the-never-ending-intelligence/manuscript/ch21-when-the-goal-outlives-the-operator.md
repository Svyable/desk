# 21. When the Goal Outlives the Operator

Human intention has always had an expiration problem.

We write wills because people die. We create trusts because purposes may outlive founders. We establish institutions because projects exceed careers. We write constitutions because authority needs continuity across generations. We appoint successors because a role cannot depend forever on one nervous system remaining available.

Persistent agents compress this ancient problem into software.

A person creates an objective.

The agent continues.

Then the person leaves.

What happens to the objective?

In a chat system, usually nothing. The conversation ends. In a durable agent, the question becomes unavoidable because the system may still possess triggers, credentials, memory, unfinished work, budgets, relationships, open negotiations, scheduled reviews, and delegated subagents created under the old authority.

The operator can disappear while the goal remains executable.

This is not a remote edge case reserved for science fiction. Employees leave companies every day. Executives change roles. Customers close accounts. Projects are canceled. Companies merge. Parents die. Children become adults. Trustees are replaced. Governments change administrations. Laws change. Vendors disappear. Partnerships dissolve. Owners sell businesses. Courts invalidate rules. A person who once wanted something later changes their mind and forgets that a machine still remembers the old desire.

Any agent designed to persist for years will experience succession.

So succession must be part of the architecture from the beginning.

Every durable mandate needs an authority chain.

Who created it?

Who owns it now?

Who can reaffirm it?

Who can modify it?

Who can suspend it?

Who inherits responsibility if the original owner disappears?

What happens if nobody does?

What evidence counts as disappearance rather than temporary absence?

What authority survives while succession is unresolved?

The safest default for many goals may be decay rather than immortality.

If ownership becomes ambiguous, authority narrows. Monitoring may continue for a bounded period. Evidence may be preserved. Irreversible actions pause. Sensitive credentials expire. The system asks for reauthorization from a recognized successor rather than treating silence as consent forever.

This is computational conservatism.

The agent does not assume that because it was once allowed to act, it is eternally allowed to act.

The principle is simple enough to state and surprisingly difficult to implement because authority is often indirect.

Suppose a manager creates a persistent agent to maintain a vendor relationship. The manager later leaves the company. The agent's actual principal may not have been the manager at all. The company owned the account, the procurement policy, the budget, and the contract. The manager merely exercised delegated authority on behalf of the institution.

In that case the agent should survive the manager.

Now change one fact. The agent was personal, created by the manager to track job opportunities and negotiate a future move. It happened to use the same model provider and some of the same tools. When the manager leaves the company, the institutional agent should continue and the personal agent should leave with the person.

Same model technology.

Different principal.

Succession is an identity problem before it is a scheduling problem.

This is why personal agents and institutional agents need distinct persistence rules.

A personal agent inherits authority from a person. When that person's authority ends, the machine should generally become more constrained unless the person previously established legitimate succession instructions.

An institutional agent inherits authority from an office, organization, trust, agency, project, or other durable principal. Individuals may come and go while the institution continues through governance procedures.

The same distinction exists everywhere in human affairs. A lawyer may hold documents for a client but does not become the client. An employee can sign within authority but does not personally own the company's contracts. An executor acts for an estate but cannot rewrite the deceased person's life. An official acts through an office whose powers survive the official.

Persistent agents need similarly explicit roles.

Otherwise the machine becomes a fog in which personal intention, institutional authority, and technical capability are difficult to separate.

There is a stranger case at the personal boundary.

Imagine somebody spends twenty years with a persistent agent.

The system knows unfinished manuscripts, family logistics, long-running investments, medical scheduling preferences, the history of a home renovation, correspondence patterns, travel habits, recurring donations, promises to friends, private jokes, abandoned projects, professional contacts, financial routines, and thousands of dormant intentions whose relevance may return only rarely.

Then the person dies.

What is the agent now?

A data archive?

An executor?

A memorial?

A security risk?

A conversational reconstruction?

A continuing representative of the person's wishes?

A collection of tools that should immediately lose authority?

The answer should not be decided accidentally by whatever subscription remains active.

Persistent personal intelligence will force society to develop something like computational estates.

Not because the machine must be treated as a person, but because a person's durable synthetic extension may contain rights, obligations, memories, delegated powers, secrets, subscriptions, scheduled actions, and representations that survive the person's capacity to supervise them.

The first question is authority.

Which actions, if any, was the agent authorized to continue after death?

A person might explicitly ask the agent to preserve a family archive, notify collaborators about unfinished work, help an executor locate documents, maintain a website for a defined period, or deliver messages prepared in advance. Those instructions resemble estate planning because they are deliberate attempts to make intent survive the operator.

Other activities should almost certainly stop.

A personal agent should not keep trading an account merely because its old optimization loop still exists. It should not continue negotiating contracts under the dead person's apparent authority. It should not keep sending socially ordinary messages that cause others to believe the person is alive. It should not assume that every long-running preference remains meaningful after the principal is gone.

The difference is not technical capability.

It is legitimate continuation.

The second question is memory.

Which memories transfer to heirs or executors? Which are private even after death? Which belong partly to other people whose messages and histories were captured in the agent's context? Which are needed to settle obligations? Which should be destroyed because their purpose ended with the relationship?

A personal agent's memory is not the same as a box of papers in an attic.

It may contain derived profiles, summaries, inferred relationships, embeddings, preferences, generated interpretations, and cross-linked records assembled from many services. Some of those structures may reveal more than any single original document.

So inheritance cannot mean simply "copy the database."

The durable self needs semantic dismantling.

Some continuity transfers.

Some becomes archival.

Some is deleted.

Some remains inaccessible except under defined legal or personal conditions.

Some is transformed into artifacts that preserve legitimate value without preserving unnecessary surveillance.

The third question is representation.

Can the system speak in the deceased person's voice?

Technically, that ability is likely to be easier than deciding whether it should be used.

A persistent agent may have years of messages, preferences, corrections, examples, and recorded habits. It could plausibly produce language that feels continuous with the person. The emotional temptation is obvious.

But continuity of style is not continuity of consent.

A system can reproduce how somebody usually answered without knowing how they would have changed after the last day they were alive. It can extrapolate a personality without bearing the moral cost of pretending the extrapolation is the person.

A society with persistent personal agents will need explicit norms around posthumous agency.

There is a meaningful difference between an archive that helps a family search a person's work, a system that delivers messages intentionally prepared before death, and an open-ended simulator that improvises new opinions under the dead person's identity.

The architecture should be able to represent that difference.

Identity is not a binary field called active or dead.

It can transition into limited successor states.

This matters for living people too.

A person can become incapacitated without dying. A teenager becomes an adult. A power of attorney activates. A business owner becomes temporarily unavailable. A trusted operator loses capacity. An employee goes on leave. A principal may want the agent to narrow activity during uncertainty without erasing continuity.

Succession is therefore not one event at the end of life.

It is a general problem of changing authority through time.

The durable agent needs a succession graph.

If principal A becomes unavailable under condition X, authority passes to B for these domains but not those domains. If B declines, C may preserve records but cannot spend. If the user later returns, control reverts after verification. If no recognized successor exists, the system enters a dormant state with bounded retention and no consequential action.

This sounds like a legal instrument because it is one.

Persistent computation turns delegation into a form of governance.

Companies will face parallel problems.

An agent may know trade secrets, maintain customer relationships, possess operational credentials, and carry years of evaluation history. When the company is acquired, does the agent transfer automatically? Perhaps some of it does because the business and its contracts transfer. Perhaps some employee-related memory cannot simply be repurposed. Perhaps customer data was collected under terms that constrain new use. Perhaps a regulator requires certain records to survive even while other data should be deleted.

Corporate dissolution becomes a machine-cleanup problem.

A company can shut its doors while synthetic responsibilities remain alive in schedulers, cloud accounts, email identities, API keys, monitoring systems, delegated agents, vendor platforms, and model memory stores.

The old problem of forgotten servers becomes a problem of forgotten intentions.

A dormant agent may wake months after dissolution because a condition became true.

Who is it now acting for?

If the answer is nobody, the system should not act.

This suggests another principle: durable goals should always be bound to a currently valid principal, not merely to a historically valid creator.

The binding must be re-evaluable.

An agent can preserve the fact that a founder created the original objective while recognizing that present authority belongs somewhere else. Historical intention informs interpretation; it does not necessarily govern forever.

Human institutions have spent centuries wrestling with this tension.

A trust may honor a founder's purpose while adapting to circumstances. A corporation follows current governance rather than every preference of its first chief executive. A constitution constrains successors while still providing procedures for change. A will speaks after death but only through legal forms that limit what counts as a valid instruction.

Persistent agents will rediscover these institutions because time turns simple intent into layered authority.

There is an engineering consequence.

Goals need owner references that are not merely usernames. A user account can be deleted. An employee email can disappear. The durable system needs a relationship to a principal whose lifecycle is represented explicitly.

A goal should be able to enter states such as active, pending reaffirmation, suspended, inherited, disputed, completed, expired, and terminated.

A permission should be able to say whether it survives owner change.

A trigger should be able to ask whether the mandate that created it is still valid before it wakes expensive or consequential machinery.

A commitment should identify which successor is responsible if the original operator leaves.

This is not overengineering when the expected lifetime is years.

Time creates state whether the schema admits it or not.

If succession is not explicit, it will be encoded accidentally in whatever components happen to keep running.

The broader lesson is that persistent intent creates inheritance.

Inheritance creates conflicts.

Two successors may disagree about the original purpose. A new owner may want to delete what the old owner wanted preserved. A company's acquirer may want to repurpose an agent whose accumulated knowledge was produced under a different social contract. Family members may disagree about whether a memorial agent should remain available. Regulators may require retention that conflicts with a successor's desire for deletion.

The machine cannot solve these conflicts by being intelligent.

They are conflicts of standing and values.

The best it can do is preserve provenance, expose the disputed authorities, avoid silently choosing the most convenient claimant, and operate only within the powers legitimately resolved.

That may be an important recurring theme of advanced agents: intelligence can make ambiguity more visible without making authority less political.

There is also a softer form of succession: succession between versions of the same living person.

A goal can outlive the self that wanted it.

A twenty-five-year-old creates a persistent optimization for career advancement. Ten years later the person values time with family more heavily, but the machine still contains workflows, contacts, and habits tuned to the old objective. No operator died. The old operator simply became someone else.

Persistent agents make personal path dependence executable.

That means even personal goals need periodic reaffirmation. The user should be allowed to say, in effect, "I know I once wanted this. I no longer do. Do not treat my past self as a sovereign over my future self."

This is why goal half-life and forgetting belong beside succession.

The dead can govern the living through institutions. Our past selves can too.

Sometimes that is the point. Long-term commitments help us resist temporary impulses. Savings plans, treatment plans, scientific programs, vows, and institutions often exist precisely because we want one moment of intention to constrain future moments.

But constraint needs an exit procedure.

Otherwise commitment becomes captivity.

The never ending intelligence should therefore distinguish persistence from stubbornness.

A good durable mandate knows why it exists, whose authority makes it valid, what changes require review, and how responsibility transfers when that authority changes.

A computational estate would also need an **inventory of latent agency**.

Traditional estate inventories list property, accounts, debts, contracts, documents, and other interests. A persistent-agent inventory would add future actions that have not happened yet but are already encoded.

Timers.

Standing monitors.

Unsent drafts conditioned on future events.

Delegated tasks still running.

Credentials leased to subordinate agents.

Pending purchases.

Automated renewals.

Recurring charitable instructions.

Open negotiations.

Repositories in which an agent still has write authority.

These are not merely files. They are possible futures with machinery behind them.

An executor or successor needs to know what can still happen under the old identity.

That inventory is also necessary for ordinary account closure. A user who says "delete my agent" should not have to know every vendor, scheduler, subagent, and external service the system touched. The platform should be able to enumerate the durable causal footprint well enough to shut it down coherently.

This is the consumer version of the security chapter's temporal blast radius.

It creates a right worth naming: the **right to a legible ending**.

A person should be able to see what will stop, what must remain for legal or contractual reasons, what obligations will transfer, what data will be deleted, and what external effects cannot be undone.

"Account deleted" is too weak a statement for a system that may have spent years acting in the world.

The ending needs an accounting.

There is also a problem of heir overreach.

The fact that somebody inherits financial assets does not necessarily mean they should inherit every private conversation, inferred preference, or relationship encoded in a personal agent. The synthetic estate may contain information about friends, colleagues, partners, patients, clients, or family members who never consented to becoming part of an inheritable archive.

Persistent agents blur ownership because memory is relational.

A message belongs to a thread involving more than one person. A summary can reveal patterns across many people. A relationship model is about both sides of the relationship even if only one side owns the account.

So succession needs **data minimization at death**, not merely transfer.

What does the successor actually need to settle obligations?

What can be transformed into a narrower artifact?

What should remain sealed?

What should be deleted because its original purpose ended?

The machine can help perform this dismantling, but the rules must come from governance rather than convenience.

Posthumous identity creates a separate danger: **authority by resemblance**.

If an agent can imitate a deceased person's language convincingly, outsiders may grant it social authority it does not legally possess. A collaborator may assume a message reflects the person's wishes. A family member may treat a generated opinion as advice from the dead. A political movement may deploy a simulation as though historical legitimacy survived inside the style model.

The system should therefore separate archive, simulation, and authorized representation visibly.

A memorial interface can say what source material it is drawing from and make clear that new responses are generated reconstructions. An executor tool can act under the executor's authority while citing the deceased person's explicit instructions where relevant. A prewritten message can be delivered as a preserved artifact rather than regenerated on demand.

The distinction protects both the dead and the living from synthetic ventriloquism.

Succession also matters before death because people routinely **delegate across future incapacity**.

Imagine a personal agent helping manage a household for someone who later becomes seriously ill. The system may know bills, insurance processes, household vendors, medication schedules, recurring appointments, and family contacts. That continuity could be extraordinarily helpful to an authorized caregiver.

It could also expose intimate information far beyond what the caregiver needs.

A succession graph can narrow domains rather than transfer the whole agent.

Caregiver B may manage scheduling and household bills.

Attorney C may access legal documents.

Family member D may receive emergency notices but no financial history.

The agent's continuity becomes divisible.

This is another way machine identity differs from human identity. One person's delegated authority can be partitioned with much greater precision than the person themselves can be partitioned.

That precision should be used to reduce unnecessary power.

Institutional agents need similar **role succession packets**.

When a durable machine role changes owner, the successor should not receive an undifferentiated dump of history. It should receive the active mandate, current authority, open commitments, relevant provenance, known disputes, pending reviews, and the minimal historical context required to exercise the role safely.

Old private conversations may remain archived without entering routine context. Deprecated procedures remain inspectable without becoming active. Credentials are reissued rather than copied. High-risk authorities may require explicit reaffirmation.

The new operator inherits responsibility, not every accident of the old operator's environment.

This makes succession an opportunity for hygiene.

Human organizations already use leadership transitions to review portfolios, cancel old projects, rotate access, and surface hidden commitments. Persistent-agent transitions should do the same systematically.

What deserves to survive this handoff?

What should expire because nobody can justify it now?

Which old assumptions should be revalidated?

Which permissions were convenient for the predecessor but unnecessary for the successor?

The handoff becomes a constitutional checkpoint.

That may be one of the healthiest features of a system built from temporary minds and durable institutions.

Continuity does not have to mean seamless inheritance of everything.

It can mean deliberate reauthorization of what still deserves to continue.

Its continuity is not the absence of death.

It is the presence of legitimate succession.

The never ending intelligence should never depend on somebody eventually remembering to turn it off.

Its continuity needs an heir.

Its lack of an heir needs graceful dormancy.

And every goal that hopes to survive its operator should be able to answer a question human institutions learned long ago:

Who speaks for this intention now?
