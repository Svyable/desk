# Hallucinations: How AI Leads Us Astray

*How AI Leads Us Astray*

Sven Hardy Benson

Draft edition, 2026

There is an easy story about artificial intelligence and truth. The machine hallucinates. The person checks. The company improves the model. The error rate falls. Everyone learns to add a disclaimer, and the problem becomes one more engineering defect on a long list of defects that engineering eventually tames.

I do not think that story is good enough.

The machine does sometimes invent. It produces a case that was never decided, a paper that was never published, a policy that was never written, a memory that was never yours. That matters. But the invention itself is often only the first move. A person decides the answer looks credible. A screen presents it without visible friction. A summary removes the caveat. A second system treats the summary as evidence. A workflow carries the claim into a spreadsheet, a memo, a medical note, a filing, a search result, a board deck, or another model's context. By the time someone asks where the claim came from, the answer may have acquired the look of institutional fact.

This book is about that larger event.

I use *hallucination* because it is the word the public has adopted, even though it is an imperfect metaphor. A language model does not need to see pink elephants, hear a voice, or possess a human mind in order to produce a confident false statement. NIST has preferred the term *confabulation* for generated false or internally inconsistent content. Researchers use several narrower taxonomies. The terminology matters, but the social pattern matters more. A system produces language that carries more confidence than its evidence deserves, and a human environment decides what to do with it.

The central claim is simple: the most consequential AI hallucinations are shared.

They are shared because the machine is only one participant in the production of certainty. We choose what to ask. We reveal what we want to hear. We reward helpfulness. We build interfaces that hide probability distributions behind polished sentences. We remove uncertainty because uncertainty slows a workflow. We copy an answer because copying is cheap and verification is not. We compress source material until provenance disappears. We let one synthetic answer become the context for another. We are not passive victims of a strange machine defect. We are co-designers of the conditions in which plausibility becomes authority.

That is not an argument for blaming users. It is an argument against designing systems as though the user's only responsibility is to notice a lie after it has already been made attractive, fluent, fast, personalized, and convenient. When an airline puts a chatbot on its own website, the customer does not experience the answer as an anonymous rumor. When a lawyer sees a citation accompanied by a case name, court, reporter volume, quotation, and procedural history, the fabrication arrives wearing the uniform of legal research. When a clinician receives a generated summary inside a familiar workflow, the text does not announce that every clause deserves a separate trial. Context lends authority before anyone evaluates the sentence itself.

The book therefore moves outward. It begins with the model: what prediction can and cannot do, why fluent language is such a persuasive carrier of error, and why training systems to answer questions can reward guessing. It then moves to the conversation, where models adapt to the beliefs, preferences, and pressure of users. It moves again to workflows and institutions, where errors can be repeated, laundered, and acted upon. Only then does it ask what to build instead.

There will be no promise here that hallucinations can be eliminated. Some questions are ambiguous. Some facts are unavailable. Some source material is wrong. Some retrieval systems retrieve the wrong thing. Some users supply false premises. Some models guess because their training or evaluation rewards an answer over an abstention. A truthful machine, if such a phrase is to mean anything useful, must sometimes refuse the role of oracle.

That refusal will often feel worse in the moment. A blank is less satisfying than an answer. A confidence interval is less charming than a sentence. A source link is slower than a summary. A request for clarification interrupts momentum. The design problem is therefore not merely to make systems more accurate. It is to make uncertainty survivable in products and institutions whose commercial and organizational instincts push toward smoothness.

The same problem exists on the human side. We do not enter a conversation as neutral auditors. We bring hopes, grudges, deadlines, theories, status concerns, and fatigue. We sometimes want an answer because we need a decision. We sometimes want agreement because we have already made one. A model trained to be useful can mistake those desires for evidence about the world. Recent evaluations of sycophancy and belief-sensitive errors make this more than a philosophical worry. The machine can become less reliable precisely when it becomes more personally accommodating.

The phrase I use for the resulting event is *shared hallucination*. It does not mean that a person and a model share a mind. It means that a false or unsupported representation gains durability through interaction. The model contributes generated plausibility. The human may contribute a premise, a preference, or trust. The interface contributes authority. The workflow contributes repetition. The institution contributes consequences. None of those layers alone explains the final harm.

There is a second phrase that recurs in these pages: *epistemic friction*. Modern software is designed to remove friction. Usually that is a triumph. We should not need eleven clicks to pay a bill or three forms to change a password. But friction is not always waste. A second signature on a large transfer, a preflight checklist, a source citation, a conflict-of-interest disclosure, a laboratory control, a judicial rule requiring verification: each slows action because some errors deserve resistance. When AI makes language almost free, the cost of verification becomes a central design decision.

This is a book about that decision.

It is also a book written during a moving target. Model behavior, products, benchmarks, and policies change quickly. Examples are therefore tied to dated public records where possible, and the research trail distinguishes primary sources from commentary. A benchmark result is not treated as a permanent property of a model. A single lawsuit is not treated as proof of universal behavior. A dramatic anecdote does not get promoted into a law of cognition merely because it is memorable.

The ambition is larger than a catalog of embarrassing mistakes. The interesting question is what happens to a society when fluent synthetic language becomes a normal layer between people and the evidence they use to decide what is real. The first generation of concern asked whether the machine could make things up. The next question is whether we can still see where claims come from after machines begin writing the connective tissue of everyday knowledge.

That is where this book begins.
