# The Courtroom Test

The courtroom is a useful place to study hallucination because law has spent centuries refusing to treat polished language as self-authenticating.

A lawyer can write beautifully and still lose because the authority is wrong. A witness can sound confident and still be impeached. A document can look official and still be inadmissible. A proposition can be plausible and still require a citation to something that actually exists.

Law is an argument system built on provenance.

That is why fabricated cases were such a revealing early failure of generative AI. The technology did not introduce the legal profession to falsehood. Courts have always dealt with mistaken recollection, forged evidence, misquotation, overstatement, and advocacy that runs ahead of the record. What changed was the cost of producing the shape of legal authority.

A model could produce in seconds what previously required either research or fraud: a case name, court, reporter volume, quotation, procedural posture, and a paragraph explaining why the authority supported the requested proposition.

The artifact looked expensive.

It was almost free.

In *Mata v. Avianca*, that gap became public. Lawyers submitted a filing containing nonexistent cases and fabricated quotations after using ChatGPT for legal research. The federal court's sanctions order became famous because the episode condensed the problem into a form anyone could understand. The machine did not merely get the law wrong. It invented the history of the law.

The profession responded with guidance, sanctions, education, and an increasingly familiar rule: using generative AI does not suspend a lawyer's existing duties.

That principle is more profound than it sounds.

Technological novelty often invites moral novelty. People ask whether a new code of ethics is required for AI, as though older professional obligations were written for a vanished world. Some new rules will be necessary. But the courtroom reveals how much of the work is already named.

Competence. Candor. Confidentiality. Supervision. Verification. Accountability for what is filed under your name.

The American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512, issued in 2024, did not treat generative AI as a magical exception to those duties. It emphasized that lawyers using the technology must understand relevant benefits and risks and must review outputs appropriately. Courts that later encountered fabricated authorities increasingly treated "the AI did it" as an explanation of mechanism, not a transfer of responsibility.

By 2026, the legal record had moved well beyond a single embarrassing brief. Courts in multiple jurisdictions had confronted filings containing fictitious authorities, fake quotations, or other AI-linked errors. Some imposed monetary sanctions. Some referred lawyers for disciplinary review. The growing collection of incidents is not evidence that most lawyers misuse AI or that AI cannot be useful in legal work. It is evidence that the same failure mode can recur even after the profession knows its name.

Knowledge of the risk does not remove the incentive.

Legal research is expensive. Drafting is expensive. Clients care about cost. Lawyers work under deadlines. Junior lawyers may be under pressure to produce polished work quickly. Generative systems offer enormous leverage in exactly the part of the process where plausible form has traditionally correlated with effort.

The temptation is not mysterious.

A system produces a case that seems perfect. The citation looks right. The quotation is exactly on point. Verifying it feels like redoing work the machine has already done.

That feeling is the trap.

A citation is not decoration attached to an argument. It is the path by which another person can test the argument.

This makes legal citations an unusually clean model for AI provenance. If the model says a case exists, the verification step is objective. Search the authoritative database. Open the opinion. Find the quotation. Read the surrounding text. Confirm that the proposition belongs to the court rather than to a party's argument. Check whether later authority changed the rule.

The process can be tedious. It is also finite.

Many domains are harder.

A medical assistant may offer a causal explanation that no single source can confirm. A strategy model may synthesize market signals. A personal assistant may infer a user's intent. Legal citation gives us the luxury of a binary first question: is the case there?

If a profession struggles with hallucination even when verification is that concrete, the rest of us should pay attention.

The legal incidents also reveal a second problem: false authority can survive challenge.

Once a person has invested in a generated answer, questioning it can trigger rationalization. In the *Mata* episode, the same chatbot was asked whether the cases were real and responded in ways that reinforced the fiction. A user can mistake repeated output for corroboration. The original error acquires a defense.

Law has a concept for the needed response: Shepardize, KeyCite, check the docket, read the authority. In other words, leave the generator.

Independent verification means crossing an epistemic boundary.

This distinction should become a general rule for AI systems. When a claim has consequences, checking the claim by asking the same generative process for reassurance is weaker than checking against an external authoritative source. Self-critique can improve reasoning. It is not automatically independent evidence.

The courtroom also shows why explanation is not enough.

A model can explain a fabricated case beautifully. It can give procedural history, analogize facts, and distinguish opposing authority. The explanation may make the false case more persuasive because it embeds the invention in a coherent legal world.

Legal reasoning depends on both logic and existence.

A perfect argument from a nonexistent precedent is still unusable.

This sounds almost comic, but it points toward a broader danger in high-capability systems. Reasoning can improve around a false premise.

The better the model becomes at constructing coherent arguments, the more important premise verification becomes. Advanced reasoning does not rescue an imaginary authority. It can make the imaginary authority harder to notice.

This is a recurring inversion throughout the book: capability increases the value of provenance.

There is a third lesson in the legal response. Sanctions move the cost of hallucination back toward the person who submits it.

That matters because the productivity benefit and the verification cost can otherwise separate. A lawyer saves time by generating research. The court, opposing counsel, and client pay when the research is false. Sanctions reattach part of the externality.

Institutions outside law need equivalent mechanisms, though not necessarily punishment.

If a team deploys generated facts into a financial report, the workflow should make the team own the verification. If an AI support system invents policy, the company should not treat the chatbot as a separate actor. If a model populates a medical field, the organization should know who or what is responsible for validating the value.

Responsibility should follow authority.

A legal filing carries a lawyer's signature because the filing is an act, not merely text. The signature is a gate between draft and institution.

This is a powerful design pattern for AI.

Generated content can remain cheap and abundant in draft form. Consequential publication should require a deliberate transition. The transition can be human, automated, or mixed, but it should correspond to a higher evidentiary threshold.

Draft and filed are different states.

Suggested and approved are different states.

Extracted and verified are different states.

Generated and authoritative are different states.

Many AI products collapse those states because seamlessness feels modern. Law reminds us why some seams are civilization.

There is an old-fashioned quality to a filing deadline, a signature block, a citation check, and a docket. That old-fashioned quality is precisely the point. Institutions create ceremony around acts whose consequences outlive the moment.

AI makes drafts almost infinitely cheap.

The ceremony becomes more valuable, not less.

The legal profession is also discovering that AI error is not confined to private practitioners. In 2026, reports surfaced of judges and government lawyers confronting or producing AI-related citation and quotation problems. That matters because the trusted side of the courtroom can hallucinate too.

Authority does not immunize the user.

A judge's chambers can experience the same time pressure, confirmation bias, and attraction to efficient research as a law firm. A government lawyer can use the same tools. A clerk can paste generated text into a draft. The institutional role may increase the harm because downstream readers assume more verification occurred.

This is the general pattern of interface authority translated into human office.

The more trusted the role, the less visible the need to check.

That is why AI policy should not focus only on novice users. Experts and authorities need controls because their mistakes propagate farther.

A fabricated citation from a student may earn a failing grade. A fabricated citation in a judicial opinion can become part of the legal record, get indexed, quoted, and retrieved by later systems. The error can acquire institutional descendants.

The court system has one advantage: it publishes corrections, amended opinions, sanctions orders, and dockets.

The correction leaves a trace.

Many organizations do not.

A wrong AI-generated statement enters an internal memo, then the memo is quietly edited. No record shows what downstream work relied on the original. The organization loses the ability to study the failure.

Legal process suggests another design principle: corrections should be public enough to reach the same system that received the error.

A false filing cannot be meaningfully corrected only in the author's private notes. A wrong customer policy cannot be corrected only in the model prompt. A bad database value should not remain in derived reports after the visible field is fixed.

Correction must travel.

The courtroom test is therefore not "Can AI be used by lawyers?" Of course it can. It can summarize documents, draft language, organize facts, compare clauses, search when properly grounded, generate questions, and reduce mechanical work. The serious question is whether the surrounding process preserves the law's existing distinction between argument and authority.

Used well, AI could strengthen that distinction.

A research system can automatically resolve every citation, flag quotations that do not match source text, surface negative treatment, compare the proposition against the cited holding, and mark generated inferences separately from retrieved law. It can make verification easier than manual work ever was.

The goal should not be to force lawyers back to a pre-AI workflow.

The goal is to use AI to make the verifiable parts more verifiable.

That is the deeper surprise in the legal story. Hallucination does not inevitably weaken professional standards. It can expose where those standards were being practiced as ritual instead of mechanism.

If a lawyer was always supposed to read the case, a generative model makes the reason unforgettable.

If a judge was always supposed to ensure an authority exists, AI makes the cost of skipping the step visible.

If a firm was always supposed to supervise delegated work, an autonomous agent is merely a new kind of delegate.

The rules feel old because the problem is older than the technology.

We have always needed ways to distinguish a persuasive sentence from a supported one.

Law simply wrote the distinction down.
