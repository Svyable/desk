# The Limit Does Not Exist: Who Gets to Draw the Line? — research brief

## Research question

How do institutions turn continuous, irregular, overlapping, or uncertain realities into discrete categories, and what happens after those categories begin distributing rights, money, care, representation, liability, or belonging?

The book should not argue that all boundaries are arbitrary. It should distinguish at least four different things that are often collapsed into one debate:

- a **physical discontinuity** that exists whether or not an institution recognizes it;
- a **measurement convention** chosen so observations can be made consistently;
- an **administrative threshold** chosen because a decision must be made;
- a **social or legal category** whose reality partly consists in people and institutions acting as if it exists.

Those can overlap. They are not interchangeable.

## Opening evidence spine

The first chapter begins with the coastline measurement problem because it isolates the mechanism without immediately importing a political argument. Mandelbrot's 1967 Science paper is the canonical mathematical reference. The chapter then moves from irregular physical edges to digital/legal representations such as Census TIGER/Line boundaries.

The next chapters should ground the same mechanism in domains where the line has consequences:

1. **Threshold mechanics.** Look for policy cliffs, regression-discontinuity examples, and administrative rules where nearly identical cases are treated differently because of a cutoff.
2. **Age.** Use the Twenty-Sixth Amendment as the cleanest American example of a precise legal boundary imposed on gradual development. Separate voting age from other age thresholds rather than pretending “adulthood” is one legal switch.
3. **Poverty.** Use Census documentation for the official poverty measure and Supplemental Poverty Measure. Preserve the Bureau's own description of the official threshold as a statistical yardstick rather than a complete account of need.
4. **Diagnosis.** Use clinical guidelines and public-health sources. Show both why thresholds are useful and why risk does not necessarily jump at the category boundary.
5. **Race and ethnicity.** Use the 2024 OMB revision to Statistical Policy Directive No. 15. Treat federal categories as data standards with civil-rights, statistical, and administrative purposes, not as claims that identity has one natural taxonomy.
6. **Species.** Use the Endangered Species Act definition and biological literature on species concepts, hybridization, subspecies, and distinct population segments. Keep law and taxonomy separate when they use the same word differently.
7. **Property and jurisdiction.** Follow cadastral surveys, river boundaries, annexation, school districts, precincts, service territories, and other cases where a line on a record changes lived incentives.
8. **Algorithmic thresholds.** Prefer real deployed systems and published decision rules over hypothetical “AI decides your life” examples. Ask what happens near the threshold, how calibration is evaluated, and whether an appeal exists.

## Source discipline

Prefer primary and first-party sources for the line itself: statutes, constitutional text, agency definitions, technical standards, original papers, court opinions, official datasets, and published clinical guidelines.

Secondary sources are useful for history and interpretation but should not be the sole evidence for a consequential threshold.

When the manuscript says a category changed, preserve both versions if possible. The change is evidence.

When a chapter uses a numeric cutoff, record:

- the exact source;
- the date or version;
- the population to which it applies;
- whether the threshold is diagnostic, screening, administrative, statistical, or legal;
- whether exceptions or confirmatory procedures exist.

## Falsification questions

The book becomes weaker if it quietly assumes every line is artificial. Each chapter should actively look for cases where a boundary is sharp, robust, and decision-relevant.

Questions to keep alive:

- Which boundaries remain stable across reasonable changes in measurement scale?
- When does a cutoff improve outcomes enough that edge-case unfairness is a tolerable cost?
- When is a common category necessary for comparability across institutions?
- Which apparently constructed categories track durable physical or biological discontinuities?
- When does adding discretion make a system less fair rather than more humane?
- Which appeals systems create inconsistency, favoritism, or strategic gaming?
- When does revising a category destroy longitudinal comparability more than it improves present accuracy?
- Are some lines valuable precisely because everybody knows they are conventions?

The conclusion should survive those questions. The target claim is not “all boundaries are fake.” It is narrower: **a consequential boundary should be judged partly by the quality of the process that drew it, the fit between its purpose and its evidence, and the system's ability to recognize when the simplification fails.**

## Working research queue

- collect primary literature on the coastline paradox and measurement scale;
- identify two strong policy-cliff case studies where the consequences of a threshold are empirically measurable;
- build a timeline of federal race/ethnicity statistical standards from 1977 through the 2024 revision;
- compare Official Poverty Measure and Supplemental Poverty Measure design choices;
- identify medical thresholds that have changed over time for evidence-based reasons;
- develop a species chapter around a case where legal protection and taxonomic uncertainty genuinely interact;
- find property/border cases where moving rivers or survey errors forced courts or agencies to decide whether the line follows geography or the record;
- collect algorithmic examples with documented thresholds, calibration data, and appeal procedures rather than relying on anecdotes.
