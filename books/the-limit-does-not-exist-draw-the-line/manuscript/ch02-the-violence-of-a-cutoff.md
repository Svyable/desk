# The Violence of a Cutoff

A child born on one side of midnight can spend a year in a different classroom from a child born on the other.

Nothing discontinuous happened to either child. The calendar did not discover a new kind of person. The school system needed a rule.

That is the basic operation of a cutoff.

Take something that changes gradually and force it to answer a yes-or-no question.

Old enough or not. High blood pressure or not. Eligible or not. Passing or failing. Inside or outside. Priority or standard. Protected or unprotected. Qualified or unqualified.

The rule creates a jump where the underlying thing may contain none.

This sounds like a defect until you try to run an institution without doing it.

A school district cannot enroll a child in 0.37 of kindergarten. An election office cannot give someone 62 percent of a ballot because they are almost old enough. A benefits agency cannot remain indefinitely undecided about every application whose facts lie near a threshold. A physician may think in probabilities, but at some point a prescription is written or it is not. A bridge is opened or closed. A loan is approved or denied. A defendant is inside one sentencing category or another.

Administration eventually asks continuous reality for a discrete answer.

The interesting question is what happens to everyone standing near the cut.

Consider school-entry dates. States and districts need to decide which children enter a grade together, so they choose a date. That date can create a large age difference inside a classroom between children who are separated by only a few days of birth. Researchers have been exploiting that bureaucratic fact for decades because it produces something unusually valuable: people who are similar in many respects but fall on opposite sides of an institutional boundary.

Joshua Angrist and Alan Krueger famously used quarter of birth and compulsory-schooling rules to study educational attainment. More recently, researchers have examined the effects of school-entry cutoffs on delayed entry, peer composition, academic outcomes, and even the social comparisons students make about their own bodies. The details vary by study, but the methodological trick is the same. The cutoff creates a discontinuity in treatment that biology did not provide on its own.

Econometricians call one family of these methods regression discontinuity designs. The name is almost too perfect for this book.

The people can be continuous.

The rule is not.

A well-designed cutoff can therefore become an accidental experiment. Compare cases just to the left and right of the boundary and, under the right conditions, the jump in outcomes can reveal what the rule itself caused.

That is useful for science. It is also a warning about governance.

A threshold does not merely describe a difference. It can manufacture one.

Suppose two children are nearly identical in age, health, family circumstances, and development. One meets the enrollment date. One misses it. From that moment forward they may encounter different classmates, different expectations, different test schedules, different sports-age positions, and different educational timelines. The initial difference was a line on a calendar. The downstream difference can become a biography.

The same structure appears in medicine, where the underlying quantity often changes continuously while the label changes at a number.

Blood pressure is a useful example because the number itself is familiar enough to feel objective. A cuff returns a systolic and diastolic reading. Those measurements carry real information. Higher blood pressure is associated with higher cardiovascular risk. Treatment can reduce that risk.

But “hypertension” is still a category placed on top of measurements and risk.

The United States provides a clean demonstration. Older federal health statistics commonly used a hypertension definition based on 140/90 mm Hg. The 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline moved the threshold to 130/80. When the National Center for Health Statistics applied the two definitions to the same 2017–2018 adult population, the estimated prevalence was 31.0 percent under the older standard and 46.6 percent under the newer one.

Fifteen percentage points of Americans did not acquire a new cardiovascular system when the guideline changed.

The line moved.

That does not mean the newer line was fraudulent. It does not mean the older line was true. It means the category was doing work.

The lower threshold reflected a judgment about evidence, risk, prevention, and when intervention becomes worthwhile. Once the judgment changed, millions of people moved categories without moving bodies.

This is why “the number is objective” is never enough.

The measurement may be objective within a known error range. The decision attached to it is another layer.

Even the World Health Organization and U.S. guidance can use different diagnostic thresholds in different contexts. That is not proof that blood pressure is socially constructed in the lazy sense of the phrase. Arteries are not voting. Risk is not imaginary. It is evidence that measurement and action are distinct problems. Different institutions can look at a continuous risk curve and choose different points at which the practical label changes.

The cutoff is where evidence meets purpose.

That sentence matters because thresholds are often argued about as if there must be one hidden number in nature waiting to be found.

Sometimes there is a physical transition. Water freezes under specified conditions. A material fails. A chemical reaction crosses an activation barrier. A transistor switches states within an engineered operating range.

Human systems borrow the visual confidence of these phase changes even when the underlying phenomenon is nothing like them.

A standardized-test score of 699 and 700 may not identify two kinds of student. A credit score one point above a lender's policy threshold may not identify a fundamentally different borrower. An income one dollar above a benefit cutoff may not identify a household with meaningfully greater material security. A blood-pressure reading one unit below a diagnostic boundary may not identify a person with meaningfully lower risk than the person one unit above it.

Yet the administrative consequences can jump sharply.

This gap between a smooth underlying variable and a discontinuous consequence is where many edge cases come from.

It is tempting to solve the problem by adding more categories. Instead of pass and fail, make five bands. Instead of hypertension and normal, create normal, elevated, stage 1, stage 2, and crisis. Instead of one income threshold, taper benefits gradually. Instead of a single age rule, evaluate maturity individually.

Sometimes that helps.

It also moves the problem.

Five bands require four boundaries. A taper replaces a cliff with a slope but still needs a beginning and an end. Individual review reduces dependence on a crude proxy but introduces discretion, cost, inconsistency, delay, and the possibility that bias moves from the rule into the reviewer.

There is no administrative system made entirely of nuance.

Even a case-by-case judgment ends with a decision.

This is the deeper reason thresholds survive criticism. They do several valuable things at once.

They compress information. They make rules legible. They allow strangers to administer the same program. They limit discretion. They make decisions auditable. They create expectations. They lower the cost of deciding again tomorrow.

A bright-line rule can be crude and still be fairer than a supposedly sophisticated system in which every official improvises.

That is easy to forget because the person harmed by a bright line is visible in a way that the person harmed by arbitrary discretion may not be.

Imagine a scholarship with a published test-score minimum. The applicant one point below the threshold has a precise complaint: one point changed everything. Replace the cutoff with holistic judgment and the rejected applicant may never know which conversation, impression, essay, recommendation, background assumption, or reviewer preference changed everything.

The line disappeared from view.

The sorting did not.

So the problem is not line versus no line.

The real choices are among kinds of lines, kinds of discretion, and kinds of error.

A threshold should therefore be judged less like a discovery and more like a piece of infrastructure.

What is it trying to accomplish?

What evidence supports placing it here?

How noisy is the measurement near the boundary?

What happens to people immediately on either side?

How large is the penalty for a false positive? A false negative?

Can the consequence be made gradual when the underlying reality is gradual?

Is there an appeal?

How often is the line reviewed?

What happens when the measurement technology improves?

Who pays the cost of being almost but not quite on the preferred side?

These questions make a cutoff less magical without making it useless.

The distinction is important because thresholds gain authority by hiding their own history.

Once a number has been printed on enough forms, repeated in enough policies, and encoded into enough software, it begins to look like a fact about the world rather than a decision about the world. A future administrator inherits the number without inheriting the argument. A database field accepts one value and rejects another. A dashboard turns the classification into a color. Contracts refer to it. Budgets are built around it. People plan their behavior around it.

The line acquires dependencies.

Then changing the threshold is no longer merely changing a definition. It can change budgets, caseloads, staffing, insurance coverage, legal obligations, school enrollment, statistical trends, historical comparability, and millions of individual expectations.

The cutoff has become infrastructure.

This is why moving a line can feel politically explosive even when the original line was never sacred.

Someone benefits from the old placement. Someone is harmed by it. Someone has built a system around it. Someone will be newly included. Someone else fears dilution, cost, congestion, stigma, liability, or loss of status. The technical argument arrives inside an existing distribution of consequences.

There is another reason the word “violence” belongs in this chapter, though it should be used carefully.

A cutoff performs a conceptual violence before it ever performs a social one. It takes variation and cuts it. It says that for this purpose, at this moment, differences within each side matter less than the difference across the boundary.

That compression is often necessary.

But necessity does not make the cut disappear.

A responsible institution should be able to say where the blade is, why it is there, and what it cannot see.

The best thresholds do not pretend to have discovered two species where there is one gradient. They announce the practical decision they are making.

That gives us the first principle for drawing lines honestly:

A cutoff should be precise about the decision without pretending the world itself is equally precise.
