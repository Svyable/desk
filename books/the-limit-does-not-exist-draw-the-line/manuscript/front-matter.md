# The Limit Does Not Exist

*Who Gets to Draw the Line?*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

## The World Does Not Arrive Pre-Sorted

There is a small violence in every clean line.

Not necessarily a moral violence. Often not even a mistake. Just the compression required to turn a messy world into a decision.

You are eighteen or you are not. You qualify or you do not. Your blood test falls inside the range or outside it. Your house is in this school district, not that one. Your income places you above the threshold. Your parcel ends here. This animal belongs to this species. This person checks this box. The border runs along this river, except where the river moved, except where the treaty meant the old channel, except where the survey disagreed, except where the people living there never accepted the premise.

The line looks precise after somebody has drawn it.

Before that, there is usually a problem.

We like to imagine categories as discoveries. Sometimes they are. Water freezes under specific physical conditions. A chromosome has a sequence. A legal document has a date. A bridge either clears the ship beneath it or does not.

But many of the distinctions that organize ordinary life are not discovered in that clean way. They are negotiated between what reality offers and what an institution needs.

An institution cannot usually administer a probability distribution.

It needs a box.

That does not make the box stupid. It makes the box powerful.

Consider a poverty threshold. The U.S. Census Bureau is unusually candid about what its official thresholds are: statistical yardsticks. They are useful for measuring poverty across a population. They are not a full description of what a family needs to live, and they do not change from one part of the country to another. Still, a threshold converts a continuous quantity called income into a category with a name. Below. Above. Poor. Not poor, at least according to that measure.

Nothing mystical happens to a family when its annual income crosses the line by a dollar.

The category changes anyway.

Medicine has the same problem under different stakes. Blood glucose, blood pressure, bone density, kidney function, tumor size, viral load, cholesterol, body temperature: bodies produce continua. Clinical practice cannot remain forever in the sentence, “It depends.” Decisions have to be made. Test again. Treat. Watch. Refer. Admit. Discharge. Insure. Reimburse. Warn.

So medicine creates ranges and thresholds. Sometimes those thresholds are backed by strong evidence. Sometimes they change as better evidence arrives. Sometimes two adjacent patients with nearly identical biology receive different labels because one number landed on opposite sides of a cutoff.

Again: the fact that a threshold is constructed does not mean it is useless.

The fact that it is useful does not mean nature put it there.

This distinction is the territory of this book.

The modern world is full of people arguing past one another because they are answering different questions about a line. One side asks whether the boundary corresponds to something real. The other asks whether the boundary is needed to make a decision. Both can be right.

A voting age is a good example because nobody seriously believes human maturity changes discontinuously at midnight on a birthday. The law still needs an age. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment does not say citizens who demonstrate sufficient cognitive maturity may vote. It says eighteen.

A precise legal boundary can govern an imprecise human transition.

That is not hypocrisy. It is administration.

The trouble begins when we forget the translation.

When a line has been used long enough, it starts to look like part of the landscape. A district boundary becomes “the neighborhood.” A racial category becomes an apparent demographic fact rather than a statistical standard with a history. A credit-score threshold becomes a description of a person instead of one model's decision rule. A species definition becomes a clean branch on a tree where reproduction, gene flow, hybridization, geography, and evolutionary history may refuse to cooperate. A shoreline becomes a fixed object on a map even though the water moves every hour.

The map hardens faster than the world.

That is why the first chapter begins at the coast.

The coastline problem is almost comically innocent compared with the lines that follow. Nobody loses Medicaid because a cartographer changed the length of Britain. Nobody is arrested because a ruler found another cove.

But the coastline gives us the mechanism in its purest form.

A boundary can become less determinate as measurement becomes more detailed.

That is backwards from the way people normally think about precision. We expect a better ruler to settle the question. We expect zooming in to reveal the true line. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it reveals that the line contains more line.

In 1967, Benoît Mandelbrot made the question famous in a paper whose title is itself a dare: “How Long Is the Coast of Britain?” He was building on earlier observations that reported coastline lengths change with the scale of measurement. A rough coastline does not behave like the edge of a tabletop. Use a shorter measuring unit and you can follow smaller irregularities. The measured length rises.

The coast has not changed.

The question has.

“How long is it?” secretly contained another question: “At what resolution?”

The same hidden parameter lives inside many categories.

How poor? Compared with which measure, household definition, benefit treatment, geography, and year?

What race? Under which reporting standard, with which ability to select multiple identities, and for what statistical purpose?

What species? Under which concept of species, using which genetic, reproductive, ecological, and geographic evidence?

Where is the border? According to which treaty, coordinate system, river channel, survey, court decision, or administrative practice?

Is the patient diabetic? According to which test, repeated under what conditions, interpreted in the presence of what other medical facts?

The closer you look, the less the question resembles the checkbox that eventually answers it.

This book is not a campaign against checkboxes.

Checkboxes are among civilization's most underrated technologies. They make systems repeatable. They make records searchable. They let millions of decisions be made without convening a philosophy seminar for each one. A world with no categories is not liberated. It is administratively impossible.

The question is what obligations appear once we admit that the line was drawn.

The first obligation is to remember what was continuous before we made it discrete.

The second is to remember who drew the line and why.

The third is to preserve some route for correction when the edge case turns out not to be an edge case at all, but a person standing in the exact place where the simplification fails.

The fourth is to distinguish measurement from meaning. A number can be precise while the category built on top of it remains contestable.

The fifth is to watch what happens after the category enters a system of incentives. Once money, rights, rankings, admissions, taxes, care, representation, or prestige attach to a boundary, people begin moving relative to it. Institutions optimize around it. Applicants learn it. Lawyers challenge it. Software encodes it. The line stops merely describing the world and starts changing behavior inside it.

That is where a classification becomes infrastructure.

And infrastructure is difficult to revise because other things begin depending on its shape.

The federal government's race and ethnicity standards are a useful case. They exist partly so data collected by different agencies can be compared and used. That consistency matters for civil-rights enforcement, health statistics, education, employment, and research. But the standards themselves have changed. In 2024, the Office of Management and Budget revised the federal framework, including a combined race-and-ethnicity question and a new Middle Eastern or North African minimum reporting category.

The old categories were not exposed as imaginary by the revision.

The revision exposed something more interesting: a category can be consequential, measurable, institutionally necessary, and historically contingent at the same time.

A civilization that understands that fact can build better categories.

A civilization that forgets it eventually mistakes its filing system for ontology.

There is another reason to care about this now.

Software is turning more of these lines into executable decisions.

A paper rule waits for a clerk. A software threshold can evaluate ten million cases overnight. A statistical category once used to describe a population can become an input to a model. A score can decide which applications receive additional scrutiny. A map polygon can determine jurisdiction automatically. A confidence threshold can decide whether an image is escalated to a human. An eligibility rule can approve or deny without anybody encountering the person at the boundary.

Automation does not create the line-drawing problem.

It removes the friction that used to remind us a line was being drawn.

That makes provenance more important. What is the threshold? Why is it there? Which evidence supports it? Who can appeal? What happens near the boundary? When was it last reviewed? Which populations were missing when the rule was designed? What happens if the underlying distribution changes?

These sound like technical questions.

They are political questions, medical questions, scientific questions, legal questions, and moral questions disguised as implementation details.

The chapters that follow move through different domains, but the structure repeats.

First, there is a continuous, irregular, overlapping, evolving, or uncertain reality.

Then somebody needs a decision.

Then a line is drawn.

Then the world reorganizes itself around the line.

The line may be excellent. It may be indefensible. Most are somewhere in between.

What matters is that we stop pretending the line arrived with the territory.
