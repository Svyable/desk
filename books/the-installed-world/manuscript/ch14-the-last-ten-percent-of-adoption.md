# The Last Ten Percent of Adoption

Long before smartphones existed and long before "digital divide" entered common political vocabulary, in 2002, the Federal Communications Commission ruled that American cellular carriers were no longer required to maintain analog AMPS network service alongside the newer digital networks that had, by that point, been available for years and carried the overwhelming majority of the country's cell phone traffic. Carriers holding the original A and B block cellular licenses nationwide were given one firm, non-negotiable federal deadline: February 18, 2008, after which every remaining analog cellular service, nationwide, could be switched off entirely and permanently. On paper, at the time, this looked like the routine, largely unremarkable retirement of a legacy technology that digital cellular service had already functionally and thoroughly superseded years earlier. In practice, the shutdown reached into small, overlooked corners of American infrastructure that nobody planning the original transition had fully or carefully accounted for in advance.

General Motors' OnStar system, the in-vehicle safety and communications service, had been built around AMPS specifically because, at the time of its original development, analog cellular offered the most comprehensive coverage available anywhere in the country — a reasonable, defensible engineering choice at the time in the 1990s that quietly became a genuine operational liability roughly a decade later, once the underlying network it depended on was scheduled for retirement. GM ultimately shut down analog OnStar service on January 1, 2008, ahead of the FCC's own deadline, after converting roughly ninety percent of its subscriber base to the newer CDMA digital standard; the remaining roughly ten percent, driving older vehicles whose onboard hardware simply could not be upgraded through any available software or replacement-module path, permanently lost the service. Home security company ADT separately petitioned the FCC to extend the AMPS deadline, disclosing that roughly 400,000 of its alarm systems still relied on analog cellular to communicate with monitoring centers, including — by one industry survey's estimate — around 138,000 households with no landline telephone at all, for whom the analog wireless alarm connection was the only backup communication path to emergency services in the home.

Contemporary press coverage from the weeks surrounding the shutdown captured the same tension repeatedly: trade reporters and consumer advocates noted that the overall migration had, by any broad statistical measure, been an unambiguous success, while individual news stories from the same period documented specific families whose home alarm system, medical monitoring device, or aging vehicle's emergency call feature simply stopped working the moment the analog signal went dark, often with little advance personal notice reaching the specific household affected. None of these holdout populations were large relative to the total number of cellular subscribers who had already migrated to digital service years earlier. That is precisely the point of this chapter. The last remaining users of an outdated standard are, almost by definition, a small percentage of the system's total user base by the time a shutdown date finally arrives. They are also, disproportionately and specifically, the users least equipped to absorb the cost of switching — older vehicles their owners could not afford to replace, alarm systems in homes without a landline fallback, exactly the populations for whom the old technology's continued function mattered most and whose voices were least likely to be heard during the years of industry planning that preceded the cutover.

## Why the last ten percent costs more than the first ninety

The economics here run directly counter to ordinary intuition about diminishing effort, in a way that consistently surprises project planners who have not encountered this specific pattern before and who default, reasonably enough, to assuming that a job ninety percent finished should require roughly a tenth of the remaining original effort to complete. Migrating the first ninety percent of a user base away from an old standard is, in almost every case this book has examined, the easier ninety percent: it includes the users with the newest equipment, the most disposable income, the strongest incentive to upgrade, and the fewest complicating dependencies. The remaining ten percent disproportionately includes exactly the opposite — older equipment that cannot simply be software-updated, lower income households for whom a forced upgrade is a genuine financial hardship, rural populations underserved by whatever replacement infrastructure is supposed to be available, and elderly or disabled users for whom a familiar interface change carries real practical and safety consequences beyond mere inconvenience.

This asymmetry means the true cost curve of a genuinely full, universal transition looks nothing at all like the smooth, gradually flattening S-curve Chapter 3 introduced through Everett Rogers's diffusion research, however well that same S-curve describes the earlier, easier phases of the identical rollout. The curve is closer to flat for a long stretch, then steep, then — in its final stretch — expensive and slow again, precisely because the remaining holdouts are the hardest cases by construction, not merely the last cases chronologically. A telecommunications company retiring an old network, a payment network retiring an old card technology, or a government retiring an old benefits system is not simply finishing the same job it started; it is solving a structurally harder version of the same problem, using the same budget line item that funded the much easier early phases.

## The liability shift that still has not fully shifted

The United States' transition from magnetic-stripe credit and debit cards to EMV chip cards illustrates the same dynamic inside consumer payments. Major card networks — Visa, Mastercard, American Express, and Discover — implemented a liability shift on October 1, 2015: after that date, fraud losses on a card-present transaction would fall on whichever party, the card issuer or the merchant, had not yet upgraded to chip-reading technology, reversing the default liability rule that had previously left issuers holding the cost. The incentive was substantial: payment card fraud losses in the United States had exceeded $16 billion in 2014 alone, and EMV chip technology, already standard across much of Europe for years by that point, had a strong evidentiary track record of reducing exactly the counterfeit-card fraud that magnetic stripes made comparatively easy.

Merchant adoption at the actual deadline was, by nearly every contemporary account and subsequent retrospective analysis, far from complete. Industry surveys around October 2015 found only about 27 percent of American merchants were EMV-capable by the liability-shift date itself, and subsequent estimates suggested that somewhere between 50 and 75 percent of merchants nationwide had either not yet upgraded their card terminals at all or had purchased compliant hardware but not yet activated it in daily operation. The gap closed only gradually over the following years, and disproportionately slowly, according to subsequent industry compliance surveys, among small independent merchants for whom a full set of new payment terminal hardware represented a genuine, non-trivial capital expense relative to their overall annual revenue, and among certain categories of terminal — older gas station pumps chief among them, whose EMV compliance deadline for fuel dispensers was repeatedly extended by the card networks themselves, from an original 2017 target eventually pushed years further out, in direct recognition that retrofitting the payment hardware inside an underground fuel pump was a categorically more expensive, more disruptive undertaking than swapping a countertop card reader. By 2018, merchants who had completed the transition were seeing roughly a 76 percent decline in counterfeit-card fraud, according to Visa's own reporting — real, substantial evidence that the technology worked exactly as intended, for exactly the merchants who had actually finished adopting it, years after the formal deadline had already passed for everyone else.

## The medical device still running an unsupported operating system

A third version of this problem persists quietly inside American hospitals, factories, and infrastructure control rooms, in a form less publicly debated than AMPS or EMV but arguably more consequential: specialized equipment — medical imaging devices, industrial control systems, automated teller machines, point-of-sale terminals — running operating systems whose vendor formally ended security support years or even over a decade earlier. Microsoft ended mainstream support for Windows XP in 2009 and extended support in April 2014, yet cybersecurity researchers and healthcare IT auditors have continued documenting XP and other long-unsupported operating systems running inside production medical equipment and industrial control systems well beyond that date, often because the specific device's manufacturer never issued, or was never required to issue, an operating-system upgrade path, and because the regulatory approval process governing medical devices in particular can require a costly, multi-year recertification process before any software change — including an operating system upgrade meant purely to close a security vulnerability — can be legally deployed on an already-approved device.

Security researchers presenting at industry conferences through the 2020s have repeatedly demonstrated proof-of-concept attacks against exactly this category of equipment, finding unsupported operating systems still exposed on hospital networks and industrial control systems years after public warnings began circulating, a persistence gap that tracks closely with the capital-budget and regulatory-recertification constraints described below rather than with any lack of awareness among the equipment's own operators. This is the last-ten-percent problem at its most consequential, because the holdout population here is not simply inconvenienced by an old standard's persistence; it is exposed to a specific, growing cybersecurity risk that grows worse every year the underlying software goes unpatched, precisely because the equipment's owners — hospitals operating on tight capital budgets, factories running equipment with a multi-decade expected service life — face exactly the same asymmetric cost structure described above: replacing a functioning, expensive piece of specialized equipment purely because its embedded operating system has aged past its vendor's support window is a much harder capital decision than replacing an ordinary office computer, and the device continues working, in the narrow functional sense the equipment's owner cares about, for years after the software underneath it has stopped receiving the updates that would keep it secure.

## The copper line nobody wants to maintain

Rural landline telephone service offers a slower-moving, still-unresolved version of this chapter's pattern, playing out over decades rather than a single cutover date. Traditional copper-wire telephone networks, built out across the twentieth century under a regulatory bargain that required carriers to serve even remote, low-density areas in exchange for broader operating privileges, have become increasingly expensive for telecommunications companies to maintain relative to the shrinking number of customers who still rely on them as their primary phone line, as cellular and internet-based calling have become the default choice for the large majority of American households. Major carriers have petitioned state and federal regulators for years, with slowly growing but genuine success across a number of states, to be released from the obligation to maintain copper infrastructure in areas where they can demonstrate an adequate wireless or fiber alternative exists, treating the shrinking population of remaining landline customers — disproportionately elderly, disproportionately rural, disproportionately lower-income — as exactly the "last ten percent" this chapter has described.

The federal Universal Service Fund and programs including Lifeline, which subsidizes phone and internet service for qualifying low-income households, exist specifically to address the gap this chapter's other cases have shown emerging without deliberate intervention: without some countervailing subsidy or regulatory requirement, the market's natural incentive is to serve the easy ninety percent well and treat the hard last ten percent as an acceptable, if regrettable, cost of otherwise efficient infrastructure modernization. The debate over exactly how fast copper retirement should be allowed to proceed, and what specific protections remaining landline customers deserve during the transition, remains active in state utility-commission proceedings across the country through the mid-2020s — an ongoing, unresolved instance of precisely the tension this chapter has traced through AMPS, EMV, and unsupported medical device software.

## The relay service behind the retired teletypewriter

A related case, carrying particular weight because it involves a population this book has not yet examined directly, concerns the retirement of TTY — teletypewriter — devices historically used by deaf and hard-of-hearing Americans to communicate over telephone lines by typing rather than speaking, a technology dating to adaptations of surplus teleprinter equipment in the 1960s and formalized through decades of subsequent federal disability-accommodation requirements. As telecommunications has shifted overwhelmingly toward internet-based and mobile-based communication, TTY's underlying infrastructure — reliant on the same analog telephone network increasingly being retired for exactly the economic reasons described above — has become progressively harder for carriers to support, even as federal regulations under the Americans with Disabilities Act continued, for years, to treat TTY compatibility as a baseline accessibility requirement.

Deaf and hard-of-hearing advocacy organizations were active, consistent participants throughout the resulting rulemaking proceedings, insisting that no legacy TTY user be left without a working option before any formal retirement date was set. The Federal Communications Commission has worked through a multi-year process to transition disability-accommodation policy away from TTY-specific requirements toward newer internet-based relay services and real-time text technology that integrate more naturally with modern smartphones and messaging platforms — a transition advocates within the deaf and hard-of-hearing community have generally welcomed as an improvement, since modern alternatives offer meaningfully better usability than a decades-old teletypewriter protocol, but one that has still required careful, deliberate regulatory sequencing specifically because a population that depends on a specific accessibility technology cannot simply be told the technology is being retired without a fully functioning replacement already in place. This case is worth including because it inverts the moral valence of this chapter's other examples: sometimes the "last ten percent" holding onto an old standard is not a population failing to keep up with progress, but a population whose specific needs the newer, more popular standard genuinely failed to address as well as advertised, until deliberate, sustained regulatory and industry attention closed that gap on the older technology's own more careful timeline.

## What responsible transition planning does differently

Placing AMPS, EMV, and embedded medical device software side by side yields a specific, actionable distinction that separates transitions this book would judge well-managed from those judged poorly managed, beyond simply whether a deadline was hit. The AMPS shutdown, whatever hardship it caused specific ADT customers and older OnStar subscribers, was preceded by a six-year notice period between the FCC's 2002 ruling and the 2008 deadline — long enough for the overwhelming majority of the affected population to migrate on a manageable timeline, even if a genuine, documented minority still fell through the gap. The EMV liability shift, by contrast, set a hard financial deadline without a comparably long, well-communicated runway for the smallest, most capital-constrained merchants, and the card networks' own subsequent decision to repeatedly extend the fuel-pump compliance deadline amounts to a tacit admission that the original timeline had underestimated exactly the "hardest last cases" dynamic this chapter has described.

The responsible pattern, visible in the more successful cases examined throughout this book, treats the last ten percent not as an afterthought to be swept up once the easy ninety percent has converted, but as the segment requiring the most deliberate planning from the outset — because it is populated, disproportionately and predictably, by exactly the users a transition's designers are least likely to hear from during the planning process, and exactly the users most likely to be genuinely harmed, rather than merely inconvenienced, when the deadline arrives regardless. ## Why "just a small percentage" is the wrong frame

It is worth naming directly the rhetorical move this chapter has tried to resist throughout, because it recurs in nearly every public debate over a transition deadline examined here: describing a holdout population as "only" a small percentage of the total, as though the percentage itself settled the question of how much attention that population deserved. Ninety percent adoption sounds, and often is presented in press releases and government progress reports, as a resounding success. But ninety percent adoption of the American population, or of American merchants, or of American telecommunications subscribers, is a remaining population measured in millions of actual people — the roughly ten percent of ADT's alarm-system customers still on analog service in 2008 represented hundreds of thousands of individual households; the merchants still lacking EMV compliance years after the 2015 deadline represented a meaningful share of small businesses nationwide; the hospitals and factories still running unsupported operating systems on specialized medical and industrial equipment represent, by any reasonable estimate, a population large enough to constitute a serious ongoing cybersecurity exposure at national scale.

Percentage framing flatters the transition's organizers and obscures the real, absolute number of people still affected once the "success" has been declared. This book's recurring central argument — that the installed world's true weight is always best measured in specific people, specific contracts, and specific pieces of equipment, rather than in tidy aggregate adoption curves — applies with particular force here, at the exact moment in a transition's life cycle when the temptation to declare victory and move on to the next project is strongest, and when the remaining, harder-to-reach population most needs the continued attention a declared victory tends to remove.

## The pattern this chapter leaves for the next

Every case examined in this chapter shares a structure worth naming explicitly before moving forward: an old standard's remaining holdouts are not a random sample of the original population, they are disproportionately drawn from groups with less capacity to absorb the cost of change, and the resulting gap between "the transition is essentially complete" and "every affected user has actually finished transitioning" tends to be measured in years, sometimes many years, after any official announcement of completion. Whether that gap closes well, as in the six-year notice period preceding the AMPS shutdown and the careful, advocacy-informed sequencing of the TTY-to-relay-service transition, or closes poorly, as in the EMV liability shift's uneven merchant adoption and the still-unresolved population of unsupported medical devices, depends less on the underlying technology than on whether the transition's designers deliberately planned for the hardest cases from the outset, rather than treating them as an inevitable, acceptable rounding error to be addressed, if at all, only after the easier majority had already been declared a success story.

Chapter 15 turns directly to the question this pattern has been circling throughout this chapter: when the cost of a transition and the benefit of a transition land on different people, who actually decides how that gap gets paid for, and by whom.
