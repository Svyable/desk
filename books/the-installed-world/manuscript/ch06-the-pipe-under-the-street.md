# The Pipe Under the Street

Somewhere in the United States, a water main breaks roughly every two minutes. That is not a figure from a single catastrophic event; it is the American Society of Civil Engineers' standing estimate of the ordinary, background rate of failure across a water distribution system built mostly out of sight, mostly decades ago, and mostly forgotten by the people who depend on it until the moment it fails beneath their street. The 2025 Infrastructure Report Card gave the nation's drinking water systems a grade of C-minus, unchanged from 2021, across a network of more than two million miles of underground pipe operated by nearly 150,000 separate public water systems. An estimated six billion gallons of treated water — water that has already been pumped, filtered, chemically treated, and pressurized at real cost — is lost to these breaks and to slower, chronic leakage every single day. More than half of public water systems surveyed identified rehabilitating or replacing aging pipe as their single most critical challenge, and the EPA's own most recent needs assessment put the national twenty-year investment requirement at $625 billion, more than $150 billion higher than its previous assessment just a few years earlier.

This chapter is about why a buried pipe is a categorically different kind of installed system from a plug standard or a software file format, even setting aside its scale and its cost, even though the underlying economic logic — an accumulated stock, expensive to replace, that other decisions have learned to depend on — is the same logic Chapters 2 and 5 already developed. A plug standard's installed base lives above ground, in plain view, in a form any electrician or engineer can inspect on request. A buried pipe's installed base lives, quite literally, out of sight, which changes not only the cost of replacing it but the cost of simply knowing, with any confidence, what needs to be replaced and when. The difference is not abstract, and it is not merely a matter of degree. It is that a pipe is buried, which means every dimension of the replacement problem — locating it, accessing it, verifying what else has since been built on top of it, coordinating with everyone whose property or utility service depends on the ground above it — is harder than it would be for almost anything installed above ground.

## The oldest infrastructure nobody can see

Water infrastructure has a peculiar property among the systems this book examines: its age is often genuinely unknown, even to the utilities responsible for maintaining it. Some sections of pipe still in active service in older American cities date to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, installed under record-keeping practices that predate digital asset-management systems by the better part of a century, sometimes under paper maps that have been lost, damaged, or simply never digitized. A utility deciding where to prioritize a limited annual replacement budget is frequently working from meaningfully incomplete information about what is actually in the ground, which materials were used at a given location and era, and which sections have already been repaired, patched, or partially relined rather than fully replaced.

Some utilities have begun using acoustic leak-detection sensors, ground-penetrating radar surveys, and machine-learning models trained on decades of prior break locations to infer pipe condition indirectly, since direct inspection of every buried segment is simply not economically feasible across a network spanning thousands of miles per city. These tools help, but they remain probabilistic estimates layered on top of genuinely incomplete historical records, not a substitute for the kind of exact, verified inventory a utility would need to plan replacement with real confidence.

This uncertainty compounds the ordinary economics of pipe replacement in a way that makes water infrastructure a genuinely harder planning problem than most of the systems examined elsewhere in this book. A utility cannot simply schedule replacement on a known design-life clock the way Chapter 4 described bridges being scheduled, because the life expectancy of a given section of pipe depends on soil chemistry, historical water pressure, material composition, and installation quality that often were never centrally recorded. Replacement, in practice, is triggered reactively almost as often as proactively — a main breaks, and the break itself becomes the utility's best available evidence about what condition the surrounding network is actually in.

## The lead pipe deadline

The most consequential current version of this problem is the national effort to replace lead water service lines — the pipes connecting a water main under the street to an individual home, historically often made of lead because the metal was durable, easy to bend around obstacles, and, before the health risks of chronic lead exposure were fully understood, considered a perfectly sensible plumbing material. The EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, finalized in October 2024, set a firm ten-year deadline requiring utilities to replace essentially every remaining lead service line in the country by 2034.

The scale of the remaining problem has itself become a moving, contested number. For years, the widely cited national estimate was roughly nine million lead service lines still in use. A subsequent EPA revision lowered that figure to approximately four million, a change some water-policy experts characterized as a legitimate data correction — utilities had, by 2024, completed much more inventory and replacement work than earlier estimates accounted for — while others raised concerns that the revision could understate the true remaining count, particularly in older cities and lower-income communities where utility records are least complete and where the lines most likely to have been missed by prior surveys are disproportionately concentrated. Estimated per-line replacement costs vary enormously depending on the source and the assumptions built into it, from the EPA's own average estimate of roughly $4,700 per line, ranging from $1,200 to $12,300 depending on local conditions, to figures used in some funding calculations closer to $12,500 per line once full excavation, restoration, and coordination costs are included. Total national cost estimates, compiled through 2025, range from roughly $45 billion to $60 billion, against approximately $15 billion in dedicated federal funding allocated so far — leaving a gap that state and local ratepayers, municipal bond issuances, or future federal appropriations will have to close, on a deadline that is now less than a decade away and does not adjust for funding shortfalls.

Every dollar of that gap illustrates a distributional pattern that recurs throughout the second half of this book: the health benefit of lead line replacement accrues overwhelmingly to the household whose water no longer passes through a lead pipe, while a meaningful share of the cost — through water rates, municipal bonds, or general tax revenue — is spread across a much broader ratepayer base, including households whose own service lines were never lead in the first place. Chapter 15 returns to this exact structure directly, because it recurs, in slightly different form, in nearly every large infrastructure replacement program examined in this book.

## The city that changed one variable

Flint, Michigan belongs in this chapter not because its crisis was primarily about installed infrastructure failing on its own, but because it shows with unusual clarity what happens when a single upstream decision interacts badly with an old, otherwise stable, installed pipe network that had been quietly managing a latent hazard for decades.

In April 2014, as a cost-saving measure during a period of financial distress, the city of Flint switched its municipal water source from Detroit's system, which drew from Lake Huron, to the Flint River, treated locally. The river water was more corrosive than the water it replaced, and the city's treatment process did not add corrosion-control chemicals at the levels needed to keep that more corrosive water from reacting with the lead service lines still connecting many Flint homes to the water main — pipes that had sat, for decades, in a kind of chemical equilibrium with the water that had always flowed through them, an equilibrium the source switch abruptly disrupted. Lead began leaching into the water supply at levels that, by the time the crisis was publicly acknowledged in 2015, had exposed thousands of children to elevated blood lead levels, with effects that pediatric researchers have documented as having lasting developmental consequences.

The Flint crisis was not, technically, a story about pipes wearing out. The lead lines in Flint had been in the ground, in most cases, for the better part of a century, and had not been the proximate cause of any comparable crisis for decades before 2014, precisely because the water chemistry flowing through them had been stable and properly treated. It is a story about how an installed system that has quietly accommodated a real underlying hazard for generations can be pushed past a threshold by a single change elsewhere in the system — a different water source, a different treatment protocol — made by decision-makers who did not fully account for how the water would interact with infrastructure nobody had thought carefully about in years. It is, in the vocabulary this chapter has been building, the clearest possible illustration of why the EPA's post-2024 lead-line replacement mandate treats the presence of lead pipe itself, rather than any particular water chemistry regime, as the hazard to be eliminated: a system that depends on perfectly maintained conditions to keep a dangerous material safely inert is one bad decision away from a public health emergency, and Flint is the specific, well-documented case that made that argument impossible to dismiss as theoretical.

## The gas line that could not be found in time

A different kind of buried-infrastructure failure struck the Merrimack Valley region of Massachusetts on September 13, 2018, when a contractor working for Columbia Gas, replacing aging cast-iron gas distribution pipe in Lawrence, Andover, and North Andover, disconnected the pressure-regulating sensors from an old gas main without properly relocating them to the new pipe being installed alongside it. The over-pressurized natural gas that resulted surged through the distribution network and into homes across the three towns, triggering more than 80 fires and explosions within a span of roughly an hour, killing one person, injuring roughly two dozen more, and forcing the evacuation of tens of thousands of residents. The National Transportation Safety Board's subsequent investigation found the failure traced directly to inadequate engineering oversight of exactly the kind of infrastructure-replacement project this chapter has otherwise described as slow and heavily coordinated for good reason: a project meant to modernize an aging pipe network instead became the mechanism of catastrophic failure, because a single step in the coordination between old infrastructure and new was skipped.

The Merrimack Valley disaster is the counterpoint this chapter needs to the argument that buried infrastructure's caution is simply bureaucratic overhead. The specific procedural step that failed — relocating pressure sensors from old pipe to new before decommissioning the old segment — is exactly the kind of "excessive" coordination requirement a project under schedule pressure might be tempted to compress. What actually happened when it was compressed was not a modest cost overrun. It was dozens of structure fires in a single afternoon. Read together, Flint and Merrimack Valley make the same point from opposite directions: buried infrastructure's slow, careful, heavily procedural replacement discipline exists because the alternative, demonstrated twice in this chapter within a five-year span, is measured in poisoned children and burning houses, not merely in delayed schedules.

## Why "just dig it up" is never just digging it up

A pipe replacement project's true cost rarely lives in the pipe itself. Excavating a city street to access buried infrastructure means first locating every other utility that may share the same corridor — gas lines, electrical conduit, fiber-optic cable, steam lines in some older downtown districts, storm sewers, and the water main itself, often laid decades apart by different agencies using different mapping conventions, some of them barely legible today. The United States operates a nationwide "call before you dig" system, coordinated through 811, specifically because unmarked or mismarked utility lines remain a leading cause of excavation accidents — including gas line strikes that have caused fatal explosions in residential neighborhoods, incidents documented by the National Transportation Safety Board across multiple states over the past two decades. Verifying what is actually buried beneath a given stretch of street, before a crew can safely begin digging, is frequently a more time-consuming and more expensive step than the excavation itself.

Once the street is open, the work rarely ends with the pipe. Utilities in many jurisdictions coordinate replacement projects deliberately — a practice sometimes called "dig once" policy — specifically to avoid opening the same stretch of pavement multiple times for water, then sewer, then gas, then fiber, each on its own independent schedule, because the marginal cost of restoring pavement, curbs, and landscaping after excavation is high enough that doing it once for several utilities at once is often cheaper than doing it separately for each. Even well-coordinated projects still have to restore road surfaces to engineering specification, verify no adjacent private property or foundation was disturbed, and, in older or more affluent neighborhoods, contend with historic district design requirements governing exactly how a repaved street or restored curb must look once the crew leaves. None of this shows up as "pipe cost" in a simple accounting of a replacement project's materials and labor. All of it is real installed-world cost, layered onto a project that a spreadsheet might otherwise price as though the only expense were the pipe.

## An asset that ages differently by material

Not every pipe under the street ages at the same rate, and the variation matters for how a utility should actually plan replacement rather than treating "aging water infrastructure" as a single undifferentiated problem. Cast iron pipe, widely installed from the late nineteenth century through roughly the mid-twentieth, is prone to corrosion and, in colder climates, to a specific failure mode where the pipe becomes brittle with age and cracks under the stress of ground movement from freeze-thaw cycles — a failure mode the Merrimack Valley disaster's underlying cast-iron replacement project was, ironically, working to prevent even as the replacement process itself triggered a different catastrophe. Ductile iron, which began replacing cast iron in new installations from the 1950s onward, is more resistant to that specific brittle-fracture failure but remains vulnerable to corrosion in aggressive soils. Concrete pressure pipe, used for large-diameter transmission mains, can fail catastrophically when internal steel reinforcing wires corrode and snap, sometimes with little external warning until the pipe ruptures. Modern PVC and high-density polyethylene pipe, now standard for most new installations, resist corrosion far better than any of their predecessors but have their own, less well-understood long-term degradation profile, since the oldest plastic water mains in the ground have not yet existed long enough for engineers to observe a full multi-decade failure curve the way they can for a century of cast iron.

This material heterogeneity means a city's underground pipe network is not one asset with one age and one clock, the way Chapter 4 could reasonably treat a vehicle fleet or a coal plant as a single, roughly comparable category. It is a patchwork of different materials, installed across different decades under different engineering standards, each with its own failure signature, buried in soil whose corrosivity itself varies block by block. A responsible utility's asset-management plan has to model all of that heterogeneity simultaneously — which is precisely why so many utilities, particularly smaller ones without dedicated engineering staff, fall back on reactive repair rather than proactive replacement: building an accurate predictive model of failure risk across a patchwork this complex is itself a nontrivial and expensive undertaking, on top of the cost of the replacement work the model would eventually recommend.

## The sewer that has to be inspected from the inside

Wastewater infrastructure carries its own distinct version of this chapter's argument, worth a brief mention because it illustrates a monitoring challenge even more severe than drinking-water pipe faces. A sanitary sewer line cannot generally be inspected through the kind of acoustic leak-detection methods water utilities use for pressurized supply pipes, because a gravity-fed sewer line, unlike a pressurized water main, does not maintain the constant internal pressure that makes acoustic anomaly detection practical. Municipal sewer inspection instead typically relies on closed-circuit television crawlers — small robotic cameras sent physically through the pipe — a slow, labor-intensive process that limits how much of a city's total sewer network can realistically be inspected in any given budget cycle, meaning most cities work through their sewer inventory on a rotating schedule spanning years, discovering a meaningful share of serious defects only when a section finally fails badly enough to surface as a sinkhole, a backup, or a sudden odor complaint rather than through proactive, comprehensive inspection.

This inspection gap compounds every other constraint this chapter has already described: a utility already working from incomplete historical records about pipe age and material, already facing a nationwide funding shortfall the EPA's own $625 billion twenty-year needs assessment documented, is also working with direct visual inspection data covering only a fraction of its total buried network in any given year — meaning the true condition of a substantial share of America's underground sewer infrastructure is not merely under-funded to repair. It is, in a very literal sense, currently unknown to the public agencies responsible for maintaining it, discovered only in the moment it finally, visibly fails.

## What is actually being protected

It would be easy, after a chapter built mostly around delay, cost, buried uncertainty, and two genuinely frightening disaster case studies, to read underground infrastructure purely as an obstacle — the installed world at its most literal, blocking progress six feet beneath the sidewalk. That reading misses something this chapter's evidence actually supports.

A water distribution system's slow, patient, heavily coordinated approach to replacement is not simply bureaucratic caution. It reflects genuine, serious risk: a hastily excavated street can rupture a gas line, undermine a building's foundation, or sever fiber-optic cable serving a hospital, each of which is a far worse outcome than a delayed replacement schedule. The 811 call-before-you-dig system, the dig-once coordination practices, and the historic-district restoration requirements that make pipe replacement slow are, in the vocabulary this book has been building since Chapter 3, exactly the kind of installed-world friction that is protecting something real rather than merely imposing cost for its own sake. The genuine failure in the water-infrastructure story is not that replacement is careful. It is that funding has not kept pace with a genuinely careful, genuinely necessary process — a $625 billion twenty-year need against a national appropriation nowhere close to that scale, a lead-line deadline arriving in less than a decade against a still-uncertain final count of lines that must be found, mapped, and replaced before it does.

The honest version of this chapter's argument, then, is not that buried infrastructure is simply slow for its own sake, nor that the caution surrounding it is always warranted. It is that the two failures examined here — one from decades of inattention meeting a single bad decision, the other from a single skipped procedural step during otherwise necessary modernization — point in opposite directions about what kind of intervention actually helps. Flint argues for more attention, more monitoring, and faster replacement of known hazards before a change elsewhere in the system pushes them past a threshold. Merrimack Valley argues for slower, more disciplined execution of the replacement work itself, not less of it. A reader tempted to draw one lesson from this chapter — replace faster, or replace more carefully — should notice that both cases, examined honestly, argue for both at once, which is a considerably harder policy position to hold than either slogan alone.
