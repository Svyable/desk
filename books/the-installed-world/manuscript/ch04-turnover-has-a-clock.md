# Turnover Has a Clock

The average car on an American road in 2025 was 12.8 years old, according to S&P Global Mobility's annual analysis of vehicle registration data — the fourth consecutive year the figure had risen, up from 12.6 years in 2024 and roughly two months older still than the year before that. Passenger cars specifically averaged 14.5 years; light trucks, which now dominate new sales, averaged 11.9 years. Vehicles in operation nationwide had climbed to about 289 million, even as new registrations passed 16 million in 2024 for the first time since 2019. The scrappage rate — the share of the fleet retired in a given year — held at roughly 4.5 percent, nowhere near fast enough to offset the growing mass of aging vehicles still on the road.

Sit with that scrappage rate for a moment, because it is the actual clock this chapter is about, and because almost every argument in the rest of this book depends on taking such an unglamorous number seriously rather than skipping past it toward the more exciting question of what the fleet will eventually look like once it finally turns over. At 4.5 percent annual turnover, it takes on the order of two decades for a genuinely new standard — a new fuel type, a new safety technology, a new charging connector — to work its way through the full installed fleet, even in the improbable case where every single new vehicle sold from this point forward adopted the standard instantly. Real transitions are slower than that ceiling, because adoption of any new standard among new vehicles itself ramps up gradually. The vehicle fleet is not resisting change out of stubbornness. It is behaving exactly the way an asset with an average dwell time in the mid-teens of years, and a low single-digit percentage of annual replacement, should behave. The clock is not a metaphor. It is arithmetic.

## Vintage capital, aging in place

Economists have a specific term for this phenomenon: vintage capital. The idea, developed across decades of growth theory beginning with economists including Robert Solow in the early 1960s, treats each piece of capital equipment — a factory, a power plant, a vehicle, a building — as embodying the technology and design choices available at the moment it was built, and then aging in place, unable to absorb every subsequent improvement without being substantially rebuilt or replaced outright. A 1998 sedan cannot simply download 2025's crash-avoidance software. A coal plant designed in 1975 cannot simply be retrofitted into a modern combined-cycle gas turbine without effectively becoming a new plant. Each vintage of capital carries its birth year's technology forward until it is retired, which means an economy's overall technology level, at any moment, is not the technology level of its newest products — it is a weighted average across every vintage still in service, most of which are considerably older than the newest available model.

This has a direct, underappreciated implication for how fast any new standard can plausibly spread through a stock of durable capital rather than through a flow of new purchases. A technology adopted by 100 percent of new vehicle sales starting tomorrow would still take on the order of a decade to reach half of the vehicles actually on the road, given how the existing fleet's age distribution and scrappage rate work, and considerably longer to approach full penetration, because the oldest, slowest-to-retire vehicles are disproportionately concentrated among the vehicles that stay in service longest — older, cheaper, often rural or lower-income owned, and less likely to be replaced on a fast cycle. This is one of the most consistent and most frequently ignored facts in forecasting technology transitions involving durable capital: the relevant question is almost never "how fast can the new thing be built." It is "how fast can the old thing be retired," and the two numbers are rarely close.

## The bridge that outlives its own design life

Different categories of installed capital carry very different clocks, and the difference is not always in the direction intuition would guess. The American Society of Civil Engineers' 2025 Infrastructure Report Card gave the nation's bridges a grade of C — unchanged from 2021 — built on new federal bridge inventory data showing the average American bridge was 47 years old, against an average designed service life of 50 years. Read plainly, that means the typical bridge in active use today is approaching the end of the design assumptions its engineers made when it was built, and a meaningful share are already past it: bridges rated structurally deficient averaged nearly 69 years old in the same dataset, compared with 44 years for bridges not rated deficient. Of the roughly 623,000 bridges the report card covers, 49.1 percent were rated in fair condition, 44.1 percent in good condition, and 6.8 percent in poor condition — a share that has actually declined from 12 percent in 2011 and 7.5 percent in 2021, evidence that active maintenance and targeted replacement can bend the aging curve, but not evidence that the underlying clock has stopped running.

A bridge's clock differs from a vehicle's clock in almost every dimension that matters for this book. Its replacement rate is a public capital-budgeting decision rather than millions of individual consumer choices; its service life is measured in generations rather than years; its failure mode, when the clock finally runs out unaddressed, is catastrophic rather than merely inconvenient, which is a large part of why the 1983 collapse of the Mianus River Bridge in Connecticut and the 2007 collapse of the I-35W bridge in Minneapolis remain reference points in American infrastructure policy decades later. A vehicle owner who ignores an aging car mostly bears the risk personally. A public agency that ignores an aging bridge past its design life is making a decision on behalf of everyone who drives across it, without necessarily consulting any of them individually about the tradeoff.

## The plant that was built to last exactly as long as it has

Power generation offers a third clock, and the coal fleet illustrates why "old" and "obsolete" are not synonyms in this book's vocabulary, however often they get used that way in casual conversation. Most of the operating U.S. coal fleet was built in the 1970s and 1980s — a boom period for coal construction driven by the era's energy policy and the aftermath of the 1970s oil shocks — which puts the typical remaining coal plant somewhere in its fourth or fifth decade of operation. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's 2025 data on plant retirements found something that should complicate any simple story about an inevitable, accelerating exit from coal: only 2.6 gigawatts of coal-fired capacity retired during 2025, the least in fifteen years, even though plant operators had begun the year with 8.5 gigawatts of retirements planned. Roughly 4.8 gigawatts of those planned retirements were delayed to a future year, and operators of two plants totaling 1.1 gigawatts canceled their retirement plans outright — in some cases under emergency orders from the federal government compelling continued operation to meet regional demand, including demand newly arriving from data centers.

The coal plant's clock, in other words, is not simply running down on a fixed physical schedule the way a bridge's design life does. It is being actively reset, plant by plant, by a demand shock — AI-driven electricity growth — that nobody modeling coal retirement schedules in 2020 had built into their assumptions. Fewer than 200 large coal units remained without an announced retirement date as of the 2025 data, and 118 of those were already at least 40 years old; roughly 36,341 megawatts of coal capacity were still scheduled to retire by 2030. But "scheduled" and "actual" have diverged before and, on this evidence, are diverging again, because an aging plant that would otherwise retire on a predictable actuarial clock is worth more to keep running than to replace the moment a large enough buyer — in this case, an electric grid straining to serve new data center load — is willing to pay for its continued output rather than wait years for a new plant's queue position to clear.

## The building that never gets torn down

Buildings run on the slowest clock this chapter examines, and the reason is worth dwelling on because it explains why urban housing markets, commercial real estate, and building-code reform all move at a pace that can feel glacial even to people who fully accept the case for changing them.

A building, unlike a car, does not wear out on a predictable mechanical schedule and get scrapped once repair costs exceed its remaining value. Structural steel and concrete, properly maintained, can outlast the humans who designed them by a century or more; a large share of the commercial and residential building stock standing in any major American or European city today was built before World War II, extensively renovated rather than demolished, precisely because demolition and rebuilding is dramatically more expensive, slower, and more disruptive to existing tenants and neighborhoods than renovating around the structure that already exists. Where a vehicle fleet turns over at something like 4.5 percent a year, whole-building replacement in a mature city typically proceeds at a fraction of one percent annually — new construction adds to the stock faster than demolition removes from it, meaning the buildings a city inherits from previous decades do not simply get replaced on a schedule; they get renovated in place, again and again, layer upon layer of code compliance and mechanical upgrades wrapped around a structural skeleton that may never be fully replaced within a single human lifetime.

This is the deep reason retrofit chapters recur throughout this book — heat pumps and induction ranges in Chapter 17, lead service lines in Chapter 15, brownfield data centers in Chapter 1 — rather than being treated as a niche subtopic. When the underlying structure changes on a near-century clock, almost every technology that plugs into a building has to be designed, from the outset, as something that will eventually be retrofitted into an existing structure rather than installed once into a new one. A wiring standard, a heating system, or a window specification that only works cleanly in new construction is, in a very real sense, opting out of most of the market it could otherwise serve, because most of the buildings that will exist in 2040 already exist today.

A useful way to picture the difference: imagine every building standing in a major city labeled with its decade of original construction. In most mature American and European cities, that label would show a long tail stretching back well over a century, with the bulk of the stock concentrated in construction booms decades in the past — postwar expansion, mid-century urban renewal, whatever local building cycle happened to coincide with periods of economic growth and available land. New construction adds a thin, visible layer to the top of that stack every year. It does not replace the layers underneath. A policy or technology that only works for the top layer is, by construction, a policy for a small and slowly growing minority of the buildings people will actually occupy for the next several decades.

## Software's clock looks fast until it isn't

It is tempting to assume software escapes this entire discussion — that code, unlike concrete or steel, can simply be rewritten and redeployed at the speed of a developer's keystrokes, making software the one category of installed capital immune to a turnover clock. Chapter 7 devotes itself to why that assumption is wrong for exactly the systems that matter most, but it is worth flagging the shape of the argument here, because it completes this chapter's survey of clocks running at dramatically different speeds within the same economy.

A consumer app can genuinely be rewritten and redeployed in weeks, because its installed base is mostly a collection of individual phones running whatever version an app store pushes to them next. A bank's core ledger, an airline's reservation system, or a government benefits-payment platform cannot, because their installed base includes decades of transaction history, interlocking downstream systems that assume specific data formats and behaviors, regulatory certifications tied to the current system's specific architecture, and a body of institutional knowledge about exactly how the system fails that took years to accumulate and cannot simply be copied into a new codebase along with the code itself. The clock on that kind of software is not measured in developer-hours. It is measured, as later chapters show in specific dollar and year terms, on a timescale closer to a power plant's than to a phone app's — which is precisely why so many of the systems examined later in this book are, on paper, decades past the point at which a purely technical assessment would have recommended replacing them.

## Reading a clock before it runs out

None of these three cases — vehicles, bridges, coal plants — obeys the same rhythm, and that is the point of walking through all three rather than picking whichever one makes the chapter's argument most cleanly. A vehicle fleet turns over through millions of individually rational, relatively small, relatively fast household decisions, bounded by a scrappage rate that moves only slowly even under strong policy pressure, as later chapters on Cash for Clunkers will show directly. A bridge network turns over through public capital budgets, bounded by the design life engineers assign at construction and by the political willingness to fund replacement before failure rather than after it. A power plant turns over through a genuinely economic decision — continue operating an asset whose capital cost is already sunk, or retire it — that can be delayed, accelerated, or reversed by a demand shock nobody building the original retirement schedule anticipated.

What unites them is not their speed but their legibility. A vehicle fleet's age distribution is public statistical record, published annually. A bridge's design life is stamped into its own engineering specification on the day it opens. A power plant's retirement economics are visible years ahead in its fuel costs and depreciation schedule to anyone willing to model them. None of these clocks were secret before they became inconvenient; each was simply easier to ignore than to read. A fleet's scrappage rate is a matter of public statistical record. A bridge's design life is stamped into its engineering specifications the day it opens. A plant's retirement economics are a function of its fuel costs, its capital depreciation schedule, and the price other generators are willing to accept, all of which are at least partially observable well before the retirement decision itself becomes public. The chapters that follow return to this idea repeatedly, because one of the most avoidable mistakes in forecasting a technology transition is treating the retirement schedule of the installed system it must replace as an unknowable black box, when in most of the cases this book examines, the clock was legible the whole time to anyone willing to look for it — an actuarial table hiding in plain sight, waiting for a forecaster to read it before betting a company, a policy, or a product launch on when the old world would finally make room.

## The clock hiding inside a household appliance

A smaller, more domestic version of vintage-capital economics runs through the ordinary major appliances sitting in most American kitchens and utility rooms, worth a brief mention here because it scales the chapter's argument down to a level every reader has direct, personal experience with. A residential water heater typically carries a manufacturer-rated service life of roughly eight to twelve years; a central air conditioning compressor, fifteen to twenty; a gas furnace, similarly fifteen to twenty; a refrigerator, ten to twenty depending on use and maintenance. None of these appliances fail all at once across the national housing stock. They fail on a distributed, actuarial schedule closely tied to their age, which means the total population of, say, gas furnaces due for replacement in any given year is a knowable, forecastable number — precisely the kind of legible clock this chapter's larger cases have already described at industrial scale, just visible here at the scale of a single household's utility closet.

This distributed, predictable replacement schedule is exactly what makes California's replacement-triggered electrification approach, examined later in this book's fourth part, a coherent policy strategy rather than a vague aspiration: regulators who know roughly how many gas water heaters and furnaces are due for natural replacement in a given county in a given year can forecast, with real confidence, how fast a replacement-triggered mandate will actually convert the existing housing stock, using nothing more exotic than the same manufacturer service-life data any HVAC contractor already consults every working day.

## When the clock gets forced

Every clock described so far is an endogenous one — a rate of replacement that emerges from millions of ordinary decisions, or from an engineering design life, or from the economics of continued operation versus retirement, without any single actor deliberately setting the pace. It is worth noting, briefly, that clocks can also be forced, deliberately, by policy — and that forcing a clock is a different, harder, and more consequential act than simply describing one, which is why it gets a full treatment of its own later in this book.

The 2009 American transition from analog to digital television broadcasting is a clean small-scale example of a forced clock, because Congress picked an exact date. The Digital Television Transition and Public Safety Act of 2005 set a firm deadline, later adjusted by the DTV Delay Act to June 12, 2009, by which every full-power television station in the country would be legally required to cease analog broadcasting. Roughly a fifth of American households still relied on over-the-air analog television with no cable or satellite subscription, meaning millions of working television sets would become unable to receive a broadcast signal the moment the switch flipped, unless equipped with a digital-to-analog converter box. The federal government did not simply mandate the switch and let the installed base of analog sets fend for itself; it created the TV Converter Box Coupon Program, ultimately funded at roughly $1.5 billion plus an additional $650 million from the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, offering households up to two $40 coupons toward the roughly $50 to $70 converter boxes needed to keep an existing analog television functional after the cutover.

The digital television transition succeeded, on its own terms, in an unusually clean and total way: on a single date, an entire national broadcasting standard changed, and the installed base of hundreds of millions of analog receivers was bridged rather than abandoned. It is worth remembering as a genuine counterexample to any temptation to read this book as an argument that installed-world transitions are always slow. They can be fast, total, and cleanly executed — but the digital television case also shows what that kind of speed actually costs: not merely engineering effort, but a dedicated, taxpayer-funded subsidy program explicitly designed to prevent the transition's costs from falling on exactly the households — often older, lower-income, and disproportionately reliant on free over-the-air television — least able to absorb a sudden, mandatory hardware purchase. A forced clock, in other words, is not free. It simply moves the cost from "time" to "money," and Chapter 15 returns directly to the question of who ends up paying it when the next mandated transition arrives without as generous a coupon program attached.
