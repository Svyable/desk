# The Mainframe Behind the App

On December 31, 1999, tens of thousands of programmers, many of them retired or nearly retired specialists brought back specifically for the occasion, sat in operations centers around the world waiting to see whether four decades of an ordinary, unglamorous cost-saving convention — storing calendar years as two digits instead of four, a choice made when memory and storage were extraordinarily expensive and every saved byte mattered across millions of stored records — would cause bank balances, pension calculations, air traffic systems, and utility billing to fail simultaneously the moment the year rolled from "99" to "00." Estimates of the total global cost of Y2K remediation, compiled by the U.S. General Accounting Office and other bodies in the years surrounding the event, ranged from roughly $300 billion to $600 billion worldwide, with the U.S. federal government alone spending more than $8 billion auditing and patching its own systems. The vast majority of that money did not go toward replacing the affected systems. It went toward finding, understanding, and surgically correcting date-handling logic embedded inside code — much of it written in COBOL, some of it decades old, some of it undocumented, some of it maintained by programmers who had never seen the original programmer who wrote it — because replacing the systems outright, on a fixed and immovable deadline measured in months, was judged less feasible than fixing them in place.

Y2K is worth opening this chapter with, in some detail, because it is the closest thing the software industry has ever produced to a controlled experiment testing this book's central question under maximum time pressure: given an unmovable deadline and unlimited global attention, would the world's most important legacy financial and government systems be replaced or repaired? The overwhelming answer, at planetary scale and across essentially every major economy that participated in the remediation effort, was repaired rather than rebuilt. A quarter century later, the same systems — patched for Y2K, patched again many times since, wrapped in newer interfaces their original 1970s authors would not recognize — are still, in a great many of the most consequential cases, running underneath the banking apps, government benefit portals, and insurance claims systems people use every day without any idea how old the machinery beneath their phone screen actually is.

## The scale nobody outside banking fully appreciates

COBOL, short for Common Business-Oriented Language, was first specified in 1959 by an industry and government committee whose most influential member was the computing pioneer Grace Hopper, explicitly designed from the outset to be readable by ordinary business managers as well as trained programmers and optimized for the kind of high-volume, transaction-processing work — payroll, billing, account balances — that banks, insurers, and governments needed to automate as they computerized through the 1960s and 1970s. Industry estimates compiled through the mid-2020s put the amount of COBOL code still running in active production somewhere north of 200 billion lines, processing a share of daily financial transaction volume that dwarfs its near-total absence from public conversation about software. Widely cited figures place COBOL-based mainframe systems behind the substantial majority of the world's ATM transactions and a large share of core banking infrastructure at major institutions including JPMorgan Chase and Bank of America, alongside government systems at the IRS, the Social Security Administration, and numerous state unemployment insurance agencies — several of which drew unusual public attention in 2020, when a sudden surge in unemployment claims during the COVID-19 pandemic strained decades-old COBOL-based state systems and briefly turned "does anyone still know COBOL" into a genuine, urgent staffing crisis rather than a rhetorical question.

The U.S. Government Accountability Office has tracked this problem formally for years. A 2025 GAO report identified eleven of the federal government's most critical legacy IT systems, spanning agencies including the Department of Health and Human Services and the Department of the Treasury, ranging in age from roughly 8 to 51 years old and collectively costing about $337 million annually simply to keep operating — a figure that covers only maintenance of the current systems, not the separate, much larger cost of eventually replacing them. As of the GAO's February 2025 assessment, agencies had completed only three of the ten modernization efforts identified back in 2019; of the seven still outstanding, four were expected to finish within a few years, two were projected to take five years or more, and one did not yet have any established completion date at all. The federal government's overall IT budget, which exceeds $100 billion annually, is estimated to spend roughly 80 percent of that sum on operating and maintaining existing systems — legacy included — rather than on new development, a ratio that has proven remarkably stable across successive modernization pushes going back well over a decade.

## Why replacement keeps losing to repair

The persistence of COBOL and the mainframe architecture underneath it is not, by most serious accounts, a story of institutional laziness or a failure to recognize the problem. It is a story about what this book has called institutional and physical weight compounding each other in a specific, unusually stubborn way.

A bank's core ledger is not simply a piece of software; it is the single authoritative record of what every customer owns, wrapped in decades of accumulated business logic — interest calculation rules, fee schedules, regulatory reporting formats, fraud-detection triggers, and integration points connecting to hundreds of downstream systems, each of which was built, at some point, by a different team, under a different set of assumptions, to talk to the mainframe in exactly the way it currently expects to be talked to. Replacing that core system does not mean writing equivalent code in a modern language. It means correctly reverse-engineering four or five decades of business rules that may exist nowhere in written documentation, verifying that the replacement produces bit-for-bit identical results across every one of billions of historical transactions during a parallel-run testing period, and doing all of this while the original system continues processing real customer money in real time, because a bank cannot simply halt account access for the months or years a careful migration would require. Chapter 12 examines several real migrations that attempted exactly this and the specific ways they went wrong; this chapter's task is simply to establish why so few institutions attempt it at all.

The mainframe hardware underneath the COBOL code compounds the problem rather than easing it, in a way that runs against most people's intuition about "old technology." IBM's current-generation mainframes are not decaying relics; they are actively engineered, regularly upgraded systems specifically designed for backward compatibility with decades of existing COBOL and its surrounding transaction-processing infrastructure, capable of extraordinarily high transaction throughput and uptime that many modern distributed cloud architectures still struggle to match for this specific workload. The mainframe survives not because nothing better has been invented, but because the specific combination of qualities it offers — proven reliability at extreme transaction volume, full backward compatibility with an enormous existing codebase, and a security and audit track record built over decades of regulatory scrutiny — has no single modern replacement that offers all three simultaneously without a multi-year, high-risk migration to get there.

## The workforce clock ticking underneath the technology clock

Even institutions willing to accept the cost and risk of migration face a second, harder constraint: a shrinking population of people who actually understand the existing systems well enough to migrate them safely. The COBOL programming workforce skews sharply older, reflecting the language's declining presence in university computer science curricula since roughly the 1990s, when object-oriented languages and later web and mobile development displaced mainframe programming from most schools' core coursework. Industry reporting through 2025 and 2026 consistently describes banks and insurers competing for a shrinking pool of COBOL-literate engineers, many well past typical retirement age, brought back as consultants specifically because the institutional knowledge of how a given bank's specific, decades-modified codebase actually behaves exists nowhere else — not in documentation, not in comments, sometimes not even in the memory of anyone still employed there.

This creates an unusual, almost inverted labor-market dynamic, one that runs directly counter to the usual pattern in which cutting-edge skills command the highest premiums and legacy skills quietly depreciate toward irrelevance: COBOL expertise, in a language most computer science graduates have never touched, commands premium consulting rates precisely because of its scarcity relative to demand, even as the language itself carries none of the prestige or career appeal of a modern stack. Job boards through early 2026 showed hundreds of open COBOL positions posted across banking, government, and insurance employers, alongside persistent employer complaints that qualified candidates simply did not exist in sufficient numbers to fill them — a mismatch that several major financial institutions have responded to by launching internal COBOL training programs for younger engineers and, in some cases, partnering directly with universities to reintroduce mainframe coursework into computer science programs that had quietly dropped it decades earlier — essentially rebuilding, from scratch and at private expense, an educational pipeline the broader software industry had allowed to disappear. Multiple operating systems and vendor-support timelines converging around 2026 and 2027 — several major mainframe-adjacent platforms losing formal IBM support in that window — are adding new urgency to a problem that institutions had, in many cases, been comfortably deferring for years.

## The bank that actually did it

It would be a mistake to conclude from this chapter's evidence that core banking migration is simply impossible. Commonwealth Bank of Australia — the country's largest bank by market capitalization — undertook exactly the kind of replacement this chapter has spent several sections explaining why institutions avoid, and largely succeeded, which makes its experience worth examining precisely because it shows both how such a migration can work and how expensive doing it properly actually is.

CBA began its core banking modernization in 2008, engaging Accenture and building on SAP's banking platform to replace a core system that had been running, by the bank's own account, for roughly forty-five years. The project was originally budgeted at approximately A$580 million over four years. By 2011, with the migration still underway, the bank publicly disclosed that the total cost had grown to roughly A$1.1 billion — an overrun of close to 90 percent relative to the original estimate, incurred not because the project failed but because a careful, risk-managed migration of this scale genuinely cost that much more than the initial estimate had captured. The bank completed the core replacement in stages through the early 2010s, moving toward real-time account processing — a meaningful technical advance over the batch-processing architecture common to older mainframe cores, in which transactions are collected and settled in scheduled runs rather than reflected instantly — while maintaining the operational reliability its 2008-era leadership had judged too risky to gamble in a single cutover. A subsequent phase, extending into the 2020s, moved significant portions of the platform to cloud infrastructure, continuing a modernization effort that, by the time of its later stages, had been running for well over a decade from its initial 2008 start.

CBA's experience offers this chapter's clearest evidence that the barrier most institutions face is not technical impossibility. It is cost, risk tolerance, and organizational patience measured in a decade or more and in billions of dollars — resources that a smaller regional bank, a mid-sized insurer, or a budget-constrained government agency typically does not have in anything like the quantity CBA was able to commit, sustained across multiple leadership changes and nearly ninety percent cost growth from the original estimate, without abandoning the project partway through. The lesson generalizes uncomfortably: a core system migration of this kind is achievable, but treating CBA's roughly billion-dollar, multi-decade undertaking as a template any smaller institution could simply scale down proportionally badly misreads what actually made it work — not primarily the technology chosen, but the sustained commitment of resources and organizational attention across a timeline most institutions, and most executive tenures, are not built to survive.

## Wrapping instead of replacing

Between the extremes of "keep the old mainframe running indefinitely" and "spend a decade and a billion dollars replacing it outright," a substantial industry has grown up around a third option: leaving the COBOL core in place while building modern software around it. API wrapping layers, screen-scraping middleware, and automated COBOL-to-Java or COBOL-to-C# transpilation tools let a bank or government agency expose a modern-looking mobile app, web portal, or third-party integration on top of a core system that, underneath the interface, is still running the same COBOL logic it ran a decade or three decades earlier. A customer checking a balance through a contemporary banking app in 2027 may well be triggering, several layers down, a COBOL routine written before that customer was born, translated into a modern API call by middleware most of the bank's own newer engineers have never had reason to look underneath.

This approach — sometimes called the strangler fig pattern, after the tropical vine that grows around a host tree and gradually replaces its structural function without the host ever being felled outright — lets institutions modernize the customer-facing layer, where competitive pressure and user expectations are highest, without accepting the risk of touching the transaction-processing core directly. It is not a permanent solution; the underlying COBOL, the underlying mainframe, and the underlying shrinking pool of engineers who understand both still age on the same clock this chapter has already described, and the wrapping layer itself becomes one more system future engineers eventually have to understand before they can safely change anything beneath it. But it is a genuinely useful middle path, and its widespread adoption across the banking and insurance sectors through the 2020s is itself informative: it is what a large fraction of the industry actually chose, revealed-preference style, when offered a spectrum of options between full replacement and standing still.

## The deadline nobody can move

The vendor-support timelines converging in 2026 and 2027, mentioned briefly above, deserve a closer look, because they illustrate a specific mechanism by which institutional weight and physical weight can force a decision even when an institution would otherwise prefer to keep deferring it indefinitely. A mainframe operating system reaching its formal end-of-support date does not stop functioning on that date. It simply stops receiving the security patches, bug fixes, and vendor engineering support that have, for decades, been the quiet backstop institutions relied on whenever a genuinely novel failure mode appeared in production. An unsupported mainframe operating system, still processing trillions of dollars in daily transactions, becomes a specific, quantifiable, and increasingly uninsurable risk the longer it runs past its support horizon, in a regulatory environment where bank examiners, insurance auditors, and cybersecurity frameworks increasingly treat unsupported infrastructure as a compliance finding in its own right, independent of whether any actual failure has yet occurred.

This is a useful illustration of how an installed system's clock, discussed abstractly in Chapter 4, actually gets enforced in practice for institutional rather than purely physical infrastructure: not by the system suddenly breaking, but by an outside party — in this case, a hardware and software vendor's own product-support roadmap, itself shaped by the vendor's business incentive to eventually retire support for its oldest platforms — unilaterally declaring that continued reliance on the old system has crossed from "acceptable operational risk" into "unacceptable audit finding." A bank that spent fifteen years successfully deferring a core-system migration decision can find that decision effectively made for it the moment its examiners start treating an unsupported operating system as a finding requiring a remediation plan with its own enforced deadline.

## The airline reservation system running the same trick

Commercial aviation offers a close cousin of the banking mainframe story, worth including because it shows the identical pattern operating in an industry most travelers never think of as running on decades-old code. Several major airline reservation systems still in active use trace their core architecture back to the 1960s and 1970s, originally built to handle seat inventory, fare calculation, and booking record management at a scale and reliability level that, much like the banking core systems examined earlier in this chapter, no full replacement effort has yet managed to displace outright across the industry. Modern airline websites and mobile apps, offering seemingly current, responsive booking interfaces, frequently sit atop exactly the same kind of wrapping-layer architecture Chapter 7 has already described for banking: a contemporary interface translating customer requests into transactions a decades-old reservation engine underneath can actually process, invisible to the traveler completing a booking in under a minute on a smartphone.

The consequences of this architecture surface publicly on the rare occasions the underlying system fails, as happened in several well-publicized airline outages through the 2010s and 2020s that grounded flights nationwide for hours, tracing back not to the customer-facing app layer but to the legacy reservation and crew-scheduling systems operating underneath it — outages serious enough to prompt congressional attention and industry self-examination, yet not serious enough, evidently, to justify the multi-billion-dollar, multi-year risk of a full core-system replacement any individual airline has been willing to gamble its entire operation on undertaking.

## What the mainframe is actually protecting

It would be easy to read this chapter as a story about institutional cowardice — banks and governments too timid or too cheap to modernize systems everyone privately agrees are outdated. The GAO's own reporting complicates that reading considerably. Federal legacy systems are not merely old; in case after case documented across successive GAO reports going back over a decade, they have also demonstrated a level of operational reliability that a rushed, underfunded modernization effort could easily degrade rather than improve, at least in the near term of a difficult transition.

This is the mainframe's version of the same tension Chapter 6 found in buried water pipe: the installed system's caution is not simply institutional inertia dressed up as prudence. A bank's core ledger processing trillions of dollars in daily transactions, or the Social Security Administration's payment system reaching tens of millions of beneficiaries every month, genuinely cannot afford the kind of failure a poorly executed migration risks, and the cost of that failure — as later chapters on real, botched core-banking migrations will show in specific dollar figures — falls immediately and visibly on real customers, not abstractly on an IT budget line. Y2K's lesson, viewed from a quarter century's distance, was never really that the world's core financial infrastructure was fragile. It was that the world's core financial infrastructure was durable enough, and depended upon closely enough, that a global deadline-driven remediation effort costing hundreds of billions of dollars was judged the more rational response than replacement, even under conditions — a fixed, immovable date, and essentially unlimited public and institutional attention — that should have made replacement look as attractive as it would ever look. The mainframe survived that test. The question this chapter leaves open, and the one the vendor-support deadlines of 2026 and 2027 are beginning to force in real time, is whether it can survive a second one: not a single date on a calendar, but a slow, permanent erosion of the workforce that has kept it running, arriving at exactly the moment a new generation of AI-assisted development tools is being pitched, with real but still largely unproven confidence, as a way to finally translate fifty years of undocumented COBOL logic into something the next generation of engineers can actually read.

The next chapter turns to a different, less visible form of installed weight: not the code itself, but the web of contracts, vendor agreements, and legal obligations that determine, often more than any technical constraint, how fast an institution is actually permitted to change what it runs.
