# Mandate, Subsidize, Wait

New York City's Local Law 154, passed by the City Council in a closely watched December 2021 vote after years of preceding advocacy and debate, formally prohibits the direct combustion of fossil fuels for heating, hot water, and cooking inside most new buildings citywide, phased in gradually, starting with smaller buildings in 2024 and extending fully to larger buildings by 2027 — a mandate, plain and simple, requiring new construction to be all-electric rather than gas-connected. The much larger federal Inflation Reduction Act, passed by Congress the following year in 2022, took a markedly different overall approach to essentially the same underlying goal of reducing fossil-fuel combustion in buildings: rather than prohibiting gas appliances, it offered the High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebate program, providing income-qualified households up to $8,000 toward a heat pump installation, and the separate HOMES program, offering performance-based rebates for whole-home efficiency retrofits — a subsidy, encouraging the same transition through financial incentive rather than legal prohibition. And across the great majority of the country, in most ordinary jurisdictions, neither a formal mandate nor a targeted subsidy governs the gas-to-electric transition at all; ordinary homeowners simply replace a failing furnace or water heater with whichever option seems most sensible to them personally at that specific moment of replacement, and the fleet gradually electrifies, or does not, according to the same ordinary turnover-clock economics Chapter 4 examined for vehicles and buildings.

These three examples are not exhaustive of every gas-to-electric building policy adopted anywhere, but they are representative enough of the full range of approaches governments have actually reached for, in real jurisdictions, facing real political and administrative constraints, to make this chapter's central comparison concrete rather than purely theoretical. This chapter names the three basic tools available to any government trying to accelerate a transition away from an installed system, and examines closely what each one actually costs in practice, who actually ends up bearing that real cost, and under what conditions each tool tends to work as intended rather than backfiring in ways this book's earlier chapters have already previewed.

## The mandate's clean logic and its real cost

A mandate is, in the simplest and most literal sense available to any government, the most straightforward of the three tools by a wide margin: a government simply prohibits the old system, or requires the new one, by a specific date, and the installed base has no choice but to comply. New York's Local Law 154 illustrates the mandate's core appeal directly — it applies uniformly, it does not require ongoing budget appropriations the way a subsidy program does, and it sends an unambiguous signal to builders, developers, and appliance manufacturers about what the market will require going forward, removing much of the market uncertainty that might otherwise slow voluntary adoption considerably in the absence of any firm, legally binding compliance date.

The mandate's cost, however, does not disappear simply because the government did not write a check for it. Chapter 8's discussion of natural gas franchise agreements already showed how a mandate can collide directly with contracts the mandating government did not write and cannot unilaterally override, and Chapter 14's discussion of the EMV liability shift showed how a mandate imposed without adequate transition time or resources for the least-capitalized affected parties can produce years of uneven, incomplete compliance rather than the clean cutover the policy's designers envisioned. A mandate's cost is real; it is simply pushed onto whoever must comply, rather than appearing as a line item in the government's own budget — precisely the dynamic Chapter 15 identified as the recurring, uncomfortable default whenever a cost-allocation question is left to run its course without deliberate intervention.

## The subsidy's honest price tag and its allocation problem

A subsidy, by design, inverts the mandate's underlying cost structure almost entirely: rather than requiring compliance and letting the affected party absorb the transition cost, the government pays some or all of that cost directly, funded through general tax revenue rather than imposed involuntarily on whoever happens to own the old system. This approach has genuine, real appeal precisely in the situations where an unaccompanied mandate would otherwise be at its cruelest and most politically indefensible — Chapter 14's account of the 2009 digital television transition paired a hard mandate with a substantial subsidy program specifically because Congress recognized that forcing millions of households to buy a converter box, with no financial assistance, would have fallen hardest on exactly the lower-income and elderly households least able to absorb the sudden expense.

The subsidy's own characteristic and most consistently recurring failure mode, made visible in detail throughout the Inflation Reduction Act's heat pump rebate rollout across dozens of separate state-administered programs, is allocation and practical access rather than the program's raw underlying cost to the federal government. By 2026, only roughly 23 states had active HOMES or HEEHRA rebate programs operating, with several states, including California, having already exhausted their initial federal funding allocation well before demand was satisfied — meaning a homeowner's ability to actually receive the subsidy depended heavily on which state they lived in and how quickly they applied relative to a fixed, exhaustible pool of money, rather than on any uniform national entitlement. A subsidy program's promise of broad accessibility is only as real as its actual funding level and the administrative capacity of whichever state or local agency is responsible for distributing it, and a chronically underfunded or slow-moving subsidy program can leave the exact population it was designed to help waiting years for money that may run out before their application is processed.

## Waiting as its own deliberate policy choice

The third tool available to any government facing this exact decision is not really a deliberate tool at all, at least not in the same active, interventionist sense the first two clearly are — it is the deliberate decision to do nothing beyond ordinary market and regulatory forces, and let Chapter 4's turnover clock run at its own natural pace. This is worth taking seriously as a genuine policy option rather than dismissing it as an absence of policy, because waiting has real, defensible advantages in specific circumstances: it imposes essentially no direct fiscal cost on already-stretched government budgets, it does not force the premature retirement of otherwise perfectly functional assets that, as Chapter 13 showed, may still have genuine remaining useful value, and it avoids the very real disruption a hard, inflexible mandate deadline can impose on ordinary households and small businesses not yet financially or logistically ready to make the transition on someone else's fixed schedule.

Waiting also carries a clear, extensively well-documented failure mode of its own, one this book has already returned to more than once from different angles: Chapter 4's examination of delayed coal plant retirements showed how "letting the market decide" can, under the right conditions, mean a transition simply does not happen on any predictable timeline at all, particularly when powerful incumbent interests have a strong financial reason to extend the old system's life indefinitely, or when — as with several 2025 coal retirements delayed under emergency federal orders — an unexpected demand shock reverses what had looked, only recently, like a settled retirement schedule. Waiting works when the underlying economics genuinely favor the new system enough that ordinary market forces will complete the transition within a socially acceptable timeframe on their own. It fails, sometimes for decades, when they do not.

## Choosing among the three, case by case

Placing New York's gas mandate, the federal heat pump subsidy, and ordinary market-driven coal retirement side by side clarifies a decision framework this chapter's evidence supports directly. A mandate makes sense when the social cost of the old system's continued operation is severe, well-documented, and difficult to address through incentive alone — precisely the case Chapter 15's account of building electrification's indoor air-quality and climate arguments makes for gas appliances in new construction, where a builder facing a mandate at the point of initial design faces a far lower switching cost than a homeowner facing a mandate to rip out an already-installed system. A subsidy makes sense when the transition is broadly desirable but the switching cost falls disproportionately on parties who did not choose the old system and cannot easily absorb an unsubsidized replacement cost — exactly the logic behind both the 2009 digital-television coupon program and the Inflation Reduction Act's income-tiered heat pump rebates, though only when the subsidy program is actually funded adequately and administered accessibly enough to reach the population it targets before the money runs out. Waiting makes sense when the underlying technology and economics are already moving in the desired direction quickly enough on their own, and forced intervention would mainly serve to strand still-productive capital or impose disruption disproportionate to any acceleration actually achieved.

## The government that reached for a mandate and pulled back

Britain's own gas boiler policy offers this chapter's clearest real-time example of a government moving between all three tools within a single decade, and it is worth tracing the sequence in detail because it shows how a mandate's political viability can erode even after formal adoption. The UK government initially floated a 2025 target date for banning new gas boiler installations in homes, later pushed that target back to 2035 as the practical difficulty of the original timeline became clearer, and then, in January 2025, abandoned the existing-homes mandate entirely, with officials explicitly stating the government would "not force anyone to rip out a working boiler." In its place, the government shifted toward exactly the second and third tools this chapter has described: the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, offering homeowners a grant of up to £7,500 toward a heat pump installation, paired with a zero rate of value-added tax on heat pump purchases, alongside relaxed planning rules intended to remove regulatory friction from voluntary adoption — subsidy and reduced friction, replacing what had originally been conceived as compulsion.

Notably, the mandate did survive in one narrower, more defensible form: from March 2027, England's Future Homes Standard requires new homes to be built with low-carbon heating, almost certainly meaning a heat pump rather than a gas boiler, a building-regulation requirement falling on developers at the point of initial construction rather than on individual existing homeowners being asked to rip out a working system. The British sequence traces almost exactly the logic this chapter's "choosing among the three" section laid out in the abstract: government retained the mandate only for the case where compliance cost was genuinely low — new construction, where a developer specifying a heat pump instead of a boiler faces little additional switching cost relative to building the alternative from scratch — and retreated to subsidy and voluntary market forces for the much harder case of an already-installed, already-functioning system in an occupied home.

## The hybrid approach most real policies actually use

Few real transitions this book has examined rely on a single tool in pure form; most combine at least two, and the specific combination reveals a great deal about what a government has judged the underlying problem to actually be. The 2009 digital television transition combined a hard mandate — a fixed shutoff date for analog broadcasting — with a substantial subsidy specifically targeted at the lower-income and elderly households the mandate would otherwise have hit hardest, an explicit acknowledgment that a mandate alone would have produced exactly the kind of uneven, harmful outcome Chapter 14 documented in the EMV liability shift's slower rollout. Cash for Clunkers, examined briefly in this book's earlier chapters, combined a voluntary subsidy with an unusual enforcement mechanism — a legal requirement that the traded-in vehicle's engine be destroyed rather than resold, preventing the subsidized vehicles from simply reentering the used-car market and undermining the program's stated environmental goal — showing that even a nominally voluntary subsidy program can carry mandate-like enforcement provisions buried inside its design.

New York's own building electrification approach, examined across the mandate for new construction, also includes state and city-level rebate programs supplementing the federal subsidies described above for existing buildings undergoing voluntary retrofit — mandate for the easier case (new construction, where compliance cost is lowest), subsidy for the harder case (existing buildings, where the switching cost this book's earlier chapters have documented at length is considerably higher), and implicit reliance on ordinary market turnover for the buildings that fall into neither category in any given budget year.

## California's replacement-triggered approach

California has pursued a fourth variant worth naming separately, because it splits the difference between mandate and waiting in a way neither pure category fully captures: rather than requiring existing gas appliances to be removed on a fixed calendar date, several California air-quality and building-code regulations instead apply specifically at the point of natural replacement — when a homeowner's existing gas furnace or water heater actually fails and needs to be replaced, the replacement unit must meet the new, effectively electric-only standard, while a currently functioning appliance faces no forced removal at all. The South Coast Air Quality Management District's rules affecting gas furnaces and water heaters in the Los Angeles basin, phased in through the mid-2020s, follow exactly this replacement-triggered structure.

This approach is, in effect, a mandate deliberately synchronized to Chapter 4's turnover clock rather than imposed against it: it guarantees eventual full transition, since every gas appliance will eventually fail and require replacement, while avoiding the specific harm this book's earlier chapters identified in a hard, calendar-driven cutover — no household is forced to discard a functioning appliance years before the end of its natural service life, and no sudden spike in installation demand overwhelms the licensed electrician and HVAC workforce Chapter 9 already showed running well short of what a rapid, all-at-once transition would require. The tradeoff is speed: a replacement-triggered mandate transitions the installed base only as fast as the underlying appliance stock's natural failure rate allows, which, for a water heater with a typical service life running well over a decade, means full compliance across the existing housing stock could plausibly take a decade or more to complete, even with the mandate technically already fully in force from its effective date.

## What the choice actually reveals

The choice among mandate, subsidy, and waiting is never purely technical, and this chapter's evidence suggests it functions as a fairly reliable, if implicit, signal of how a government has weighed the competing concerns this book's Part Four has raised: how severe is the social cost of continued delay, how capable is the affected population of absorbing an unsubsidized transition cost, how confident is the government that ordinary market forces will complete the job without intervention, and, finally, how much genuine fiscal capacity and sustained administrative competence does the government in question actually possess to fund and manage a large subsidy program well over many years, rather than badly for only a year or two before funding quietly runs out.

A government reaching immediately for a mandate, without adequate transition time or subsidy support for the least-capable affected parties, is implicitly betting that the social cost of delay outweighs the transition cost imposed on whoever must comply — a bet that can be entirely correct, as with genuinely urgent public-health or safety concerns, or badly miscalibrated, as several of this book's earlier chapters on rushed, poorly sequenced transitions have shown. A government reaching for a subsidy without adequately funding or administering it is implicitly making a promise it may not actually be able to keep at the scale the underlying problem requires. And a government choosing to wait, without seriously interrogating whether the underlying market forces are actually strong enough to complete the transition within an acceptable timeframe, risks discovering, the way several of this book's coal-retirement and copper-telephone-line cases have shown, that "the market will handle it" was never actually a plan at all — merely the absence of one, dressed in the more comfortable language of restraint.

## The enforcement question every tool eventually faces

One dimension connects all four approaches examined in this chapter and deserves explicit attention before moving on: a policy tool's formal design matters less than its actual enforcement capacity, a lesson several of this book's earlier chapters have already demonstrated from different angles. Cash for Clunkers' scrappage requirement worked specifically because the destroyed-engine provision was straightforward to verify and enforce at the point of trade-in, leaving little room for evasion. The EU's common charger directive succeeded, where the earlier 2009 voluntary memorandum failed, precisely because it closed a specific enforcement loophole — the adapter exemption — that had rendered the voluntary version toothless in practice. Detroit's water shutoffs, by grim contrast, were enforced with striking consistency and speed against individual households while enforcement of the underlying affordability and capital-planning failures that created the crisis in the first place proceeded far more slowly, if at all, illustrating how enforcement capacity itself is rarely allocated evenly among the parties a policy nominally governs.

California's replacement-triggered gas appliance rules face their own distinct enforcement question, largely unresolved as of this book's writing: verifying, at scale, across millions of individual household appliance replacements handled by tens of thousands of independent contractors, that every single replacement genuinely complies with the new standard requires an inspection and permitting infrastructure considerably more extensive than the rule's drafters may have fully funded, a gap that echoes Chapter 16's finding that Fayette County's water utility lacked the technical monitoring capacity to catch a serious violation before residents themselves discovered it. A mandate, a subsidy, and a replacement-triggered hybrid are each, in the end, only as effective as the institution responsible for enforcing or administering them is actually equipped, staffed, and funded to do the job — a mundane, unglamorous administrative reality that determines, more reliably than any theoretical comparison of the three tools' underlying economic logic, which transitions in this book's evidence actually succeeded on their stated timeline and which quietly slipped for years past it.

This book's final chapters turn from the menu of tools available to the specific, harder question of when accelerating a transition actually goes too far — destroying real, still-productive value in the name of progress that a more patient approach would have captured without the waste.
