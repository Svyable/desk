# The Contract Still Runs

In 1898, Britain signed a treaty with the Qing dynasty leasing a rural expanse north of Hong Kong Island and Kowloon — a region that came to be called the New Territories — for ninety-nine years. The lease was, in the context of the period's imperial land grabs, a relatively modest instrument: an extension of British administrative control to secure a defensible buffer around the existing Hong Kong colony, signed with little expectation on either side, according to most historical accounts, that the specific expiration date would matter much to anyone by the time it arrived. It mattered enormously. As July 1, 1997 approached, the fact that the New Territories lease — covering the majority of Hong Kong's land area and, by the later twentieth century, most of its population and economic activity — would legally expire on that exact date became the forcing mechanism behind the entire negotiation between Britain and China over Hong Kong's future, embodied in the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration, which extended Chinese sovereignty over the whole of Hong Kong, including the parts Britain had held outright rather than merely leased, because splitting the territory back into leased and unleased portions had become administratively and economically unworkable after nearly a century of the colony functioning as a single integrated system, with water, power, transport, and housing all built across a boundary line that had stopped meaning anything to the people actually living and working on either side of it.

Nobody negotiating the lease terms in the closing years of the nineteenth century was thinking seriously, in any operational sense, about what the world would actually look like when 1997 finally arrived. The number ninety-nine simply sounded, at the time, like a duration functionally equivalent to forever — long enough that the people signing the document would certainly be dead before it mattered, which is precisely why ninety-nine years became, and largely remains, the conventional default length for a very long lease across much of the world's legal tradition, from British Commonwealth property law to Chinese state land grants today. A number chosen partly because it felt to its drafters like it would never actually arrive turned out, in the end, to arrive on a specific, unmovable calendar date and reshape the politics, sovereignty, and daily governance of a major world city that neither set of original signatories could have fully pictured.

This chapter is about that specific property of contracts, and it is worth distinguishing carefully from every other form of installed weight examined so far. A physical asset, whatever its resistance to change, decays, corrodes, or becomes technically outdated on a schedule set at least partly by physics and by use. A contract's duration is a pure human choice, encoded once, in language, that keeps a specific arrangement legally binding for exactly as long as its authors decided, regardless of what has actually changed in the surrounding world since — sometimes to everyone's lasting benefit, sometimes as a trap nobody involved quite meant to build, and sometimes, as the sections below show, as something in between that only reveals which kind it was decades after the ink has dried.

## The power plant that cannot retire on schedule

A more mundane, more contemporary version of the same mechanism runs through American electricity markets today, and it connects directly back to the coal-retirement puzzle Chapter 4 introduced. A power plant's retirement decision is not simply an economic calculation about fuel costs, maintenance expenses, and the price its output can currently fetch. It is also, frequently, a legal calculation bounded by long-term power purchase agreements — contracts obligating a plant's owner to deliver electricity to a specific buyer, at a specific price, for a specific term, sometimes twenty years or more, signed at a moment when both parties judged the arrangement mutually beneficial and neither anticipated the market conditions that would exist by the contract's later years.

Chapter 1's Crane Clean Energy Center restart itself rests on exactly this mechanism, in reverse: Constellation's twenty-year power purchase agreement with Microsoft is precisely the kind of long-duration contract that makes an enormous capital commitment — $1.6 billion to restart a mothballed reactor — financeable in the first place, because the contract guarantees the revenue needed to justify the investment regardless of how broader electricity prices move over the following two decades. The same instrument that makes new or revived generation financeable also makes old generation harder to retire once it exists: a coal plant operator bound by a long-term capacity contract to a regional grid operator, or by a legacy power purchase agreement with a utility that itself has ratepayer obligations built around that specific plant's output, cannot simply walk away from the arrangement the moment the underlying asset becomes economically marginal, without triggering contractual penalties, regulatory review, or — in some documented cases — a grid reliability finding empowering regulators to order continued operation regardless of the owner's own preference. The 2025 EIA data on delayed coal retirements, discussed in Chapter 4, reflects this dynamic directly: several of the retirements pushed back that year were delayed specifically under emergency federal orders compelling continued operation to preserve grid reliability, orders that override what would otherwise have been the plant owner's own contractually and economically driven exit timeline.

## The lease under the cell tower

Telecommunications infrastructure runs on a version of this same mechanism at a much smaller, more granular scale, and it is worth examining because it shows how contract duration can function as a deliberate business strategy rather than merely an inherited historical accident. Cell towers are overwhelmingly built on land the tower operator does not own outright, but leases — from a farmer, a church, a municipality, a private landowner — under agreements that, in the American telecommunications industry, commonly run for initial terms of five to ten years with multiple renewal options that, taken together, frequently extend the practical relationship toward several decades, sometimes explicitly structured with escalating rent and long strings of automatic renewal periods that can push the effective total duration close to the ninety-nine-year convention this chapter opened with.

Tower operators and the wireless carriers that lease space on their structures value this durability for a straightforward reason: a cell site's value depends heavily on continuity of coverage at a specific physical location, and relocating even a single tower — securing new land, new permits, new zoning approval, new construction, new backhaul connections to the fiber or microwave network carrying the signal onward — can take years and cost far more than negotiating a lease renewal with an existing landowner ever would. The specialized real estate investment trusts that came to dominate American tower ownership over the 2000s and 2010s built entire business models around acquiring and consolidating these long-duration ground leases, treating contract duration itself as the asset being purchased, in some cases as much as the physical steel structure standing on the land. A wireless network's installed base, at the most granular physical level, turns out to be substantially a portfolio of decades-long paper agreements with individual landowners who may have no idea their signature on a modest annual rent check helped make an entire regional wireless network durably, contractually immovable.

## Vendor contracts as their own kind of installed base

A third version of contractual durability operates inside institutions rather than across property lines, and it connects this chapter directly back to Chapter 7's mainframe discussion. Large enterprise software vendors — the companies that sell and maintain the systems banks, insurers, hospitals, and government agencies run their core operations on — frequently structure their commercial relationships around long-term maintenance and support agreements that, once signed, create switching costs extending well beyond the software's technical characteristics.

A government agency's enterprise resource planning contract, or a hospital system's electronic health records agreement, typically bundles licensing, ongoing support, security patching, regulatory-compliance updates, and often the vendor's own proprietary data formats into a single multi-year relationship that becomes progressively more expensive to exit the longer it runs — not because the underlying technology becomes more essential with age, but because the accumulated custom configuration, integration work, staff training, and historical data migration involved in switching vendors grows every year the relationship continues. Procurement specialists sometimes describe this dynamic bluntly as vendor lock-in, and it recurs specifically in the public sector, where lengthy competitive-bidding requirements designed to ensure fair contracting can paradoxically make switching vendors slower and more expensive than in private markets, adding formal procedural duration on top of the technical and data-migration switching costs already present. A ten-year-old enterprise software contract is not merely an old contract. It is frequently the legal shell wrapped around exactly the kind of deeply embedded legacy system Chapter 7 described, with its own renewal clauses now functioning as one more reason replacement keeps losing to renewal.

## The pension that outlives the mine

Labor agreements can bind an industry to its own past even more durably than a lease or a power purchase agreement, because a pension or retiree health-care commitment, once made, follows the worker rather than the workplace, and often runs for the remainder of a human life rather than for a fixed contractual term negotiated in advance. The coal industry's own retiree benefit structure is one of the clearest American examples: multiemployer pension and health funds established through decades of collective bargaining between coal operators and the United Mine Workers of America created obligations that survive individual mine closures, individual company bankruptcies, and, in some structural respects, even the shrinking of the underlying industry that originally financed them. When a coal company closes a mine or exits the business entirely, the pension and health obligations owed to its retired miners do not simply disappear with the company — they can fall to remaining industry participants, to a federal backstop, or, in cases of fund insolvency, become a matter of emergency congressional action, as happened when Congress acted in 2019 to shore up the multiemployer pension system covering retired miners after a wave of coal company bankruptcies threatened the fund's solvency.

This matters directly for this book's argument because it means an industry's willingness or ability to accelerate its own decline is shaped not only by the physical and technical clocks described in earlier chapters, but by decades-old labor contracts obligating current and former operators to fund the retirement of workers who may have left the industry, or the workforce entirely, years or decades before a specific plant's retirement date arrives. A coal company deciding whether to accelerate a mine's closure is not making a purely forward-looking economic calculation; it is weighing that calculation against retiree obligations that a labor agreement, signed in some cases half a century ago, has already fixed in place regardless of what the underlying market now looks like.

## The franchise agreement under the gas main

A related, more geographically diffuse version of this mechanism governs the natural gas utilities that Chapter 17 examines directly in the context of building electrification. Municipal gas utilities in the United States typically operate under franchise agreements — contracts between the utility and the city granting it the right to install and operate gas distribution infrastructure under public streets, often for terms running twenty to thirty years or longer, frequently including exclusivity provisions or renewal structures that make an early, unilateral municipal exit from the arrangement legally and financially complicated even when a city government has independently decided, on climate or public-health grounds, that it wants to accelerate a transition away from gas-based heating and cooking.

These franchise agreements were not written with electrification debates in mind; most predate any serious municipal discussion of all-electric building codes by decades, and their exclusivity and duration provisions reflect an entirely different, considerably more mundane original purpose — giving a utility enough contractual certainty over its service territory to justify investing in buried gas infrastructure in the first place, the same logic Chapter 6 already established for water pipe. The consequence, playing out in city council debates and utility regulatory proceedings across multiple states through the mid-2020s, is that a city's legal ability to accelerate its own building-electrification timeline is bounded not only by the physical retrofit costs Chapter 17 discusses, but by contracts most of the officials currently debating the policy did not write, did not negotiate, and in many cases have never personally read in full.

## Ninety-nine years, revisited

It is worth returning briefly to the number that opened this chapter, because ninety-nine years turns out to be a remarkably persistent convention across legal traditions that otherwise share little else, and the persistence itself says something about how institutions handle the discomfort of committing to genuine permanence. English common law, dating to feudal-era restrictions on land tenure, has long treated leases nominally exceeding a certain very long threshold with suspicion, close to functioning as an outright transfer of ownership rather than a temporary arrangement — which pushed centuries of drafters toward round numbers just under that threshold, with ninety-nine becoming the most common convention purely by repeated custom rather than any specific statutory requirement. China's post-1949 land-use rights system, rebuilt from the 1980s onward after decades in which private land tenure had been abolished entirely, independently arrived at maximum lease terms in the same general range — up to seventy years for residential land and up to fifty years for industrial use, depending on classification — for essentially the same underlying reason: a term long enough to make substantial capital investment rational for the leaseholder, without being drafted as permanent ownership in a legal system whose government wanted to formally retain ultimate title.

The through-line connecting Hong Kong's 1898 lease, an American cell tower's renewal-laden ground lease, and a modern Chinese industrial land grant is not coincidence. It is convergent evolution toward the same practical answer to the same underlying problem: how do you write a contract long enough to make a large, illiquid, physically embedded investment worthwhile for the party making it, without either side having to formally reckon, in the document itself, with what the world might actually look like by the time the term expires? Ninety-nine years, or seventy, or fifty, function as a kind of institutional shorthand for "long enough that this feels permanent to everyone signing it" — right up until the date arrives, on schedule, exactly as written, and turns out to matter enormously to people who were not yet born when the number was chosen.

## What a good long contract is actually for

It would be easy, having spent three sections on retirement delays, tower-lease lock-in, and vendor dependency, to read long-duration contracts purely as a source of installed-world friction — one more mechanism by which the past constrains the future longer than anyone intended. That reading misses why long contracts exist in the first place, and misses it in a way this book has warned against since Chapter 3: not every source of installed-world weight is merely a cost.

A twenty-year power purchase agreement is what makes a billion-dollar reactor restart financeable at all; without a legally durable revenue guarantee spanning most of the asset's productive life, Constellation could not have justified the capital outlay to its own investors, and Three Mile Island's Unit 1 would very likely still be sitting dark. A long-term cell tower lease is what lets a wireless network make capital-intensive, physically fixed infrastructure investments with confidence the underlying land will still be available in ten years to justify the expense. A stable, well-negotiated enterprise contract can give an agency or hospital a predictable cost structure and guaranteed vendor support across a technology's genuinely useful operating life, rather than forcing costly, disruptive re-procurement on a shorter cycle than the underlying system actually needs. The contract's duration is not the problem. The problem, in each of this chapter's harder cases, is a contract whose duration outlived the conditions that made it a good idea to sign, with no efficient mechanism built in for either party to notice that moment has arrived and act on it before the calendar, rather than the underlying economics, makes the decision instead.

## The recording contract as a cautionary parallel

Outside infrastructure entirely, the music industry's decades-long history of long-duration artist recording contracts offers a useful, more human-scale illustration of the same durability-versus-fairness tension this chapter has traced through utilities and telecommunications. Standard record-label contracts through much of the twentieth century, and in modified form well into the twenty-first, frequently bound an artist to a label for a period spanning several albums, sometimes stretching across a decade or more once contractual renewal and delivery obligations were fully accounted for, regardless of how the artist's career, popularity, or negotiating leverage might have changed in the intervening years. High-profile public disputes between prominent recording artists and their labels over exactly these long-duration terms have recurred across multiple decades, each dispute turning on a version of the same underlying question this chapter has asked of coal plants, cell towers, and enterprise software vendors: did the original signatory genuinely understand, at the moment of signing, what a contract of this duration would actually cost them once circumstances changed, and does the contract include any meaningful mechanism for revisiting those terms once it becomes clear how lopsided the original bargain has turned out to be.

The parallel matters for this chapter's broader argument because it shows the same ninety-nine-year logic operating at a far more personal, far more emotionally charged scale than a municipal water franchise or a coal plant's power purchase agreement: a young, unestablished artist signing a first recording contract is, in a very real structural sense, in the same negotiating position as Britain's 1898 diplomats securing the New Territories lease — agreeing to a duration that feels comfortably distant and abstract at the moment of signing, only to discover, years later, exactly how binding and how consequential that distant-feeling number actually turns out to be once it finally, predictably, arrives.

## When contracts build in their own exit

Not every long-duration agreement locks its parties into an unchangeable arrangement, and it is worth closing this chapter with the mechanism sophisticated drafters have developed specifically to avoid the trap Hong Kong's 1898 negotiators walked into unknowingly: the renegotiation clause. Modern power purchase agreements, unlike many of their mid-twentieth-century predecessors, increasingly include change-in-law provisions, periodic price-reset mechanisms, and defined early-termination options — often with a specified buyout formula — precisely because sophisticated counterparties on both sides have learned, from decades of watching older contracts become mismatched with reality, that a term measured in decades needs some structured mechanism for revisiting its own assumptions before the world changes enough to make the original bargain absurd rather than merely imperfect.

The existence of these clauses does not eliminate the installed-world weight a long contract creates; it merely converts an all-or-nothing lock-in into a negotiated, bounded flexibility that still favors the party with more bargaining leverage at the moment renegotiation actually becomes necessary. A grid operator with many alternative generation sources can use a change-in-law clause to renegotiate favorable terms from a struggling coal plant; an individual homeowner whose gas franchise-bound utility controls the only legal path to a new service connection has no comparable leverage to invoke on their own behalf. The presence or absence of a well-drafted exit mechanism, more than the raw duration number on the contract's first page, increasingly determines whether a multi-decade agreement functions as productive long-term commitment or as the kind of trap this chapter's harder cases have described — a distinction well-resourced institutional counterparties have gotten considerably better at protecting themselves against than individual citizens, small municipalities, or, as it happened in 1898, an empire that assumed ninety-nine years was close enough to forever not to bother thinking through what came after.

The next chapter turns from paper commitments to human ones — the workers who have learned, sometimes across an entire career, exactly how an old system fails, and what happens to that specific, hard-won knowledge when the system it describes is finally replaced.
