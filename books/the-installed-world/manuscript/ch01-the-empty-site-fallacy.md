# The Empty-Site Fallacy

On September 20, 2024, Constellation Energy announced that it would spend $1.6 billion to do something the American power industry had never done before: restart a nuclear reactor that had already been shut down and decommissioned. The reactor was Unit 1 at Three Mile Island, the plant outside Middletown, Pennsylvania, whose name had been synonymous with nuclear catastrophe since its sister reactor, Unit 2, suffered a partial meltdown in 1979. Unit 1 itself had run safely for decades afterward and been retired in 2019 for ordinary economic reasons — cheap natural gas had made it unprofitable, not unsafe. Now it was getting a new name, the Crane Clean Energy Center, and a new customer. Constellation had signed a twenty-year agreement to sell its electricity to Microsoft, which needed the power for its data centers and, more specifically, for the AI models running inside them.

The plant was originally expected back online in 2028. By late 2025, Constellation had moved the target up to 2027, backed by a billion-dollar loan from the Department of Energy.

Here is the detail worth sitting with. Microsoft did not build a new power plant. Microsoft did not even build a new data center campus next to an existing plant, the way a company confident in its own greenfield mythology might have chosen to. It bought two decades of output from a reactor that had already been built, already been licensed, already had a cooling tower standing on the riverbank, already had a switchyard connected to the transmission grid, and already employed people in the region who understood, at the level of muscle memory, what that specific reactor sounded like when something was wrong. Reviving it was still an enormous undertaking — new fuel, new safety reviews, an NRC relicensing process, refurbished turbine components, a rehired and retrained workforce — but every one of those tasks was cheaper and faster than starting over on an empty parcel of land.

This is not the story artificial intelligence is usually told through. The dominant narrative of the 2020s AI buildout is a story about invention: new chips, new model architectures, new data center designs, new financing structures capable of moving hundreds of billions of dollars toward compute nobody had needed five years earlier. It is a story that treats the technology as the whole subject and the physical world as a stage set that will simply resize itself around the technology's ambitions.

The Crane Clean Energy Center says otherwise. So does almost everything else about how AI infrastructure actually got built between 2023 and 2026.

## The queue nobody can see from the pitch deck

By the end of 2025, more than 2,060 gigawatts of generation and storage capacity was sitting in interconnection queues across the United States, waiting for permission to connect to the grid, according to data compiled by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's Electricity Markets and Policy department, which has tracked these queues for more than a decade. By 2026 the cumulative figure being discussed across the industry — including projects still in early planning stages — had swelled past 2,600 gigawatts, with a median wait time approaching five years between a project's initial application and its actual commercial operation.

In Texas, where the deregulated ERCOT grid is often held up as the fastest-moving power market in the country, the queue for large loads — the category that includes data centers — had reached 410 gigawatts by 2026, with data centers alone accounting for roughly 87 percent of that demand. Industry analysts tracking the 16 gigawatts of new data center power capacity planned for 2026 found that only about 5 gigawatts had entered active construction by early in the year, with projections that 30 to 50 percent of the remainder would slip into 2027 or later.

None of this is a story about AI models being hard to build. It is a story about the grid — a system whose major components were designed, sited, and financed across the twentieth century for a demand curve that grew a few percentage points a year, not for a single new category of customer that can each request hundreds of megawatts on a timeline set by chip release cycles.

The bottleneck is not abstract. It has a part number. Large power transformers — the devices that step voltage up and down between generation, transmission, and distribution — were running average lead times of 128 weeks for standard units and 144 weeks for the generator step-up transformers used at power plants, according to supply-chain data collected in 2025, with the largest, most specialized units facing waits as long as four years. Demand for generator step-up transformers had grown 274 percent since 2019; demand for substation power transformers was up 116 percent over the same period. Prices had risen 77 percent for power transformers and 45 percent for generator step-ups, driven by a shortage of the specialized grain-oriented electrical steel used in transformer cores, much of which is manufactured by a small number of mills worldwide. Hitachi Energy and Siemens each announced roughly half-a-billion-to-billion-dollar domestic manufacturing investments in 2025 to expand capacity — Hitachi's new South Boston, Virginia plant is scheduled to come online in 2028. The gap between now and then is not something money alone closes faster; steel mills and transformer factories take years to permit, build, and staff regardless of how much capital shows up at once.

Turbines tell a similar story from the generation side. GE Vernova's backlog of gas turbine orders and slot-reservation agreements reached 116 gigawatts by the second quarter of 2026, with the company targeting at least 125 gigawatts by year's end and planning to expand annual production from around 20 gigawatts of turbine output to 30 gigawatts by 2030. Siemens Energy's backlog reached 69 gigawatts over the same period. The entire global market for large gas turbines is effectively controlled by three manufacturers — GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries — none of which can add a new factory line in under several years.

Every one of these numbers describes the same underlying fact from a different angle: the physical machinery that turns electricity into a deliverable commodity — steel, copper, turbine blades, licensed engineers, permitted land, interconnection agreements — cannot be summoned at the speed of a funding round. The AI industry's capital is essentially unlimited by the standards of prior technology cycles. Its access to already-built, already-connected, already-licensed infrastructure is not.

## Why the reactor was faster than the vacant lot

This is the specific mechanism that gives this book its subject, and Three Mile Island is a clean illustration of it because the comparison is so direct. Building an equivalent 835 megawatts of new nuclear generation from an empty site in Pennsylvania would have meant years of environmental review, a fresh NRC licensing process for a new design, new transmission interconnection studies, new cooling infrastructure, and a construction timeline that recent American nuclear projects — Georgia's Vogtle Units 3 and 4 came in roughly seven years behind schedule and tens of billions of dollars over budget — suggest would run past 2035, if it happened at all. Reviving Unit 1 instead meant working with an existing containment structure, existing turbine hall, an existing interconnection to the transmission grid that had simply gone quiet rather than being decommissioned, and a regulatory pathway for restart that, while still rigorous, was categorically different from licensing a new design from scratch.

The empty site was not, in other words, the fast option. The installed asset was.

This inverts the assumption embedded in almost every popular account of how new technology spreads. The assumption — call it the empty-site fallacy — is that a superior new system competes against an absence: against the theoretical version of the old world in which nothing is yet built, no contracts are yet signed, no workforce is yet trained, and no capital is yet sunk into the incumbent approach. Under that assumption, adoption should track roughly with technical merit, discounted a little for switching cost and inertia. A better battery chemistry displaces a worse one. A faster chip displaces a slower one. A cheaper way to generate electricity displaces a more expensive one, more or less on the timeline the underlying economics would predict.

The empty site almost never exists. What exists instead is a parcel of land with an easement running through it, a permitting authority with a multi-year backlog, a transmission operator managing a queue with thousands of ahead-of-you projects, a steel supply chain with a years-long lead time, and — if you are lucky — a retired power plant that already solved half of those problems decades ago and is simply waiting for someone to notice it is still standing there.

## The pattern repeats outside the data center

The instinct to treat this as a story specific to AI or to electricity misses how general the mechanism is. Consider brownfield data center development itself, independent of the power question. Industry analyses published through 2025 and 2026 found that converting an existing industrial building into a data center — reusing the shell, the foundation, the loading infrastructure, and often the utility corridors already connecting it to the grid — typically runs 30 to 50 percent cheaper than constructing an equivalent facility from bare ground, and can be completed in twelve to twenty-four months, often half the duration of a comparable greenfield project. That gap exists for the same reason the reactor beat the vacant lot: a brownfield site has already absorbed the years of permitting, environmental review, and utility negotiation that a greenfield site still has ahead of it. What looks, from the outside, like a company choosing an unglamorous former steel mill or paper plant over a purpose-built campus is often simply a company doing the arithmetic on how long an interconnection queue actually takes and concluding that an old building with an existing utility connection beats a beautiful empty field with none.

The container ship tells an older version of the same story, and it belongs in this chapter because it is the exception that proves how the rule actually works. When Malcolm McLean loaded fifty-eight standardized steel boxes onto a converted tanker called the Ideal X and sailed from Newark to Houston in April 1956, he was not building on an empty site either — he was fighting one. The existing installed world of American shipping was a dense, load-bearing structure: longshoremen's unions whose members were paid by the hour to hand-load break-bulk cargo, port authorities with piers designed for that older method, and a New York Harbor establishment that, by most accounts, actively resisted the change while nearby Newark and Port Elizabeth in New Jersey welcomed it and built the specialized infrastructure containers required. New York's port business measurably declined relative to Newark's over the following years precisely because Newark was willing to become new installed infrastructure — cranes, container yards, road access — while New York tried to preserve its old installed infrastructure and workforce. McLean's innovation did not defeat the installed world in the abstract. It found a specific piece of the installed world, in New Jersey, willing to be replaced by a new one, and by 1968 the International Organization for Standardization had codified standardized container dimensions, giving the new system its own installed base to protect. Within roughly fifteen years of the Ideal X's first voyage, containerization had reorganized global shipping; by the end of the twentieth century, container ships were carrying close to 90 percent of the world's traded cargo by value. It is one of the fastest wholesale replacements of an installed industrial system in modern economic history, and it happened not because McLean ignored the installed world but because he understood exactly which piece of it he needed to defeat, and where a more cooperative piece of it was waiting to take the business instead.

That contrast — a nuclear reactor revived because starting fresh was slower, a shipping industry transformed in roughly a decade and a half because the entrant found willing new infrastructure to build on — is the tension this book spends the rest of its chapters working through. The installed world is not a uniform wall. It has doors. Finding them, forcing them, or building around them is a different skill from inventing the technology that needs to pass through in the first place, and it is a skill most accounts of technological change do not teach because they are not paying attention to it.

## Even the greenfield site is not empty

If any single project should have been the empty-site fallacy's best case — a purpose-built factory, on undeveloped desert land, backed by tens of billions of dollars and an act of Congress — it was TSMC's semiconductor fabrication complex in north Phoenix, Arizona. The company announced its first Arizona fab in 2020, expanded the commitment to two plants and roughly $40 billion by 2022, and broke ground on genuinely vacant land. There was no old reactor to revive, no former steel mill to gut and rewire. This was as close to a blank slate as modern industrial policy gets.

It still slipped. The first fab, originally targeted for volume production of 5-nanometer chips by late 2024, was upgraded mid-build to the more advanced 4-nanometer process and pushed to the first half of 2025. The second fab, originally expected in 2026, was delayed to 2027 or 2028. TSMC's own public explanation, echoed by reporting throughout 2024 and 2025, was not a shortage of land, water rights, or capital. It was a shortage of workers who knew how to install and calibrate the specific tool sets a leading-edge fab requires — a skill set that, in practice, existed mainly in Taiwan, built up over decades inside TSMC's own domestic supply chain of equipment technicians, cleanroom specialists, and process engineers. Arizona building trades unions, for their part, raised separate concerns about safety practices and questioned whether the labor shortage was being used as a rationale to bring in workers from Taiwan rather than train and pay for a domestic workforce at prevailing wages — a dispute that itself took months of negotiation to resolve.

Land was empty. Expertise was not. The workforce capable of running a modern chip fab is itself a form of installed infrastructure — a stock built up over decades inside a small number of companies and a small number of countries — and it does not scale by wiring a check. This is worth flagging early, because it previews an argument this book returns to directly in Chapter 9: the installed world is not only steel, concrete, and copper. It is also the trained, licensed, experienced people who know how a system is supposed to behave and, more importantly, how it behaves right before it fails. A fab built in eighteen months on empty land can still wait years for the workforce that makes it worth having built.

## The exception that explains the rule

It would be a mistake to read Three Mile Island, the grid queue, and TSMC's Arizona delays as proof that the installed world always wins, always slows things down, always adds years. The container ship's own history argues against that reading, and it is worth returning to it briefly, because a theory that only explains slow transitions is not yet a good theory.

Malcolm McLean did not defeat the installed world of 1956 shipping through sheer technical superiority. Break-bulk cargo handling — longshoremen manually loading crates, barrels, and sacks into a ship's hold, one at a time — was itself a mature, heavily unionized, deeply installed system, with its own workforce, its own pay structures, and its own political constituencies in every major port city. McLean's containers threatened all of it simultaneously: fewer dockworkers needed per ton of cargo moved, different port layouts required, different crane infrastructure, different relationships between shipping lines and rail carriers.

What made containerization's roughly fifteen-year takeover possible was not the absence of an installed world to fight. It was McLean's ability to find a specific seam in that installed world — a port authority in New Jersey willing to build new infrastructure rather than defend old infrastructure — and scale outward from there once the new system had its own installed base to protect. New York Harbor, whose entrenched longshoremen's union and legacy pier layout had the most to lose, saw its relative share of the region's shipping business decline for years as traffic moved to Newark and Port Elizabeth, which had made the opposite bet. By 1968, when the International Organization for Standardization formalized global container dimensions, the new system was no longer an insurgent fighting the installed world; it had become the installed world, with its own ports, its own cranes, its own workforce, and its own resistance to whatever came next.

The lesson is not that persistence always pays off, or that every incumbent system is one clever workaround away from being routed. It is that the installed world is not monolithic. It has weaker points and stronger points, willing partners and immovable defenders, and the technologies that spread fastest are usually not the ones with the best underlying engineering — they are the ones whose builders correctly identified where the installed world was ready to be replaced and where it was not.

## What the reactor's second life actually cost

It is worth dwelling a moment longer on the Crane Clean Energy Center's specific numbers, because they quantify the empty-site fallacy in a way few other cases in this book can match with such precision. Constellation's $1.6 billion restart budget, financed in part by a $1 billion Department of Energy loan secured in November 2025, covers new nuclear fuel, refurbished turbine components, an updated cooling system, a rehired and retrained workforce, and the extensive Nuclear Regulatory Commission relicensing review a facility cannot skip merely because its containment structure has been standing, unused, since 2019. None of that is cheap or fast in any absolute sense — restarting a mothballed nuclear plant remains, by any ordinary business standard, an enormous undertaking. The relevant comparison is not to a costless alternative. It is to what an equivalent 835 megawatts of brand-new nuclear generation would actually have required: a fresh environmental review process typically taking years on its own, a new NRC licensing docket for whatever reactor design was selected, new transmission interconnection studies joining the same multi-year queue Chapter 1's own opening section already described straining under more than two thousand gigawatts of pending projects, and a construction timeline that Georgia's Vogtle nuclear expansion — the most recent large-scale American nuclear construction project, completed in 2023 after running roughly seven years behind its original schedule and tens of billions of dollars over its original budget — suggests would not plausibly have finished before the mid-2030s at the earliest, if it finished at all without further delay.

Set against that counterfactual, Constellation's $1.6 billion and roughly three-year restart timeline was not merely the cheaper option. It was very plausibly the only option capable of delivering meaningful new carbon-free capacity to the regional grid before the end of this decade, a timeline constraint that mattered enormously to Microsoft's own stated 2030 sustainability commitments. The empty-site fallacy's cost, in this specific case, was not merely theoretical. It was the multi-year, multi-billion-dollar gap between what a truly greenfield reactor project would have required and what an already-licensed, already-connected, merely mothballed one actually did — a gap large enough to functionally rule the greenfield option out for anyone genuinely trying to add new nuclear capacity to the American grid within the current decade rather than the next one.

## What "empty" actually means

It is worth being precise about what the empty-site fallacy gets wrong, because the error is subtler than simply forgetting that old buildings exist. Nobody drafting an AI data center strategy in 2024 literally believed the American electrical grid was empty. The fallacy operates one level up: it is the assumption that the installed world's resistance can be treated as a cost to be priced in — a delay, a line item, a risk factor in a financial model — rather than as a structural pacing mechanism that determines, more than almost anything else, how fast the technology can actually reach the world.

A pitch deck can say a data center campus will be online in eighteen months. A spreadsheet can amortize permitting risk as a probability-weighted delay. Neither of those documents can manufacture a generator step-up transformer faster than the grain-oriented electrical steel supply chain allows, or train a licensed high-voltage lineman faster than the apprenticeship programs that produce them, or shorten an interconnection study queue that is itself a consequence of thousands of other projects making the identical bet at the identical moment. The empty-site fallacy is not a failure of arithmetic. It is a failure to notice that the thing being modeled as a cost is actually a clock, running at a speed nobody in the room controls.

This book's argument is that this clock — the pace at which an already-built, already-financed, already-licensed, already-staffed world can absorb or be replaced by something new — deserves to be treated as seriously as the technology itself. Not because the technology does not matter. The reactor would not have been worth reviving if Microsoft did not need the electricity, and Microsoft would not have needed the electricity if the underlying AI systems were not doing something genuinely new. The point is narrower and, I think, more useful: technical superiority buys you the right to compete for a place in the installed world. It does not exempt you from having to find one.

The chapters that follow trace this mechanism across territory far from data centers and nuclear reactors — plumbing, keyboards, bank ledgers, car fleets, kitchen ranges, rural broadband, and the standard gauge of a nineteenth-century railroad. The claim connecting all of it is the one this chapter has tried to make concrete rather than abstract: before asking whether a new system is better, ask what already occupies the site it needs, what already depends on staying there, and what it will actually cost — in years, in dollars, in transformers nobody can build fast enough — to make room.
