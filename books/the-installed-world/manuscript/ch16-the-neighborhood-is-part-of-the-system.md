# The Neighborhood Is Part of the System

In Fayette County, Georgia, ordinary residents first began noticing something quietly, persistently wrong with their household water pressure in the spring of 2025. When county water officials investigated, they discovered two unauthorized water lines feeding QTS Realty's data center campus, the largest in the state, which by that point had drawn the equivalent of roughly 5,500 backyard swimming pools' worth of water since its first meter came online in February 2024. The discovery arrived during a period when much of Georgia was experiencing severe to exceptional drought conditions, serious enough that Governor Brian Kemp had declared a state of emergency, and local officials had been asking residents to voluntarily scale back their own household water use even as the data center continued consuming water at industrial scale for its cooling systems.

Neighboring jurisdictions facing similar proposals took note of Fayette County's experience almost immediately, and the political response spread with striking speed across the wider Atlanta metropolitan region within a single legislative cycle. The Fayette County episode was not an isolated dispute. By the end of 2025, several independent industry-tracking organizations monitoring this exact trend nationally counted at least 48 separate data center projects, worth a combined roughly $156 billion, that had already been blocked outright or significantly, materially delayed by organized community opposition efforts, with 188 distinct local opposition groups active across 40 states. Multiple Georgia jurisdictions — Chamblee, Fayetteville, and others — moved to restrict or outright ban new data center development within their limits, citing water consumption, electricity demand, noise from cooling equipment, and the loss of land that might otherwise have supported housing, retail, or public green space.

Georgia's own state legislature took up the question directly in the years following these disputes, debating proposed legislation that would require greater water-use disclosure and give local governments stronger tools to negotiate binding usage limits before, rather than after, a data center campus breaks ground — a legislative response that mirrors, in its own way, the community-board reforms New York eventually adopted decades after the Cross-Bronx Expressway's construction, discussed in the next section.

This chapter is about a category of installed-world cost this book has mostly treated abstractly so far: the specific, geographically concentrated burden a new or expanding piece of infrastructure imposes on the neighborhood, town, or region it physically occupies, regardless of how diffusely the infrastructure's benefits are distributed across a much larger population elsewhere. A data center's compute serves customers and users scattered across the country or the world. Its water consumption, its constant mechanical noise, its physical land use, and its considerable strain on the local grid all land entirely and exclusively on the one specific community hosting it — a mismatch in geographic scope that recurs across nearly every large infrastructure project this book has examined, and that deserves its own extended treatment because the mismatch is spatial in a way Chapter 15's cost-allocation analysis, focused mainly on who pays financially, did not fully capture.

## The expressway that erased a neighborhood

The starkest historical illustration of this chapter's argument comes from mid-twentieth-century American highway construction, and no case is more thoroughly documented than New York's Cross-Bronx Expressway, built under the direction of urban planner Robert Moses between 1948 and 1972. The expressway's route was drawn directly through East Tremont, a densely populated, largely working-class and immigrant Bronx neighborhood, displacing an estimated several thousand households whose apartment buildings were condemned and demolished to make way for the roadway — a specific, deliberately chosen route that planners could have shifted several blocks in either direction to avoid the neighborhood's most densely populated sections entirely, according to later analyses and contemporary community objections, but did not, in part because the alternative routes would have required more complex and costly engineering around existing infrastructure.

The Cross-Bronx Expressway's construction is now widely cited by urban historians and planning scholars as a foundational case study in how mid-century infrastructure decisions concentrated cost on specific, often minority and lower-income neighborhoods while distributing the resulting benefit — faster regional automobile travel — across a much broader population that never set foot in the neighborhoods being demolished to provide it. The expressway itself became, in the decades following its completion, a symbol of the broader phenomenon urban planning researchers call the "highway to nowhere" or "urban renewal" pattern: interstate highway construction across dozens of American cities in the 1950s and 1960s that disproportionately routed through and destroyed Black and immigrant neighborhoods, a pattern documented extensively enough that the U.S. Department of Transportation itself formally acknowledged this history in later decades and, through programs like the Reconnecting Communities initiative launched in the 2020s, began funding efforts to repair or reverse some of the physical damage those earlier highway routing decisions caused.

## The compute that drinks the town's water

Return to Georgia's data center conflicts with the Cross-Bronx Expressway's history in view, and the underlying structural similarity becomes clear despite the vast difference in scale, era, and technology involved. A data center campus, like an expressway, delivers its primary benefit to people far removed from its physical location — in the data center's case, to cloud customers, AI users, and enterprise clients potentially anywhere in the world, connected only by fiber-optic cable rather than by road. Its costs — water drawn from a shared regional supply, competing directly with residential and agricultural use during exactly the drought conditions Georgia was experiencing; noise from industrial cooling equipment running continuously; local road wear from constant construction and maintenance traffic; and, in some documented cases, the conversion of land that local zoning had previously reserved for housing or public use — land entirely on the specific community hosting the facility, whether or not that community's own residents will ever meaningfully benefit from the compute capacity being built in their backyard.

The specific mechanism by which this mismatch becomes politically visible, in Georgia's case as in the Bronx decades earlier, is a sudden, concrete, individually experienced harm: low water pressure in someone's kitchen faucet, discovered by an ordinary homeowner and eventually traced back to unauthorized water lines feeding a facility whose true operational scale residents may not have fully understood or anticipated when it was originally approved. Abstract arguments about aggregate water consumption or aggregate electricity demand rarely mobilize sustained community opposition on their own; a neighbor's dry tap, like a demolished apartment building, makes the cost impossible to treat as a mere statistic.

## Why local approval processes keep failing to catch this in advance

Utility regulators and county planning staff, interviewed in subsequent local reporting on the Fayette County episode, generally acknowledged they had lacked the technical capacity to independently verify a data center operator's own water-use projections at the time the original permits were approved, relying instead on figures the developer itself supplied. A reasonable question follows directly from both cases: why does a formal local approval and permitting process — public hearings, environmental review, zoning board votes — not reliably catch and address this mismatch before construction begins, rather than after residents start noticing dry taps or demolished apartment buildings? The pattern across both this chapter's cases points toward a specific, recurring failure: the entity proposing the project typically possesses far more detailed technical information about the project's actual resource consumption, traffic impact, and noise profile than the community reviewing the proposal does, and the review process itself is often structured around a single up-or-down approval decision made before construction begins, based on projections the developer itself supplies, with limited built-in mechanism for revisiting or enforcing those projections once the facility is actually operating at full scale.

Contemporary accounts of the Cross-Bronx Expressway's construction, including detailed reporting collected by Robert Caro in his Pulitzer Prize-winning biography of Moses, describe residents who organized petitions, attended what few public hearings existed, and proposed specific alternative routes shifting the highway a few blocks north — proposals city planning officials reviewed and ultimately rejected in favor of the original alignment, judging the engineering and cost tradeoffs unfavorable relative to the human cost of displacement, a cost that fell overwhelmingly on households with far less capacity to relocate, litigate, or otherwise absorb the disruption than the process assumed. Robert Moses, holding extraordinary consolidated authority over New York's public works agencies across multiple decades, was famously able to route infrastructure with minimal binding community input at all, a governance structure later planning reforms — including the community board system New York adopted in subsequent decades — were specifically designed to prevent from recurring. Georgia's data center approvals, by contrast, generally did pass through nominally more participatory local zoning and utility review processes, yet Fayette County's own water utility apparently did not detect the unauthorized water connections feeding QTS's facility until residents' complaints prompted an investigation — suggesting that formal participatory process, without adequate technical monitoring capacity on the reviewing government's side, does not by itself guarantee the mismatch this chapter describes gets caught before real harm accumulates.

## The county that asked for the burden

Loudoun County, Virginia, offers this chapter's most important counterexample, because it complicates any temptation to read this chapter as a simple morality tale of communities uniformly resisting unwanted infrastructure. Loudoun's stretch of Northern Virginia, informally known across the industry as "Data Center Alley," has actively courted and welcomed data center development for more than two decades, offering tax incentives and expedited permitting specifically to attract the industry, because the resulting commercial property tax revenue has become large enough to fund a substantial share of the county's public schools and other services without a correspondingly large increase in residential property taxes. By the mid-2020s, according to figures Loudoun County officials themselves have repeatedly cited in public budget presentations, data centers reportedly generated well over half of the county's total local tax revenue in a given year, transforming what looks, from Georgia's perspective, like an unwelcome imposition into what Loudoun's own elected officials have repeatedly described as a deliberate, broadly popular economic development strategy.

Loudoun's experience does not, on close examination, actually contradict this chapter's central argument at all; if anything, placed carefully alongside Fayette County's very different experience, it sharpens that argument considerably. The same infrastructure that Fayette County residents experienced as an unwanted, poorly monitored drain on a scarce shared resource, Loudoun County residents experienced as a wanted, actively negotiated trade: real local costs — increased traffic, visual and noise impact from an increasingly dense cluster of enormous windowless buildings, competition for grid capacity that has, by some local reporting, begun straining Loudoun's own electricity infrastructure — accepted deliberately, with eyes open, in exchange for a specific, substantial, well-understood local benefit that Fayette County's arrangement conspicuously lacked. The difference between the two counties is not the presence or absence of local cost. It is whether the hosting community negotiated a benefit commensurate with that cost, and whether the community had the information and leverage, at the time of approval, to actually strike that bargain rather than discovering the true scale of the cost only after construction was complete.

## The power line nobody wants running through their county

A related, currently unresolved version of this chapter's problem is slowing the very energy transition several earlier chapters have described as urgently necessary: new high-voltage transmission lines needed to connect renewable generation and other new power sources to the broader grid face some of the most persistent, well-organized local opposition of any infrastructure category examined in this book, for reasons that map directly onto this chapter's spatial-mismatch framework. A transmission line's benefit — cheaper, more reliable, often cleaner electricity — is diffused across an entire regional grid and every household it serves. Its cost — a visible steel-tower corridor cutting across farmland, forest, or residential viewsheds, condemnation proceedings against specific landowners whose property sits directly in the planned route, and localized property value impacts documented in multiple academic studies of transmission siting — concentrates entirely on the specific communities and landowners along the chosen route, who typically receive no meaningful share of the broader regional benefit the line is built to provide.

Independent grid analysts and federal energy regulators alike have repeatedly flagged transmission siting delays, often stretching a full decade or more from initial proposal to energized line, as one of the single largest practical bottlenecks constraining the pace of the American electricity grid's broader expansion and modernization, a problem regulators, utilities, and clean-energy developers have struggled to solve for years without simply overriding local objections through eminent domain — a tool that resolves the immediate siting dispute while doing nothing to address the underlying mismatch this chapter has identified, and that tends, unsurprisingly, to generate exactly the kind of sustained local resistance and prolonged legal challenge that further slows the very transmission buildout Chapter 1's discussion of grid interconnection queues already showed straining under enormous unmet demand.

## What has actually changed between the two cases

It would understate genuine progress to leave the impression that nothing has improved between the Cross-Bronx Expressway's era and today's data center conflicts. The sheer number and speed of local government responses to data center proposals through 2025 — moratoriums, outright bans, and new zoning restrictions adopted across multiple Georgia jurisdictions within a matter of months once the water and power impacts became publicly visible — reflects a local governance capacity to respond that simply did not exist, or was not exercised, during the era of unchecked mid-century highway construction. Communities today have more legal tools, more media attention, more organized advocacy infrastructure, and considerably more institutional standing — precisely the factor Chapter 15 identified as decisive in determining whether an unfavorable cost allocation gets renegotiated — than East Tremont's displaced residents had available to them in the 1950s.

What has not changed, across seventy years and a complete transformation in the underlying technology, is the basic spatial mismatch this chapter has traced: a piece of infrastructure whose benefit radiates outward to a broad, diffuse population while its cost concentrates on the specific place where it physically sits. Better local governance tools, better data, and a genuine, hard-won body of environmental justice research and law have made that mismatch far more visible and far more politically contestable than it was in Robert Moses's era, but they have not made the underlying mismatch itself materially smaller. ## Environmental justice as a formal research field

The pattern this chapter has traced through highways, data centers, and transmission lines has its own established academic and policy vocabulary, worth naming explicitly because it did not emerge from this book's specific examples but from decades of prior research documenting the same underlying dynamic across an even broader range of infrastructure categories. Sociologist Robert Bullard's research beginning in the late 1970s, examining the siting of landfills, waste facilities, and polluting industrial infrastructure disproportionately in and around Black communities in the American South, became foundational to what researchers and, eventually, federal policy came to call environmental justice: the systematic finding that the communities bearing the concentrated local costs of infrastructure — pollution, noise, land seizure, reduced property values — have historically been disproportionately lower-income, disproportionately non-white, and disproportionately lacking the institutional standing, discussed at length in Chapter 15, needed to prevent the siting decision or to negotiate meaningful compensation for it.

Federal environmental justice policy, formalized through a 1994 executive order directing federal agencies to identify and address disproportionate environmental burdens on minority and low-income populations, and expanded through subsequent administrations with varying degrees of enforcement priority, represents an institutional attempt to build the kind of advance-detection mechanism this chapter's earlier section found generally missing from local approval processes — a formal requirement that agencies actually assess, before approval, whether a proposed infrastructure project's burdens will concentrate disproportionately on a specific vulnerable population, rather than discovering that concentration only after residents notice their water pressure has dropped or their neighborhood has been demolished. The framework's existence does not guarantee the outcome; enforcement intensity has varied considerably across administrations and specific agency decisions, and critics have documented cases where environmental justice review was conducted as a procedural formality rather than a substantive check capable of actually altering a project's location or design. But its existence as formal federal policy reflects a hard-won, decades-long institutional acknowledgment that this chapter's spatial mismatch is not an unfortunate but unavoidable side effect of infrastructure development. It is a predictable, patterned outcome that deliberate institutional design can at least partially anticipate and address, even when it cannot fully prevent.

## Reading the mismatch before it becomes a crisis

Pulling together the highway, the data center, the transmission line, and the formal environmental justice framework built to address versions of all three, a practical diagnostic emerges that a community, a developer, or a regulator can apply before a project's local costs become a genuine crisis rather than a manageable, negotiated tradeoff. Ask, first, how geographically concentrated the project's costs actually are relative to how geographically diffuse its benefits are — a data center's water and noise impact concentrates within a few miles of its physical footprint while its computing benefit reaches customers continents away, exactly the kind of extreme mismatch that predicts sustained local resistance once the true scale becomes visible. Ask, second, whether the hosting community has genuine, informed bargaining power at the moment of approval, comparable to what Loudoun County exercised over two decades of deliberate courtship and negotiation, or whether the approval process is structured, as Fayette County's arguably was, around information asymmetry that leaves the community discovering the project's true scale only after construction is complete and the water lines are already drawing at industrial volume. Ask, third, whether any formal review mechanism — environmental justice assessment, a transmission siting board, a zoning process with genuine technical monitoring capacity — exists to catch a dangerous mismatch before it hardens into the kind of politically explosive standoff this chapter's cases have both, in their own eras, eventually become.

The next chapter turns from this diagnosis to the specific menu of policy tools — mandate, subsidy, and patient waiting for the ordinary turnover clock to do the work — that governments actually reach for once a mismatch like this has been identified and a decision about how to address it can no longer be deferred.
