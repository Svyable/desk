# The Installed World

There is a moment in almost every optimistic forecast about technology where the ground disappears. A slide shows adoption curves for the smartphone, the internet, the automobile — all rising in the familiar S-shape that Everett Rogers described in *Diffusion of Innovations*, first published in 1962 and revised across five subsequent editions as new technologies kept confirming the pattern he had generalized from decades of agricultural, medical, and consumer research. Early adopters, Rogers found, are followed by an early majority, then a late majority, then laggards, and the curve — plotted against time — looks roughly like a stretched letter S, slow at first, steep in the middle, flattening as the market saturates. The forecast then asks a simple question: how many years until the new technology climbs that curve? And it answers by looking at how fast previous technologies climbed theirs.

What the slide leaves out is the ground the curve is standing on. Rogers's own research was full of it — his most famous case study, the slow adoption of boiled water and later hybrid seed corn among Iowa farmers in the 1920s and 1930s, was largely a story about social systems, trusted messengers, and existing farming practices, not a pure story about the technology's merit. But the diffusion curve, once extracted from that context and turned into a management slide, tends to get treated as a property of the technology alone — as if a self-driving car, a heat pump, and a new banking core system all face the same kind of resistance, differing only in degree.

They do not. And the reason they do not is the subject of this book, which needs a name precise enough to survive contact with very different kinds of resistance: a keyboard layout, a rail gauge, a COBOL mainframe, a coal plant, a mortgage. This chapter proposes one.

## A definition worth pinning down

Call it **the installed world**: the entire accumulated stock of physical infrastructure, technical systems, standards, contracts, trained labor, and institutional expectation that a new technology, policy, or practice must coexist with, migrate from, route around, or replace before it can become ordinary.

Three features of that definition are doing real work, and it is worth being explicit about each of them, because sloppy use of "the installed world" — treating it as a synonym for inertia, tradition, or resistance to change — would waste the concept before this book has gotten any real use out of it.

First, the installed world is *accumulated*, not merely present. Chapter 1's grid transformers did not become scarce overnight; the underinvestment, the narrow supplier base, the specialized steel supply chain all built up over years of a demand curve that assumed slow, predictable growth. Chapter 2's spreadsheet installed base did not appear the day Lotus 1-2-3 shipped; it grew macro by macro, trained user by trained user, file by saved file, for a decade before a rival product had to reckon with it. Understanding an installed world requires understanding its history, not just its current inventory — because the history explains both why the stock exists in its current shape and how fast a comparable stock could plausibly be rebuilt.

Second, the installed world spans categories that are usually studied separately. Economists who study infrastructure tend to focus on physical capital: pipes, plants, roads, grids. Economists who study network effects tend to focus on standards and platforms: file formats, connectors, protocols. Labor economists study workforce skill formation. Political scientists and legal scholars study regulatory path dependence and the durability of contracts and institutions. Each of those literatures has real, well-developed answers about its own slice of the problem. Almost none of them talk to each other, because a specialist studying grid transformers has little professional reason to read a specialist studying keyboard standards. This book's claim is that they are describing versions of the same underlying mechanism — decisions, once made and depended upon by other decisions, becoming expensive to unmake — and that the mechanism is easier to see, and easier to reason about in a specific case, once its physical, technical, and institutional forms are treated as a single object rather than four separate disciplines.

Third, and most important for the arguments ahead, the installed world is not automatically the villain of this book. A rail gauge that lets a freight car travel from Georgia to Oregon without being unloaded is installed-world friction against any competing gauge — and it is also one of the most valuable pieces of infrastructure in American economic history. A building code that slows a housing developer down is installed-world friction against faster construction — and it is also, in the ordinary case, the reason the building does not catch fire from a preventable wiring fault. Distinguishing the friction that is protecting something real from the friction that is merely the cost of yesterday's convenience is the actual analytical task this book sets for itself, chapter after chapter. Simply naming the installed world does not resolve that question. It just makes it possible to ask cleanly.

## Four questions, one method

Chapters 1 and 2 already put four recurring questions to work without fully naming them. It is worth stating them together here, because every chapter from this point forward is a variation on the same method, applied to a different piece of the installed world.

*What was the site actually holding before the new system arrived?* Three Mile Island's "empty" alternative — a freshly built reactor on undeveloped land — was never actually empty in the relevant sense: an empty site still requires environmental review, a fresh licensing process, and years of interconnection studies that an already-connected reactor site had already completed. Treating a site as blank because nothing is visibly standing on it is the specific error Chapter 1 named the empty-site fallacy.

*What has learned to depend on the installed system?* An ATM network's value depended on how many branches already existed to host it. A spreadsheet's price depended on how many other users, files, and trained employees already assumed its macro language. Every dependency of this kind is a separate reason changing the installed system costs more than building the next unit of it did.

*What does the transition actually cost, once every dependent party is counted?* TSMC's Arizona fabs were delayed not by a shortage of capital or land but by a shortage of workers trained to install and calibrate specific tool sets — a cost the original announcement's timeline had not adequately priced in. Counting the true cost of a transition means counting costs that show up on someone else's ledger: the retraining budget, the parallel-operation period, the stranded asset, the disrupted customer, the retrained regulator.

*Who decided, and who pays?* The EU's common charger directive was, among other things, a decision that consumers and the environment had been paying for a coordination failure manufacturers had no individual incentive to fix. Chapter 15 and Chapter 16 return to this question directly, because the party that benefits from replacing an installed system and the party that bears the transition cost are frequently not the same party, and that mismatch shapes political fights over infrastructure far more than raw economics does.

## Two forms of installed weight

It helps, going forward, to distinguish two forms the installed world takes, because they resist replacement through different mechanisms and respond to different remedies.

The first is *physical weight*: concrete, steel, copper, land, and the licenses and permits attached to them. Physical weight resists replacement mainly through the sheer time and capital required to build an equivalent alternative — a transformer factory, a nuclear reactor, a fiber trunk line, a bridge. Physical weight can, in principle, be overcome by spending enough money fast enough, but "fast enough" is bounded by physical constraints that money cannot simply dissolve: a factory takes years to build regardless of how many dollars are thrown at it, a transmission line requires right-of-way negotiations that proceed at the speed of the slowest landowner, and a skilled workforce cannot be trained faster than the apprenticeship or certification process allows.

The second is *institutional weight*: contracts, standards, regulatory classifications, professional credentials, and the expectations built around all of them. Institutional weight resists replacement mainly through coordination costs and legitimate procedural requirements rather than through the raw unavailability of steel or labor — a regulator has to be persuaded, a contract has to expire or be renegotiated, a professional certification has to be updated, a standard has to be revised by the body that owns it. Institutional weight can sometimes move faster than physical weight, because rewriting a rule is quicker than pouring concrete, but it can also move slower, because a legitimate rulemaking process has procedural protections — public comment periods, appeals, judicial review — that a construction schedule does not.

Most real transitions involve both forms of weight simultaneously, tangled together in ways that make them hard to address independently. The COBOL banking systems examined in Chapter 7 are physically installed — mainframes, storage, decades of transaction records — and institutionally installed, wrapped in regulatory audit requirements, decades-old service contracts, and a professional certification pipeline that has been quietly shrinking for a generation. Solving the physical problem (migrating the code) without solving the institutional problem (retraining auditors, renegotiating vendor contracts, requalifying the new system under banking regulation) does not actually complete the transition. This book's later chapters try to keep both forms of weight in view at once, because treating either one as if it were the whole problem is how transition timelines get built on hope rather than evidence.

## What this is not a book about

It is worth being explicit, this early, about two arguments this book deliberately does not make, because both are easy to slide into once "the installed world" starts to feel like a satisfying explanation for everything.

This is not a declinist argument that societies are becoming too encumbered by their own infrastructure to progress. Chapter 1 already showed a counterexample sitting right next to the grid-queue story: container shipping displaced an entire mature, unionized, physically embedded global industry in roughly fifteen years, once a builder found the right seam in the installed world to work through. The chapters ahead include more such cases — fast transitions, not only slow ones — because a theory that only explains delay would be a theory of complaint, not a theory of mechanism.

Nor is this a technologist's argument that regulation and legacy institutions are simply obstacles standing between the present and a better future, waiting to be swept aside by sufficiently determined founders. Some installed-world friction is exactly that: rent-seeking, protectionism, or simple failure to modernize. A great deal of it is not. The building code that slows a contractor down usually reflects a real fire, a real collapse, or a real injury that produced it. The bank regulation that slows a fintech startup down usually reflects a real financial crisis that an earlier, less regulated system produced. The rest of this book tries to hold both of those possibilities open simultaneously in every case it examines, because collapsing them into a single verdict — inertia bad, disruption good, or the reverse — is exactly the kind of simplification that makes the installed world so easy to misjudge from a pitch deck and so hard to actually navigate from inside a permitting office, a union hall, or a legislature.

## Why "friction" undersells it

Business writing has a stock vocabulary for describing everything this book is calling the installed world: friction, inertia, drag, resistance to change. Those words are not wrong exactly, but they share a common flaw. They describe a force acting against motion without describing what the force is made of, which makes them almost useless for deciding what to do about any specific case.

"Friction" is a fine word for a coefficient in a physics equation, where it can be measured, reduced through lubrication, and treated as more or less uniform across similar surfaces. It is a poor word for a nuclear plant's licensing requirements, a poor word for the specific shortage of grain-oriented electrical steel, and a poor word for a union's legitimate concern about a contractor's safety record — three things Chapter 1 alone put in front of the reader, none of which respond to the same kind of "lubrication." A regulatory delay yields to different interventions than a supply-chain shortage, which yields to different interventions than a skilled-labor gap, which yields to different interventions than a genuine, well-founded safety objection. Calling all four "friction" flattens exactly the distinctions a person actually trying to move a project forward needs to keep straight.

This book resists the temptation to reach for a single master metaphor precisely because the installed world does not behave like a single kind of substance. Sometimes it behaves like debt, accumulating interest the longer a decision goes unaddressed — the framing this book's sibling volume, *Adaptation Debt*, develops for the commitments an organization builds around its own past success. Sometimes it behaves like an ecosystem, where each species (each dependent contract, workflow, or trained habit) has adapted to the others in ways that make removing any single one riskier than it looks in isolation. Sometimes it behaves like a queue, with a knowable, if frustratingly long, wait time, the way the interconnection backlog behaves. And sometimes it behaves like a vote, in which every dependent party gets a say — formal or informal — over whether a change proceeds, the way a zoning board, a union local, or a legacy vendor's contract renewal clause can each function as an effective veto point.

The chapters ahead use whichever of these registers fits the specific case, rather than forcing every example into one recurring image. A reader looking for a single unifying metaphor to carry through the whole book will not find one here, on purpose. The unifying claim is narrower and, this book argues, more useful: whatever form the resistance takes — debt, ecosystem, queue, or veto — it is worth naming specifically enough to know which lever might actually move it, rather than filing it under a generic complaint about the pace of change.

## A note on scale

One more clarification is worth making before the book moves into its case studies, because the examples ahead range from a single kitchen appliance to an entire national electrical grid, and a reader could reasonably wonder whether the same concept is doing useful work at both ends of that range.

It is, but not because the installed world is the same size everywhere. It is because the same four questions — what is already there, what depends on it, what the transition costs, and who pays — apply whether the "site" in question is a single rented apartment with a gas line already run to the stove, or a continental grid with two thousand gigawatts of generation waiting in a connection queue. What changes across that range is not the method but the number of dependent parties, the size of the capital involved, and the length of time a transition realistically takes. A single household's gas-to-induction conversion might be delayed by a landlord's reluctance and a $1,000 appliance cost; a continent's grid interconnection queue is delayed by permitting law, a global transformer-steel shortage, and a specialized workforce that takes years to train. Both are installed-world problems in the strict sense this chapter has defined. Neither is smaller in kind than the other — only in scale, in the number of decisions that have already been made and now have to be unmade or worked around.

Keeping that distinction in view matters for a practical reason: it is tempting, once a concept like this has a name, to reach for it as an explanation for every kind of slowness, including slowness that has nothing to do with installed systems at all — bad management, simple incompetence, a project that was never adequately funded to begin with. The installed world is a real and underappreciated constraint. It is not a universal alibi, and the chapters ahead try to show, case by case, where it is doing genuine explanatory work and where a slow transition has other, more ordinary causes that happen to be less interesting to write about.

## A term that has to survive translation across disciplines

One last test this chapter's proposed term has to pass before the book puts real weight on it: does "the installed world" actually hold together when a reader carries it out of this book and into a specific professional context — a utility regulator's rate case, a hospital IT director's budget request, a city council's zoning debate — or does it collapse back into whatever domain-specific vocabulary that context already uses. The answer, based on how this book's later chapters actually deploy the term, is that it survives translation precisely because it does not try to replace domain-specific vocabulary. A utility regulator will keep using "rate base," "stranded cost," and "interconnection queue." A hospital IT director will keep using "legacy system," "vendor lock-in," and "unsupported operating system." A zoning board will keep using "nonconforming use," "franchise agreement," and "easement." None of those terms needs replacing, and this book has used all of them, in their proper professional context, throughout.

What "the installed world" adds is not a replacement vocabulary but a bridging one — a term general enough to let a reader recognize that a rate-base fight, a legacy-system migration, and a zoning dispute are, underneath their different professional languages, instances of the same recurring structural problem, worth analyzing with the same four questions even though no working regulator, IT director, or zoning commissioner would ever naturally use the same word to describe all three. That bridging function, more than any claim to novel discovery, is this term's actual contribution, and it is worth stating plainly here, at the end of Part One, before the book's remaining chapters put the term to work across still more professional vocabularies it has no intention of displacing.

## Reading the rest of this book

The four parts that follow are organized around the four questions above, in roughly the order a person actually encountering an installed system would ask them. Part One, which this chapter closes, has argued that the site is never empty and named what occupies it. Part Two — "The Weight of What Works" — spends five chapters on what has learned to depend on installed systems: standards, physical infrastructure, legacy software, contracts, and trained people, in that order, because each later chapter's dependency turns out to rest partly on the one before it. Part Three — "Transition Is Its Own System" — treats the process of changing an installed system as a subject in its own right, with its own economics, rather than as a brief interval between an old world and a new one. Part Four — "The Politics of Replacement" — takes up the question of who decides and who pays, because by that point in the book it should be clear that this is rarely a purely technical question, however much it gets discussed as one.

None of this is meant to produce a formula. The installed world resists formulas for the same reason it resists a single metaphor: it is made of too many different kinds of material, accumulated under too many different historical circumstances, to reduce to one number or one rule. What the chapters ahead offer instead is a discipline — the same four questions, asked patiently, of case after case, until the pattern connecting a nuclear reactor, a bank's mainframe, a kitchen stove, and a rural broadband contract becomes hard to unsee.

The chapters that follow move from definition to mechanism. Chapter 4 asks a question implicit in everything said so far: if different pieces of the installed world resist replacement to different degrees, do they also resist it on different *schedules* — and can those schedules be read in advance, the way an actuary reads a mortality table, rather than discovered only in hindsight?
