# Run Both

The Internet Engineering Task Force finalized the core specification for IPv6, the successor to the addressing protocol underlying the entire internet, in 1998, in direct response to the well-understood, increasingly urgent problem that IPv4's roughly 4.3 billion possible addresses would eventually run out as internet-connected devices multiplied past anything the protocol's 1980s designers had anticipated. More than a quarter century later, the transition is still not complete. As of early 2025, global IPv6 traffic to Google's own services — one of the most widely cited real-world adoption benchmarks — stood at slightly over 43 percent. By late March 2026, that figure crossed 50 percent for the first time, a milestone that took twenty-eight years to reach after the protocol's original publication, with substantial regional variation still visible: the United States barely above 50 percent, France, Germany, and India considerably higher at 80, 75, and 74 percent respectively, while large parts of Africa remained below 5 percent.

The internet did not simply flip a switch from IPv4 to IPv6 at any point during those twenty-eight years, and it could not have, because doing so would have instantly disconnected every device, network, and service still running the older protocol from every other device, network, and service that had already switched. Instead, the internet's core infrastructure spent decades running both protocols simultaneously — a configuration engineers call dual-stack operation, in which routers, servers, and end-user devices maintain the ability to communicate over either IPv4 or IPv6, translating and routing between them as needed, so that the transition could proceed gradually, network by network, without any single moment at which the whole system had to cut over at once.

This chapter is about that specific, underappreciated transition strategy: deliberately operating an old system and a new one side by side, often for years, sometimes for decades, precisely because the alternative — a single, instantaneous cutover — carries a level of risk that almost no consequential installed system can actually absorb, however tempting a clean, one-time switch might look on a project timeline.

## Why the clean cutover is usually the riskier option

It seems intuitive that a single, well-planned cutover — shut down the old system on a Friday night, bring up the new one over the weekend, resume operations Monday morning with everything running on the new platform — should be simpler, cheaper, and faster than the alternative of running two systems simultaneously for months or years. In practice, the opposite is usually true for any system consequential enough to matter, for a reason this book has already established across several earlier chapters: an installed system's true behavior, including its edge cases, its undocumented dependencies, and its interactions with dozens of other systems built to expect it, is never fully knowable in advance, no matter how thorough the migration planning has been.

A parallel run — operating the old and new systems side by side, ideally processing identical real-world inputs through both and comparing the outputs — converts that unknowable risk into an observable, correctable one. If the new system produces a result that diverges from the old one, the discrepancy shows up during the parallel period, while the old system is still authoritative and available as a fallback, rather than showing up for the first time after a full cutover, when the old system may already be decommissioned and unwinding back to it could take days the organization does not have. This is precisely the logic that governs why the ERP migration failure statistics discussed in earlier chapters so consistently identify inadequate testing and rushed cutover timelines as leading causes of catastrophic failure: the parallel-run period is not overhead to be minimized. It is the mechanism by which a migration actually gets tested against reality, at a scale and under conditions no pre-launch testing environment can fully replicate.

## The hybrid vehicle as a rolling parallel run

Automotive electrification offers an unusually visible, consumer-facing example of the same strategy operating at the level of an entire product category rather than a single organization's internal systems. A hybrid electric vehicle, combining a conventional gasoline engine with an electric motor and a modestly sized battery, is not simply a compromise between an internal-combustion vehicle and a fully electric one. It is, functionally, a rolling parallel-run architecture: the vehicle carries both propulsion systems simultaneously, drawing on whichever is more efficient or more available at a given moment, specifically so that the driver never has to depend entirely on a charging infrastructure that, as earlier chapters have documented, remains genuinely incomplete in large parts of the country.

This design choice has direct commercial consequences that connect back to Chapter 5's account of the recent slowdown in pure battery-electric vehicle sales following the expiration of federal purchase incentives. Hybrid and plug-in hybrid sales have, through the mid-2020s, continued growing even as pure battery-electric growth slowed, a pattern consistent with many buyers implicitly choosing the parallel-run option over the full cutover — accepting a more expensive, more mechanically complex vehicle in exchange for insurance against exactly the installed-world gaps this book has spent its middle chapters documenting: charging connector fragmentation before NACS standardization, grid interconnection delays constraining new charging infrastructure buildout, and a rural and lower-income geography where reliable public charging access remains genuinely uneven. The hybrid vehicle's continued market relevance, years after some industry forecasters expected it to be a brief bridge technology quickly superseded by full electrification, is itself evidence for this chapter's central claim: when the new system's supporting installed world is not yet fully built, a genuinely well-engineered parallel-operation product can remain the economically rational choice for a much longer period than early adoption curves predicted.

## What a parallel run actually costs

It would be a mistake to present parallel operation as a free lunch, and this chapter's earlier chapters have already supplied enough evidence to know better. Running two systems simultaneously means, almost by definition, paying for two systems simultaneously — the operating cost of the legacy platform does not disappear the moment a new one comes online, and in many cases actually increases temporarily, because the old system must now also support the reconciliation, comparison, and data-synchronization processes the parallel run requires, work it was never originally designed to do.

The dual-stack internet transition illustrates this cost at a scale spanning the entire global network. Every router, every piece of network infrastructure, and every piece of client software that supports both IPv4 and IPv6 simultaneously carries genuine additional complexity relative to a hypothetical network running only one protocol: more code paths to test and secure, more configuration surface for something to be set up incorrectly, and — a point network engineers have raised as a specific concern through the transition's later years — a larger attack surface for security vulnerabilities, since flaws can now hide in either protocol's implementation or in the translation layer connecting them. This is a genuine, non-trivial cost that has been paid, quietly, by essentially every organization operating internet infrastructure for more than two decades, in exchange for avoiding the catastrophically larger cost of forcing a hard cutover that would have disconnected a meaningful share of the existing internet the moment it happened.

## Knowing when to stop running both

A parallel-run strategy that never ends is not a transition; it is simply a permanently more expensive way of operating two systems instead of one, and this chapter's final task is distinguishing a well-managed parallel period from an indefinitely deferred decision dressed up in transition language. The internet's IPv6 rollout offers a useful, if sobering, cautionary data point on this question: twenty-eight years is an extraordinarily long parallel-run period by any standard, and the reasons it has taken this long trace directly back to the installed-base economics Chapter 2 described — IPv4 connectivity remains valuable precisely because so much of the existing internet still uses it, which reduces any individual network operator's incentive to fully retire IPv4 support as long as a meaningful share of the internet they need to reach has not yet done so either, a coordination problem that has proven far more durable than the original protocol designers anticipated when they set out to solve internet address exhaustion in the 1990s.

The organizations that manage parallel operation well, across the cases this book has examined, tend to share a specific discipline: they set an explicit, monitored end date for the parallel period from the outset, define concrete criteria for when the new system has proven itself reliable enough to become authoritative, and treat continued operation of the old system past that point as an active decision requiring renewed justification, rather than a default that persists simply because nobody has gotten around to turning it off. Commonwealth Bank of Australia's core-banking migration, examined in Chapter 7, followed roughly this discipline, moving in planned, monitored stages rather than either an immediate cutover or an open-ended dual-running arrangement. ## Simulcasting as an older ancestor of the same idea

Before the internet's protocol transition and before hybrid vehicles, broadcasters faced essentially the same architectural problem when television itself converted from black-and-white to color in the 1950s and 1960s, and their solution previewed the dual-stack logic this chapter has spent several sections describing in a modern context. The National Television System Committee's 1953 color standard was deliberately engineered for backward compatibility: a color broadcast signal was constructed so that black-and-white television sets, of which tens of millions were already in American homes, could still decode and display the same broadcast correctly, simply rendering the color information as shades of gray rather than failing to display the picture at all. This was not a minor technical nicety. It was the entire reason the transition to color television could proceed gradually over roughly fifteen years, from the first regular color broadcasts in the mid-1950s to color sets finally outselling black-and-white sets by the late 1960s, without ever forcing a single household to replace a working television set on a broadcaster's schedule rather than their own.

Compare this directly to the abrupt 2009 digital-television cutover examined in Chapter 4, and the contrast illuminates something this chapter has not yet made explicit: backward-compatible simulcasting and a hard cutover with a subsidy program are two structurally different answers to the same installed-base problem, and the choice between them depends heavily on whether the new signal format can, in principle, be made legible to old receivers at all. Color-over-black-and-white broadcasting could be engineered for graceful degradation because the underlying signal was still, at its core, an analog waveform that an older receiver's electronics could partially interpret. Digital broadcasting could not be made legible to a purely analog receiver's circuitry no matter how cleverly engineered, because the two systems encode information in fundamentally incompatible ways — which is precisely why the 2009 transition required a hard date and a converter-box subsidy rather than decades of gentle backward-compatible coexistence. The engineering question of whether a bridge is even physically possible, in other words, often determines the transition strategy more than any institutional preference for one approach over another.

## What determines which strategy is available

This chapter has now examined four cases — internet protocols, hybrid vehicles, enterprise core-banking migrations, and broadcast television — each choosing a different point along the same spectrum between an instant, total cutover and an indefinitely sustained parallel operation. Placing them side by side clarifies what actually determines where a given transition lands on that spectrum, beyond simply an organization's risk tolerance or institutional culture.

Three factors recur across every case. First, technical bridgeability: can the new system, like color television's backward-compatible signal, be engineered so the old system can still partially interpret it, or is a translation layer — like IPv6-to-IPv4 gateways, or a bank's parallel-processing reconciliation system — required to keep both sides functioning during the transition. Second, the cost of maintaining both systems simultaneously relative to the cost of a failed cutover: a hybrid vehicle's dual powertrain is expensive to manufacture but cheap relative to the cost, to the driver, of being stranded without a charging station on a long trip, while a bank's parallel-run testing period is expensive to operate but trivially cheap relative to the cost of corrupting a customer's account balance during a botched migration. Third, and perhaps most decisive across the cases this book has examined, whether a credible outside party — a regulator, in television's case; a competitive market pressure, in the hybrid vehicle's case — is available to enforce an actual end date on the parallel period, because, as the next section argues, parallel operation left to drift indefinitely on its own institutional momentum rarely ends itself.

## The bridge that becomes the destination

There is a specific failure mode worth naming before this chapter closes, because it recurs often enough across the cases this book examines to deserve its own label: the parallel system, originally conceived as a temporary bridge, quietly becomes a permanent destination because the incentives that would push an organization to finish the transition never fully materialize. IPv4 illustrates this dynamic at internet scale — every individual network operator has faced, for over two decades, a private incentive structure in which the cost of maintaining IPv4 support falls on their own budget while the benefit of fully retiring it accrues mainly to the broader internet's eventual simplification, a collective-action mismatch structurally similar to the household electrical-socket standoff Chapter 5 described, except playing out over internet protocols rather than physical plugs.

The same dynamic appears at a much smaller organizational scale whenever a "temporary" workaround system, built to bridge an old platform and a promised new one, ends up quietly absorbing so much operational dependency that retiring it becomes its own significant migration project. Enterprise software consultants who study failed and stalled modernization efforts have documented this pattern repeatedly: a bridging tool, originally scoped for a six-month transition window, becomes permanent infrastructure precisely because it worked well enough that nobody felt urgent pressure to finish decommissioning the system it was meant to temporarily connect. Chapter 11's discussion of the broader adapter economy returns to this exact tension directly, because a well-built adapter is, by its nature, good enough to reduce the pain of staying on the old system indefinitely — which is precisely what makes it valuable during a transition, and precisely what makes it dangerous to an organization that actually intends the transition to finish on schedule.

## The double-entry ledger as the original parallel run

It is worth noting, briefly, that this chapter's core discipline predates every digital example examined so far by several centuries. Double-entry bookkeeping, formalized in its now-familiar form by the Italian mathematician Luca Pacioli in a 1494 treatise, requires every financial transaction to be recorded twice, once as a debit and once as a matching credit, specifically so that any recording error becomes visible as an imbalance between the two independently maintained totals rather than passing through unnoticed in a single, unverified record. Modern financial audits still rely on essentially this same principle, extended considerably: an auditor reconciling a company's books against independent bank statements, supplier invoices, and other external records is running precisely the kind of parallel-verification process this chapter has described for banking migrations, testing one record against another independent one specifically because a single unverified system, however carefully built, cannot be trusted to catch its own errors.

The throughline from Pacioli's fifteenth-century ledger to a modern bank's core-system parallel run is not a stretch; it is, if anything, the oldest and most thoroughly proven version of this chapter's entire argument, and it is worth remembering, amid the specific technical vocabulary of dual-stack networking and hybrid propulsion architecture, that the underlying discipline this chapter recommends is not a recent invention born of digital-era caution. It is one of the oldest, most battle-tested risk-management habits in the entire history of organized commerce, simply rediscovered, again and again, by every generation of engineers who initially assumed a single, unverified system would be good enough.

## A brief return to the reactor

It is worth connecting this chapter's argument back to Chapter 1's opening case, because the Crane Clean Energy Center's restart is itself, in a specific technical sense, an instance of the same run-both logic operating at grid scale. The regional transmission grid Three Mile Island Unit 1 rejoins in 2027 does not simply wait, idle, for the reactor's single 835 megawatts to arrive. Natural gas turbines, the coal plants whose retirements Chapter 4 described being delayed under emergency federal orders, and every other generation source already connected to the grid continue operating throughout the years-long restart process, precisely because the grid as a whole cannot afford to treat any single generation project as a hard, all-or-nothing dependency. The reactor is being added to an already-running system, in parallel with everything already supplying it, rather than replacing anything on a fixed handoff date — the same underlying logic, at the scale of an entire regional power grid, that governs a single bank's core-system migration or a single household's decision to buy a hybrid rather than a pure electric vehicle.

## Reading the parallel-run decision correctly

Pulling this chapter's evidence together, across an internet protocol, a car's propulsion system, a bank's ledger, a broadcast signal, and a regional power grid: the decision to run two systems side by side rather than executing a single clean cutover is not a sign of institutional timidity, however much it can look that way to an outside observer impatient for visible progress and unaware of what a failed cutover would actually cost. It is, in every well-documented case examined here — the internet's protocol transition, a hybrid vehicle's dual propulsion architecture, a bank's core-system migration testing period — a deliberate, rational response to the same underlying fact this book has returned to since Chapter 3: an installed system's true behavior can never be fully known in advance, and the cost of discovering an unknown failure mode after an irreversible cutover is almost always higher than the cost of running both systems long enough to discover it safely, while the old system remains available as a working fallback.

None of the five cases this chapter has walked through — the internet, the hybrid vehicle, the bank ledger, the color television signal, the power grid absorbing a revived reactor — chose parallel operation because its designers lacked the confidence to commit to the new system outright. Each chose it because the specific, calculable cost of running two systems for a bounded period was smaller, by a wide and demonstrable margin, than the cost of discovering a critical, unanticipated failure only after the old system had already been shut down and was no longer available to fall back on. That is not caution for its own sake, and it should not be mistaken for timidity by anyone judging the pace of the transition from outside. It is closer to an insurance premium, paid deliberately and knowingly, against a specific, well-understood risk that every one of these institutions had good reason, whether from their own institutional history or from a close neighbor's well-publicized failure, to take seriously rather than gamble against.

The genuine risk this chapter has tried to isolate honestly is not that organizations choose to run both. It is that they fail to set, monitor, and actually enforce the specific conditions under which the parallel period ends — treating a bridge, built for a specific, bounded purpose, as though it could safely become a permanent piece of the installed world in its own right, without anyone ever formally deciding that it should. Chapter 12 examines what happens to organizations that skip this discipline — that begin a migration with every intention of running both systems only briefly, and instead find the transition stretching years past its original schedule, with neither the old system fully retired nor the new one fully trusted.
