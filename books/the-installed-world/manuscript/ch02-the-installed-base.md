# The Installed Base

In the early 1970s, American banks began installing automated teller machines, and economists later noticed something odd about which banks moved first. It was not, primarily, the banks serving the most tech-forward customers, or the banks facing the fiercest local competition. It was disproportionately the large banks — the ones that already operated many branches.

Garth Saloner and Andrea Shepard, studying the diffusion of ATMs across the American banking industry in a paper published in the *RAND Journal of Economics* in 1995, found that a bank's own network size — the number of branches it already operated — was one of the strongest predictors of how early it adopted automated teller machines. The mechanism was not mysterious once named. An ATM is only as useful as the number of places a customer can find one. A bank with two hundred branches could roll out ATMs across its own existing branch network and immediately offer customers access at two hundred locations. A bank with six branches installing the identical machines offered access at six. The technology was the same. Its value to a customer, and therefore its value to the bank deciding whether to buy it, depended entirely on how much installed infrastructure — in this case, branches — already existed to plug it into.

This is the concept economists call network effects, and it is the mechanism by which an installed base stops being a passive record of past decisions and becomes an active force shaping which decisions get made next. The idea did not begin with ATMs. Jeffrey Rohlfs described the interdependence of value in telephone networks as early as 1974: a telephone is worthless to someone if nobody else owns one, and its value to every existing owner rises, at least up to a point, with every new subscriber who joins. But the ATM case is a clean illustration of something the telephone story can make sound too abstract — that network effects are not only about how many strangers use a product. They can operate entirely inside one company's own existing footprint, turning "how much have we already built" into a decisive input on "what should we build next."

## What an installed base actually buys

An installed base is not simply a large number of past customers. It is a large number of *decisions already made in a compatible direction* — decisions by customers, by suppliers, by regulators, by competitors, by the original company itself — that all currently assume a particular standard, format, connector, or platform will keep existing. Each of those decisions is a small vote for the installed base's continuation, and because those votes accumulate rather than reset, an installed base tends to become more valuable, not less, the longer it survives.

Economists Joseph Farrell and Garth Saloner formalized part of this dynamic in the 1980s, modeling how markets with strong network effects can become locked into a single standard even when a better alternative exists, because no individual user wants to be the first to switch to something nobody else has adopted yet. Michael Katz and Carl Shapiro extended the analysis through the following decade, examining how compatibility, standardization, and the size of a technology's existing installed base shape competitive strategy — when a firm benefits from making its product compatible with a rival's installed base, and when it benefits from deliberately breaking compatibility to force a new standard.

The empirical size of this effect can be striking. Research on the enterprise spreadsheet software market — a market that, in the 1980s and 1990s, saw incumbents like Lotus 1-2-3 displaced only slowly despite competitors offering comparable or superior features — found that a one percent increase in a spreadsheet product's installed base was associated with roughly a 0.75 percent increase in the price customers would pay for it. That is a remarkable number. It says the market was not simply pricing the spreadsheet's features. It was pricing access to the ecosystem of macros, trained users, file formats, and complementary tools that had already accumulated around that specific product. A better spreadsheet with a smaller installed base was, in a very literal sense, worth less to a buyer than a merely adequate spreadsheet with a larger one.

## The keyboard that became a parable

No installed-base story has been told, retold, and then aggressively second-guessed as many times as the standard QWERTY keyboard layout, and it belongs in this chapter precisely because the argument about it illustrates how carefully installed-base claims need to be checked before they get treated as economic law.

The received version, popularized by the economic historian Paul David in a widely cited 1985 paper, goes like this: the QWERTY layout was designed in the 1870s partly to slow typists down and prevent the mechanical arms of early typewriters from jamming, not to optimize typing speed. In 1936, the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard was patented, arranging letters to reduce finger travel and, according to David's account, improve typing speed and reduce errors. David cited U.S. Navy testing from the World War II era suggesting the increased efficiency from switching to the Dvorak layout would repay the cost of retraining a typist within roughly ten days. Yet QWERTY remained standard. David's explanation was that the installed base of QWERTY typewriters, QWERTY-trained typists, and QWERTY-based training materials had grown large enough, early enough, that no individual typist, employer, or manufacturer had an incentive to switch unilaterally — even though the market as a whole might have been better off if it had. QWERTY became the canonical example of "lock-in": an inferior standard persisting because everyone is individually rational to keep using what everyone else already uses.

Economists Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis spent much of the following decade taking this story apart. In their own research, later collected in *The Economics of QWERTY*, they went back to the original Navy studies David had cited and found the methodology deeply compromised — the tests had been overseen by Lieutenant Commander August Dvorak himself, the keyboard's own inventor, raising obvious questions about impartiality, and later, more carefully controlled studies found the claimed Dvorak advantage to be marginal at best and possibly nonexistent. Liebowitz and Margolis's broader argument was not that installed bases never matter. It was that QWERTY specifically may not be an example of the market choosing an inferior standard at all — it may simply be a case where the alternative was never as superior as its advocates claimed, and the market's failure to switch reflected accurate information rather than a coordination trap.

Both halves of that debate matter for this book. The lock-in story is real: markets with strong network effects genuinely can settle on a standard that a full, coordinated switch would improve upon, and Farrell and Saloner's models, along with plenty of contemporary industry cases, show the mechanism operating in practice. But the QWERTY case specifically is a caution against reaching for "installed base as villain" too quickly. Sometimes an old standard survives because switching really is coordinated, costly, and individually irrational even though the alternative is genuinely better. Sometimes an old standard survives because the alternative was never as good as its champions insisted, and the installed base gets blamed for a technical failure that was never really about installation at all. Distinguishing the two requires actually checking the evidence for the supposedly superior alternative — a step the popular version of the QWERTY story skipped for the better part of a century.

## The format war that turned licensing into installed-base strategy

If the ATM story shows an installed base accumulating almost by accident, inside one company's own branch network, the home videocassette format war of the late 1970s and early 1980s shows two companies consciously building rival installed bases against each other, with a clear winner and a clear, documented reason why.

Sony launched Betamax in Japan in May 1975. JVC followed eighteen months later, in September 1976, with VHS. On several technical dimensions that mattered to early reviewers, Betamax held its own or led — sharper picture quality was a commonly cited advantage in the format's early years. What decided the war was not picture quality. It was licensing strategy, and licensing strategy is, in essence, a decision about how fast to grow an installed base and how much control to give up in exchange for growing it faster.

Sony treated Betamax the way a confident engineering-led company often treats its own invention: closely held, licensed reluctantly, positioned for premium margins on a smaller number of machines. JVC did the opposite. It licensed the VHS format broadly and cheaply to nearly anyone who wanted to build a machine — Matsushita (the parent of Panasonic), RCA, Sharp, Hitachi, Zenith, and a long list of others were soon manufacturing VHS decks, competing with each other on price in a way no single Betamax manufacturer had to. The predictable result was that VHS machines proliferated faster and got cheaper faster, which meant more households owned VHS decks, which meant video rental shops — an entirely new industry taking shape at the same historical moment — had a straightforward commercial incentive to stock more VHS tapes than Betamax tapes, which meant a household choosing its first VCR was choosing the format with the larger rental catalog, which meant still more households bought VHS, which meant still more manufacturers built VHS decks. Every step in that loop reinforced the one before it. By the time consumer electronics review panels' quality preferences had a chance to matter, the installed base had already made the decision for most buyers.

This is the mechanism Katz and Shapiro's compatibility research formalizes: a firm holding a technology with network effects faces a genuine strategic choice between guarding its standard tightly, capturing more value per unit sold but growing its installed base slowly, or licensing broadly, capturing less value per unit but growing the installed base — and therefore the standard's ultimate market power — much faster. JVC bet on breadth. Sony bet on control. The installed base rewarded breadth, decisively, within about a decade.

The lesson generalizes well beyond consumer electronics, and it recurs later in this book in less obviously commercial settings: the railroad companies that agreed to a shared gauge in 1886 were making the same trade JVC made — sacrificing some individual control over their own network in exchange for compatibility with a much larger one. So, in a different register, was the EU when it decided that letting each phone manufacturer keep its own proprietary charging standard was no longer worth the cost of the incompatibility it created.

## When regulators decide the installed base is the problem

Not every network-effect standoff gets resolved by markets working it out on their own. Sometimes a government decides the installed base itself — specifically, the proliferation of incompatible installed bases — has become the actual harm worth regulating.

The European Union's common charger directive is a useful recent case because it targeted the problem at exactly this level of abstraction. Rather than mandating a specific technology on quality grounds, EU regulators observed that consumer electronics manufacturers had each built their own proprietary charging connector and, in doing so, had each built their own small installed base of compatible cables, chargers, and accessories — Apple's Lightning connector, various manufacturers' proprietary micro-connectors, and eventually the industry's gradual, uncoordinated drift toward USB-C. The Commission estimated that discarded and unused chargers were generating more than 11,000 tons of electronic waste annually, a cost that fell on consumers and the environment rather than on the manufacturers whose incompatible standards created it.

The regulation, finalized in 2022 and taking effect for phones, tablets, cameras, headphones, game consoles, and similar devices on December 28, 2024 — with laptops following by April 28, 2026 — did not ask which connector was technically superior. It simply mandated that manufacturers converge on one, USB-C, built to a specific published interoperability standard, IEC 62680-1-3. The interesting economic feature of this policy is what it reveals about the EU's own theory of the installed-base problem: left alone, manufacturers had incentives to maintain their own proprietary installed bases — each Lightning cable a customer already owned was one more reason to buy another Apple device rather than switch — even though a single shared standard would have been more efficient for the market as a whole. That is close to a textbook description of a coordination failure, and it is also close to a textbook description of why some installed-base problems require an outside party with the authority to simply pick a winner, rather than waiting for competition to converge on one voluntarily. Chapter 17 returns to this idea directly, when the book asks when a government should mandate a standard, when it should subsidize a transition, and when it should simply wait for market forces to do the work.

## Two kinds of network, one chicken-and-egg problem

The ATM and the VHS deck illustrate what economists call direct network effects: the value of the product rises with the number of other people using that same product. A second, equally important pattern is indirect network effects, where the value of a product rises with the number of complementary products built to work with it, and vice versa — a loop that creates the classic chicken-and-egg problem facing any new platform.

A video game console is the cleanest illustration. A console is nearly worthless without games, and a game studio has little reason to spend two or three years building a game for a console with no installed base of owners to buy it. Console makers have spent decades solving this problem through installed-base engineering rather than pure engineering: selling hardware near or below cost to grow the installed base of owners quickly, paying studios directly to secure exclusive titles that give early adopters a reason to buy in before the broader game library exists, and — once an installed base reaches a critical mass — watching third-party studios volunteer to build for the platform without being paid to, because the installed base has become large enough to justify the investment on its own. The installed base, in other words, is not merely a byproduct of a console's success. Building it deliberately, often at a short-term loss, is close to the entire strategy.

This two-sided structure explains why installed-base thinking cannot stop at "how many units have we sold." A platform with ten million device owners and no complementary developers is often worth less, strategically, than a platform with three million device owners and an enthusiastic developer community — because the second platform's installed base is compounding in both directions at once, while the first one may simply be sitting there. The distinction matters later in this book when the subject turns from consumer electronics to physical infrastructure: an electrical grid with millions of connected devices but no capacity to interconnect new generation is not the same kind of installed base as a grid actively adding both supply and demand in step. Some installed bases compound. Others simply accumulate.

## What Microsoft Office's decades of dominance actually teach

A more contemporary illustration of installed-base economics, running for nearly four decades rather than the roughly two the ATM story covered, is worth adding here because it shows the same mechanism operating inside a market most readers encounter directly in their own working lives. Microsoft Office, and specifically the .doc and .xls file formats its Word and Excel applications popularized beginning in the 1980s and 1990s, achieved and has largely retained market dominance despite decades of competing office-productivity software from Google, Apple, and a rotating cast of open-source alternatives, none of which has managed to displace it as the default enterprise and government standard, even when a specific competing product's individual features were, by most independent reviews, at least as good and sometimes measurably better.

The mechanism tracks Chapter 2's ATM and spreadsheet evidence closely: a business choosing office software is not evaluating Word or Excel purely on their own technical merits. It is evaluating how well a candidate product interoperates with the billions of existing files, macros, and templates already created in Microsoft's formats, sitting on corporate file servers, embedded in decades of email attachments, and referenced in training materials an entire enterprise workforce has already learned. Google Docs and other cloud-native competitors have made real inroads specifically in contexts — startups, schools, smaller organizations with less accumulated legacy content — where the installed base of existing Office files was smaller and the switching cost correspondingly lower, exactly the pattern this chapter's installed-base framework predicts. Large, established enterprises and government agencies, sitting on decades of accumulated Office-formatted institutional memory, have shown far more persistent loyalty to the incumbent format, not out of simple habit, but because the installed base of files, training, and interoperability requirements genuinely does make switching considerably more expensive than a feature-by-feature product comparison alone would suggest.

## The installed base as an asset, not only a barrier

It would be easy, after two case studies about lock-in and one about regulators overriding it, to conclude that an installed base is mostly a drag on progress — a weight that slows technically superior alternatives and occasionally requires government intervention to dislodge. That conclusion undersells what an installed base is actually for.

Go back to the ATM story that opened this chapter. The banks with the largest existing branch networks were not simply the banks best positioned to exploit an artificial lock-in advantage. They were the banks whose installed infrastructure made the new technology genuinely more useful, to real customers, faster. A customer choosing a bank in 1975 partly on the basis of ATM access was not being tricked by a network effect; she was making a rational judgment that a bank with more machines in more locations offered a more useful product. The installed base was not an obstacle standing between customers and the best technology. It was the mechanism that made the technology valuable in the first place, because value, for a network good, cannot be separated from the size and shape of the network it plugs into.

This is the distinction the rest of this book leans on. An installed base can be the reason a genuinely better standard cannot get a fair hearing — the lock-in story. It can also be the reason a merely adequate standard becomes, in practice, the better choice once its network of compatible users, trained operators, and complementary products is counted — the QWERTY revisionists' caution. And it can be the reason regulators decide that letting competing installed bases multiply indefinitely is itself the harm, worth overriding through a mandate. All three dynamics are visible in the chapters that follow, applied to systems considerably heavier than a spreadsheet macro or a keyboard layout: the physical gauge of a railroad, the voltage of a wall socket, the language a bank's core ledger still runs in.

It is worth being honest about what this chapter has not yet done. Every example so far — ATMs, spreadsheets, keyboards, videocassettes, game consoles, phone chargers — lives inside a market where switching, however costly, is at least conceivable within a normal product cycle. A bank can install new ATMs. A studio can port a game to a rival console. A manufacturer can redesign a charging port by its next hardware refresh. None of those installed bases required decades to build in the first place, and none of them are bolted into the ground.

That distinction is not a minor footnote. It is the fault line the rest of this book runs along. A software standard's installed base is made of decisions: files saved in a certain format, macros written for a certain application, muscle memory built around a certain layout. A physical standard's installed base is made of decisions plus concrete, copper, steel, and right-of-way — decisions that were poured, buried, bolted, and licensed, sometimes generations before anyone alive today had a vote on them. The economics are the same shape. The exit costs are not remotely the same size. A company can announce a new file format on a Tuesday and have most of its customers migrated within a product cycle or two. A country cannot announce a new railroad gauge on a Tuesday. The next chapter turns from installed bases that live mostly in software, licensing agreements, and consumer habit toward the installed bases that live in the ground — and gives the whole accumulated stock, physical and institutional together, its name.
