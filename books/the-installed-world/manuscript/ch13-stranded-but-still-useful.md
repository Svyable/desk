# Stranded but Still Useful

The B-52 Stratofortress, the United States Air Force's long-range strategic bomber, flew its first prototype test flight in 1952 and entered operational service with the B-52A variant in 1954. The Air Force's current plans call for keeping the aircraft flying into the 2050s — a service life approaching a full century, spanning the Cold War, the Vietnam War, the fall of the Soviet Union, the September 11 attacks, and, by the time the plan concludes, the better part of the twenty-first century's first half — alongside the far newer Northrop Grumman B-21 Raider stealth bomber the aircraft is meant to complement rather than be replaced by. Rather than retiring the aircraft's original Pratt & Whitney TF33 engines, installed since the 1960s, the Air Force is executing a roughly $2.56 billion re-engining program to fit the fleet with new Rolls-Royce F130 engines, a variant of a commercial turbofan design, with the modification program's critical design review clearing in May 2026 and aircraft modifications beginning that year. Structural inspections and periodic upgrades to the airframe's wiring, avionics, and other subsystems have continued across the aircraft's service life as a matter of routine, ordinary maintenance, but the airframe itself — the fuselage, the wing design, the overall aerodynamic architecture that a 1950s engineering team originally drew up — is not being replaced. It is being kept flying, deliberately and expensively, because seventy years of accumulated operational reliability, crew familiarity, and a demonstrated ability to carry an enormous and varied weapons payload over intercontinental range have proven more valuable to the Air Force than the cost and risk of designing, certifying, and fielding an entirely new aircraft to perform the same mission.

This chapter is about assets like the B-52, and the honest, sometimes uncomfortable accounting required to distinguish them from assets that merely look similar on the surface: technologically superseded by decades, in the narrow sense that a modern aircraft designer starting from a blank sheet of paper today would not reproduce a 1950s airframe design, and yet still economically and operationally rational to keep using, sometimes for generations past what any forecast at the time of their creation would have predicted. Being merely old, this chapter argues at length, is simply not the same thing as being genuinely obsolete, and confusing these two very different judgments is one of the single most consistent errors this book has found so far in how installed systems get evaluated by outside observers encountering them for the very first time, without the accumulated context their long operational history actually carries.

## The radio nobody meant to keep

A smaller, more consumer-facing version of the same phenomenon played out across the American automotive industry beginning around 2022 and 2023, when several major automakers — by one industry count, eight of twenty major manufacturers surveyed — began removing AM broadcast radio receivers from new electric vehicle models, citing genuine technical interference between AM radio's relatively low-frequency signal and the electromagnetic noise generated by an EV's high-voltage battery and motor systems. From a pure engineering standpoint, the decision was defensible: AM radio, a technology dating to the earliest decades of commercial broadcasting, genuinely is more susceptible to this specific interference than FM radio or digital streaming, and eliminating a feature that worked poorly in the new vehicle architecture looked, to the engineers making the call, like a reasonable modernization rather than a meaningful loss.

The reaction from broadcasters, safety officials, and eventually Congress reframed the decision entirely. AM radio remains the backbone of the United States' Emergency Alert System, the mechanism by which federal, state, and local officials broadcast urgent warnings — severe weather, wildfire evacuation orders, missing-child alerts, and, in the system's original Cold War-era conception, warning of nuclear attack — to the public, and it carries a specific advantage over cellular networks and even FM broadcasting during a genuine large-scale disaster: AM signals travel farther, penetrate buildings and terrain more reliably at long range, and depend on infrastructure that tends to survive power outages and network congestion better than cellular data. Ford, after direct engagement from members of Congress including Representative Josh Gottheimer, reversed its own plan to drop AM radio from 2024 model-year vehicles, and by early 2025 the bipartisan AM Radio for Every Vehicle Act had advanced through the Senate Commerce Committee with more than sixty cosponsors, aiming to require the feature as standard equipment nationwide regardless of any individual automaker's engineering preferences.

AM radio's persistence is a clean illustration of a mistake this chapter warns against directly: evaluating an old technology purely on the axis where it looks weakest — audio fidelity, interference resistance, the everyday listening experience most drivers actually care about — while ignoring the specific, narrower function it still performs better than any newer alternative. A modern EV's infotainment system streams music with vastly superior sound quality than AM broadcasting ever offered. None of that matters during a hurricane evacuation with cellular towers down and the power grid failing, which is precisely the scenario AM radio's continued mandated presence is designed to protect against — a scenario rare enough that most drivers will never personally experience it in their lifetime behind the wheel, and important enough, on the occasions when it does occur somewhere in the country, that Congress judged it worth overriding individual automakers' own engineering and cost-driven design preferences in order to guarantee the option remains reliably available nationwide, in every vehicle, regardless of any single manufacturer's private calculation about how many customers would ever actually notice or care.

## The reserve nobody can sell

A very different, considerably higher-stakes version of "stranded but still useful" runs through global energy markets, and it is worth examining because it inverts this chapter's framing in an instructive way: rather than an old asset proving unexpectedly valuable, it describes assets whose owners consider them valuable but which climate policy increasingly treats as assets that should never be used at all. The Carbon Tracker Initiative, a London-based financial think tank, popularized the term "unburnable carbon" starting with a influential 2011 and 2013 report series, arguing that the world's proven fossil fuel reserves — coal, oil, and gas already discovered, already booked on company balance sheets, already factored into share prices — substantially exceed the total amount that could be burned while keeping global warming within internationally agreed temperature limits. Carbon Tracker's analysis estimated that a carbon budget consistent with limiting warming to two degrees Celsius would leave the large majority of currently booked fossil fuel reserves permanently unusable, with more recent Carbon Tracker analysis putting potential stranded value in oil and gas assets alone above $1 trillion globally, more than half of it held by publicly listed companies whose shareholders have, in most cases, not yet fully priced that risk into the companies' market valuation.

This is "stranded but still useful" run in reverse: an asset that remains genuinely, physically capable of producing exactly the product it was developed to produce — recoverable oil, extractable coal — while an entirely separate, external constraint (in this case, a global climate budget rather than any technical failure) renders using it, at full historical scale, incompatible with a broader goal society has collectively, if unevenly, committed to. The concept illuminates something the B-52 and AM radio cases do not: an asset's "usefulness," in the sense this chapter has been building, is not solely a technical property of the asset itself. It is a judgment made relative to a specific goal, a specific constraint, and a specific set of institutions empowered to enforce that constraint — which is why the same fossil fuel reserve can be simultaneously a genuinely valuable, physically intact energy resource to the company that owns it and a liability its own shareholders, auditors, and regulators are increasingly required to treat as a potential write-down waiting to happen.

## The payphone that legally cannot disappear

A final, smaller case brings this chapter back to street level. New York City, along with several other American jurisdictions, has maintained legal requirements obligating telecommunications providers to keep a minimum number of public payphones operational in specific circumstances, even as cellular phone ownership has become close to universal and the payphone's ordinary commercial use has collapsed to a vanishingly small fraction of what it once was. The justification, consistent across the jurisdictions that have kept such requirements, mirrors AM radio's logic closely: a payphone represents a communication option that does not depend on an individual owning a functioning, charged cell phone with active service, a real gap for lower-income residents, for anyone whose phone has died or been lost or stolen, and, again, during genuine emergencies when cellular networks become congested or fail entirely, precisely the moments when the ability to reach emergency services matters most and is least guaranteed by the newer, more popular technology that has otherwise made the older one almost entirely obsolete for everyday use.

The economics of maintaining this requirement have shifted dramatically over the decades, worth noting because it illustrates yet another version of this book's recurring theme: what began, decades ago, as an ordinary, profitable public utility gradually became a money-losing regulatory obligation, maintained by providers not because payphones generate meaningful revenue any longer but because the accessibility and emergency-communication rationale, formalized in municipal franchise and telecommunications regulation, has outlasted the underlying commercial case entirely. New York's own payphone story took an unusual turn worth noting as a coda: rather than being fully decommissioned, many of the city's remaining physical payphone kiosks were converted, beginning in the mid-2010s, into LinkNYC terminals — providing free public WiFi, device charging, and phone calls from the same street-corner locations the original payphone infrastructure had already occupied for decades, a literal physical retrofit of installed urban infrastructure onto a new use, made economical precisely because the site, the power connection, and the municipal right-of-way agreement were all already in place and did not need to be renegotiated from scratch.

## The analog gauge behind the glass cockpit

Commercial aviation offers one more case worth examining, because it shows deliberate, engineered redundancy rather than simple reluctance to retire an old system. Modern airliners, from the Boeing 787 to the Airbus A350, are built around digital "glass cockpits" — large electronic displays that have almost entirely replaced the dozens of individual mechanical gauges that once covered an older aircraft's instrument panel, showing airspeed, altitude, attitude, and dozens of other flight parameters through software-driven screens fed by digital sensors and flight computers. Buried in that same modern cockpit, tucked into a small panel that most passengers would never notice and many pilots hope never to need, is a small cluster of standalone analog backup instruments — a mechanical or electromechanical standby attitude indicator, altimeter, and airspeed indicator, wired independently of the aircraft's primary digital flight-display system and, in most designs, independently powered as well.

These backup instruments are not vestigial leftovers awaiting a future redesign that finally removes them. They are a deliberate, certified safety requirement, present specifically because aviation regulators and aircraft designers have concluded that a fully digital flight-display system, however reliable in the overwhelming majority of cases, represents a single point of failure that a genuinely safety-critical aircraft cannot be allowed to depend on entirely. A software failure, an electrical fault affecting the primary display system, or a sensor malfunction feeding bad data to the digital flight computers could, in principle, disable or corrupt every screen in a glass cockpit simultaneously — a scenario analog instruments, wired through an entirely separate and mechanically simpler pathway, are specifically designed to survive. The old technology's persistence here is not an accident of institutional inertia. It is a deliberate, engineered acknowledgment that the newest system's greatest strength — deep integration, shared data, a single coherent digital architecture — is also, in the rare worst case, its greatest vulnerability, and that a genuinely different, independent, older technology is the most reliable available hedge against that specific failure mode.

## When the old technology becomes a luxury good

A final variant of this chapter's pattern deserves acknowledgment because it complicates the purely functional framing the cases above have emphasized: sometimes an old technology persists not because it performs a specific safety-critical function better than its replacement, but because its very obsolescence becomes a source of value in its own right. The Swiss mechanical watch industry survived what the trade still refers to as the "quartz crisis" of the 1970s and 1980s, when Japanese quartz movements — dramatically more accurate, cheaper to manufacture, and requiring none of the intricate hand-assembled mechanical engineering a traditional watch demanded — nearly destroyed an entire national industry built around a technology quartz had rendered functionally obsolete by every conventional timekeeping metric. Rather than disappearing, high-end Swiss mechanical watchmaking repositioned itself over the following decades around precisely the qualities that made it functionally inferior: visible mechanical complexity, hand craftsmanship, multi-generational brand heritage, and a deliberate rejection of the convenience quartz offered, sold at price points now routinely reaching tens of thousands of dollars for objects that keep time measurably less accurately than a five-dollar quartz watch.

This case sits at the edge of this chapter's argument rather than comfortably inside it, and it is worth including precisely for that reason. The B-52, AM radio, and the standby flight instrument all persist because they perform a real, specifiable function better than their replacement under some real, specifiable condition. The mechanical watch persists because a portion of the market has come to value the old technology's obsolescence itself — its scarcity, its visible human labor, its connection to a manufacturing tradition — as a distinct form of worth that has little to do with the timekeeping function a watch nominally exists to perform. Readers should hold this case a little more skeptically than the others, as a reminder that not every argument for an old system's continued value is a strictly functional argument in this chapter's narrower, stricter sense, even when it is a perfectly legitimate and economically real argument on its own separate, aesthetic terms that this book has no particular quarrel with.

## What separates rational persistence from mere nostalgia

Placing the B-52, AM radio, stranded fossil fuel reserves, and the public payphone side by side surfaces a test worth naming explicitly, because this chapter's argument only holds if it can distinguish genuine rational persistence from simple resistance to letting go of something familiar. In each case examined here, the old asset's continued usefulness traces to a specific, narrow function that a newer alternative genuinely does not yet replicate as reliably: intercontinental payload capacity built and proven over decades of operational history, in the B-52's case; resilience during grid and network failure, in AM radio's and the payphone's case; physical energy content that remains real and extractable regardless of the political and financial constraints layered on top of it, in the stranded-reserve case.

None of these cases argues that old is automatically better, and this chapter is careful to say so directly, because the temptation to overcorrect — treating every legacy system's persistence as evidence of hidden, underappreciated value — would be exactly the kind of romantic error this book has warned against since its opening pages. The B-52's airframe survives because its specific mission profile has proven durably valuable, not because seventy-year-old aircraft are inherently superior to new ones; plenty of Cold War-era aircraft designs have been retired precisely because their specific missions did not survive the decades as well. The genuinely useful discipline this chapter leaves behind is narrower and more actionable than either blanket position: before writing off an old system as simply outdated, or defending it as simply irreplaceable, ask what specific, narrow function it still performs better than any available alternative, under what specific conditions, and how often those conditions actually arise — a hurricane, a grid failure, a seventy-year-old bomber crew's confidence in an airframe whose failure modes are, after decades of operational experience, more thoroughly understood than any brand-new design's could possibly be yet.

## A closing test, applied honestly

It is worth applying this chapter's own test to itself before moving on, because a book built around defending installed systems' hidden value risks becoming exactly the kind of uncritical apology for inertia this book has repeatedly promised not to write. Ask, of each case examined here, what specific evidence would change the verdict. The B-52's persistence would look far less rational if the Air Force's own internal cost analyses showed the re-engining program's $2.56 billion price tag exceeding what a comparable new-build long-range bomber would cost across an equivalent service life — a comparison the Air Force's own budget justifications suggest favors re-engining, but one worth watching rather than assuming settled permanently. AM radio's mandated persistence would look considerably weaker if cellular network resilience during major disasters improved enough, through satellite-connectivity features increasingly built into consumer smartphones, to close the specific reliability gap the emergency-broadcast argument depends on — a real, ongoing technological trend this chapter's argument should not pretend is static. The standby flight instrument's justification would erode if aviation safety data began showing digital flight-display failures occurring at a rate low enough that the added weight, cost, and complexity of a separate analog backup system no longer paid for itself in expected lives saved.

None of these tests currently overturns this chapter's central cases, based on the best evidence available at the time of writing, though a careful reader should treat every one of them as a live, ongoing question rather than a settled conclusion this book is asking anyone to simply accept on faith. All of them are worth watching, because the entire argument of this chapter — that persistence can be rational rather than merely inertial — only remains credible if it stays genuinely falsifiable, chapter by chapter and case by case, rather than hardening into the same blanket defense of the status quo this book has warned against holding onto too tightly since its very first pages.

The next chapter turns to a related, harder problem: not systems that remain rationally useful despite their age, but the specific bottleneck created when the very last holdouts on an otherwise nearly complete transition turn out to matter far more than their small remaining numbers would suggest.
