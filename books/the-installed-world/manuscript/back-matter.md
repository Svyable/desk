## Epilogue

A reader who has come this far may be tempted to reduce the book to a single warning: do not underestimate the installed world. That warning is true, but it is not the point.

The point is that the installed world is not an obstacle standing between the present and a better future. It is the material the future is made of. Every socket that already fits a plug, every pipe that already reaches a house, every worker who already knows how the machine fails, every contract that already allocates a risk, is either a wall or a foundation depending on how the next system is designed. The same installed base that stalls one entrant becomes the distribution channel for another. The same compatibility requirement that looks like a tax on innovation is, from another seat at the table, the reason two hundred million devices can already talk to each other without anyone negotiating twice.

None of the technologies discussed in these chapters — the transformer, the turbine, the heat pump, the model that runs in a data center built into a former steel mill — arrives into an empty room. It arrives into a world that has already made a great many decisions, most of them for reasons that made sense at the time, and asks to be let in. Sometimes it should be let in quickly. Sometimes the caution embedded in the old system is protecting something the new one has not yet learned to value. The discipline this book has tried to practice, chapter after chapter, is telling those two situations apart with evidence instead of temperament.

If there is a single instruction worth carrying forward, it is a modest one. Before asking whether a new system is better, ask what has already learned to depend on the old one, what that dependency actually costs to unwind, and who is being asked to pay it. That question will not resolve every argument about telecom standards, grid interconnection queues, gas ranges, or COBOL. It will at least make sure the argument is about the right thing.

This book leans on a public record built by engineers, economists, regulators, journalists, and utility workers who wrote down what an installed system actually costs to change — a debt this manuscript can only try to repay by citing them accurately and representing their disagreements honestly.
