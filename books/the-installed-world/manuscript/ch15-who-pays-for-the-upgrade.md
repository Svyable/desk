# Who Pays for the Upgrade?

The Detroit Water and Sewerage Department built the pipe network beneath and around the city of Detroit for a metropolitan population that, at its 1950s peak, exceeded 1.8 million people. Much of that sprawling infrastructure was financed with municipal debt, structured from the outset on the ordinary, entirely reasonable assumption underlying nearly all public utility financing: a large, stable, or growing customer base would repay the bonds gradually through water rates over decades, spreading the fixed cost of pipes, treatment plants, and pumping stations across enough ratepayers that no individual household's bill needed to be especially high. Detroit's actual population instead collapsed steadily over the following decades, falling from that 1950s peak to roughly 680,000 by the 2010s, a decline driven by deindustrialization, suburban flight, and decades of disinvestment that left the city's water system's fixed infrastructure costs — the same pipes, the same treatment capacity, much of it built for a metropolitan area that had spread further outward even as the city core itself emptied — spread across a dramatically smaller, and dramatically poorer, remaining ratepayer base.

The result, by the early 2010s and worsening steadily through the decade that followed, was a water affordability crisis documented extensively and repeatedly by academic researchers, investigative journalists, and civil rights organizations working independently of one another yet arriving at strikingly consistent conclusions: Detroit's average residential water bill, at roughly $87.54 a month by one widely cited analysis, could consume as much as 25 percent of disposable income for a household living below the poverty line — against a U.S. Environmental Protection Agency affordability benchmark suggesting water costs should not exceed 4.5 percent of disposable income. Facing mounting unpaid customer bills, a shrinking rate base unable to service its inherited obligations, and a water department carrying roughly a third of the city's $18 billion in total municipal debt at the exact moment of Detroit's landmark 2013 municipal bankruptcy filing — itself the largest such filing in American history at the time — the city initiated a wave of shutoffs beginning in 2014 that became, by most accounts, the largest residential water shutoff campaign in American history — an estimated 141,000 shutoffs between 2014 and 2020 alone, falling overwhelmingly on low-income households in a city where a large share of residents were already living below the poverty line.

Journalists, academic researchers, and the ACLU of Michigan spent years documenting the human dimension behind these aggregate statistics — families boiling stored water for basic sanitation, children removed from homes under child-welfare rules that treated a lack of running water as evidence of parental neglect rather than as a downstream consequence of a municipal financing structure none of those families had any hand in designing decades earlier, and a United Nations special rapporteur who, after a 2014 visit, characterized the shutoffs as a violation of the international human right to water and sanitation, a characterization Detroit and Michigan state officials disputed at the time. This chapter is about the question Detroit's water crisis poses with unusual, almost mathematical clarity: when an old system's infrastructure cost was fixed decades ago, at a scale built for a population and an economic base that has since changed dramatically and irreversibly, who actually bears that fixed, largely unchangeable financial obligation once it meets a shrunken, considerably poorer group of people who are still legally required to pay for it, whether or not they had any part in the original decisions that created it? The answer, across nearly every case this book has examined, is rarely "whoever benefits most from the system's continued operation." It is, far more often, "whoever has the least ability to negotiate, relocate, or opt out."

## The mismatch this book has been circling

Nearly every chapter in this book's third part, dealing with the mechanics of transition itself, touched this exact question in passing without ever pausing to name it directly and examine it on its own terms. Chapter 6's lead-line replacement program spreads its cost across a broad ratepayer base while its health benefit concentrates specifically on households whose service line was actually lead. Chapter 8's gas franchise agreements bind an entire municipality's electrification timeline to contract terms negotiated decades before any individual resident had a voice in the matter. Chapter 14's AMPS shutdown and EMV liability shift both imposed real costs on specific, identifiable, disproportionately vulnerable populations — the elderly, the rural, the under-capitalized small merchant — while the aggregate benefit of the underlying transition accrued broadly across an entire national economy.

Detroit's specific case makes the underlying mechanism unusually legible and unusually hard to look away from, because the mismatch runs through a single, transparent, almost brutally simple number: a fixed infrastructure cost, built for a population that no longer exists at the scale it once did, divided across the population that remains. But the same mechanism, in less numerically explicit form, runs through every transition this book has examined where an old system's cost structure was set under one set of assumptions and its bill eventually comes due under an entirely different one.

## The utility rate base as an installed-world ledger

American utility regulation offers a formal, legally codified version of the same mechanism, worth examining because it shows how deeply "who pays" is embedded in the actual accounting rules governing infrastructure investment, not merely in ad hoc political fights over specific projects. Regulated utilities — electric, gas, and water providers operating as government-sanctioned monopolies within a service territory — are typically permitted to earn a regulated rate of return on their "rate base," the total value of capital infrastructure they have built and maintain, recovered through the rates charged to current customers. When a utility invests in grid modernization, storm hardening, or the kind of transformer and interconnection capacity Chapter 1 described as increasingly scarce, that investment is added to the rate base and recovered, over years or decades, through every subsequent ratepayer's bill — including ratepayers who never asked for the specific investment, who may not benefit from it as directly as a large data center customer whose demand actually necessitated it, and who, in many documented rate cases before state public utility commissions, have raised exactly this objection: why should an ordinary residential customer's bill rise to fund grid capacity built primarily to serve a handful of enormous new industrial or data-center loads.

Consumer advocates appearing before state public utility commissions have raised versions of this exact objection in rate case after rate case, arguing that residential customers should not be asked to subsidize grid capacity whose primary beneficiary is a small number of enormous, highly profitable commercial and industrial customers. This dynamic has become an active, contested subject in utility regulatory proceedings across multiple states through the mid-2020s, as data center electricity demand has surged: some state regulators have begun requiring or considering special rate classes and cost-allocation rules specifically to ensure that large new industrial loads pay a cost-reflective share of the grid investment their own demand necessitates, rather than allowing that cost to be socialized across the entire residential ratepayer base by default under conventional rate-base accounting rules that predate the current scale of data-center demand entirely. The debate is, in miniature, this chapter's entire argument playing out inside a regulatory hearing room: an installed system's upgrade cost has to land somewhere, the traditional accounting rules governing where it lands were not written with the current situation in mind, and the parties best positioned to argue for a favorable allocation are not always the parties who can least afford an unfavorable one.

## Congestion pricing as the mirror image

It is worth examining a case that runs in the opposite distributional direction from Detroit's water crisis, because it clarifies that this chapter's pattern is not simply "poor people always pay." New York City's congestion pricing program, which began charging most vehicles a fee to enter Manhattan's central business district starting in January 2025 after years of legal and political delay, was explicitly designed to fund transit infrastructure improvements — subway and bus system upgrades — using toll revenue collected disproportionately from drivers, who skew toward higher incomes than the transit-riding population the revenue is meant to benefit. The policy inverts Detroit's pattern: rather than a shrinking, low-income population bearing a fixed infrastructure cost inherited from a wealthier past, congestion pricing deliberately routes a cost toward a wealthier, car-owning population to fund infrastructure serving a broader, more economically diverse ridership.

The comparison matters a great deal to this chapter's larger argument, because it demonstrates clearly that "who pays" is not simply an inevitable, mechanical, unavoidable outcome dictated by an old system's underlying engineering or its original financing structure. It is a genuine, contestable policy choice, made deliberately by specific institutions holding specific legal authority to allocate cost differently than a purely mechanical rate-base or franchise-agreement default would produce on its own. Detroit's water department, operating under municipal bankruptcy pressure and traditional utility-debt-service accounting, made one such choice, largely by default rather than deliberate design. New York's transit authorities, backed by state legislation specifically designed to redirect cost allocation, made a different one, quite deliberately. The existence of a genuine choice, visible once these two cases sit side by side, is this chapter's central claim: nothing about an installed system's inherited cost structure requires it to land on whoever has the least power to object, even though that is where it lands by default with striking, uncomfortable consistency across the cases this book has actually documented.

## Who has the standing to renegotiate the default

The pattern connecting Detroit, the utility rate base, and New York's deliberate reversal points to a specific, practical question worth carrying into this book's final chapters: what determines whether a cost allocation gets renegotiated away from its historical default, the way New York's transit funding was, versus persisting largely unchallenged, the way Detroit's water-debt allocation did for years before drawing sustained national attention. The recurring factor across this book's cases is institutional standing — organized advocacy, legal representation, media attention, and political leverage sufficient to force the question of allocation onto an agenda that would otherwise simply apply the existing rule by default.

The pandemic offered an unplanned, involuntary test of how quickly this default could actually change once sufficient political will existed: Detroit and many other cities suspended water shutoffs entirely during the COVID-19 emergency, on the straightforward public-health logic that a household without running water could not reliably wash hands or maintain basic hygiene during an infectious-disease crisis — a reversal of years of prior policy accomplished within weeks once the argument for change reached the right decision-makers with the right urgency attached to it, a speed that stands in some tension with how slowly the underlying affordability problem itself had moved through the political system in the years beforehand. Detroit's water shutoffs eventually drew exactly this kind of sustained attention — from the ACLU of Michigan, from United Nations human-rights observers who took the unusual step of characterizing the shutoffs as a violation of the human right to water, from academic researchers who quantified the affordability gap in terms regulators and journalists could not easily dismiss — and that attention did produce real, if partial and contested, policy changes in subsequent years, including expanded low-income water assistance programs. But the years between the original 2014 shutoffs and any meaningful policy response illustrate a cost this chapter has not yet named directly: the lag between an inherited cost landing on a population with limited standing to contest it, and that population successfully organizing enough institutional attention to force a renegotiation, is itself a real cost, borne disproportionately, once again, by exactly the population least equipped to absorb the wait.

## The flood policy that subsidizes yesterday's building code

The National Flood Insurance Program offers a fourth version of this chapter's pattern, distinct from Detroit's shrinking-population problem and closer, in structure, to the utility rate-base case, but worth its own examination because the subsidy involved is unusually explicit and long-lived. For decades, the federally backed program that provides flood insurance to homeowners in flood-prone areas — coverage most private insurers had judged too risky to offer at an affordable price — has charged many "pre-FIRM" properties, built before their community's first Flood Insurance Rate Map was issued, premiums set well below the actuarial cost of the risk they actually represent, on the reasoning that homeowners who built or bought before flood maps existed could not reasonably have priced that risk into their original purchase decision.

Congress created the program in 1968 specifically because private insurers had largely withdrawn from flood coverage as a viable line of business, judging the risk too concentrated and too catastrophic to price profitably through ordinary actuarial methods, leaving flood-prone communities with essentially no private insurance option until the federal government stepped in to fill the gap. The subsidy has persisted for decades past its original justification for a specific, structural reason directly relevant to this book: the program's own finances depend on a broad base of premium-paying policyholders, including newer, more accurately priced policies, to keep the overall system solvent, which means every attempt to bring pre-FIRM premiums closer to actuarial reality — including the 2021 "Risk Rating 2.0" methodology overhaul — has triggered significant political resistance from long-tenured homeowners facing dramatic premium increases on properties they may have owned for decades under the old, subsidized pricing. The program's cumulative debt, run up substantially after major hurricanes including Katrina and Sandy required benefit payouts the accumulated premium base could not cover, has itself become a federal fiscal liability ultimately backstopped by general taxpayers — meaning the specific historical accident of when a given house was built, relative to when its community's flood map was drawn, continues determining who pays what share of flood risk decades after the map itself was published, with the gap increasingly filled by the broader taxpaying public rather than by the specific property owners whose homes actually sit in the floodplain.

## The special assessment and the neighbor who cannot afford it

A smaller-scale, more geographically immediate version of this chapter's pattern appears in the special assessment district, a common municipal financing tool through which a city funds a localized infrastructure upgrade — new sidewalks, sewer line replacement, street repaving — by billing the specific property owners within the benefiting district directly, rather than spreading the cost across the general municipal tax base. The logic has real appeal: a sidewalk repair primarily benefits the households on that specific street, so billing those households directly, rather than the entire city's taxpayers, aligns cost with benefit more precisely than a general tax increase would.

The mechanism breaks down badly, in ways municipal finance researchers and legal aid organizations working directly with affected homeowners have documented repeatedly over many years, when a mandatory infrastructure upgrade lands on a block with a mix of longtime, often lower-income or elderly homeowners and newer, wealthier arrivals, because a special assessment bill of several thousand dollars, due within a fixed and often short repayment window, can represent a minor inconvenience to one household on a given street and a genuine, occasionally home-threatening financial emergency to a neighbor two doors down, even though both households are being billed for functionally the same sidewalk. Some more forward-thinking jurisdictions have built in income-based deferral, hardship exemption, or partial forgiveness programs specifically to soften this exact mismatch; many others, working from older or more rigid statutory frameworks, simply have not, and the resulting foreclosures — rare in absolute number, but real and well documented in local reporting from cities including Detroit itself, where unpaid special assessments have in some cases contributed to tax foreclosure proceedings against long-tenured homeowners — represent this chapter's pattern operating at its smallest, most granular, most individually consequential scale: a single sidewalk, a single unlucky bill, a single household with no realistic path to pay it on the timeline the city's financing mechanism demanded.

## A practical test for reading any cost-allocation fight

Four cases — Detroit's water debt, utility rate-base accounting, New York's congestion pricing, and federal flood insurance — suggest a short, practical test worth applying to any installed-world upgrade this book's readers encounter outside its pages. First, identify who actually benefits from the system continuing to operate or being upgraded, distinguishing the immediate, visible beneficiary from the broader, more diffuse one; a data center benefits immediately and specifically from new grid capacity, while a residential ratepayer benefits only indirectly, if at all, from that same investment. Second, identify who is legally or contractually obligated to pay under the existing default rule, which is very often a different party entirely, fixed by an accounting convention, franchise agreement, or subsidy formula written years or decades before the current beneficiary population existed in its present form. Third, ask whether anyone currently has the institutional standing — legal representation, organized advocacy, media attention, or formal regulatory process — to actually contest that default allocation, because a mismatch between beneficiary and payer that nobody has the standing to contest tends, on the evidence this chapter has assembled, to simply persist indefinitely rather than resolve itself through some natural process of fairness reasserting itself over time.

This test will not, and cannot reasonably be expected to, settle every case cleanly — Detroit's own crisis remained genuinely contested for years even after gaining national attention, and flood insurance reform remains a live, unresolved fight in Congress as this book is being written. But it at least directs attention toward the actual mechanism this chapter has tried to isolate, rather than toward the vaguer, less useful question of whether an infrastructure cost is simply "too high," a framing that obscures far more than it reveals about who set the price, who is paying it, and whether either fact was ever actually decided by anyone accountable for the outcome.

The next chapter widens this question from who bears an infrastructure cost individually to a related, geographically concentrated version of the same problem: what happens to an entire neighborhood, town, or region when the installed system running through it changes, whether or not any individual resident asked for the change at all.
