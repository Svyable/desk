# The Socket in the Wall

Every hotel room in the world contains a small, involuntary museum of the nineteenth century's unresolved arguments. A traveler moving from Tokyo to Berlin to Mumbai to New York carries a bag of adapters not because any single plug design has ever failed at its job, but because roughly fifteen distinct plug and socket standards remain in active use across the world's electrical systems, alongside at least two incompatible voltage regimes — broadly 100 to 127 volts in North America, much of Central America, and parts of Asia, against 220 to 240 volts across most of Europe, Africa, Asia, and Australia — and two incompatible frequencies, 60 hertz against 50 hertz, layered on top of both. None of this reflects a shortage of good engineering. It reflects the fact that electrification happened country by country, sometimes city by city, across roughly a century, with each jurisdiction's choices locking in before anyone had a serious opportunity to coordinate with its neighbors, and locking in hard enough that no later coordination effort has ever been worth its cost.

This chapter is about that specific, physical kind of lock-in — not the software and consumer-electronics standards Chapter 2 already covered, but the physical standards that plug directly into infrastructure, where the installed base is not merely millions of trained users or saved files but millions of buildings, meters, transformers, and appliances built to one exact physical and electrical specification. Physical standards behave like the network-effect standards Chapter 2 described, with one important difference: the cost of being wrong, or of switching later, is denominated in concrete and copper rather than in retraining or file conversion, which makes the stakes of getting a physical standard right the first time considerably higher, and the cost of correcting a bad one considerably longer to pay down.

## The war that decided which wire reaches your house

The clearest founding case is the so-called War of Currents, fought in the United States between roughly 1886 and the early 1890s. Thomas Edison had built his early electrical distribution systems around direct current, or DC, which could not easily be transformed to higher or lower voltages and therefore required generating stations within about a mile of the customers they served — a genuinely local model of electrification. George Westinghouse, licensing patents developed by Nikola Tesla and others, promoted alternating current, or AC, which could be generated at high voltage, transmitted efficiently over long distances, and then stepped down by transformers for safe local use — a model that could serve customers dozens or hundreds of miles from the generating station.

Edison, whose commercial position depended on the DC standard he had already built, fought the alternating-current alternative aggressively, including a well-documented public campaign emphasizing AC's dangers, publicity stunts electrocuting animals with alternating current, and lobbying in favor of using AC generators for the first electric chair executions specifically to associate the rival technology with death in the public mind. The campaign did not work. AC's ability to transmit power efficiently over distance made it dramatically cheaper to serve a large, dispersed population, and by the 1890s — decisively demonstrated by the Niagara Falls hydroelectric project, which used alternating current to send power more than twenty miles to Buffalo, New York, a distance that made no commercial sense under a direct-current model — AC had won the standard-setting battle for good. Every electrical outlet in a modern American home descends from that specific late-nineteenth-century decision, made under commercial and reputational combat rather than through any centralized standards process, and every appliance manufactured for the American market since has had to conform to it.

By 1893, Westinghouse had also won the contract to light the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago using alternating current, a highly visible public demonstration that reached tens of millions of fairgoers and did more to normalize AC in the public imagination than any technical paper could have. The same year, Westinghouse secured the contract to build the generators at Niagara Falls. Edison's own company, meanwhile, had already begun quietly incorporating AC technology into its later designs even while publicly campaigning against it, and General Electric — formed in 1892 from a merger that included Edison's electrical businesses — went on to become one of the dominant manufacturers of AC equipment within a decade of the war it had ostensibly lost. The commercial argument, in other words, settled faster and more completely than the personal one; Edison's public opposition to alternating current outlasted his own company's practical embrace of it.

The relevant lesson for this book is not that the better technology won, although in this case it arguably did. It is that the standard that won did so during a narrow historical window — before millions of buildings had been wired, before hundreds of millions of appliances had been manufactured to a specification, before an entire literate public had learned which outlet shape was safe to touch. Once that window closed, changing the underlying standard became close to unthinkable, not because AC and DC's relative merits had changed, but because the installed base behind whichever standard won had grown too large to coordinate a switch away from.

## Fifteen plug shapes and no incentive to converge

The world's proliferation of incompatible plug shapes illustrates what happens when that narrow historical window closes independently in dozens of jurisdictions rather than once, globally. The United Kingdom's distinctive rectangular-pin BS 1363 plug, standardized in the years after World War II, includes a built-in fuse in every plug — a design choice reflecting Britain's postwar ring-circuit wiring topology, in which multiple outlets share a single circuit protected by a higher-rated breaker than any individual appliance should see, making a fuse in the plug itself, rather than only at the panel, a genuine safety necessity under that specific wiring architecture. Continental Europe's round two-pin Europlug and its grounded CEE 7 variants reflect a different, radial wiring tradition, where each outlet or small group of outlets has its own dedicated circuit breaker, making an in-plug fuse redundant. North America's flat-bladed NEMA plugs reflect still another century-old design lineage. None of these differences is arbitrary in the sense of being random; each reflects a real historical wiring decision made for real reasons, at a real moment, by a real national standards body — but no two systems reflect the *same* decision.

Once each system reached its own installed base of tens of millions of buildings, outlets, and appliances, the incentive to unify collapsed for a straightforward economic reason: the entire benefit of a single global electrical standard would be captured mainly by future construction and future appliance manufacturing, while the entire cost of converting the existing stock would fall on every household, landlord, and electrician currently operating under the standard being replaced. No single country's government has judged that trade worthwhile, even though international standards bodies including the International Electrotechnical Commission have periodically proposed harmonized designs since as early as the 1930s. The socket in the wall is, in this sense, one of the purest illustrations available of an installed base large enough, and distributed across enough independent political jurisdictions, that coordinated replacement is not merely difficult. It has never once, in nearly a century of on-and-off international discussion, been seriously attempted at scale.

## A connector that converged in under three years

Set against that century of stasis, the recent standardization of electric-vehicle charging connectors in North America is worth examining closely, because it shows the opposite outcome — genuine, fast convergence — under conditions that, on paper, looked at least as difficult to coordinate as a household electrical socket.

Through the early 2020s, the North American EV charging market was split between Tesla's proprietary connector, used exclusively across Tesla's own extensive Supercharger network, and the Combined Charging System, or CCS, connector used by essentially every other automaker and by the publicly funded charging networks — including Electrify America and ChargePoint — that were being built out with federal infrastructure funding. Then, in November 2023, Ford became the first major non-Tesla automaker to announce it would adopt Tesla's connector, rebranded the North American Charging Standard, or NACS, giving Ford customers access to Tesla's much larger and, by most public reliability surveys of the period, better-maintained Supercharger network. General Motors, Rivian, Honda, Nissan, Toyota, Hyundai, Kia, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, and most of the rest of the industry announced their own NACS adoption within the following twelve months. In August 2024, SAE International formally standardized the connector as SAE J3400, converting what had begun as one manufacturer's proprietary design into an open, published technical standard that any manufacturer could build to without a bilateral licensing negotiation with Tesla. By 2025, the Joint Office of Energy and Transportation — the federal body coordinating national charging infrastructure policy — was treating J3400 as the default connector specification for federally funded charging stations going forward, and networks including Electrify America and ChargePoint had begun adding NACS connectors alongside their existing CCS hardware. Stellantis, the last major holdout, announced plans to begin adopting NACS with select 2025 models, later clarifying a broader shift for its 2026 model-year electric vehicles.

The entire convergence, from Ford's initial announcement to near-universal industry adoption and formal standardization, took roughly two years — a timeline that would have seemed implausible applied to household electrical sockets, where no comparable convergence has occurred in a century of trying. The difference is instructive. EV charging connectors, in 2023, sat on a much smaller and much younger installed base than household electrical sockets: millions of vehicles and thousands of charging stations, built over roughly a decade, rather than billions of outlets and appliances built over a century. Automakers other than Tesla had a clear, immediate, competitively urgent reason to switch — access to a charging network their own customers were visibly choosing Tesla over in cross-shopping comparisons — while consumers switching a household outlet standard would gain nothing individually distinguishable from what they already have. And critically, adapters existed and were manufactured at scale during the transition, letting vehicles built to the older CCS standard continue charging at NACS-equipped stations and vice versa, which meant the installed base of vehicles already on the road did not have to be stranded the moment the new standard was announced — an option Chapter 11 examines directly as one of the most important tools for managing exactly this kind of transition.

## The voluntary standard that failed first

The North American charging connector's fast convergence looks even more striking set against a closely related case that failed for over a decade before a mandate finally succeeded where cooperation had not: the European Union's long fight over mobile phone chargers, which this book's earlier chapter on installed bases touched only briefly and which deserves its full history here, because the sequence — voluntary agreement, quiet failure, eventual mandate — is a pattern this book returns to in Chapter 17's direct treatment of when governments should simply pick a winner.

In June 2009, following pressure from the European Commission, fourteen major mobile phone manufacturers — including Apple, Nokia, Samsung, and most of the rest of the industry that mattered at the time — signed a voluntary memorandum of understanding committing to harmonize chargers for data-enabled phones sold in the EU around a common specification based on the USB 2.0 micro-B connector. It was, on its face, exactly the kind of industry self-regulation policymakers prefer: no legislation required, broad voluntary buy-in, a clear technical target. The European Commission's own later assessment concluded it had failed. The agreement's central weakness was that it permitted manufacturers to comply using an adapter rather than a native connector, which let a company continue shipping its own proprietary port — Apple's 30-pin connector, and later its Lightning connector, chief among them — while technically satisfying the letter of the MoU by including or making available an adapter cable. A 2014 compliance survey found that 99 percent of smartphones sold in the EU were technically covered by a compatible charging solution, a number that looked like unambiguous success until the fine print revealed it counted Apple as compliant purely because of the bundled adapter, not because the device itself used the shared connector the memorandum had been written to establish. The MoU expired in 2014. The industry proposed a follow-up voluntary agreement in 2018; the European Commission rejected it, noting that a fast-emerging alternative — USB-C — had by then made a voluntary process pointed at the older micro-USB standard obsolete before it could even be renewed.

What finally worked was not a better voluntary agreement. It was legislation that closed the specific loophole the 2009 MoU had left open: a legally binding requirement that covered devices use USB-C natively, not merely offer an adapter that technically bridged to it, finalized in 2022 and effective from December 28, 2024 for phones, tablets, cameras, and a wide list of other electronics, with laptops added from April 28, 2026. The fifteen years separating the failed 2009 memorandum from the binding 2022 regulation are worth sitting with, because they show something the North American charging-connector story, for all its speed, does not: sometimes an installed base does not converge through voluntary industry cooperation no matter how much time is given for it to happen, because at least one major manufacturer retains a durable competitive reason — proprietary accessory sales, ecosystem lock-in, differentiated hardware margins — to avoid genuine convergence for as long as a loophole allows it. Ford had a competitive reason to adopt NACS quickly: access to a charging network its customers already preferred. No comparably strong competitive reason ever emerged to make Apple abandon Lightning voluntarily, and none ever would have, absent a rule that closed the adapter loophole outright.

## The cost nobody chose to pay twice

One more feature of the household-voltage story deserves attention before this chapter turns to what makes a standard worth fighting over, because it shows how an early, seemingly minor engineering choice can impose a permanent efficiency cost that nobody today would choose deliberately, yet nobody has found worth the price of correcting.

North America's roughly 120-volt residential standard, inherited from the low-voltage DC systems Edison built before AC won the broader war, requires appliances to draw roughly twice the current that an equivalent-wattage appliance would draw on the 230-volt standard common across Europe and much of the rest of the world, to deliver the same power. Higher current, for a fixed wattage, means thicker wiring is needed to keep resistive losses and heating within safe limits, which is one reason branch-circuit wiring in American homes tends to be heavier gauge, for equivalent loads, than in countries built around a higher residential voltage — a real, if modest, additional material cost embedded in essentially every building built to the American standard, compounding across tens of millions of structures. It is also part of why some especially power-hungry American appliances, clothes dryers and electric ranges chief among them, are wired to a dedicated 240-volt circuit rather than the standard 120-volt outlets used almost everywhere else in an American home — a workaround that lets those specific appliances behave more like their European counterparts, at the cost of a second, incompatible outlet standard living inside the very same house.

None of this is catastrophic. It is exactly the kind of installed-world cost this book asks readers to notice rather than ignore: small, real, permanent, invisible to almost everyone paying it, and utterly uneconomic to fix retroactively across an entire continent's existing building stock, however clean the case for a different original choice might look in hindsight to an electrical engineer starting from a blank sheet of paper today.

## The connector standard nobody has managed to fix

USB itself, the connector this chapter has already followed through the EU's charger mandate, carries its own smaller installed-base story worth a brief final look, because it shows the same convergence-versus-calcification dynamic playing out over a shorter, more recent timeframe than the household electrical outlet's century-long stalemate. Early USB standards proliferated into a genuinely confusing family of connector shapes — USB-A, USB-B, Mini-USB, Micro-USB, and eventually USB-C — each solving a real engineering problem the previous shape had not adequately addressed, but each also adding one more incompatible physical connector to an already crowded consumer landscape of cables, chargers, and devices. USB-C, introduced commercially around 2014 and 2015, was explicitly engineered to finally end this proliferation: reversible, capable of carrying substantially more power and data than its predecessors, and designed from the outset as a genuine universal replacement rather than merely another entry in the family.

A decade after USB-C's introduction, the older Micro-USB connector still ships on a meaningful share of budget electronics, inexpensive peripherals, and older device stock still working through its natural retirement, an installed base large and cheap enough that fully displacing it, even with a technically superior and now legally mandated successor, has taken the better part of a decade and, per Chapter 5's earlier discussion of the EU directive's own multi-year compliance runway, is still not fully complete even where the underlying regulation has already taken legal effect. USB's own family history is, in miniature, this chapter's entire argument: even a standard specifically engineered by its own creators to solve the fragmentation problem definitively still has to overcome the installed base its own predecessors left behind, on a timeline measured in years rather than announcements.

## What makes a standard worth fighting over

Comparing the household outlet's century of stasis with the charging connector's two-year convergence yields a reasonably precise answer to a question this chapter has been building toward: a physical standard converges quickly when its installed base is still young and small relative to the population it will eventually need to serve, when a clear competitive or safety incentive gives at least one major party a strong individual reason to move first, and when a bridging technology — an adapter, in the charging case — exists to prevent the existing installed base from being stranded during the transition. A physical standard calcifies, by contrast, once its installed base has grown large enough that the primary beneficiaries of convergence are future users rather than current ones, once no single actor has enough at stake to absorb the coordination cost of moving first, and once the technical or safety case for the incumbent standard, however historically contingent its origin, has become genuinely intertwined with the wiring and appliances already built around it.

It is worth noticing, too, that both convergence and calcification can coexist inside the very same technology at the very same moment. NACS converged in North America within two years; the same charging standards fragmentation persists, largely untouched by the American story, across Europe and China, each of which had already built out large, separately standardized charging networks — the Combined Charging System variant CCS2 in Europe, GB/T in China — before Tesla's American connector had any chance to become the obvious default there too. A standard's fate is never decided globally in one motion. It is decided market by market, installed base by installed base, and a reader tracking any single standard's progress needs to ask the convergence question separately for every jurisdiction where an independent installed base has already had the chance to form.

Every standards fight examined in the rest of this book — the railroad gauge in Chapter 6, the mainframe transaction format in Chapter 7, the induction range in Chapter 17 — sits somewhere on the spectrum between these two poles, and one useful discipline this chapter leaves behind is a habit of asking, for any standard someone proposes to change: how old and how large is the installed base actually being asked to move, who benefits enough to go first, and does a bridge exist, or need to be built, to keep everyone else connected while it does. Those three questions will not settle every argument about a plug, a gauge, or a data format on their own, but they at least separate the standards genuinely worth fighting over from the ones that were only ever going to calcify no matter how the fight went.
