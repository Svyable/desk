# Design for the World That Exists

In March 2007, Safaricom, then and now Kenya's dominant mobile network operator, launched a modest new service called M-Pesa — "M" standing simply for mobile, "pesa" the ordinary Swahili word for money — built to let Kenyans send and receive cash using nothing more than an ordinary mobile phone. The service's design choices, in hindsight, look almost too obvious to count as innovation at all: it ran over SMS text messages and USSD codes, the same basic signaling protocols every mobile phone sold in Kenya already supported, including even the cheapest, most basic feature phones that made up the very overwhelming majority of the entire country's mobile handsets at that particular time. It required no smartphone, no app, no data plan, no internet connection, and, crucially, no formal bank account of any kind — only an ordinary phone, a national identification card, and reasonably convenient access to one of the small shopkeepers and kiosk owners Safaricom recruited as cash-in, cash-out agents, deliberately embedding the entirely new digital financial service directly into the ordinary physical retail infrastructure Kenyans already used every day to buy phone airtime, bread, and cooking oil.

The results that followed launch were fast enough, and large enough, to genuinely surprise even Safaricom's own senior executives, who had modeled a considerably slower initial adoption curve. Daily new registrations exceeded 5,000 by August 2007, five months after launch, and approached 10,000 a day by December of that same year. Within a few years, M-Pesa had become the dominant channel through which a large share of Kenya's population — a population where formal bank branch access remained sparse, particularly outside major cities, and where a majority of adults had never held a conventional bank account — moved money, paid bills, and saved. Subsequent academic research, including a widely cited and frequently referenced National Bureau of Economic Research working paper specifically studying M-Pesa's underlying economics in detail, documented in careful empirical detail the service's rapid, genuinely broad-based adoption across income levels and its measurable, statistically significant effects on financial inclusion and household resilience during periods of economic shock, effects researchers attributed directly to the service's low barrier to entry relative to conventional banking infrastructure that would have taken decades to build out physically across Kenya's more rural regions.

Safaricom itself, in later public accounts of the service's early development, described the agent-network design as a deliberate, calculated response to Kenya's specific banking landscape at the time — a landscape marked by far too few physical bank branches to adequately serve a still-largely-rural national population, but paired with an unusually dense, already-existing network of small kiosks and neighborhood shops already trusted by ordinary Kenyans, every single day, for everyday cash transactions of every kind, infrastructure the company recognized it could repurpose rather than needing to build from nothing. This chapter's central argument is that M-Pesa's remarkable success was not primarily a story about mobile money as an abstract technological concept, which several earlier, less successful mobile payment ventures elsewhere in the world had also attempted. It was a story about installed-world discipline applied with unusual rigor: Safaricom built a genuinely new financial product by deliberately refusing to require any new hardware, any new network infrastructure, or any new consumer behavior beyond what an ordinary Kenyan mobile subscriber and an ordinary Kenyan small shopkeeper already possessed and already did every day. The service did not wait for smartphones to become affordable, for bank branches to expand into rural areas, or for a generation of Kenyans to become comfortable with digital interfaces they had never encountered before. It designed itself to fit precisely inside the installed world that already existed, at the exact moment it launched, rather than the installed world its designers might have hoped would exist in five or ten years.

## The lesson this book has been building toward

Every chapter in this book's third and fourth parts has, in one way or another, documented what happens when a system's designers fail to take this discipline seriously: TSB's migration collapsed partly because its designers underestimated how deeply the old Lloyds system's specific behavior had become embedded in customer expectations. NPfIT unraveled because its central planners imagined a single uniform clinical workflow that did not actually match the hundreds of genuinely different local workflows already installed across England's hospitals. The empty-site fallacy that opened this book describes exactly the same error at the level of physical infrastructure: treating a site, a market, or a user base as a blank slate rather than as a place already filled with specific, particular, load-bearing commitments a new system has to actually reckon with.

M-Pesa's design offers this book's clearest positive counterexample, and it is worth stating the underlying principle in its most general form before this chapter moves through its remaining cases: a new system spreads fastest not when it is most technically advanced in the abstract, but when it minimizes the distance between what it requires and what its intended users already have, already know, and already do. This is not a claim that ambition or genuine technical advancement should be abandoned. M-Pesa was, by any reasonable measure, a genuinely novel and technically sophisticated system, combining telecommunications infrastructure, cryptographic security, and an entirely new agent-network business model nobody had assembled in quite that combination before. The novelty lived in the business model and the financial product. The interface, deliberately, lived entirely inside the installed world Kenyan mobile users already inhabited.

## The library card that predicted the streaming subscription

Netflix's own early history offers a second, very different illustration of the identical discipline, worth including because it shows the same principle operating inside a wealthy country's already-dense infrastructure rather than a developing economy's comparatively sparse one. When Netflix launched its DVD-by-mail rental service in 1997, streaming video over the internet was not a viable consumer technology — home broadband penetration was minimal, and the video compression and bandwidth required to stream a feature-length film reliably would not become practical for the mass market for roughly another decade. Netflix's own founders could, in principle, have simply waited several more years for that streaming infrastructure to mature naturally before launching any consumer product at all. Instead, the company built its entire initial business model around infrastructure that had existed, largely unchanged, for well over a century: the United States Postal Service's first-class mail network, reaching essentially every American household daily, at a cost per delivery low enough to make mailing a lightweight DVD, tucked inside its now-famous distinctive red envelope, genuinely economically viable at ordinary consumer subscription price points.

Netflix's DVD-by-mail service was not a stopgap the company grudgingly tolerated while waiting for streaming technology to arrive; it was, for the better part of a decade, the company's entire business, and it succeeded specifically because it required nothing from its subscribers beyond what every American household already had: a mailbox, a postal carrier, and a willingness to plan movie-watching a few days in advance rather than instantly. When broadband infrastructure and video compression technology matured enough, around 2007, to make streaming genuinely practical, Netflix transitioned deliberately and gradually, maintaining its DVD service for well over a decade afterward — into the 2020s, in fact, before finally discontinuing physical discs in 2023 — precisely because a meaningful share of its subscriber base, particularly in areas with slower internet infrastructure, still depended on the postal-based version of the service the company had originally built to fit the installed world of 1997, not the installed world Netflix's founders may have hoped would exist a decade later.

## The website that still renders on a decade-old browser

Web design offers a third, more technical illustration of the same discipline, formalized into an explicit engineering principle rather than left implicit in a founder's business instinct. Web standards bodies, individual browser developers, and the broader community of professional web engineers have, for several decades now, operated under a widely shared design philosophy often summarized as "graceful degradation" or, in its more ambitious form, "progressive enhancement": a website should be built so its essential content and functionality remain accessible even to an older browser, a slower connection, or a device lacking support for the newest web technologies, with modern visual polish and interactive features layered on top as genuine enhancements for users whose equipment can support them, rather than as requirements that lock older or lower-capability users out entirely.

Major technology companies including Google have published extensive public engineering guidance built around this exact philosophy, explicitly instructing developers to test new features against older browser versions and slower network connections before shipping, treating that testing discipline as a core, non-negotiable part of responsible web development rather than an optional accessibility nicety. This principle echoes Chapter 10's account of color television's backward-compatible signal design directly, and it is worth drawing the connection explicitly because the underlying engineering logic is identical across a sixty-year gap and a complete change of medium: a system designed with graceful degradation in mind treats its own installed base of existing users, on existing equipment, as a genuine design constraint to be respected rather than an inconvenience to be assumed away, and in doing so avoids exactly the kind of last-ten-percent exclusion Chapter 14 documented across AMPS, EMV, and unsupported medical device software. Major government and public-service websites, in particular, have increasingly adopted formal accessibility and backward-compatibility standards specifically because a public agency, unlike a private company optimizing for its most profitable customer segment, cannot simply write off the citizens still using older technology as an acceptable loss.

## Why this discipline is harder than it sounds

It would understate the real difficulty of this chapter's central lesson to present it as simply obvious good practice that careless designers merely forget to apply. Designing deliberately for the installed world that exists carries a genuine, often underappreciated cost of its own: it constrains what a system can do, sometimes significantly, relative to what it could accomplish if freed entirely from legacy compatibility requirements. A streaming-only Netflix launched in 1997 would have been technically impossible; a DVD-by-mail Netflix necessarily could not offer instant access, one of the features that eventually made streaming so much more valuable once the infrastructure caught up. A graceful-degradation website necessarily cannot use every cutting-edge browser feature its most technically sophisticated users' devices could support, because doing so would break the experience for users on older equipment the design is trying to accommodate.

This is precisely the tension Chapter 13 examined from a different angle: an old system's persistence is not always simple stubbornness, and a new system's compatibility with that old system is not always cost-free caution. Every case in this chapter made a real, calculated tradeoff, sacrificing some technical ambition in exchange for genuinely broader, faster adoption — and the discipline this chapter is actually recommending is not "always design for the lowest common denominator forever," but rather the more specific, more demanding judgment call Chapter 5's NACS-versus-household-outlet comparison already modeled: correctly reading how large, how entrenched, and how essential the installed base you are designing around actually is, and calibrating the compatibility investment to match that reality rather than either ignoring it or over-indexing on it indefinitely past the point where it still serves the system's actual users.

## The counterexamples worth taking seriously

Fairness to this chapter's argument requires acknowledging genuine counterexamples — cases where a system succeeded precisely by refusing to accommodate the installed world at all, betting instead that users would adapt to something genuinely new rather than requiring the new thing to adapt to them. The original Apple iPhone, launched in 2007, deliberately abandoned the physical keyboard that had defined smartphone design until that point, a design choice contemporary critics, including several prominent technology executives running rival companies at the time, publicly predicted would fail commercially, specifically because it broke so completely and so abruptly with users' existing physical typing habits and long-standing expectations about how a phone keyboard should feel and behave. The iPhone's touchscreen interface required users to learn an entirely new way of interacting with a phone, with no graceful degradation path back to the physical-keyboard interface millions of BlackBerry and feature-phone users already knew well.

The iPhone succeeded anyway, spectacularly, which complicates any simple version of this chapter's rule. The more careful reading, consistent with the rest of this book's evidence, is not that breaking with the installed world always fails and fitting it always succeeds, but that the calculation depends heavily on how much genuine, felt value the break delivers relative to the switching cost it imposes — the same cost-benefit comparison Chapter 5's NACS case and Chapter 2's QWERTY discussion both turned on. Apple's touchscreen interface delivered large enough gains in capability, most visibly a genuinely usable mobile web browser and, within a year, a functioning third-party app ecosystem, that enough users judged the relearning cost worth paying quickly. M-Pesa and Netflix, by contrast, were entering markets where the switching cost of requiring new hardware or new infrastructure would have foreclosed adoption among the overwhelming majority of their addressable population before the product's value could ever be demonstrated to them at all. Both approaches can genuinely work, given the right underlying conditions, and both have real, well-documented historical failures behind them too. The discipline this chapter recommends is therefore not a blanket rule favoring compatibility over disruption in every single case without exception, but a clear-eyed, honest assessment of which situation a given system actually faces before committing to one strategy or the other.

## When fitting the installed world becomes its own trap

It is worth naming directly a risk this chapter's own argument could otherwise obscure: a design philosophy built entirely around accommodating the installed world, taken too far or held onto too long past its usefulness, can become exactly the kind of adaptation debt this book's sibling volume examines at length — a successful early strategy that calcifies into a constraint on the same system's own later evolution. Netflix's continued operation of its DVD-by-mail service for over a decade after streaming became broadly viable was a genuine, deliberate accommodation of its existing subscriber base's installed reality; it was also, by the company's own later account, an operation that consumed real logistical and financial resources long after streaming had become the company's obvious central business, resources that a less patient competitor might have redirected sooner.

The distinction this chapter's earlier sections have tried to maintain throughout applies here with full force: designing for the installed world that exists is not the same instruction as designing for the installed world that existed five years ago and refusing to notice it has changed. M-Pesa's own designers have continued evolving the service considerably since 2007, adding smartphone app interfaces, integration with formal banking products, and cross-border functionality, as Kenya's own installed base of smartphones, banking infrastructure, and financial literacy has itself matured over nearly two decades. The discipline this chapter recommends is not a one-time design decision made at launch and then defended indefinitely against all subsequent change. It is a standing practice of continually rechecking what the installed world actually looks like now, rather than assuming the installed world a system was originally built to fit remains frozen in the moment of that system's own creation.

## Reading the installed world as a design brief

The practical instruction this chapter leaves behind, distilled from M-Pesa's agent network, Netflix's mailbox, and the web's graceful degradation, is a specific discipline any team designing a new system meant to reach a broad population can actually apply. Before specifying what the new system requires, inventory what its intended users already reliably have: what hardware, what infrastructure, what habits, what trusted intermediaries, what existing trust relationships. Ask which of those existing elements the new system can build directly on top of, converting an installed-world constraint into a genuine distribution advantage, the way Safaricom's agent network turned Kenya's existing informal retail economy into M-Pesa's cash-in and cash-out infrastructure without having to build a single new physical branch. And ask, honestly, which specific new requirements are genuinely unavoidable — a smartphone for an app whose core function cannot exist on a feature phone, a broadband connection for a service whose value depends entirely on speed — versus which requirements are simply the path of least resistance for the system's own designers, imposed on users because building around the installed world would have been more work, not because the installed world made the feature genuinely impossible to deliver.

## What competitors who ignored this lesson learned

A brief look at mobile money ventures that failed where M-Pesa succeeded sharpens this chapter's argument by contrast. Several early mobile banking pilots elsewhere in the developing world through the 2000s required smartphone applications, formal bank account linkage, or agent networks the venture itself had to build from scratch in markets lacking Kenya's specific combination of a dominant, trusted mobile carrier and a dense existing informal retail sector — and several of these efforts struggled to reach anything close to M-Pesa's adoption curve, not primarily because their underlying financial technology was inferior, but because they required a meaningfully larger set of infrastructure and behavioral changes from their intended users before the service could deliver any value at all. Researchers studying comparative mobile money adoption across different developing economies have repeatedly identified this exact factor — how much pre-existing infrastructure and behavior a given service could build directly upon, versus how much it had to construct from nothing — as one of the strongest predictors of which mobile money ventures achieved broad, rapid adoption and which stalled at a small, committed user base regardless of how well the underlying technology performed on its own technical merits.

This book's final chapter turns from designing new systems to fit the installed world toward the harder, more general closing question every case examined across these nineteen chapters has been building toward: what does it actually mean, in practice, for a forecast, a policy, or a product plan to take the installed world seriously as its starting point rather than as an inconvenient afterthought — and what changes, concretely, once it does.
