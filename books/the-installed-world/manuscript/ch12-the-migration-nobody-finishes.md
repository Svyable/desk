# The Migration Nobody Finishes

On the evening of Sunday, April 22, 2018, TSB Bank, a mid-sized British retail bank with roughly 5.2 million customers, switched off its old IT platform — inherited from its former parent company, Lloyds Banking Group — and switched on a new one, called Proteo4UK, built and operated by the technology arm of its Spanish owner, Sabadell. The migration had been planned for years, budgeted carefully, staffed with experienced consultants, and tested, according to TSB's own later public account, extensively and repeatedly before the final go-live decision was made. Within hours of going live, customers began reporting they could not access their accounts. Some who could log in saw other customers' balances and transaction histories displayed instead of their own. TSB's digital banking channels were taken offline entirely as the scale of the failure became clear.

The numbers that followed, compiled by regulators, journalists, and TSB's own subsequent public disclosures, describe one of the most thoroughly documented IT migration disasters in modern banking history. Roughly 1.9 million of TSB's 5.2 million customers were locked out of their accounts. Average phone wait times on the first day reached ninety minutes. By ten days after the migration, TSB had logged 33,101 formal complaints — an increase of 1,156 percent over its pre-migration baseline. Fraud attempts against the bank's now-vulnerable systems peaked, roughly three weeks in, at seventy times normal levels, as criminals recognized a genuine opportunity in the chaos. The problems TSB's own engineers had confidently promised would be resolved within a matter of days instead took eight full months to fully and finally address. The total cost to the bank, once customer compensation, remediation, and lost business were tallied, reached roughly £330 million, and chief executive Paul Pester lost his job over it. In December 2022 — more than four years after the migration itself — Britain's Financial Conduct Authority and Prudential Regulation Authority fined TSB a combined £48.65 million for operational risk management and governance failures connected to the episode, one of the largest such penalties either regulator had issued for an IT failure.

This chapter is about migrations like TSB's, and there is no shortage of them across every industry and every decade this book has examined so far: transitions begun with every reasonable intention of finishing cleanly, on schedule, that instead reveal — usually all at once, usually at the worst possible moment — that the old system's true dependencies were never as fully mapped as the migration plan assumed.

## What the post-mortem actually found

Regulatory investigations into the TSB failure, conducted patiently over the following years by both the Financial Conduct Authority and the Prudential Regulation Authority, ultimately running to hundreds of pages of findings, identified a specific, almost mundane root cause beneath the dramatic customer-facing chaos: TSB had not adequately assessed whether its Spanish technology partner, SABIS, actually had the capability to deliver a migration of this scale and this specific regulatory complexity, because SABIS had never before built or operated a retail banking platform meeting the particular scale, customization requirements, and regulatory obligations that the UK market and TSB's specific customer base required. The core system itself, once live, could not reliably handle the transaction volume and concurrent user load a British high-street bank's customer base actually generated — a mismatch between the tested environment's assumptions and production reality that mirrors, almost exactly, the parallel-run logic Chapter 10 described as the reason careful organizations avoid exactly this kind of single, high-stakes cutover.

TSB had, by most accounts, believed it was executing the kind of careful, well-tested migration this book's earlier chapters described as the responsible approach. The specific failure was not a lack of caution in the abstract. It was an incomplete accounting of what the old Lloyds-inherited system actually did, at the specific scale and complexity TSB's real customer base demanded — precisely the "what has learned to depend on the installed system" question this book has returned to in chapter after chapter, answered incompletely, with consequences that took eight months and hundreds of millions of pounds to fully resolve.

## The program that spent ten years and ten billion pounds finding out

A larger, slower, and in some ways more instructive failure unfolded across the previous decade in a different corner of British public administration: the National Programme for IT, an effort launched by the National Health Service in 2002 to build a single, integrated electronic patient records system connecting hospitals and general practitioners across all of England. Originally budgeted at roughly £6 billion across contracts awarded to major technology firms including Accenture, CSC, Fujitsu, Atos Origin, and BT, the program grew, delayed, and struggled for nearly a decade before the coalition government formally dismantled it in 2011. Final cost estimates vary depending on what is counted, ranging from roughly £10 billion to £12.7 billion against government estimates that the program had generated only around £2.6 billion in realized benefit by the time it was wound down.

The specific mechanism of NPfIT's failure differs from TSB's in almost every particular — a decade-long, incrementally unraveling public-sector program rather than a single catastrophic weekend cutover — but the underlying pattern this chapter is tracking is the same. NPfIT's designers underestimated, repeatedly and across multiple contractor relationships, how deeply each individual NHS trust's existing clinical workflows, record-keeping conventions, and locally customized software had already become embedded in day-to-day patient care, treating what looked from Whitehall like a single, uniform "old system" to be replaced as though it were actually hundreds of subtly different local systems, each with its own dependencies nobody in the central program had fully mapped before contracts were signed and deadlines set. Clinician opposition, which grew steadily across the program's life as frontline staff encountered systems that did not match how their specific hospital or practice actually worked, was not simply resistance to change in the abstract. It was, in case after case documented by subsequent parliamentary inquiries, a symptom of exactly the installed-world mapping failure this book has argued underlies so many transitions that look, from the outside, like simple stubbornness or bureaucratic dysfunction.

## Why the failure always looks avoidable in hindsight

Both TSB and NPfIT share a feature common to nearly every well-documented migration disaster examined by researchers and consultants who study this pattern professionally: the specific dependency that ultimately caused the failure was, in hindsight, discoverable in advance, and often had been flagged, in some internal document or some frontline employee's warning, well before the migration proceeded regardless. TSB's own subsequent regulatory findings noted inadequate due diligence on its technology partner's actual capability — a gap that, in principle, a more thorough pre-migration assessment could have identified. NPfIT's clinician complaints about workflow mismatches began circulating within individual NHS trusts years before the program's eventual dismantlement, documented in trust-level meeting minutes and professional body correspondence that later parliamentary inquiries were able to review in detail.

This "it was foreseeable" pattern is worth treating carefully rather than as simple evidence of institutional negligence, because it recurs with such consistency across migration failures in wildly different industries and countries that a more structural explanation deserves consideration: large organizations undertaking a major systems migration face a genuine, difficult trade-off between the cost of fully mapping every dependency in advance — a process that, for a system as complex as a national health service's clinical workflows or a bank's decades of accumulated transaction logic, can itself take years and cost enormously — and the cost of proceeding on an incomplete map and discovering the gaps during or after cutover. Neither TSB nor the NHS chose incomplete mapping because nobody involved cared about the risk. Both organizations, like most that experience this kind of failure, made a specific, bounded bet about how much dependency-mapping was "enough," and lost that bet in a way that looks, after the fact, like it should have been obvious — a hindsight bias that makes every migration disaster read, in retrospect, as more preventable than it may have actually been in real time, with the information and budget actually available to the people making the decision.

## The migration that never officially ends

A third pattern, distinct from both TSB's sudden collapse and NPfIT's decade-long unraveling, deserves its own attention: the migration that technically completes, in the sense that a project is formally closed and declared finished, while a meaningful share of the old system's actual functionality quietly persists underneath, unofficially, because some specific dependency was judged too costly or too risky to fully migrate before the deadline arrived. Enterprise technology consultants who study large migrations across industries have documented this pattern repeatedly: a project declared "complete" on schedule, while a shrinking but persistent set of legacy processes, spreadsheet workarounds, or parallel manual procedures continues quietly compensating for functionality the new system never actually replicated, sometimes for years after the official go-live date, known to the specific employees who maintain the workaround but invisible to executive dashboards reporting the migration as finished.

Auditors and consultants specializing in post-migration reviews describe encountering this pattern often enough that some have adopted informal internal terminology for it — a "shadow system" or "ghost process" running alongside the officially completed migration, sustained by one or two employees who quietly understood that the new system, whatever the project closure documentation claimed, did not yet do everything the business genuinely needed done. This pattern connects directly back to Chapter 9's discussion of tacit knowledge and Chapter 11's account of adapters that quietly become permanent: an organization's own internal accounting of "we finished the migration" can diverge substantially from the operational reality of what its employees are actually doing to keep the business running, and the gap between the two often only becomes visible when the specific employee maintaining the workaround retires, is reassigned, or leaves — at which point the organization discovers, sometimes years after declaring victory, that the old system was never actually fully retired at all, merely hidden well enough that nobody with the authority to notice had reason to look.

## The country that migrated almost everything

It would be a disservice to this chapter's argument to leave the impression that large-scale government or financial-system migrations are simply doomed by their own complexity, because one small European country offers a genuinely instructive counterexample. Estonia, following its independence in 1991, built its government IT infrastructure largely from scratch through the 1990s and 2000s, without the multi-decade accumulated legacy systems that burdened the NHS, TSB, or the American COBOL-dependent institutions examined in Chapter 7. By the mid-2010s, Estonia had built X-Road, a decentralized data-exchange layer connecting government agencies, banks, and other institutions, underpinning digital services that let citizens file taxes in minutes, access medical records, vote online, and conduct the overwhelming majority of routine government interactions digitally, widely cited by international e-government researchers and organizations including the OECD as among the most successful national digital-government transformations attempted anywhere.

Estonia's success is genuinely instructive, but the comparison to NPfIT and TSB requires an important qualification this book has emphasized before: Estonia was building on a much smaller, much younger installed base than Britain's NHS or a decades-old British retail bank, precisely the empty-site advantage Chapter 1 warned against overestimating in isolation, but real nonetheless at national government scale — a country of roughly 1.3 million people, with limited pre-existing digital infrastructure to migrate away from, was able to design its systems' foundational architecture correctly from an early, relatively uncluttered starting point in a way that a country the size of Britain, with a National Health Service already carrying decades of accumulated, locally divergent legacy systems built up since its 1948 founding, structurally could not replicate simply by wanting to. Estonia's genuine achievement does not disprove this chapter's argument about migration difficulty; it demonstrates, instead, exactly the installed-base-size effect Chapter 2 first introduced, operating at the scale of an entire nation's government rather than a single company's software platform.

## What actually distinguishes a survivable migration

Placing TSB and NPfIT against Chapter 7's Commonwealth Bank of Australia success story yields a more useful lesson than simply "be more careful," because CBA's own migration was not free of serious difficulty — its budget grew by close to ninety percent from original estimates, and its total timeline stretched across more than a decade. The meaningful difference was not the absence of unexpected problems, which every large migration examined in this book has encountered in some form. It was CBA's staged approach, moving major functionality in bounded phases with each phase's success validated before the next began, rather than committing to a single, irreversible cutover the way TSB did, or to a single, centrally designed architecture imposed uniformly across hundreds of genuinely different local sites the way NPfIT attempted.

The staged approach does not eliminate the risk this chapter has documented, and no institution examined anywhere in this book has found a way to make a genuinely large, genuinely old migration entirely safe. It bounds it, converting a single catastrophic failure mode into a series of smaller, more survivable ones, each generating real information about the next phase's actual dependencies before the organization commits further capital and reputation to a plan built on an incomplete map. TSB's next major technology decision, after the 2018 disaster, notably followed a more staged, more cautiously validated path than its predecessor — a lesson the institution appears to have absorbed the expensive and painful way, at a cost of £330 million, a regulatory fine, and a chief executive's career, that this chapter's other cases suggest could plausibly have been learned for a small fraction of that price, had the original dependency-mapping effort simply been funded and staffed to match the actual complexity of what it was attempting to move.

## The unknown unknown, made concrete

It is worth pausing on a specific phrase this chapter has used more than once — the "unknown unknown" dependency — because it names precisely the epistemic problem underlying both TSB's collapse and NPfIT's decade-long unraveling, and it is worth grounding in a concrete example rather than leaving it abstract. Y2K remediation teams, examined in Chapter 7 as evidence that the world chose repair over replacement under maximum pressure, discovered during their own audits a recurring category of problem that maps directly onto this chapter's argument: date fields buried inside decades-old code, sometimes undocumented, sometimes embedded inside third-party libraries or hardware firmware nobody currently on staff had originally written, that no complete inventory of the system had captured before the remediation effort began. Finding these fields required, in many documented cases, painstaking manual code review across millions of lines, precisely because no automated tool or existing document could reliably identify every place a two-digit year might be silently stored and used in a calculation.

TSB's migration and NPfIT's rollout both encountered their own versions of this same category of problem: a dependency that existed, that mattered enormously once exposed, and that no pre-migration audit — however diligent by ordinary industry standards — had successfully surfaced in advance. The uncomfortable implication, consistent with the evidence this chapter has assembled, is that "unknown unknown" dependencies are not primarily a symptom of insufficient effort or insufficient competence. They are a structural feature of any sufficiently old, sufficiently large installed system, whose true complexity has often exceeded what any single institutional memory, however well-maintained, can fully capture in documentation — which is precisely why the staged, incrementally validated migration strategy examined in this chapter's final section functions less as a best practice and more as a genuine risk-management necessity for systems old and large enough to guarantee that some dependency will not have been found in advance, no matter how much time and money the pre-migration audit consumed.

## The insurance industry's own version of the same story

Insurance carriers, running their own decades-old policy administration systems, have experienced migration failures echoing TSB's and NPfIT's closely enough that industry consultants specializing in insurance-technology transitions treat the pattern as its own well-recognized category, distinct from banking migrations only in the specific regulatory and actuarial complexity involved. A policy administration system carries decades of in-force contracts, each with its own coverage terms, rating rules, and claims history that must migrate accurately to a new platform without altering a single policyholder's contractual rights — a requirement at least as unforgiving as a bank's transaction-integrity demands, and one that has produced its own quieter but genuinely costly string of delayed, over-budget, and occasionally abandoned core-system replacement projects across the industry, seldom reaching the same public visibility as TSB's consumer-facing collapse simply because an insurer's quieter back-office migration failure rarely locks an individual policyholder out of their account in the same visible, immediate way that reporters, regulators, and the affected public all tend to notice right away.

## Who bears the cost of finding out

One final pattern deserves attention before this chapter closes, because it recurs across every case examined here and previews an argument this book develops fully in Part Four: the cost of a migration's failure rarely falls evenly on the parties responsible for the decisions that caused it. TSB's £330 million cost and £48.65 million regulatory fine were ultimately borne by the bank's shareholders and, through the compensation program the FCA required, partially offset for affected customers — but the 1.9 million customers locked out of their accounts for days, some unable to pay bills or access funds during that period, bore a real cost of their own, in time, stress, and in some documented cases genuine financial hardship, that no compensation payment fully reversed. NPfIT's failure fell on British taxpayers broadly, through the roughly £10 billion in public funds ultimately written off, while the clinicians whose workflow concerns had gone unheeded for years bore a different cost: continuing, for the length of the program's operation, to work around systems that did not match how their hospitals or practices actually functioned, a burden that showed up as lost clinical time rather than as a line item any government audit fully captured.

This asymmetry — between the institution that decides to migrate and the people who absorb the cost when the migration goes wrong — is not unique to IT projects, and readers of this book's later chapters will recognize it as a specific instance of the "who decided, and who pays" question this book has asked since Chapter 3. A migration's architects rarely bear the same downside their customers, patients, or citizens bear when the unknown unknown finally surfaces, which is itself part of why the incentive to invest adequately in dependency-mapping before a migration begins is often weaker than the eventual cost of skipping it would justify, if that cost were fully internalized by the people making the initial go/no-go decision.

The next chapter now turns to a related but genuinely distinct question, one this chapter's cases have only gestured at in passing: what happens to the assets left behind by a migration, however well or poorly executed — the old system, no longer central, not yet fully decommissioned, and, in a surprising number of cases, still quietly doing real, valuable, and occasionally quite surprising work for the organization that supposedly left it behind years ago.
