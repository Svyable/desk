# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*The Installed World* asks why the arrival of a superior technology so often takes years or decades longer than its engineering readiness would predict, and argues that the dominant constraint is usually not consumer preference, regulatory hostility, or a lack of vision. It is the accumulated physical, technical, and contractual stock already in place — the installed world — that the new system must coexist with, migrate from, route around, or replace.

The working claim:

> **Adoption is constrained less by the quality of a new system in isolation than by the cost of making it coexist with, migrate from, or replace the enormous stock of systems already embedded in physical and institutional life.**

This is a synthesis, not a new economic mechanism. Diffusion-of-innovation research (Rogers), path-dependence and lock-in research (David, Arthur), and network-effects and standards-competition research (Farrell and Saloner, Katz and Shapiro, Liebowitz and Margolis) already describe pieces of the underlying dynamics. The book's job is to hold those pieces together against real, checkable turnover data — vehicle fleet age, building stock age, transformer lead times, ERP migration failure rates, grid interconnection queues — rather than resting on the diffusion curve as metaphor alone.

## What would make the book wrong?

The thesis should weaken if the evidence shows, consistently rather than occasionally:

- Superior new technologies displace installed systems at a pace set mainly by their intrinsic quality, largely independent of turnover cycles, compatibility requirements, transition financing, and distributional politics.
- Installed-base effects are a minor friction easily overcome by sufficient venture financing or policy will, rather than a structural pacing mechanism.
- Historical "slow transition" cases (electrification, container shipping, digital television) turn out on close inspection to have been paced mainly by invention timing rather than by the cost of coexisting with or replacing what already existed.
- Contemporary transitions (EV charging standardization, grid interconnection, heat pump retrofits) proceed at the pace forecasters expected once government funding is applied, without persistent bottlenecks in labor, components, permitting, or financing.

The manuscript actively looks for counter-cases: transitions that moved fast despite a large installed base (container shipping's roughly fifteen-year global takeover; the NACS charging-connector consolidation that took under three years from Ford's first announcement to near-universal automaker adoption), because a framework that only explains slow transitions is not yet a good theory of the mechanism.

## Falsification and counterevidence discipline

Every chapter should preserve at least one piece of real counterevidence: a transition that moved fast, an installed system that deserved to survive, a mandate that backfired, a subsidy that was captured by the wrong party, or a case where "the installed world" is a convenient excuse rather than a real constraint. The Guardrails sections of the original book proposal apply throughout: inertia is not destiny, and legacy infrastructure should not become a universal alibi for delay.

## Evidence lanes

1. **Technology diffusion and installed-base economics** — Rogers's diffusion curve, Arthur's increasing returns, Farrell/Saloner and Katz/Shapiro network-effects models, Liebowitz and Margolis's QWERTY revisionism.
2. **Infrastructure turnover and asset lifetimes** — vehicle fleet age (S&P Global Mobility), bridge and road condition (ASCE Infrastructure Report Card), building stock replacement rates, power-plant retirement schedules (EIA).
3. **Standards, compatibility, and network effects** — the 1886 Southern railway gauge conversion, containerization and ISO 668, the EU common-charger directive, the Tesla NACS/SAE J3400 connector consolidation, IPv6/IPv4 dual-stack transition.
4. **Enterprise and public-sector legacy systems** — COBOL banking infrastructure, GAO federal legacy-IT reports, ERP migration failure and cost-overrun studies.
5. **Energy transition and physical retrofit** — grid interconnection queues (Berkeley Lab/LBNL), transformer and gas-turbine lead times, coal-plant retirement delays, heat-pump and induction-range retrofit economics, brownfield versus greenfield data-center construction.
6. **Policy instruments for replacement** — Cash for Clunkers, the 2009 digital-television transition and converter-box coupon program, the EPA Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, BEAD rural-broadband deployment, premature-scrappage and embodied-carbon research.
