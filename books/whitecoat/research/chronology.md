# Chronology

- 1940s — Camp Detrick becomes a center of U.S. biological-warfare research during World War II.
- 1947 — Nuremberg Code articulates voluntary consent as essential in human experimentation.
- 1954 — Operation Whitecoat begins at Fort Detrick with conscientious-objector medical research volunteers.
- 1958–1968 — later retrospective literature identifies forty tularemia clinical studies conducted under Operation Whitecoat during this interval.
- 1960s — Whitecoat continues infectious-disease challenge, vaccine, treatment, and vector-related research within the Army's defensive medical program.
- 1969 — President Richard Nixon renounces the U.S. offensive biological-weapons program.
- 1973 — Operation Whitecoat ends.
- 1970s — U.S. human-subject protections develop rapidly in the aftermath of public research controversies; Belmont-era ethics framework follows later in the decade.
- 1998 — Robert L. Mole and Dale M. Mole publish *For God and Country: Operation Whitecoat, 1954–1973*, an important participant/institutional history requiring comparison against primary files.
- 2005 — Pittman et al. publish long-term health assessment of former Whitecoat volunteers; no conclusive association between investigational-agent exposure and adverse health outcomes is identified in the studied cohort.
- 2019 — peer-reviewed retrospective analysis reuses Whitecoat pneumonic-tularemia records to examine disease progression and tetracycline efficacy.
