# Contradiction and Incentive Audit

## Army / USAMRIID incentives

The Army had incentives to present Whitecoat as ethical, useful, voluntary, and distinct from offensive biological warfare. Those incentives do not invalidate Army records; they increase the value of locating refusal records, adverse events, abandoned studies, legal reviews, and internal criticism rather than relying only on commemorative histories.

## Seventh-day Adventist institutional incentives

Church leaders had reasons to present noncombatant military service as principled, useful, and patriotic. That could encourage positive accounts of Whitecoat. It could also have made the church an advocate for members inside the Army. Recruitment, pastoral support, publicity, and protocol approval must not be conflated.

## Volunteer incentives

Participants may have had reasons to remember Whitecoat with pride: service identity, religious meaning, community recognition, scientific contribution, and ordinary retrospective coherence. Negative recollections deserve equal care. Memory decades later is evidence about lived experience, not a substitute for protocol records.

## Critic incentives

Cold War human-experimentation narratives can reward aggregation: grouping Whitecoat with deceptive or coercive programs creates a stronger moral story. Comparison is legitimate only when differences in consent, subject status, disclosure, risk, and treatment are preserved.

## Scientific incentives

Investigators had incentives to produce interpretable challenge data and publish useful countermeasure results. Controlled infection reduces scientific uncertainty while moving some risk onto volunteers. Publications may underrepresent recruitment context or adverse operational details that were not central to the scientific endpoint.

## Core contradictions to preserve

- Volunteer status vs military dependency.
- Conscientious refusal as evidence of autonomy vs conscientious-objector status as institutional vulnerability.
- Defensive medical purpose vs dual-use value of dose-response and aerosol data.
- Medical benefit to future populations vs no necessary therapeutic benefit to the challenged volunteer.
- Formal consent vs social and religious pressure.
- Reassuring long-term follow-up vs inability to prove universal absence of individual harm.
- Institutional pride vs possible genuine ethical innovation.

No final version should resolve these contradictions by rhetorical confidence alone.
