# When Labor Stops Bargaining

The first mistake in arguments about automation is to ask whether a job exists.

Jobs are bundles.

A customer-support agent listens, searches, explains, reassures, types, decides when to escalate, learns the company’s products, absorbs abuse, recognizes unusual cases, and sometimes persuades a customer not to leave. A lawyer researches, drafts, negotiates, interprets ambiguity, manages a client, takes responsibility, and stands behind a filing. A nurse observes, documents, administers, reassures, notices change, improvises around missing information, and works inside a licensed chain of responsibility.

Technology does not usually replace the bundle all at once.

It enters through tasks.

That is the central insight of the task-based approach to automation developed by Daron Acemoglu, Pascual Restrepo, and others. In a 2024 review updated in 2025, Acemoglu, Fredric Kong, and Restrepo distinguish technologies that augment workers, automate tasks, improve capital, and create new tasks for labor. Those categories matter because they can raise productivity while producing very different labor-market outcomes.

This is more than an academic distinction.

A tool that makes a worker faster can raise the worker’s value.

A tool that makes the worker’s most valuable task reproducible by capital can reduce it.

The same interface can do both.

Return to the customer-support study.

The generative-AI assistant observed conversations and suggested responses to human agents. Workers remained responsible for the chat and could ignore or edit the suggestions. Productivity rose about fifteen percent on average. Less experienced and lower-skilled agents gained the most.

This is precisely the kind of result that should make a simple automation pessimist uncomfortable.

The system did not merely remove labor.

It made people better at the job.

Less experienced workers gained access to patterns that had previously been embedded in stronger workers’ behavior. The technology compressed part of the experience curve.

That can be an extraordinary benefit.

It can also change what experience is worth.

Suppose an employer once paid a premium for workers who had spent two years learning how to handle difficult customers because that knowledge could not be acquired quickly. If an AI assistant lets a new worker perform much closer to the experienced worker after two months, the new worker is empowered relative to the old training curve.

The experienced worker’s scarcity may decline.

No one has to lose a job for the bargain to move.

Expertise can become less valuable because expertise has been distributed.

This is a familiar pattern in technological history.

A machine tool can make a less-skilled operator capable of producing a part that once required a highly skilled craft worker.

A spreadsheet can let one analyst perform calculations that once required a larger clerical staff.

A camera with automatic exposure lets a novice produce technically acceptable photographs without mastering light meters and film response.

The technology democratizes capability and attacks scarcity at the same time.

Whether that is good depends on where you stand and what else changes.

David Autor has argued that AI could be used to broaden access to expert decision-making rather than simply automate expert labor. In his 2024 NBER paper [“Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs,”](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140) he describes a possibility in which AI lets more workers perform valuable judgment tasks currently concentrated among highly trained professionals.

That is almost a mirror image of gradual disempowerment.

AI can increase the number of humans whose judgment matters.

A medical worker with strong decision support may handle cases previously reserved for a physician.

A technician may diagnose equipment that once required a senior specialist.

A paralegal may perform work that once required a lawyer.

A worker can move upward into a task rather than downward out of one.

The direction is not written into the model.

A 2026 paper by Acemoglu, Autor, and Simon Johnson, [“Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence,”](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34854) makes the distinction explicit. They separate labor-augmenting technologies, automation, expertise-leveling, capital augmentation, and technologies that create new tasks for human workers. Their argument is not that all AI harms labor. It is that the design of technology affects whether human expertise becomes more valuable or more replaceable.

For this book, that is the empirical hinge.

Gradual disempowerment is much more plausible in a world where AI primarily reduces institutions’ need for human judgment.

It is less plausible in a world where AI continually creates new forms of valuable human judgment faster than it automates old ones.

The difference can be hidden by aggregate employment.

A 2025 NBER paper by Menaka Hampole, Dimitris Papanikolaou, Lawrence Schmidt, and Bryan Seegmiller found evidence that tasks with greater AI exposure subsequently experienced reduced labor demand, while overall employment effects at adopting firms were modest because productivity gains increased demand elsewhere. The within-firm composition changed even when the total headcount did not collapse.

That is exactly why the job count is an incomplete measure of power.

A company can employ the same number of people and depend on them differently.

Workers may move from producing to monitoring.

From deciding to approving.

From creating to selecting.

From knowing to checking.

From solving the ordinary case to handling the exceptional one.

Some of those moves increase status and discretion.

Others leave the human with responsibility after the information required to exercise it has migrated into the machine.

The title of the next chapter belongs to that second possibility.

Before getting there, the labor bargain needs one more distinction.

Wages are not a complete measure of worker power.

A worker can earn more while having less control over pace, schedule, task sequence, evaluation, or the conditions under which discipline occurs.

A delivery driver can receive higher pay and be managed more intensively by an app.

A warehouse worker can earn a premium in a tight labor market while having each movement measured against a target.

A software engineer can use AI to complete more work while management raises expected output because the tool is now standard.

A doctor can be paid well while a reimbursement system increasingly determines which decisions are economically possible.

Worker welfare is multidimensional.

So is bargaining power.

Acemoglu and Simon Johnson return to this in their discussion of machinery and labor during the Industrial Revolution. In [“Learning from Ricardo and Thompson,”](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32416) they emphasize that mechanization in textile production could raise productivity while degrading autonomy and failing to deliver corresponding wage gains for long periods. Their historical argument is not a template that can simply be pasted onto AI. Modern institutions, labor markets, education, ownership, and technologies differ radically.

The case is useful because it breaks the automatic link between productivity and worker benefit.

Output is an engineering statistic.

Distribution is an institutional outcome.

AI can make a firm richer without specifying who receives the gain.

The gain can appear as lower consumer prices.

Higher profits.

Higher wages.

Shorter hours.

More employment.

More investment.

Executive compensation.

Tax revenue.

Or some mixture.

The technology does not settle the bargaining problem.

Bargaining institutions do.

The Hollywood strikes make this visible because performers and writers negotiated before the technology had settled into a mature production system.

A performer’s face and voice are unusual assets.

They are inseparable from a person and reproducible through digital technology.

Before high-quality synthetic replication, bringing a performer back usually required bringing the performer back.

That dependence creates leverage.

A producer who wants an additional line, a reshoot, a different language version, or another use of a performance may need a new session or a new agreement.

A digital replica changes the technical outside option.

The union contract changes the legal one.

SAG-AFTRA’s 2023 agreement established informed-consent and compensation provisions around covered digital-replica uses. The agreement did not preserve human leverage by preventing replication from existing. It altered the conditions under which the new outside option could be used.

The union converted a threatened material dependency into an explicit contractual right.

That move belongs near the center of this book.

Gradual disempowerment is most dangerous where important human power remains implicit.

If a performer matters only because the studio technically cannot reproduce the performance, improved replication weakens the performer.

If the performer has enforceable rights over the use of the likeness and performance, the right can survive the technical change.

The right may be imperfect.

Enforcement can be difficult.

Contracts expire.

New categories of use appear.

But the basic mechanism is clear.

Institutions can detach power from necessity.

The Writers Guild’s 2023 agreement provides another version. The Guild established that generative AI is not a writer under the contract and defined how AI-generated material interacts with writers’ covered work. Again, the point was not to make generation technically impossible. It was to preserve bargaining categories after technical substitution became more credible.

Whether these protections remain strong as models improve is an open question.

That uncertainty is exactly what makes them useful evidence.

Power can be codified.

It can also be renegotiated when the underlying dependence changes.

A right that exists only because the stronger party currently accepts it is different from a right supported by law, coordination, reputation, market pressure, and durable institutions.

This leads to a more precise version of the gradual-disempowerment fear.

The problem is not that machines perform labor.

The problem is that technological substitution can weaken the mechanisms by which labor converts its necessity into a claim on the surplus.

Economists call one part of this rent dissipation.

Acemoglu and Restrepo have studied automation in settings where workers earn wages above their outside options because particular tasks, institutions, or employment relationships give them rents. Their work argues that automation can target such tasks and dissipate those rents even when the technology raises productivity.

The word rent can sound like an accusation.

In economics it can mean income above the minimum required to keep a resource in its current use.

Some rents reflect market power.

Some reflect skill.

Some reflect bargaining.

Some reflect institutional arrangements.

Some allow workers to share in the surplus created by a productive enterprise.

Automation can change who captures them.

This is the bargaining-power version of replacement.

A machine does not need to do the entire job.

It needs to become a credible alternative at the margin where the worker was previously scarce.

There is another consequence that disappears when work is treated as a wage transaction.

Employment creates relationships.

People learn whom to call.

Managers accumulate informal obligations.

Workers exchange favors.

A supervisor bends a schedule because she knows a worker’s family situation.

A technician teaches a new hire something no manual contains.

A local employer sponsors the team because the plant manager lives in town.

None of these arrangements is guaranteed to be benign. Workplaces contain favoritism, exclusion, harassment, and informal power that formal systems can reduce.

They also contain reciprocal ties that do not survive every efficiency improvement.

Acemoglu and Alexander Wolitzky model part of this in their 2024 NBER paper [“Employment and Community: Socioeconomic Cooperation and Its Breakdown.”](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32773) Their framework examines how employment relationships and community cooperation can reinforce one another, and how monitoring technology, automation, outsourcing, and other changes can weaken softer relational arrangements even when the changes appear economically rational.

The paper is theoretical.

The mechanism is recognizable.

A firm that can monitor a delivery worker perfectly needs less trust.

A company that can replace local expertise with remote automation needs fewer long-term relationships with a local workforce.

A platform that assigns jobs through software does not need a dispatcher who knows which worker is handling a sick child.

Removing discretion can remove abuse.

It can remove accommodation too.

Power sometimes lives inside discretion.

That makes it dangerous and useful.

A worker who needs mercy from one supervisor is vulnerable.

A worker whose circumstances can be represented in an appeal process has a more durable form of protection.

Automation often exposes the difference because informal arrangements disappear when decisions are formalized.

The human bargain has to move from relationship to rule.

This is another way gradual disempowerment can occur without unemployment.

An institution can become less dependent on a relationship while retaining the people.

The worker stays.

The company no longer needs to know the worker.

That sounds sentimental until the relationship was carrying information the formal system does not contain.

Consider scheduling.

A manager can build a schedule from availability, skill, demand, seniority, and labor rules.

An algorithm can optimize the same variables at a scale and speed the manager cannot match.

If the data are complete, the algorithm may be fairer and more consistent.

If the worker’s actual constraint is “my mother’s chemotherapy moved to Thursday and I have not yet updated the system,” the manager’s local knowledge can matter.

A good automated system can include exception handling.

Then the question moves again.

Who has authority to approve the exception?

How costly is appeal?

Does the worker know why the request failed?

Can a human change the rule?

The path from output to contest becomes part of the labor bargain.

This is where a productivity study and a power study ask different questions about the same system.

The productivity study asks how many tasks are completed, how quickly, and with what quality.

The power study asks who can alter the process when the process conflicts with a human interest.

Both matter.

The mistake is to assume improvement in one settles the other.

The same mechanism can improve fairness.

A closed craft monopoly can exclude outsiders.

An expert profession can use scarcity to charge high prices.

A union can bargain for terms that raise costs for consumers.

Automation can open access.

The existence of worker power is not proof that every use of it is socially optimal.

That is why “preserve worker power” is too crude as a policy principle and too sentimental as a book thesis.

The better question is what happens to human influence over production when technological systems become the dominant outside option.

If labor loses leverage, ownership can compensate.

A worker who owns a meaningful share of the automated capital can receive income and governance rights even if the firm no longer needs the worker’s labor.

If ownership is broad, automation can sever the old connection between work and income without necessarily severing the connection between humans and economic power.

If ownership is concentrated, the same productivity gain can move bargaining power toward a smaller group of humans.

The machine is identical in both scenarios.

The institution is different.

The firm-level evidence gives another reason to resist dramatic early conclusions.

A Census-based study of more than 300,000 U.S. firms, later published in a 2025 volume on technology and productivity, found that adoption of advanced technologies such as AI, robotics, specialized software, and cloud computing was concentrated among larger firms. Adopters had higher labor productivity and lower labor shares, but firms reported limited or ambiguous effects on employment levels. The combination matters.

A technology can change the division of income between labor and capital without producing a visible wave of layoffs.

It can change skill requirements.

It can change which firms gain scale.

It can make some tasks cheap enough that the organization expands.

Headcount can look stable while leverage, task content, and the distribution of returns move.

This is precisely the kind of gradual change that a jobs-only dashboard misses.

This is why the phrase “AI versus humans” obscures the most immediate distributional conflict.

For a long time, the first stages of AI-driven disempowerment may be humans using AI to gain leverage over other humans.

An employer gains an outside option.

A consultant gains scale.

A founder needs fewer employees.

A worker with AI competes against a worker without it.

A large firm gains automation capital a smaller firm cannot afford.

A small firm gains capabilities that previously required a large staff.

The lines cross.

There is no single human side.

That makes the systemic version harder, not easier, to evaluate.

If some humans become much more powerful through AI, they can preserve human control in a literal sense while broad human bargaining power declines.

A company controlled by one human owner and ten thousand AI agents is still human-controlled by one definition.

The ten thousand people who might once have been employees experience the change differently.

Humanity is a bad unit for distributional analysis.

It becomes relevant only when machine alternatives become competitive across so many domains that even the most powerful human principals face the revocation problem from Chapter 1.

That is a long way from a customer-support assistant.

The path starts with something smaller.

The person does not disappear from the workplace.

The workflow becomes less dependent on the person’s judgment.

OECD researchers now have evidence that this is not hypothetical.

A 2025 employer survey across France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the United States found algorithmic-management tools already widely used in many workplaces. Managers reported perceived improvements in decision quality while also identifying problems such as unclear accountability and difficulty following the logic of some systems.

The manager remains.

The decision process changes.

That is where power becomes harder to see.

A worker negotiating with a human supervisor knows where judgment sits.

A worker negotiating with a score has a different problem.

The threshold the book cares about is therefore not mass unemployment. It is a change in credible refusal. When a worker’s no can be routed around cheaply, the worker has less leverage. When a worker can use the same technology to find alternatives, create output independently, coordinate with others, or force a better bargain, leverage can move the other direction. The machine changes the bargaining set. Institutions decide how the gain is divided.

That is a more ordinary process than replacement.

It can happen while employment looks healthy, while wages rise, and while every participant can point to a useful tool. It is also easier to miss because no headline announces a change in bargaining power.

The next chapter is about the human who still has the title after the score has learned to say no.
