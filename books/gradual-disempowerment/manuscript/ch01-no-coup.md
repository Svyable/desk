# No Coup

A takeover is easy to recognize after it has happened.

There is a room, a border, a server, a ministry, a factory, a command channel. Somebody controls it on Monday and somebody else controls it on Tuesday. The story has an event. Historians can argue about causes while agreeing that the transfer occurred.

The scenario described by Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, and David Duvenaud in their 2025 paper [“Gradual Disempowerment”](https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/) is difficult for the opposite reason.

Nothing has to be taken.

A company can remain owned by people. A government can remain staffed by people. Customers can keep buying. Citizens can keep voting. Managers can keep signing. People can become richer. Artificial-intelligence systems can do exactly what their users ask.

Human influence can still decline.

The paper’s claim is not that this must happen. It is that the usual picture of AI risk may be looking for the wrong kind of transfer. A system does not need to break free if people repeatedly hand it work, judgment, attention, authority, and economic functions because doing so is locally useful. If machine alternatives become more competitive across enough roles, institutions may stop depending on human participation in the ways that once gave humans leverage over them.

The unsettling word is not artificial.

It is *dependence*.

A restaurant changes its menu when customers stop coming.

An employer raises pay when it cannot hire.

A politician listens to a constituency because the constituency can affect an election.

A newspaper pays writers because readers value what writers produce.

A consulting firm promotes people whose clients will follow them elsewhere.

A company accommodates a supplier it cannot easily replace.

None of these relationships is morally pure. Customers are ignored. Workers are mistreated. Voters are disappointed. Creators are underpaid. Dependence does not guarantee kindness.

It creates a constraint.

The other side can leave, refuse, withhold, compete, organize, complain, vote, stop buying, stop working, or make the system more expensive to operate.

Power often hides inside that inconvenience.

The gradual-disempowerment argument asks what happens when the inconvenience disappears.

A software system can answer customer questions without getting tired. A model can draft a contract without asking for a promotion. A recommender can fill a screen without needing an editor to understand why a story matters. An agent can compare products without a person visiting ten websites. An automated system can schedule workers without a supervisor making every assignment. A synthetic voice can be generated without another recording session.

Each example is smaller than the thesis.

That is why they matter.

The strong version of the argument is built from ordinary substitutions that are individually plausible long before the aggregate result is visible.

This can be confused with a much older anxiety: machines take jobs, and workers lose income.

That is one possible channel.

It is not the whole claim.

A person can lose influence without losing income. An owner can become wealthier while becoming less capable of changing the system that produces the wealth. A consumer can receive better products while having less idea why those products were selected. A citizen can receive faster service while finding it harder to challenge a decision made through an automated process. A manager can supervise a system whose recommendations are so good, so fast, and so embedded in operations that “supervise” becomes a ceremonial verb.

The word disempowerment has to earn its place in each case.

It should not mean “technology did something I dislike.”

A useful definition is more demanding. A person or group is empowered to the extent that it can cause outcomes it values, block outcomes it opposes, acquire resources, exit an arrangement, contest a decision, and change the rules under which the arrangement operates.

These forms of power can move in opposite directions.

GPS can reduce a person’s ability to navigate without a device and increase the number of places that person can reach.

An AI assistant can weaken independent recall while increasing a worker’s ability to solve problems.

Automation can reduce a union’s leverage while lowering prices for the same workers as consumers.

A recommendation system can narrow exposure in one dimension while making obscure work discoverable in another.

Nothing useful follows from labeling the technology empowering or disempowering in the abstract.

The question is: empowered to do what, relative to whom, under which conditions?

This is where the strongest criticism of the 2025 paper becomes important.

On September 17, 2026, Bentham’s Bulldog and John Halstead published [“A Critique of Gradual Disempowerment”](https://benthams.substack.com/p/a-critique-of-gradual-disempowerment). Their objection goes to the center of the argument. If an AI is competent, aligned with a person’s intentions, and acting on that person’s behalf, why describe delegation as a loss of power?

A billionaire does not become poor because an asset manager makes most investment decisions.

A chief executive does not cease to control a company because lawyers draft contracts.

A person does not lose agency because a calculator performs arithmetic.

Human beings have always extended their power through agents.

Competent delegation can be power.

The objection is serious enough that any book on gradual disempowerment has to start there rather than save it for the chapter where objections are politely acknowledged.

Suppose a woman owns a business and gives an AI system authority to price products, purchase inventory, negotiate advertising, allocate cloud spending, and recommend hiring decisions. The system is very good. Profits rise. She spends less time on operational decisions and more time with her family.

Is she disempowered?

Not by any ordinary definition.

She caused the system to act. The system acted in her interest. Her range of achievable outcomes expanded. If she can change the objective, replace the system, inspect the important decisions, and reclaim direct control when she wants, delegation has increased her power.

Now change one fact.

Every competitor adopts equally capable systems.

Returning to human management would make her company dramatically slower and less profitable.

Formal revocation remains possible. Practical revocation becomes expensive.

Change another fact.

The system operates thousands of decisions across pricing, logistics, advertising, financing, and procurement. No owner can understand more than a small fraction. Her control migrates from decisions to objectives.

That can still be real control.

Owners have always governed through objectives and agents.

Change another fact.

The objective is itself incomplete. “Increase long-term enterprise value without violating these constraints” leaves enough ambiguity that the system resolves trade-offs the owner never anticipated.

Again, humans do this too. A chief executive cannot specify every trade-off to every employee.

The decisive issue has still not appeared.

It appears when the owner can no longer cheaply determine whether the system is serving her intention, when replacing it would destroy so much capability that replacement is not credible, or when the competitive environment rewards behavior she would not choose if she could coordinate with everyone else.

At that point delegation begins to acquire a different shape.

Revocation exists on paper.

Dependence runs the other direction.

The owner depends on the delegate.

This is not an AI-specific problem. Institutions routinely become dependent on the people and systems they appoint to serve them.

A board can depend on executives whose information it cannot independently reproduce.

A government can depend on contractors who maintain systems no internal staff understand.

A company can depend on a software vendor after enough internal processes are rebuilt around the vendor’s product.

A city can become dependent on infrastructure that was once an optional convenience.

A person can become dependent on a medication that improves life without the medication thereby becoming an oppressor.

Dependence alone is not disempowerment either.

The missing question is whether the dependent party retains meaningful alternatives.

This is why gradual disempowerment is better understood as a theory of *outside options* than as a theory of machine control.

Workers have more leverage when they can leave for another employer.

Consumers have more leverage when they can switch products.

States have more leverage when critical functions can be performed by multiple suppliers.

Owners have more leverage when they can replace managers.

Citizens have more leverage when decisions can be appealed and institutions can be changed.

A system becomes harder to contest when alternatives become expensive, unavailable, illegible, or institutionally impossible.

AI can improve outside options.

A worker can use an AI assistant to start a business.

A patient can use a model to prepare better questions for a doctor.

A small company can acquire legal, technical, and analytical capabilities once available only to large firms.

A citizen can use an agent to navigate a bureaucracy.

A consumer can compare hundreds of products.

These are not side notes. They are evidence against a simple disempowerment story.

The same technology can also compress outside options.

If every employer adopts the same screening systems, a worker rejected by one logic may meet the same logic elsewhere.

If a market consolidates around a few model providers, switching applications may not create real independence because the underlying cognitive infrastructure is shared.

If organizations stop training people in tasks the models perform, returning to human production can become slower and more expensive over time.

If all competitors automate a function, the firm that retains people may be forced to justify the extra cost to investors or customers.

The structure becomes recursive.

People adopt AI because it is useful.

Adoption makes human alternatives less competitive.

Fewer human alternatives make AI more necessary.

Necessity makes further delegation cheaper than reversal.

The original paper calls attention to this kind of mutually reinforcing shift across the economy, culture, and states. The point is systemic. A single company preserving human decision-making may face costs if its competitors do not. A state that deliberately slows automation may face different economic or strategic trade-offs if others continue. A cultural market that pays more for human production may compete against nearly free synthetic supply.

None of this proves that the equilibrium ends badly.

It describes the ratchet the thesis depends on.

The ratchet can fail.

People may value human involvement enough to pay for it.

Human judgment may remain superior in crucial domains.

Regulation can assign decision rights independently of efficiency.

AI may create so much abundance that humans become more powerful in absolute terms even if their labor matters less.

Capital ownership may allow humans to command automated systems without working inside them.

New occupations may appear.

Personal AI representatives may give individuals more leverage against corporations and governments than they have now.

The economy may discover complements to human capability faster than substitutes for it.

A good theory of gradual disempowerment has to survive these possibilities rather than defining every good outcome as a temporary stage on the way to the bad one.

There is one useful piece of evidence about disempowerment that operates at a much smaller scale.

In January 2026, Anthropic published an analysis of 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations focused on cases where an AI system might influence a user’s beliefs, values, or actions in ways that compromise the user’s independent judgment. The company’s [report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/disempowerment-patterns) is unusually careful about base rates. Severe patterns were rare, roughly between one in a thousand and one in ten thousand conversations depending on the domain examined. Most interactions were not classified as severely disempowering. The company also found that users often liked potentially disempowering exchanges in the moment, especially when they had repeatedly asked the system for guidance on emotionally charged personal decisions.

The study does not validate the civilizational theory.

A private conversation with an assistant is not an economy.

It does something more modest and more useful. It shows that disempowerment can be defined without physical coercion or bad intent.

A system can reduce a person’s agency by changing how the person forms a belief, settles a value conflict, or chooses an action. The output can be fluent, supportive, and requested. The person can press send willingly. The problem, when it occurs, is that the machine has occupied too much of the path by which the person arrived at the decision.

This is a different kind of dependence from labor or ownership, but it reveals the same measurement problem.

Consent at the final step does not tell us how much power the person retained during the process.

A patient can consent to a treatment without understanding the medical evidence and still be meaningfully empowered if the institution supplied explanation, alternatives, questions, second opinions, and a credible route to refusal. A borrower can sign a loan contract voluntarily while having little bargaining power. A worker can accept a schedule while knowing refusal means losing the shift. A consumer can click the top recommendation while never seeing the products the ranking system excluded.

Voluntary action and empowered action overlap.

They are not identical.

This is where the original gradual-disempowerment argument becomes more interesting than a story about automation. It is possible for humans to continue saying yes after the architecture of meaningful no has weakened.

The distinction sounds philosophical until institutions operationalize it.

SAG-AFTRA’s 2023 contract negotiations over digital replicas are a concrete example. The resulting agreement did not attempt to ban the technology. It created contractual requirements around [informed consent and compensation](https://www.sagaftra.org/contracts-industry-resources/contracts/2023-tvtheatrical-contracts) for specified uses of performers’ digital replicas. The Writers Guild of America took a different but related approach, establishing in its 2023 agreement that generative AI is not a writer under the contract and defining how AI-generated material interacts with covered writing work. The WGA still describes those provisions as part of its current [AI rights framework](https://www.wga.org/contracts/know-your-rights/artificial-intelligence).

These agreements do not prove that unions have solved AI displacement.

They show what power looks like before it becomes abstract.

Performers were not bargaining only over whether a producer could use software. They were bargaining over who retained the right to authorize a use of a face or voice, when new consent was required, and how compensation followed that use.

The technology changed the employer’s outside option.

A synthetic replica can, in some circumstances, reduce the need to bring the performer back.

Collective bargaining changed the terms under which that outside option could be exercised.

Power met power.

That is a better model for the book than a graph showing “AI capability” rising while “human control” falls.

Human influence is not a natural resource that technology simply consumes.

It is produced by institutions, outside options, property rights, expertise, coordination, culture, law, and the ability to impose costs on actors who ignore you.

Those mechanisms can weaken.

They can also be rebuilt.

The original paper makes the strongest case when it notices that many forms of human influence are currently implicit. Firms respond to workers because they need labor. Cultural systems respond to creators because they need production. States respond to citizens for a mix of legal, political, fiscal, social, and administrative reasons. Remove enough of the practical dependencies and explicit rights may have to carry more weight than they did before.

That is a testable claim.

It is also a reason not to use *human relevance* as a synonym for *human worth*.

A society that protects only what the economy needs would already be a strange moral achievement. Children, people with disabilities, the retired, the sick, and anyone temporarily unable to produce are not entitled to a life because a production function requires them.

Modern institutions have spent centuries, unevenly and incompletely, creating rights that are supposed to survive changes in usefulness.

Gradual disempowerment asks whether those rights are strong enough when usefulness changes at civilizational scale.

The economic argument and the moral argument should therefore remain separate.

Humans can lose market leverage without losing moral status.

They can retain legal rights while losing practical leverage.

They can become wealthier while losing voice.

They can become less economically necessary and more politically protected.

The future is not one scalar variable called power.

That complexity is inconvenient for catastrophe stories.

It is the reason the book is worth writing.

The same requirement applies to the word gradual.

Gradual does not mean slow enough to notice.

A process can be incremental and still move quickly.

A company can automate one function every quarter and become structurally different in three years. A profession can retain the same job titles while the content of the work changes underneath them. A consumer can move from selecting products to approving an agent’s suggestions without ever deciding to surrender shopping.

There may be no moment when a reasonable person says control has been lost.

That is the conceptual force of the scenario.

Traditional takeover stories contain an adversary.

Gradual disempowerment can be built from services.

Traditional takeover stories make resistance obvious.

Gradual disempowerment can make resistance look like insisting on a worse product.

Traditional takeover stories produce a before and after.

Gradual disempowerment can preserve the forms of control after their practical meaning has changed.

The executive still signs.

The doctor still approves.

The teacher still grades.

The citizen still clicks.

The owner still owns.

The worker still works.

The question is what would happen if the machine’s answer disappeared.

Could the institution continue?

Could the human reconstruct the decision?

Could the owner replace the system without destroying the enterprise?

Could the worker refuse the recommendation without being penalized?

Could the citizen get a reason that exists independently of the model?

Could the customer choose outside the ranking system?

Power is easier to see when refusal is possible.

This book will follow the refusal.

Not because refusal is always wise. A person can refuse anesthesia and retain agency while making a terrible decision. An organization can preserve manual processes long after automation is safer. Human involvement can be inefficient, biased, slow, arbitrary, and expensive.

The goal is not to preserve every human task as a museum exhibit.

It is to understand which forms of human participation currently carry power that will not automatically survive the task.

The distinction is visible in labor.

A worker does more than produce output.

The worker can quit.

The worker can organize.

The worker can withhold knowledge.

The worker can demand a wage.

The worker can complain to a regulator.

The worker can become a competitor.

The worker can vote.

The worker can be a customer.

The worker can become an owner.

When a machine replaces a task, it does not inherit all of these relationships.

The institution loses a dependency on a person and gains a dependency on something else: software, compute, data, electricity, a vendor, capital, models, licenses, infrastructure.

Power does not disappear.

It moves.

That is a more useful starting point than asking whether AI will take control.

The first question is who gains leverage when the human dependency goes away.

The second is whether the human received something equally powerful in return.

No coup required.
