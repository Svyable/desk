# The Manager Becomes a Signature

A manager used to be easy to locate.

The person might be unfair, brilliant, distracted, vindictive, generous, exhausted, arbitrary, or competent. The worker knew where the judgment lived.

Ask why the shift changed and there was a person to ask.

Ask why the promotion went elsewhere and there was a person who had made, defended, or inherited the decision.

Ask for an exception and someone could say yes.

The modern workplace has been making that person less singular for decades.

Scheduling software allocates shifts.

Performance systems rank workers.

Fraud tools flag anomalies.

Routing software assigns jobs.

Recruiting systems filter candidates.

Dashboards define targets.

Workforce-management systems forecast demand and translate the forecast into staffing.

Some of these systems use machine learning.

Some are ordinary rules.

The distinction matters technically and can matter legally. It matters less to the worker when the system is the thing saying no.

The International Labour Organization defines [algorithmic management](https://www.ilo.org/algorithmic-management-workplace) broadly as the use of algorithmic systems and worker data to organize, assign, monitor, supervise, and evaluate work. The ILO is careful to note that this does not require artificial intelligence. A fixed rule can manage.

That history is useful because it shows that gradual disempowerment does not begin with generative AI.

The workplace has been learning to convert judgment into systems for a long time.

Time clocks.

Piece rates.

Industrial engineering.

Call-time metrics.

Route optimization.

Quality scores.

Warehouse scanners.

Sales dashboards.

Computerized scheduling.

AI makes the systems more flexible.

The power shift can happen before the system is intelligent in any rich sense.

A human manager decides under conditions of incomplete information.

An algorithmic manager can ingest more data, apply rules consistently, monitor continuously, and calculate trade-offs at a scale no supervisor could match.

That can be a genuine improvement.

Workers routinely complain about inconsistent managers for good reason.

Two people can receive different treatment because one supervisor likes a worker and another does not.

A manager can forget.

A manager can discriminate.

A manager can reward the person who speaks most confidently in a meeting.

A manager can schedule badly because the spreadsheet is painful.

A well-designed system can reduce some of this arbitrariness.

The 2025 OECD work on algorithmic management is useful because the evidence refuses a simple story. Its cross-country employer survey covered more than six thousand mid-level managers in France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the United States. The OECD found these tools already widely used in many workplaces. Managers often reported improvements in decision quality and their own job satisfaction.

The same managers reported concerns.

In the OECD’s [full report](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/how-widespread-is-algorithmic-management-in-workplaces_cda7a114-en/full-report.html), nearly two-thirds of managers using algorithmic-management tools reported at least one concern about their effects on workers. Among the most common were unclear accountability when a decision went wrong, difficulty following the logic behind decisions, and inadequate protection of worker health.

The combination is more interesting than either result alone.

Managers think the tools help.

Managers also see the authority problem.

This is gradual disempowerment in miniature.

Nobody has to prefer less accountability.

The organization adopts a system because the system produces useful decisions.

The useful decisions make the system more central.

As the system becomes more central, fewer people understand or reproduce the decision process.

The manager remains accountable in the organizational chart.

The manager becomes less independent in practice.

At first, the machine advises.

Then the advice becomes the default.

Then deviation requires explanation.

Then deviation requires approval.

Then nobody remembers what the workflow was before the recommendation existed.

The title of the chapter is not quite right.

The manager does not become a signature all at once.

The manager becomes a signature one successful recommendation at a time.

This is not specific to management.

A doctor can face the same transition with clinical decision support.

A credit analyst can face it with risk models.

An auditor can face it with anomaly detection.

A teacher can face it with automated scoring.

A judge can face it with risk assessments.

A pilot can face it with flight automation.

The person nominally responsible for the decision can become the last human step in a process whose epistemic center has moved elsewhere.

The human-in-the-loop label hides this.

A loop can contain a human and still give the human almost no leverage.

The worker sees a recommendation.

The worker can click approve.

The worker can technically refuse.

Refusal requires writing a justification, finding a supervisor, accepting delay, and taking responsibility for overruling a system with a strong performance record.

The formal option and the practical option diverge.

This is the same revocation problem from Chapter 1 moved inside one decision.

The system does not need legal authority to acquire practical authority.

It needs default status plus a cost to dissent.

Automation bias research has studied versions of this problem for decades. People can over-rely on automated recommendations, especially when systems are usually reliable or when users have weak independent information. The phenomenon is not universal. Expertise, interface design, incentives, training, and task characteristics all matter.

The important point here is institutional.

A worker asked to review a machine decision needs more than permission to disagree.

The worker needs enough time, information, competence, and authority for disagreement to change the outcome.

Remove any one and human oversight can become decoration.

That word—oversight—appears constantly in rules for automated systems because the problem is already visible.

The European Union’s 2024 Platform Work Directive offers a concrete example. Its provisions on algorithmic management require covered digital labor platforms to provide human oversight of automated monitoring and decision systems. The directive says the people responsible for oversight must have the competence, training, and authority necessary to perform the role, including authority to override automated decisions.

The wording is revealing.

A human body in the process is not enough.

The person must be able to understand and act.

That is an institutional recognition of practical power.

The directive also gives people performing platform work rights related to automated systems, including protections around certain personal data and mechanisms for human review. These provisions are one jurisdiction’s response to one employment form. They do not establish an ideal model for every workplace.

They make the architecture visible.

An algorithm decides or supports a decision.

A human is assigned oversight.

The law asks whether the human has actual authority.

That is almost the whole book in miniature.

The difficult part begins after the law says yes.

Competence has to be maintained.

A manager who reviews machine scheduling every day may stop practicing the scheduling task.

A recruiter who sees model-ranked candidates may become better at evaluating finalists and worse at searching the whole pool.

A supervisor whose dashboard identifies low performers may spend more time acting on rankings and less time observing work directly.

Over time, the human’s independent model of the system can weaken.

That is where this book touches *Abdicating Intelligence* without becoming the same book.

Cognitive atrophy is one mechanism by which formal authority can lose substance.

It is not the only one.

Scale can do it even if the manager remains fully competent.

Imagine a human reviewer who could independently evaluate every decision if given twenty minutes.

The automated system makes ten thousand decisions per day.

Competence survives.

Capacity does not.

The reviewer becomes statistically responsible.

She samples.

She audits.

She handles appeals.

She does not decide the ordinary case.

This can be excellent system design.

Airlines do not require an executive to approve every route adjustment.

Banks do not require the board to inspect every transaction.

Large organizations must delegate.

The question is whether the sampling and appeal architecture is strong enough to keep the automated process responsive to human correction.

Practical control at scale looks different from direct control.

It depends on indicators.

Audit access.

Escalation.

Independent evidence.

Random review.

Error reporting.

Ability to stop the system.

Ability to change the objective.

Ability to compensate people harmed by errors.

Ability to detect failures that the system’s own metrics do not report.

That last one is difficult.

An algorithmic manager sees what the data infrastructure can represent.

A person sees some things the data miss.

A worker is struggling because a parent is dying.

A delivery is late because a customer needed physical help.

A support call lasted too long because the documented procedure was wrong.

A nurse spent longer with a patient because the patient was frightened.

A salesperson’s conversion rate is down because the territory was changed.

A warehouse worker is slower because a piece of equipment is intermittently failing.

These explanations can be added to data.

Then they become legible.

The organization cannot add every possible future explanation in advance.

Human discretion has historically absorbed some of the unmodeled world.

Algorithmic management can make that discretion look like inconsistency.

Sometimes it is inconsistency.

Sometimes it is the institution noticing reality.

This is why the best comparison is not human judgment versus objective algorithm.

Both are systems of selective attention.

A human manager misses patterns in large datasets.

An automated manager misses context outside its inputs.

The organization decides which blindness is easier to tolerate.

AI can reduce the trade-off by processing unstructured information.

A model can read a worker’s explanation.

It can summarize circumstances.

It can interpret natural language rather than forcing every exception into a dropdown menu.

This may make algorithmic management more humane.

It can also absorb another reason the human manager was necessary.

The system learns to process the exception.

The exception handler becomes less scarce.

Again, capability and power move together in a way that is difficult to classify morally.

A worker should want a system capable of understanding context.

A worker should also care who controls the objective the context is being interpreted against.

Suppose an AI manager perfectly understands that a driver stopped for ten minutes to help an elderly customer carry groceries inside.

The system still has to decide whether that behavior is good.

If the objective values customer care, the stop can count positively.

If the objective values delivery time above everything else, the richer understanding merely produces a more confident penalty.

Better cognition does not choose the value function.

Management has always contained values disguised as metrics.

Revenue.

Output.

Quality.

Safety.

Speed.

Attendance.

Customer satisfaction.

Turnover.

Fairness.

The argument is over trade-offs.

A human manager makes the trade-off implicitly or explicitly.

An algorithmic system makes it through objectives, constraints, learned patterns, or policies.

The power question is who can change those trade-offs.

There is a second asymmetry in algorithmic management that is easy to miss.

The organization can become much more visible to itself while the worker sees less of the organization.

A traditional supervisor watches ten people and remembers imperfectly.

A platform can watch ten thousand workers through location, acceptance rates, completion times, customer ratings, cancellation patterns, idle periods, device signals, and other traces.

The worker does not receive a symmetric view of the system evaluating her.

She may see a score.

She may not see how the score was produced, which comparison group matters, how recent behavior is weighted, what threshold triggers intervention, or whether the model has changed since last month.

Information becomes asymmetric in a new direction.

This matters because bargaining requires a model of the other side.

A worker negotiating with a supervisor can learn what the supervisor cares about.

A driver can learn which dispatcher will approve a change.

A team can compare how managers respond to different requests.

An opaque scoring system can make this social learning less useful. The organization retains memory at scale while the worker encounters the organization through outputs.

This does not require secrecy.

A company can publish the broad rules and still operate a system too complex for an individual worker to predict.

Credit systems already teach this distinction. A borrower can know which categories affect a score without being able to reconstruct every model output. Search rankings can publish principles without making each ranking predictable. Complexity can produce opacity without deception.

AI increases the range of signals such systems can interpret.

Natural-language models can analyze messages.

Computer vision can inspect activity.

Predictive systems can infer risk from patterns spread across data sources.

The manager gains a denser representation of the worker.

The worker may receive a thinner representation of management: a prompt, a target, a warning, a route.

Power can come from that difference in legibility.

The EU Platform Work Directive’s algorithmic-management provisions respond partly to this problem by requiring forms of transparency and human oversight for covered platform work. The rules are not proof that a particular regulatory design solves the asymmetry. They are evidence that lawmakers recognized a practical distinction between an automated decision existing and the affected person being able to understand, contest, and obtain human review of it.

The architecture of appeal is more important than the presence of a complaint button.

Suppose a worker is automatically deactivated.

The system offers an appeal.

The appeal takes six weeks.

The worker loses income during the six weeks.

The reviewer receives the same underlying score and no additional evidence.

The final response says the original decision was consistent with policy.

Formally, the person had recourse.

Practically, the system may have had finality.

Now change the design.

The worker sees the reason.

The worker can supply counterevidence.

A reviewer independent of the original decision has authority to restore access.

The appeal is fast enough that winning matters.

The organization records reversal rates and uses them to improve the system.

That is a different distribution of power even if the same algorithm made the first decision.

Contestability is not a philosophical ornament.

It is an operational property.

The same principle applies inside a company when an employee appeals a performance score or a manager challenges a staffing recommendation. A system can be highly automated and still preserve meaningful human authority if disagreement has a credible path.

The cost of that path determines whether it survives.

A ten-second override is different from a forty-page exception process.

A human review that happens before harm is different from one that arrives after the consequences are irreversible.

A supervisor empowered to say no is different from a supervisor measured on how often she agrees with the system.

Formal rights are implemented in latency, staffing, evidence, and incentives.

This is one reason the gradual-disempowerment question becomes difficult to answer from organizational charts.

The chart can stay identical.

The manager can keep the title, salary, office, and legal responsibility. The worker can keep the right to complain. None of those facts tells us how much a disagreement can still alter the system.

The friction attached to dissent can change everything. Power often survives first as a right and disappears later as a right that no longer changes what happens in time to matter materially to the person affected.

This is where workers can lose influence even when the system treats each individual more consistently.

A supervisor who knows a team may be persuadable.

A central optimization policy may not be.

A local manager can bend a rule.

A platform can make the rule identical across a country.

Uniformity removes favoritism.

It also removes local bargaining.

The ILO’s 2024 comparative research on algorithmic management in logistics and healthcare found both efficiency gains and concerns about job quality and intrusive surveillance. The point is not that one side cancels the other.

Efficiency can be the mechanism by which the system spreads.

Once a tool makes operations cheaper, more predictable, or easier to scale, organizations have reasons to adopt it even if the adoption changes worker autonomy.

The trade-off does not have to be decided maliciously.

The dashboard wins because the dashboard works.

This is the form of gradual disempowerment worth taking seriously.

The worker does not wake up one morning under machine rule.

The supervisor gets better software.

The software identifies patterns the supervisor missed.

The company standardizes its use.

Targets become more consistent.

Exceptions become more formal.

Headquarters sees the workforce more clearly.

The local supervisor sees less that matters outside the system.

Workers learn how to satisfy the metric.

The metric becomes a feature of the job.

Eventually the person titled manager may be managing less than the software.

The job can still require judgment.

Someone must explain the score.

Someone must handle the angry worker.

Someone must make the call the system cannot make.

Someone must sign.

The signature matters if it can still change the decision.

Otherwise it is an interface.

This is the first place the book needs a rule strict enough to survive every industry:

**Do not count humans. Count vetoes.**

How many people sit in the workflow tells us little.

Can the human stop the outcome?

Can the human demand another explanation?

Can the human change the objective?

Can the human override without being punished simply for disagreeing?

Can the affected person reach someone with that authority?

Can an independent process review the decision?

Can the system operate if enough humans refuse?

Those questions reveal power.

A company can answer yes to all of them and still use algorithmic management heavily.

That is important.

Automation and human authority are not opposites.

The organization can decide to preserve vetoes even when software handles most routine decisions.

The cost may be higher.

The result may be slower.

Human reviewers may introduce inconsistency.

Some vetoes will be abused.

The trade-off is real.

That is why the strongest gradual-disempowerment argument relies on competition.

A company that preserves costly human discretion may face a rival that does not.

The rival may make faster decisions.

It may scale further.

It may run with fewer managers.

If the cost difference is large enough, human vetoes can begin to look like inefficiency.

That does not make their removal inevitable.

It makes their value something the institution has to recognize explicitly.

A worker once knew where judgment lived.

The new question is whether anyone still has the right to move it.
