# The Bargain Hidden in Dependence

Imagine an employer cuts wages by ten percent.

The cleanest version of the competitive labor-market model says the response should be brutal. Workers doing the same job can leave for other employers paying the market wage. A firm that insists on paying less loses its workforce. Competition disciplines the employer.

Real labor markets are stickier.

In a 2024 review of research on [monopsony power in labor markets](https://www.nber.org/reporter/2024number1/monopsony-power-labor-markets), economists Suresh Naidu and Arindrajit Dube summarized a large body of evidence showing that workers do not move as freely as the simplest model assumes. Search takes time. Jobs differ in location, schedule, colleagues, benefits, stability, commute, status, and compatibility with family life. People may know that another employer pays more and still stay.

The result is power.

A firm does not need to be the only employer in town to have some discretion over wages. It needs workers whose outside options are costly enough that a pay cut does not empty the building.

That point gives gradual disempowerment an economic vocabulary that is more useful than the usual language of replacement.

Power depends on alternatives.

The employer has power when workers have poor outside options.

Workers gain power when employers have poor outside options.

A company desperate for a scarce engineer can complain about the engineer’s salary and pay it anyway.

A worker facing one realistic employer can dislike the offer and accept it anyway.

The same structure appears outside labor.

A customer with ten credible suppliers can negotiate differently from a customer with one.

A hospital dependent on one drug manufacturer behaves differently from a hospital able to substitute three products.

A cloud customer that can move workloads easily has more leverage over a provider than one whose entire architecture has been built around proprietary services.

An owner who can replace a manager cheaply can delegate more comfortably than an owner whose company would collapse if that manager walked out.

Power appears in the space between “I dislike this” and “I can do something about it.”

That space is what AI can change.

Suppose a company needs two hundred people to operate a customer-support function.

The workers do not all have equal bargaining power. Some can leave easily. Some cannot. The company does not have infinite power either. If too many experienced agents leave, service deteriorates. Hiring and training cost money. Customers become angry. Managers spend time recruiting.

The workers are necessary enough to constrain the company.

Now introduce an AI assistant that makes each worker more productive.

The first effect can be good for everybody.

A large field study by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond followed 5,172 customer-support agents after the staggered introduction of a generative-AI assistant. In the published 2025 *Quarterly Journal of Economics* paper, [“Generative AI at Work,”](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658) access to the system increased issues resolved per hour by about fifteen percent on average. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers gained the most. The researchers also found evidence of learning and improvements in aspects of the work experience.

That is an empowerment story if the unit of analysis is the worker performing the task.

The worker can resolve more problems.

The novice gains access to patterns associated with stronger performers.

The customer may receive faster service.

Now change the unit of analysis.

If the same customer volume can be handled by fewer workers, the company’s dependence on any given worker may fall.

That possibility is not a measured result of the study. The study was about productivity, not a long-run bargaining equilibrium.

The distinction is the point.

Productivity and power are not the same variable.

A worker can become more productive and less scarce.

A worker can become more productive and more valuable.

Which one happens depends on demand, ownership, labor-market structure, how the technology changes tasks, and what the firm does with the additional output.

This is why arguments about AI and work become confused so quickly.

One side shows that a tool helps workers.

Another side warns that the tool will reduce jobs.

Both can be looking at real mechanisms occurring at different margins.

If lower costs increase demand enough, employment can grow even as each worker becomes more productive.

If demand is fixed, higher productivity can reduce headcount.

If AI makes novices productive faster, firms may need less experience.

If experts become capable of overseeing much larger operations, their leverage may rise.

If the technology makes expertise easier to encode and distribute, some experts may lose the scarcity that produced their wage premium.

The important question for gradual disempowerment is not whether employment rises or falls in aggregate.

It is whether the institution’s need for human participation changes.

Need is rarely binary.

A hospital can need nurses while still having considerable wage-setting power.

A software company can need engineers while treating some roles as interchangeable.

A film studio can need performers while acquiring technological alternatives for particular uses of a face, voice, or performance.

A school can need teachers while using software to absorb some preparation and grading work.

Dependency has intensity.

Economists already know how to think about one part of this through labor-supply elasticity.

If workers leave quickly when wages fall, the firm faces an elastic labor supply and has less wage-setting power.

If workers stay, the firm has more.

Naidu and Dube describe evidence that even in urban labor markets, workers are often less responsive to wage changes than a perfectly competitive model predicts. Other research has estimated substantial labor-market power through job differentiation, search frictions, and employer concentration. A 2026 NBER review by Sydnee Caldwell, Dube, and Naidu describes the field as large enough that the central question has shifted from whether monopsony exists to how its sources and magnitudes vary across settings.

AI adds another source of employer outside options.

Not “replace all labor.”

Replace enough of this task.

Reduce training time.

Make one experienced worker capable of supervising more output.

Turn tacit knowledge into suggestions.

Standardize work that used to require judgment.

Make the service easier to offshore or centralize because language assistance reduces local skill requirements.

The company does not need a humanoid robot at every desk.

It needs the alternative to become credible enough to affect the bargain.

This is what workers in the entertainment industry understood during the 2023 strikes.

The issue was not that studios had already replaced actors and writers with AI.

They had not.

The issue was that generative systems changed future outside options.

For performers, a digital replica could allow later uses of a person’s likeness or performance without the same form of return to set. SAG-AFTRA’s eventual agreement established requirements around consent and compensation for specified digital-replica uses.

For writers, the Writers Guild of America negotiated rules defining how AI-generated material interacts with covered writing and preserving the contractual status of human writers under the agreement.

These were labor negotiations about a technology that could alter the employer’s alternative to hiring the same person again.

That is a power problem before it is a replacement problem.

The difference becomes obvious in a bargaining room.

A union does not need to prove that a technology can replace every member.

It cares whether the technology changes the employer’s threat point.

If a producer can credibly say, “We can do this without you,” the negotiation changes.

If workers can credibly say, “Production does not happen without us,” the negotiation changes the other way.

Collective bargaining exists partly to aggregate a dependence that an employer might otherwise distribute across individuals.

One worker can be replaceable.

A coordinated workforce can make replacement expensive.

Automation can attack the structure at either level.

It can replace individuals.

It can also reduce the number of people whose coordination is necessary to stop production.

This is one reason automation and union power have a relationship that cannot be reduced to wages.

A factory with a thousand workers contains more potential points of human leverage than a factory producing the same output with fifty, even if the fifty are paid more.

That does not mean the fifty are powerless.

They may be highly specialized.

The facility may depend on each one.

A small number of critical workers can possess enormous leverage.

Again, the variable is dependence, not headcount.

This is where the gradual-disempowerment thesis becomes more precise.

Human beings currently occupy many bottlenecks.

A bottleneck is inconvenient for the system and powerful for the person sitting in it.

The skilled technician who knows how to restart a legacy machine.

The nurse licensed to perform a procedure.

The lawyer required to sign a filing.

The performer whose face the audience wants.

The engineer who understands the one undocumented subsystem.

The sales representative whose clients trust her.

The manager with authority to approve the exception.

Organizations try to reduce bottlenecks because bottlenecks are operational risk.

Documentation reduces dependence on one expert.

Cross-training reduces dependence on one worker.

Standardization reduces dependence on local judgment.

Automation reduces dependence on human action.

These are sensible managerial practices.

They can also be understood as a transfer of bargaining power from the bottleneck to the system.

No manager needs to think about it that way.

A firm that removes single points of human failure becomes more resilient.

A person who was the single point of failure becomes less indispensable.

This is one reason technological progress can empower a society while disempowering particular roles.

People benefit enormously from systems that do not collapse because one person knows the password.

Patients should not depend on one doctor’s memory.

Airplanes should not be safe only when one gifted mechanic is working.

Financial records should not exist solely in one bookkeeper’s head.

Civilization advances partly by taking critical knowledge out of fragile individual custody.

The moral is not “preserve bottlenecks.”

The question is what replaces the power that bottlenecks accidentally supplied.

Professional licensing is one answer.

Ownership is another.

Collective bargaining is another.

Legal rights are another.

Reputation is another.

Scarce human preference is another.

A society can choose to give people influence even when production no longer needs them.

But that requires explicit institutions.

Implicit power is easier.

If the company cannot operate without you, the argument for listening to you comes preinstalled.

The original gradual-disempowerment paper is strongest when it points to this hidden subsidy.

Modern systems may be more aligned with human interests than they appear because humans are everywhere inside the production function.

Companies want healthy workers partly because unhealthy workers cannot work.

Governments need educated populations partly because administration, industry, military capacity, tax collection, and innovation depend on human competence.

Platforms care about users partly because users supply attention, content, data, and revenue.

Cultural institutions care about creators partly because creators make the things being distributed.

Remove the dependency and explicit principles have to do more work.

This does not mean institutions become hostile once people are unnecessary.

A pension fund does not hate retirees because retirees do not work.

A hospital does not cease to care about unconscious patients because they cannot bargain.

A family does not allocate food only to the members with the highest productivity.

Human societies contain moral commitments precisely because need and deservingness are not the same thing.

The gradual-disempowerment scenario becomes catastrophic only if those commitments prove weaker than the dependencies they replaced.

That is an empirical and institutional question.

The opposite possibility deserves equal attention.

AI could improve outside options for humans faster than it improves outside options for institutions.

A worker with a capable AI may be able to freelance, start a company, learn a new skill, negotiate a contract, or identify a better job more easily.

A small firm may gain access to expertise that previously required a large legal or technical department.

An individual may be able to challenge a bureaucracy using an agent that reads regulations and prepares appeals.

A creator may reach an audience without a large production organization.

These changes reduce dependence on institutions.

Power can move toward the individual.

This is why the same technology can produce both sides of the book’s argument.

AI is an outside-option machine.

It gives firms alternatives to workers.

It gives workers alternatives to firms.

It gives consumers alternatives to experts.

It gives experts alternatives to support staff.

It gives small organizations alternatives to large organizations.

It gives large organizations alternatives to specialized human departments.

Who gains leverage depends on which alternative improves faster.

The answer can vary by occupation, market, country, firm, and year.

The distribution problem becomes sharper once ownership enters the same transaction.

A worker may lose leverage over wages while gaining wealth through a pension fund invested in the employer.

A homeowner may benefit from lower service prices produced by automation while a child entering the labor market encounters fewer entry-level roles.

A founder may use AI to remove the need for a department and then discover that the resulting company is easier for three people to operate, increasing the founders’ control enormously.

A shareholder can be empowered by exactly the change that disempowers a worker.

This sounds like an ordinary conflict over surplus because it is one.

AI does not abolish political economy.

It changes the production function inside it.

That is one reason the phrase “AI will disempower humans” can become less useful than “AI changes which humans the system depends on.”

The second sentence creates questions the first one conceals.

Does a company become less dependent on labor and more dependent on capital?

Does it become less dependent on local workers and more dependent on a model provider?

Does it become less dependent on management judgment and more dependent on data infrastructure?

Does a creator become less dependent on a studio and more dependent on a distribution platform?

Does a patient become less dependent on a physician for information and more dependent on a model company for interpretation?

Every substitution creates a new dependency somewhere.

The gradual-disempowerment scenario becomes a human problem when the replacement dependencies point toward actors that do not transmit influence back to a broad human constituency.

A company dependent on customers still has a reason to satisfy customers.

A company dependent on one model provider has a reason to satisfy the model provider.

A state dependent on taxpayers has a reason to preserve a taxable economy.

A state whose revenue comes overwhelmingly from automated capital-intensive industries could, in theory, become less dependent on the labor income of ordinary citizens while remaining deeply dependent on owners, infrastructure operators, and technical systems.

That conclusion cannot be assumed from the revenue structure alone. Modern states have legal and political obligations that are not reducible to tax dependence. The example matters because it shows the direction of the mechanism: remove one dependency and another constituency can become more important.

Institutions are maps of dependencies.

The map is rarely written down.

A hospital’s organization chart shows reporting lines. It does not show which nurse knows the workaround that prevents a particular device from delaying a procedure.

A company’s balance sheet shows assets and liabilities. It does not show the customer whose departure would trigger five others.

A government budget shows revenue and spending. It does not show which forms of administrative knowledge are held by retiring staff.

A software architecture diagram shows services. It may not show the one vendor engineer everybody calls when the distributed system behaves strangely.

Power lives in these undocumented edges.

Automation is often a project to formalize them.

Turn the workaround into software.

Turn the trusted relationship into a CRM record.

Turn the experienced judgment into a model.

Turn the exception process into a rule.

Turn the person who knows into a system that remembers.

The organization becomes less fragile.

The person becomes less singular.

This is usually good engineering.

It is also exactly why the book cannot treat human indispensability as an uncomplicated virtue. A civilization in which every important process depends on irreplaceable individuals would be brittle, unequal, and difficult to scale.

The objective is not maximum dependence on humans.

It is preserving meaningful human power as dependence becomes less necessary.

That may require replacing accidental leverage with deliberate rights.

A worker should not need to be operationally irreplaceable to deserve safe conditions.

A citizen should not need to be economically indispensable to deserve political standing.

An artist should not need monopoly control over production technology to retain authorship rights.

A patient should not need medical expertise to deserve an explanation.

These statements sound obvious because many modern institutions already accept versions of them.

Gradual disempowerment tests how much those commitments depend on the old material facts beneath them.

If the rights remain enforceable after the dependency disappears, automation can remove bottlenecks without removing power.

If the rights become symbolic because nobody with practical leverage can enforce them, the old dependency was doing more work than the law admitted.

The phrase human disempowerment risks hiding this distribution.

There is no single bargaining table called humanity.

Some humans own the machines.

Some sell labor to the owners.

Some regulate the owners.

Some buy the products.

Some build competitors.

Some hold pensions invested in the companies reducing labor demand.

Some are children.

Some are retired.

Some live in places where the relevant technology is barely available.

A change that disempowers one group can empower another human group.

That makes aggregation difficult.

Suppose automation raises total output by twenty percent, doubles the income of capital owners, reduces the wages of one occupational group, lowers consumer prices, and funds a larger public transfer system.

Did humans gain power?

The sentence is underspecified.

Which humans?

Power over what?

Income can be aggregated more easily than agency.

That may be one reason economics often talks about welfare rather than empowerment.

The book cannot avoid the harder variable.

Gradual disempowerment is not mainly a claim about how much stuff people have.

A person can receive a generous transfer and have little control over the institution paying it.

A child can be materially secure and appropriately lack adult authority.

A shareholder can be wealthy and unable to understand the company whose shares produce the wealth.

A citizen can enjoy excellent public services and have limited ability to change them.

Material welfare matters.

Control matters too.

The two should not be collapsed.

The next chapter takes the distinction into the workplace after the worker remains employed.

Because losing the job is not the only way work can lose power.

Sometimes the person keeps the chair.

The decision leaves first.
