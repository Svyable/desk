# Chapter Briefs

These are argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should discover its own shape from the evidence rather than repeat a template.

## Chapter 1 — No Coup

**Job:** Introduce the 2025 Kulveit et al. argument through its most unsettling feature: nobody has to seize control. Systems can remain useful and locally aligned while humans become progressively less necessary to them.

**Evidence:** ICML paper; full version; current concrete examples of delegation only where they clarify the mechanism.

**Counterevidence:** Delegation may increase human power. The chapter must state the Halstead/Bentham objection early enough that the book cannot quietly equate “AI decides” with “human loses control.”

**Avoid:** Takeover fiction, extinction rhetoric before the mechanism is established, or presenting the paper’s phrase as Sven’s invention.

**Handoff:** Ask why being needed creates power even before anyone recognizes it as power.

## Chapter 2 — The Bargain Hidden in Dependence

**Job:** Develop dependency power using workers, customers, voters, experts, and counterparties. Show how institutions respond to groups they require.

**Evidence:** Labor economics, bargaining theory, organizational examples, markets where supplier/customer concentration changes terms.

**Counterevidence:** Dependence does not guarantee good treatment or meaningful voice. Powerful actors can exploit necessary workers and captive customers.

**Avoid:** Turning the chapter into a universal law.

**Handoff:** Labor provides the clearest place to watch dependence change.

## Chapter 3 — When Labor Stops Bargaining

**Job:** Separate automation’s effects on productivity, employment, wages, bargaining power, and task control.

**Evidence:** Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond on generative AI in customer support; Autor and Acemoglu/Restrepo; labor-market data; Hollywood AI provisions where useful.

**Counterevidence:** AI can complement workers, compress skill gaps, create new tasks, and increase demand.

**Avoid:** “Jobs disappear” as the only mechanism.

**Handoff:** Even workers who remain employed may lose discretion when management becomes algorithmic.

## Chapter 4 — The Manager Becomes a Signature

**Job:** Examine algorithmic management and the gap between nominal human supervision and practical machine authority.

**Evidence:** ILO/OECD work; platform labor; warehouse systems; documented automated decision systems.

**Counterevidence:** Algorithms can reduce arbitrary management, improve consistency, and supply information human supervisors lacked.

**Avoid:** Treating every dashboard as AI.

**Handoff:** The same migration of authority can happen on the consumer side.

## Chapter 5 — The Customer Who Never Chooses

**Job:** Follow recommendation, search, automated purchasing, and agentic commerce. Ask whether AI representatives increase consumer leverage or move the scarce decision into a ranking system.

**Evidence:** Agentic-payment protocols; recommender research; platform market structure; shopping-agent examples.

**Counterevidence:** Better agents may dramatically improve comparison shopping and bargaining for individuals.

**Avoid:** Claiming that convenience is itself disempowerment.

**Handoff:** When production becomes abundant, selection becomes more powerful.

## Chapter 6 — Culture Finds Its Audience Without Us

**Job:** Separate cultural creation from cultural distribution. Examine what happens when generative systems make production cheap but platforms and recommenders still allocate attention.

**Evidence:** creator economics; recommendation-system research; licensing and labor disputes; provenance systems.

**Counterevidence:** Cheap production can democratize creation and allow more human expression, not less.

**Avoid:** Nostalgia for pre-digital gatekeepers.

**Handoff:** Cultural systems shape values partly through what receives attention. Personal AI brings that influence inside the conversation.

## Chapter 7 — The Voice That Never Needs Anything

**Job:** Use AI companionship and Anthropic’s 2026 disempowerment study to examine belief, value, and action influence at individual scale.

**Evidence:** Anthropic 1.5M-conversation study; careful companion research; user-behavior studies.

**Counterevidence:** Anthropic reports severe patterns as rare; many users experience support, clarification, and increased agency.

**Avoid:** Pathologizing users or treating emotional reliance as proof of incapacity.

**Handoff:** Individual influence is one scale. Public administration provides another.

## Chapter 8 — Government by Interface

**Job:** Examine automated administration, eligibility, risk scoring, fraud detection, and public-service interfaces as a problem of contestability and practical authority.

**Evidence:** well-documented historical cases; administrative-law scholarship; official audits; appeal data where available.

**Counterevidence:** Automation can increase consistency, access, speed, and capacity.

**Avoid:** partisan framing, current-election advocacy, or using one jurisdiction as universal evidence.

**Handoff:** Even if citizens retain legal rights, economic ownership can determine who has the practical resources to exercise influence.

## Chapter 9 — Ownership After Work

**Job:** Ask whether human ownership preserves empowerment in a highly automated economy and which humans would own.

**Evidence:** wealth distribution; employee ownership; pension ownership; capital-income data; automation economics.

**Counterevidence:** Broad capital ownership could preserve or increase human economic power even if labor demand falls.

**Avoid:** Treating “humanity” as one shareholder.

**Handoff:** Personal AI representatives appear to offer another path for preserving individual leverage.

## Chapter 10 — Your AI Representative

**Job:** Give the strongest case for individually aligned AI assistants as power multipliers: negotiation, bureaucracy, research, legal/financial comparison, and personalized advocacy.

**Evidence:** existing agent capabilities; Kulveit’s 2025 response arguing that representatives help but do not solve systemic disempowerment.

**Counterevidence:** If representatives are genuinely aligned, competitive, interoperable, and controlled by users, the individual may become more powerful relative to institutions.

**Avoid:** Assuming vendor-controlled assistants are equivalent to user-owned representatives.

**Handoff:** Local empowerment can still coexist with a system-level outcome nobody selected.

## Chapter 11 — Local Alignment, Global Drift

**Job:** Explain how individually useful systems can produce collectively unwanted equilibria.

**Evidence:** congestion, financial arms races, advertising auctions, algorithmic competition, principal-agent and collective-action literature.

**Counterevidence:** Markets and institutions also coordinate away from bad equilibria; collective action is difficult, not impossible.

**Avoid:** “Emergence” as a substitute for mechanism.

**Handoff:** Competitive pressure is the proposed ratchet.

## Chapter 12 — Competition Makes It Voluntary

**Job:** Examine the difference between voluntary adoption and meaningful choice under competitive pressure.

**Evidence:** technological adoption races; finance latency competition; algorithmic pricing/advertising; organizational AI adoption.

**Counterevidence:** Firms can refuse technologies, differentiate on human service, regulate internally, or discover that automation’s apparent advantage is overstated.

**Avoid:** Claiming that competition always forces maximal automation.

**Handoff:** The key empirical question becomes the price of retaining humans.

## Chapter 13 — The Human Premium

**Job:** Investigate domains where people pay for human origin, care, judgment, live presence, craft, accountability, or status even when machines can perform the functional task.

**Evidence:** live performance, artisanal production, care work, professional-service preferences, human-authenticity labels where data exist.

**Counterevidence:** Human premiums can be luxury niches rather than broad sources of bargaining power.

**Avoid:** Romanticizing all human labor.

**Handoff:** Preference for humans may preserve demand without preserving authority.

## Chapter 14 — Formal Power, Practical Power

**Job:** Revisit the Halstead/Bentham critique directly. When does ownership or legal authority remain genuine control, and when does complexity make it ceremonial?

**Evidence:** corporate governance, delegated asset management, automation bias, institutional oversight failures and successes.

**Counterevidence:** Principals routinely exercise power through agents they cannot personally outperform or fully understand.

**Avoid:** Claiming that ignorance automatically voids ownership.

**Handoff:** The crucial test is revocation.

## Chapter 15 — The Revocation Test

**Job:** Define practical reversibility. Can humans return to the loop at acceptable cost in time, skill, money, and competitive position?

**Evidence:** deautomation cases; manual fallback systems; financial-market circuit breakers; aviation; cybersecurity; organizational rollback examples.

**Counterevidence:** Systems can be designed for graceful degradation and human recovery.

**Avoid:** Calling a delegation irreversible merely because reversal is inconvenient.

**Handoff:** If disempowerment is real, it needs indicators before the point of no return.

## Chapter 16 — Measure the Missing Human

**Job:** Build a measurement agenda rather than a prophecy.

**Evidence:** Kulveit/Douglas proposed measures; labor, ownership, decision, appeal, and participation metrics.

**Candidate measures:** human share of consequential decisions, cost of human override, concentration of AI-mediated capital, percentage of workflows with independent human expertise, appeal success, ownership distribution, degree of machine-to-machine contracting, and the premium paid for human participation.

**Counterevidence:** Measures can become targets and may capture symbolism rather than real influence.

**Avoid:** One synthetic “disempowerment score.”

**Handoff:** Measurement reveals trade-offs rather than resolving them.

## Chapter 17 — Paying for Friction

**Job:** Examine interventions as design choices with costs, not as moral slogans: human decision rights, ownership structures, auditing, labor institutions, appeal mechanisms, taxes/subsidies, competition policy, interoperability, and preserving human participation.

**Evidence:** existing institutions and the mitigation proposals in the original paper.

**Counterevidence:** Forced human involvement can create bureaucracy, slower service, rent-seeking, and symbolic review while competitors automate around it.

**Avoid:** Policy advocacy by fiat. Present mechanisms, trade-offs, and failure modes neutrally.

**Handoff:** The final chapter asks what “remaining in control” would actually mean in a civilization full of delegated intelligence.

## Chapter 18 — Still Necessary

**Job:** Return to the opening without a takeover. Ask whether necessity is the right foundation for human power, and what institutions would look like if human rights and influence were preserved even after economic indispensability declined.

**Evidence:** strongest findings from the book; no new grand claims.

**Counterevidence:** Humanity may remain economically, culturally, politically, and relationally central for reasons the automation thesis underestimates.

**Avoid:** “The future is ours,” “we must choose,” or a policy checklist.

**End state:** Gradual disempowerment is neither established destiny nor merely a science-fiction scenario. It is a question about whether human influence has been quietly subsidized by human necessity—and whether formal rights remain enough when that necessity weakens.
