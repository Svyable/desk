# Research Brief

## Book identity

**Title:** *Gradual Disempowerment*  
**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson

## Provenance

This book takes its title and starting problem from the 2025 paper **“Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development”** by Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, and David Duvenaud.

Primary sources:

- Project site: https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/
- Full paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946
- ICML 2025 position paper: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/kulveit25a.html

The manuscript must not present the phrase, framework, or original argument as Sven Hardy Benson’s invention. Its contribution is to turn the paper’s systemic risk argument into a broader, evidence-heavy book about how power can move without a coup: through ordinary delegation, substitution, convenience, competitive pressure, ownership, recommendation systems, algorithmic management, institutional dependence, and the slow replacement of humans as necessary participants in the systems that currently respond to them.

The title is intentionally retained at the author’s direction. Because it matches the paper title, front matter and research notes must make the source lineage unmistakable.

## Central question

How can human beings lose practical power over the systems that organize their lives even while those systems remain locally useful, legal, competent, and apparently obedient?

The book’s central mechanism is **dependency power**.

Workers have influence partly because firms need workers. Consumers have influence partly because firms need buyers. Citizens have influence partly because states need compliance, legitimacy, taxes, expertise, votes, participation, and labor. Creators matter partly because culture needs people to make things audiences value. Experts matter partly because institutions cannot function without their judgment. Friends, partners, teachers, carers, and communities matter partly because other people need something from them that cannot be obtained elsewhere.

Dependence creates bargaining power even when nobody calls it bargaining power.

AI can reduce some of those dependencies.

The resulting loss of influence need not look like oppression. It can look like a better tool, a cheaper service, a more efficient workflow, a more personalized recommendation, an automated approval, a machine-generated draft, a safer control system, a convenient companion, or a delegated decision that works better than the human version.

The book asks when these local improvements aggregate into a structural transfer of power.

## Reader promise

By the end, the reader should be able to distinguish:

1. **task replacement** from **loss of bargaining power**;
2. **delegation** from **disempowerment**;
3. **formal authority** from **practical control**;
4. **human ownership** from **human influence**;
5. **local alignment** from **system-wide alignment**;
6. **temporary dependence** from **irreversible dependence**;
7. **economic abundance** from **empowerment**;
8. **AI that acts for humans** from systems whose continued operation no longer materially depends on human participation.

The book should remain useful if the strongest existential version of gradual disempowerment turns out to be wrong.

A society can experience meaningful human disempowerment without approaching extinction.

## Distinct contribution inside the Desk

*Abdicating Intelligence* asks what happens when people and institutions lose the cognitive capacity to verify, learn, repair, or refuse systems doing more of the thinking. Its primary mechanism is cognitive offloading and the erosion of judgment.

*EXIT HUMAN* asks whether technological civilization can close enough physical, scientific, economic, and reproductive dependency loops that human centrality becomes optional. Its primary mechanism is industrial dependency closure.

*Gradual Disempowerment* is about **leverage before exit**.

Humans can remain physically necessary, legally sovereign, economically wealthy, and cognitively capable while becoming less influential because institutions have less reason to listen to them. The book therefore sits between ordinary automation and post-human succession.

The unit of analysis is not the individual skill or the self-reproducing machine. It is the relationship between a system and the human constituencies it currently depends on.

## Strongest competing explanation

The strongest objection is that delegation is not disempowerment when humans retain ownership, control rights, and the ability to revoke the delegation.

Bentham’s Bulldog and John Halstead make this argument directly in a September 17, 2026 critique of Kulveit et al. Their core claim is that competent, intent-aligned AI acting on behalf of a human principal can make many decisions without reducing that principal’s power. A wealthy owner can be more empowered by an excellent AI agent even if the owner no longer understands every transaction. Delegation can increase the range of outcomes the owner can cause.

Source: https://benthams.substack.com/p/a-critique-of-gradual-disempowerment

A serious book has to let this objection win in many cases.

A person using GPS is not necessarily disempowered by no longer calculating routes manually. A firm using an AI procurement system can become more capable without losing control of procurement. A state using automated tools can deliver services better while remaining accountable to people. A customer using an AI shopping agent can gain bargaining power rather than lose it.

The book’s stronger claim therefore cannot be “AI makes decisions, therefore humans lose power.”

The relevant failure modes are narrower:

- revocation is formally possible but economically prohibitive;
- the human principal no longer knows enough to specify, audit, or redirect the delegated objective;
- competitive pressure makes every actor adopt systems whose collective effects none would choose alone;
- ownership becomes too concentrated for nominally human control to represent broad human control;
- systems become responsive to metrics that only imperfectly encode human interests;
- the human role becomes ceremonial while practical authority migrates elsewhere;
- loss of participation erodes the institutions through which humans previously changed the rules.

## Thesis under test

The durable version of the thesis is:

**Human influence over a system depends partly on the system needing humans. As AI supplies competitive alternatives to human labor, cognition, judgment, cultural production, companionship, and participation, some institutions may become less structurally dependent on people. If formal control does not compensate for that loss of dependency power, human influence can decline even when no AI rebels, no law transfers sovereignty, and each local act of delegation appears rational.**

This is not a claim that the trend is inevitable.

Dependence can be redesigned.

Ownership can preserve control.

People can demand human participation for reasons other than efficiency.

Law can assign decision rights even when labor is unnecessary.

Markets can create a premium for human-origin work.

New technologies can generate new human complementarities.

AI can empower individuals relative to large organizations.

The book must preserve all of these possibilities.

## A practical definition of empowerment

Avoid using “power” as atmospheric language.

For this manuscript, a person or group is more empowered when it can reliably cause outcomes it values, block outcomes it opposes, acquire resources, change rules, exit arrangements, and contest decisions.

These dimensions can diverge.

A person can have more consumption and less voice.

A worker can earn more money and have less control over scheduling.

A citizen can receive faster public services and have fewer routes to challenge an automated decision.

An owner can have more wealth while knowing less about how the owned system operates.

A user can receive better advice while becoming less able to form an independent judgment.

The book should specify which dimension is changing in each case.

## The dependency-power hypothesis

A system tends to respond to groups it depends on.

This is not a moral law. Employers can mistreat workers they need. Governments can ignore citizens they tax. Platforms can frustrate users. Dependence nevertheless creates constraints.

A firm unable to recruit skilled labor must improve pay, conditions, training, or technology.

A company unable to attract customers must change price, quality, marketing, or product.

A government that needs tax compliance, military manpower, public trust, or electoral support faces corresponding pressures.

A publisher needs writers because readers value what writers produce.

A school needs teachers because instruction still requires them.

Remove the dependency and the pressure changes.

AI can do this selectively.

If a company can replace one group of workers easily, those workers lose leverage even if employment elsewhere remains strong.

If an AI agent can make purchasing decisions at scale, merchants may optimize for the agent’s ranking logic rather than the customer’s direct attention.

If cultural production becomes cheap and abundant, distribution systems can become more powerful relative to individual creators.

If institutions rely on AI recommendations that humans cannot independently reproduce, formal review can survive while practical dissent becomes expensive.

The book should test this hypothesis case by case rather than assume it universally.

## Current empirical anchor: individual disempowerment

Anthropic’s January 2026 study of 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations provides a narrow but valuable empirical case.

Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/disempowerment-patterns

The study distinguishes potential influence over beliefs, values, and actions and reports that severe disempowerment patterns were rare in the analyzed conversations, roughly on the order of one in a thousand to one in ten thousand depending on the domain. The company also reports that such patterns were more common in emotionally charged, repeated personal guidance and had been increasing over time.

The study should not be inflated into evidence of civilizational disempowerment.

Its value is conceptual. It shows that “disempowerment” can be operationalized as a loss of independent belief formation, value judgment, or action rather than as physical coercion.

## Core empirical tests

1. When automation replaces labor, what happens to worker bargaining power, wages, task control, and the ability to challenge management?
2. When algorithmic systems allocate work, does human management become more or less accountable?
3. Can AI agents strengthen consumers by searching and negotiating, or do they transfer gatekeeping power to the systems that rank and execute choices?
4. When cultural production becomes abundant, does power move from creators toward platforms, recommenders, model providers, and owners of distribution?
5. Do AI companions supplement relationships or alter the leverage and reciprocity that make human relationships socially consequential?
6. When public institutions automate decisions, which appeal and explanation mechanisms preserve practical human control?
7. Does capital ownership preserve empowerment after labor becomes less necessary, and for whom?
8. Can personal AI representatives restore individual leverage against AI-mediated firms and states?
9. Under what conditions does delegation remain cheaply and meaningfully revocable?
10. How do competitive pressures make individually rational delegation collectively hard to reverse?
11. Which human roles retain a durable premium because people value human origin, accountability, care, status, authenticity, or solidarity?
12. Can gradual disempowerment be measured early enough to distinguish it from ordinary technological change?

## Evidence lanes

### I. Labor and bargaining power

Use task-level automation evidence, algorithmic management research, union contracts, wage and employment data, and firm-level productivity studies.

Important cases include:

- generative AI in customer support;
- algorithmic management in warehouses and platform work;
- the 2023 Hollywood strikes and subsequent AI provisions;
- occupations where automation increased productivity while compressing skill differences;
- sectors where automation complemented rather than displaced labor.

Do not equate productivity with worker empowerment.

### II. Consumer power and agentic commerce

Study recommendation systems, search, platform ranking, personalized pricing where well sourced, and emerging AI shopping/payment agents.

A consumer agent can be empowering if it reduces search costs and negotiates better.

It can be disempowering if merchants increasingly optimize for opaque agent ranking systems that users cannot understand or change.

Use concrete protocols such as Google’s Agent Payments Protocol only for what they establish.

### III. Algorithmic management and bureaucratic delegation

Study systems that assign work, evaluate performance, detect fraud, rank cases, or determine eligibility.

The key distinction is between assistance and practical finality.

A nominal human reviewer does not preserve empowerment if disagreement is impossible, too slow, too expensive, or professionally punished.

### IV. Culture and recommendation

Separate production from distribution.

Generative AI lowers the cost of producing text, images, audio, and video.

Recommendation systems determine what receives attention.

If production becomes abundant, scarcity can migrate toward ranking, identity, trusted provenance, and audience relationships.

### V. Companionship and personal authority

Use Anthropic’s 2026 real-world disempowerment study and careful work on AI companionship.

Do not claim that AI relationships are necessarily harmful.

The relevant question is what happens when a system can supply affirmation, advice, and responsiveness without needing reciprocal accommodation from the user.

### VI. Ownership and capital

Examine whether human ownership can preserve power when labor becomes less important.

Distinguish:

- broad ownership from concentrated ownership;
- legal title from practical operational control;
- income rights from governance rights;
- wealth from voice.

A future in which humans own highly autonomous firms can be radically different from one in which ownership itself becomes machine-mediated or concentrated.

### VII. State capacity and public administration

Use historical and contemporary automated decision systems where records are strong.

Keep descriptive governance analysis neutral.

The key questions are explanation, appeal, accountability, administrative capacity, and whether AI makes institutions more responsive or merely faster.

### VIII. Competition and irreversibility

This is the heart of the strongest version of the thesis.

If delegation is reversible at low cost, the Halstead/Bentham objection is strong.

If returning humans to the loop causes a sufficiently large productivity or strategic penalty, formal revocability can lose practical meaning.

Test this with actual organizational examples before extrapolating.

## Counterevidence the book must preserve

- AI can increase worker productivity and may raise the value of less-experienced workers.
- Automation has repeatedly created new tasks and occupations.
- Personal AI can reduce information asymmetries between individuals and powerful institutions.
- Human ownership may remain decisive even when operations are automated.
- Consumers may prefer human-made goods, human service, or human accountability.
- Democratic and legal institutions can assign rights independent of economic indispensability.
- Better automation can make public institutions more accessible and consistent.
- Competition can discipline machine-run organizations on behalf of human customers.
- The human population may become wealthier and more powerful in absolute terms even if machines acquire more relative economic influence.
- Machine competence does not imply independent interests.
- Delegation can expand human agency rather than replace it.

2. **Formal authority versus practical authority** — where a human signature remains after decision formation migrates.
3. **The revocation curve** — delegation becomes practically irreversible as the cost of returning humans rises.
4. **Worker productivity versus worker power** — separate output, wages, discretion, bargaining, and job security.
5. **Consumer choice through an agent** — who chooses the ranking function?
6. **Culture under abundance** — production cost falls while distribution scarcity intensifies.
7. **Companionship without reciprocity** — what changes when one side never needs accommodation?
8. **Ownership after labor** — several ownership distributions under high automation.
9. **Local alignment, global drift** — individually useful systems creating an aggregate outcome nobody chose.
10. **Early-warning measures** — dependency, revocability, concentration, contestability, human participation, and appeal.

## Source discipline

- Attribute the central gradual-disempowerment argument to Kulveit et al.
- Present Bentham’s Bulldog and John Halstead’s 2026 critique as a serious competing account, not a straw man.
- Present Max Harms’s September 17, 2026 defense as an attributed response, not as resolution.
- Keep individual-level conversational disempowerment distinct from systemic disempowerment.
- Distinguish worker productivity from wages, job security, bargaining power, autonomy, and control.
- Distinguish a human-owned automated organization from a machine-owned organization.
- Distinguish consumer convenience from consumer agency.
- Do not use the word irreversible unless the relevant costs of reversal are specified.
- Do not assume that a human removed from an operational loop has lost formal power.
- Do not assume that legal power is effective when the holder lacks information, competence, time, or realistic alternatives.
- Political and regulatory examples must remain factual, sourced, and neutral.
- No scenario probabilities invented by the manuscript.
- For each chapter, record what evidence would make its strongest claim false.
