# Back Matter

## Source Note

*The Expiration Machine* is written as narrative nonfiction and systems analysis. Its factual examples are grounded in primary or first-party materials wherever practical, including federal agencies, standards bodies, internet-governance organizations, and official technical documentation.

The research trail for the manuscript lives beside the book in `research/`. The source ledger records the principal materials used to establish claims about passports, pharmaceuticals, food labeling, cryptographic key lifetimes, password guidance, debt maturity, patent terms, warranties, environmental permitting, reactor licensing, temporary cloud credentials, and domain expiration.

The book distinguishes sourced facts from synthesis. Claims about what a particular agency, standard, rule, or system does should be checkable against the research trail. Broader ideas such as “expiration as scheduled doubt,” “the future receives a vote,” and “bounded trust with durable foundations” are the author's interpretive framework.

No source should be treated as timeless. Rules, standards, product documentation, and regulatory guidance change. Readers working from a later edition should consult the then-current primary source before relying on a specific procedural detail.

## A Note on Scope

This book is not legal, medical, financial, cybersecurity, or regulatory advice. Its examples are used to study the design of temporary trust across domains. A useful analogy between systems does not erase the differences among those systems.

The recurring question is structural: when should an earlier judgment continue to govern, and when should reality get another vote?
