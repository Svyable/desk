# Epilogue — Tomorrow's Date

Tomorrow, millions of dates will quietly change status.

A token will stop authenticating.

A warranty will enter its final day.

A passport will lose another day of remaining validity.

A permit will move closer to renewal.

A domain registrar will send a reminder.

A bond will be one day nearer maturity.

A patent will be one day nearer the public domain.

A bottle in a cabinet will remain chemically almost indistinguishable from what it was at 11:59, while the institution behind its label will stand one day closer to the edge of what it has formally promised.

Most of these transitions will not feel dramatic.

That is the success condition.

A good expiration system rarely resembles a bomb with a timer attached. It resembles a well-designed handoff. The old proof gives way to the new one. A term ends and another begins. A temporary privilege returns to the public. A stale credential dies before anyone remembers to revoke it. A human being continues while the disposable evidence around that person is refreshed.

The date is not the story.

The story is what the date allows us to avoid.

Permanent permission.

Permanent monopoly.

Permanent stale evidence.

Permanent forgotten authority.

Permanent obligations that nobody would agree to if they had to be granted forever.

Civilization has learned to make enormous commitments by refusing to make all of them permanent.

That may be one of its quietest inventions.

We can lend because debt matures.

We can delegate because authority can expire.

We can tolerate temporary exclusivity because it ends.

We can rely on credentials because they can be refreshed.

We can grant operating permission because there will be another checkpoint.

We can say yes because the yes has edges.

But the machine must remain subordinate to the people and purposes it serves.

Some things should not be made precarious merely because software makes rechecking cheap. Some rights should not need recurring proof. Some relationships become more valuable when the question stays closed for a while. Some expiration rituals should themselves be retired.

The difficult work is not adding clocks.

It is choosing where time deserves authority.

Tomorrow's date will arrive automatically.

Whether anything should change when it does is a human decision.
