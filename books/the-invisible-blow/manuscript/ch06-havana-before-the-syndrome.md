# Havana Before the Syndrome

The first Havana cases were experienced before there was a Havana Syndrome to experience.

That temporal fact matters.

Once a named syndrome exists, later reporting occurs inside a changed cognitive and institutional environment. People know the symptom list. Supervisors know the term. Physicians know the controversy. Journalists know the suspected mechanism. Intelligence officers know that an incident may trigger a security investigation. None of that means later reports are false. It means the conditions of observation have changed.

The earliest cluster therefore deserves disproportionate attention.

The National Academies described reports beginning in late 2016 among U.S. Embassy personnel in Cuba. Some individuals reported sudden onset of a loud sound perceived as directional, pain or pressure in the ears or head, dizziness, tinnitus, visual problems, vertigo, and cognitive difficulty. Those features became the template against which later cases were compared. The committee also emphasized heterogeneity and the nonspecific nature of many longer-term symptoms.

A forensic investigator would like to go backward from that description to a physical scene. Where exactly was each person? What windows, walls, metal structures, electrical systems, elevators, rooftops, transmitters, neighboring residences, security devices, and sight lines were present? Who else was nearby? Did multiple people perceive the event simultaneously? Did moving several feet change it? What did microphones record? What did spectrum analyzers record? Were there contemporaneous medical measurements? Were there environmental samples?

For many early cases, those data are incomplete, unavailable publicly, or were never collected.

That is not unusual. Nobody builds an instrument suite for an event before the category exists.

The difficulty is what happens next. Investigators often use the early reports to define a case, then use the case definition to collect later reports, then use the accumulated reports to infer what the original phenomenon was. This can create a circular category. The broader the definition becomes, the more likely it is to absorb migraine, vestibular disorders, infections, anxiety, environmental exposures, ordinary acoustic events, and unrelated neurological problems. The apparent syndrome grows while the signal-to-noise ratio falls.

The 2022 IC experts panel tried to solve this by focusing on a narrower subset with four core characteristics: acute onset of audio-vestibular sensory phenomena; near-simultaneous symptoms such as vertigo, imbalance, or ear pain; a strong sense of locality or directionality; and lack of a known environmental or medical explanation. This was a mechanism-oriented subset, not a declaration that every reported AHI shared a cause.

That distinction should have been tattooed on the public conversation.

The phrase “more than a thousand cases” sounds evidentially powerful until the case taxonomy is understood. If most reports have ordinary explanations and a small residue has unusual characteristics, the residue should not inherit the denominator’s rhetorical weight. Conversely, if the residue contains repeated, specific features across locations and people, it should not be dismissed merely because the larger pool is noisy.

The correct unit of analysis may be the event, not the syndrome.

This is inconvenient for institutional care. Benefits, treatment pathways, and administrative programs need categories. People cannot be told to wait for causal certainty before receiving vestibular rehabilitation or headache treatment. Congress responded with the HAVANA Act and other measures because care is a policy decision under uncertainty. One can support care without asserting a foreign weapon. The government occasionally failed to keep those propositions separate, and affected personnel paid the price.

The same separation protects science. If compensation depends on proving an attack, patients may feel forced to defend one causal theory to preserve legitimacy. If skeptical analysts believe accepting a mechanism will automatically imply geopolitical attribution, they may overcorrect toward mundane explanations. A bad administrative coupling can contaminate the evidence.

The first Havana cases deserve to be treated neither as sacred origin stories nor as debunked folklore.

They are scenes with missing instruments.

The rest of this book is partly an attempt to infer what those instruments should have been.
