# A Thousand Classified Documents

In February 2022, the U.S. intelligence community did something rare. It told the public how much secret material a scientific panel had seen.

ODNI said the AHI Experts Panel received dozens of briefings and more than a thousand classified documents. The panel included expertise in acoustics, biology, medicine, psychiatry, ionizing radiation, environmental science, and radio frequency. Its mandate was mechanism. It was explicitly not asked to determine attribution.

That division of labor is one of the best decisions in the entire episode.

Attribution poisons mechanism analysis when it arrives too early. If the question is “did Russia attack us,” every technical result becomes geopolitical evidence. A null lab result may be read as exoneration. A plausible physical pathway becomes accusation. Scientists become unwilling participants in an intelligence verdict.

The panel instead asked what could produce the core features of a narrowed case subset.

Its answer was that pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly RF, plausibly could. It also said sources existed that could produce the required stimulus, could be concealed, and could have moderate power requirements. Ultrasound remained plausible in circumstances where the source could be close to the target. Other proposed mechanisms were less consistent with the defined core characteristics.

The declassified summary is carefully worded because much of the supporting material remains secret. That makes it stronger and weaker at once.

It is stronger because the panel had access to information open-source researchers do not possess: classified incident reports, sensitive intelligence, and findings of compartmented programs. It is weaker as public science because outsiders cannot inspect those inputs, reproduce the analysis, or know which claims depended on secret data rather than published physiology.

A thousand classified documents are not a thousand pieces of independent evidence.

They may contain duplication, reporting chains that circle back to one source, speculative intelligence, adversary disinformation, technical studies of varying quality, and assessments built on earlier assessments. Intelligence volume is not evidentiary independence.

The panel almost certainly knew this. The public summary, however, cannot show its work in the way a methods section can.

This is the central pathology of classified science. Secrecy may be necessary to protect sources, but it converts peer review into credentialed trust. The outsider is asked to accept that capable people saw things they cannot show. Sometimes that is the least bad option. It is never equivalent to reproducibility.

The solution is not automatic declassification. Some technical collection should remain secret. The solution is to decompose conclusions into the largest possible unclassified substrate. Physics can often be published even when intelligence cannot. A device family can be described by feasible ranges of power, aperture, frequency, and coupling without revealing which foreign facility was observed building one. Medical hypotheses can be tested openly. Detector specifications can be published. Red-team experiments can be blinded.

The 2022 panel’s biggest contribution may therefore be its architecture rather than its conclusion.

It separated mechanism from attribution.

The next step should have been to separate classified evidence from classified reasoning as far as possible.

Instead, the public was left with a remarkable sentence: pulsed electromagnetic energy plausibly explains the core characteristics.

Then everyone went back to arguing about Russia.
