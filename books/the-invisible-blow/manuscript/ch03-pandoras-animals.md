# Pandora’s Animals

Project PANDORA belongs in this story because it is a reminder of how governments learn when the phenomenon cannot be ethically reproduced in the form that actually worries them.

You do not begin by beaming diplomats. You begin with dosimetry, tissue, animals, behavioral assays, and every proxy that appears close enough to the feared mechanism to be informative. Then you discover that proxies are treacherous.

Microwave-bioeffects research has always been unusually sensitive to the geometry of the experiment. Frequency matters. Pulse width matters. Peak power matters. Average power matters. The size and orientation of the exposed body matter. Reflections inside a chamber matter. Tissue dielectric properties matter. The distance between source and subject matters. Whether the relevant effect is thermal, thermoelastic, vestibular, neural, vascular, or merely behavioral matters. Two experiments described casually as “microwave exposure” can be physically different events.

This is one reason the literature became easy to weaponize rhetorically. A person can find a paper showing a biological effect and cite it as if “RF” were one substance with one dose-response curve. Another person can point to exposures under safety limits and declare the entire idea impossible. Both arguments can skip the pulse structure that may be doing the interesting work.

The military problem is even harder. A covert system is not constrained to the exposure patterns common in telecommunications. Civil safety standards are designed around ordinary emitters and recognized hazards, not around an adversary deliberately optimizing peak fields, pulse repetition, focusing, or unusual modulation to exploit a biological response. This does not invalidate safety standards. It means they answer a different question.

The reverse error is to treat every unconstrained parameter as a magic knob. “Maybe the pulse is exotic” is not engineering. A real device must obey power, aperture, propagation, coupling, concealment, thermal management, aiming, and range constraints. It must work through or around whatever structure stands between source and target. If it operates in a city, it must coexist with a crowded electromagnetic environment. If it produces a strong field at a victim, it may produce detectable emissions somewhere else.

The useful legacy of PANDORA is not a verdict but a demand for experimental specificity.

A serious AHI hypothesis should be expressible as a system. What waveform? What carrier frequency? What pulse width and repetition rate? What peak and average power at the source? What antenna dimensions? What expected field at the target after propagation and wall losses? What tissue interaction is proposed? What immediate sensation should appear? What injury, if any, should remain? What instrument would detect the emission? What mundane technologies could generate a confusingly similar signal?

Without those questions, “directed energy” becomes a literary genre.

The 2022 IC experts panel pushed the discussion closer to engineering. Its declassified summary did not merely say electromagnetic energy was imaginable. It said pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly radio frequency, plausibly explained the core characteristics in a subset of cases and noted that sources could exist with moderate power requirements and concealability. The panel also considered ultrasound plausible in some close-access scenarios but less broadly applicable. Its role was mechanism, not attribution. That separation is essential.

The panel had access to more than a thousand classified documents, which means the public cannot reproduce the reasoning from open sources. This creates a peculiar asymmetry. We can see the conclusion and some methodological language, but not the full technical evidence. A critic can reasonably say that plausibility without disclosed parameters is difficult to audit. A defender can reasonably say some relevant foreign-capability intelligence cannot be published without burning sources and methods. Both can be true.

The public response often filled the missing space with certainty.

What should have happened instead is a second PANDORA built for the twenty-first century: open where possible, instrumented beyond embarrassment, and explicitly designed to distinguish mechanisms. Human exposure work would require strong ethics constraints. But tissue phantoms, instrumented head models, animal-free physical surrogates, vestibular and auditory models, RF chambers, propagation tests through realistic structures, and detector development can answer a large fraction of the engineering questions without recreating an alleged attack.

There is a deeper lesson in the name. Pandora’s box is usually remembered as the container that released evils into the world. The overlooked part is what stayed behind: hope. Government threat research often lives in the opposite arrangement. The feared capability is outside the box, in rumor and classified reporting. Inside are small, imperfect experiments trying to establish what physics permits.

The honest output of those experiments is often not belief or disbelief.

It is a smaller box.
