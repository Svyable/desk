# Weapon Physics Without a Weapon

The easiest directed-energy weapon to imagine is a flashlight with malice.

That mental model is wrong enough to be useful.

A real RF system has an emitter, power source, switching electronics, feed network, antenna or aperture, control system, aiming problem, thermal budget, and propagation environment. The target is not a point. It is a lossy, frequency-dependent biological volume inside clothing, architecture, and clutter.

Start with range. At distance, beam divergence and inverse-square-like spreading for unfocused regimes punish small transmitters. A larger aperture can produce a narrower beam, but aperture size affects concealability. Higher frequencies can support tighter beams for a given aperture but interact differently with walls and tissue. Lower frequencies penetrate some materials better but demand larger antennas for comparable directivity.

Then add the building. Reinforced concrete, metalized glass, wiring, plumbing, furniture, and reflections create a multipath environment. Sometimes reflections can concentrate fields locally. Often they simply make aiming unpredictable. A covert device that works reliably through arbitrary hotel walls is a more demanding claim than a device that works with line of sight.

Then add the waveform. If short pulses matter, the source must generate high peak power with the needed repetition structure. Energy storage can make high peaks possible without absurd average draw, but components still have limits. Switching leaves signatures. Thermal management remains. A backpack-sized unit is not impossible in the abstract; feasibility depends on what field must reach the target.

Then add physiology. The microwave auditory effect requires less causal ambition than persistent neurological injury. Producing a click is one problem. Producing vertigo is another. Producing long-lasting cognitive symptoms without obvious heating or structural injury is a much stronger claim. A mechanism may involve secondary pressure waves, vestibular perturbation, neural stimulation, vascular effects, or pathways not yet well characterized. Each must be tested rather than stacked.

This engineering burden is why device allegations in 2026 are interesting but not dispositive.

In January 2026, House Homeland Security Republicans opened an inquiry into media reports that Homeland Security Investigations had acquired a device allegedly connected to AHI concerns, reportedly for an eight-figure sum. The committee letter explicitly framed the reports as allegations and asked DHS for procurement and testing details. The release is at https://homeland.house.gov/2026/01/15/homeland-republicans-investigate-reports-of-havana-syndrome-device-acquired-by-dhs-under-the-biden-administration/. A purchase, if confirmed, would prove acquisition and investigative interest. It would not prove that the device caused historical incidents.

In February 2026, the Washington Post reported that a Norwegian government scientist had built a powerful pulsed-microwave apparatus in 2024, tested it on himself while trying to demonstrate harmlessness, and experienced neurological symptoms that people familiar with the episode considered similar in some respects to AHI. The Post also reported U.S. official visits to Norway. The story is at https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/02/14/havana-syndrome-cia-norway-experiment/. This is credible investigative reporting based largely on unnamed sources. It is not a peer-reviewed experiment. Self-experimentation without public dosimetry and controls cannot establish a mechanism.

Still, the report changes the empirical landscape if its core facts hold. It suggests that compact or at least practically constructible pulsed-energy systems can produce unexpected human effects under some conditions. That is a capability clue, not an attribution bridge.

The bridge requires matching.

What were the Norwegian waveform parameters?

What symptoms occurred, on what timescale, at what exposure?

What objective findings followed?

Does the apparatus produce the locality and directionality reported in core AHI cases?

Could it operate at the relevant distances and through the relevant structures?

Could it be concealed and powered?

Would its emissions have been detectable by sensors available at the sites?

Until those questions are answered, “a device exists” is a sentence with too much empty space around it.

Weapon physics without a weapon is speculation.

A weapon without matched incident physics is a curiosity.

The book refuses to confuse the two.
