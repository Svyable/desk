# The MRI That Found Nothing

In March 2024, NIH published the kind of result anomalous fields often claim never appears: a large, government-backed study that found mostly nothing distinctive.

Researchers examined U.S. government personnel and family members who had reported anomalous health incidents and compared them with matched controls. Advanced MRI analysis found no significant group differences in brain structure or function after correction for multiple comparisons. A companion clinical and biomarker study found no broad pattern of abnormalities that explained the participants’ symptoms, though imbalance, fatigue, post-traumatic stress, depression, and persistent postural-perceptual dizziness appeared in parts of the affected group.

The papers are in JAMA. NIH’s public summary is at https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-studies-find-severe-symptoms-havana-syndrome-no-evidence-mri-detectable-brain-injury-or-biological-abnormalities.

A null result like this creates two opposite temptations.

The first is to say the patients were fine.

They were not. Many reported severe, persistent symptoms. A normal MRI is not a certificate of wellness. Migraine, vestibular disorders, concussion symptoms, neuropathic pain, and numerous other conditions can be clinically significant without producing a diagnostic structural image.

The second temptation is to say the study proves nothing because the injuries might have been transient or too subtle to detect.

That move can immunize any hypothesis against evidence. If every null finding is reinterpreted as “the damage vanished before measurement,” the theory stops making useful predictions.

The NIH results should narrow the claim.

They make it harder to argue that the studied AHI population shares a persistent, MRI-visible brain injury of the sort earlier public rhetoric sometimes implied. They do not rule out an acute external exposure. They do not rule out transient physiological effects. They do not determine whether narrow core cases differ from the broader recruited population. They do not identify cause.

Timing is crucial. Many participants were evaluated long after their reported events. If a mechanism caused a brief vestibular or neurophysiological disruption followed by persistent functional symptoms, structural MRI could be normal later. That pathway is plausible in several medical contexts. But a proponent must then specify what early marker should have been present and how quickly it decays.

The null should force better timing.

This is why an AHI response protocol should behave like stroke medicine rather than an archaeology expedition. Immediate standardized examination matters. Blood and other samples should be collected on a clock. Vestibular and auditory testing should occur early. Environmental measurements should be captured before the physical scene changes. Delayed studies are still valuable, but they answer different questions.

The NIH papers also complicate the language of “brain injury.” Earlier studies from the University of Pennsylvania had reported neuroimaging differences in smaller groups. NIH did not replicate those group-level findings. Scientific correction is not betrayal. The correct response is to understand differences in samples, methods, timing, and statistical thresholds.

A field that cannot survive a null MRI never had a scientific hypothesis.

The stranger conclusion is that the null result leaves the central mystery almost where it began. Something can produce an acute, severe sensory event without leaving a durable structural signature. That is true of many ordinary phenomena. It would also be true of some proposed directed-energy mechanisms.

The MRI found nothing decisive.

That is not the same thing as nothing happening.
