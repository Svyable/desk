# The Cricket and the Recording

One of the strangest turns in the Havana story involved an insect.

Recordings associated in public reporting with some of the sounds in Cuba were analyzed by researchers who argued that the acoustic structure closely matched the calling song of the Indies short-tailed cricket. That finding became a powerful skeptical object: if the notorious “sonic attack” could be a cricket, perhaps the entire story had collapsed.

This is exactly the kind of result that should be welcomed and then kept within its jurisdiction.

Identifying a recorded sound does not automatically identify the cause of a person’s symptoms. A recording may capture the environmental sound that a person noticed after an event, a coincident insect, a device artifact, or the actual stimulus. If microwave auditory perception were involved, an ordinary microphone would not necessarily record the internally generated auditory sensation at all. Conversely, invoking the Frey effect to dismiss every inconvenient recording is equally illegitimate.

The cricket is a lesson in evidence type.

Acoustic analysis can answer: what source is consistent with this waveform? It cannot by itself answer: what produced the vestibular symptoms of a specific person at a specific time? Medical examination can characterize deficits, but it may not identify an external source. Intelligence collection can reveal whether an adversary researched a capability, but not whether that capability was present in the room. Each stream has boundaries.

The public debate repeatedly mistook contradiction between streams for proof that one stream was fraudulent.

Suppose a person reports a piercing sound and vertigo. A phone later captures a cricket-like signal outside the residence. There are at least several live possibilities. The cricket caused the sound and the symptoms had another cause. The cricket caused the sound and contributed to distress but not persistent illness. The recording is unrelated to the acute episode. The person experienced a nonacoustic stimulus while an insect happened to be audible. The report itself may have changed through memory and retelling. A competent investigation ranks these possibilities with evidence rather than selecting the funniest one.

Humor is a terrible forensic instrument.

The acoustic hypothesis deserves more serious treatment than the jokes gave it. Ultrasound can interact with tissue; intense airborne or structure-borne sound can produce discomfort and other effects. The 2022 IC experts panel considered ultrasound a plausible mechanism in some close-access scenarios, while finding it less likely to explain the broader pattern because propagation and access constraints are demanding. That is a useful engineering conclusion. It does not need a villain.

The cricket also illustrates why simultaneous multimodal recording is so valuable. If an event occurs while high-bandwidth microphones, ultrasonic sensors, RF receivers, magnetic-field sensors, and inertial/environmental monitors are synchronized, candidate mechanisms can be compared against the same clock. A microphone can say whether an external acoustic waveform was present. An RF sensor can say whether a pulse occurred. A wearable can record head motion, heart rate, and onset timing. None alone is decisive. Together they turn a story into an experiment the world accidentally ran.

Without that instrumentation, the evidentiary hierarchy becomes distorted. The thing that leaves the clearest trace wins attention, even if it is not causally central.

Crickets leave excellent recordings.

Covert beams, if they exist, may not.
