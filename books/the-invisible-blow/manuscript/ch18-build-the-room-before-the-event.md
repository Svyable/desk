# Build the Room Before the Event

The next anomalous health incident should be boring.

Not for the person experiencing it. For the investigator.

A boring investigation begins with synchronized clocks and instruments already turned on.

The room has an RF receiver covering a deliberately broad range, with high-rate capture triggered by unusual peaks or pulse structures. It has a second independent sensor architecture so one instrument cannot hallucinate the event alone. It has microphones extending into ultrasound, vibration sensors on walls and windows, magnetic-field monitoring, power-line logging, temperature, pressure, and network telemetry. Sensitive diplomatic spaces require careful security design, but the technical problem is solvable.

The person has a panic button that does not imply panic.

Pressing it freezes pre-event buffers, timestamps the onset, begins high-resolution recording, and initiates a medical protocol. The first exam is standardized. Audiology and vestibular testing occur quickly. Biological samples are collected on defined intervals when ethically appropriate. The scene is preserved like a crime scene without assuming a crime occurred.

The detector records raw data.

Analysts do not receive a red light labeled ATTACK.

That distinction matters. Automated systems should flag anomalous waveforms, not geopolitical causes. A burst at 3 GHz may be radar, interference, equipment failure, a nearby transmitter, or something intentionally directed. Classification comes later.

The equipment is red-teamed.

Engineers deliberately expose it to Wi-Fi bursts, cellular transmissions, radar sidelobes, microwave ovens, faulty switching supplies, ultrasound emitters, mechanical vibration, and benign pulsed sources. They test saturation, aliasing, intermodulation, spoofing, and clock drift. The detector learns its own humiliations before an adversary teaches them.

The system is deployed in control sites where no AHI has been reported.

Background matters. Embassies and secure facilities are electromagnetically weird places. Without baseline distributions, every spike becomes suspicious after the fact.

The protocol also includes negative cases. If a person experiences an acute episode and the instrument suite shows no unusual RF, acoustic, ultrasonic, magnetic, or environmental event within its validated detection envelope, the result should count. It may not eliminate unknown mechanisms, but it should move probabilities.

The medical side needs controls too. Comparable personnel without reported incidents should undergo portions of the same vestibular, cognitive, and imaging batteries so investigators know the background rate of abnormalities. The 2024 NIH work moved in this direction by using matched controls. Future work needs the timing advantage the NIH cohort often lacked.

Finally, the intelligence side should preregister its analytic questions as far as operationally possible. What observations would increase the probability of foreign action? What would decrease it? What collection should exist if an adversary program is active? What alternative hypotheses compete? How will dissent be preserved?

This sounds bureaucratic because it is.

Mysteries become science when procedure arrives before meaning.

The entire AHI controversy has been forced to infer a transient physical event from late medical measurements, human memory, classified fragments, and institutional behavior. That is an absurd way to study a field phenomenon if there is any chance it will happen again.

We do not need to decide whether Havana was a weapon before building the system that could catch one.

That may be the only conclusion in this book that does not require a confidence level.

Build the room.
