# The Frey Click

Allan Frey’s 1961 paper should be mandatory reading before anybody is allowed to say “Havana Syndrome” on television.

Not because it solves Havana. Because it strips away one bad argument.

Frey reported that people exposed to appropriately pulsed radio-frequency energy could perceive sounds described as buzzing, clicking, hissing, or knocking. The effect has since been called the microwave auditory effect or Frey effect. Later reviews, including work by James C. Lin and others, describe the dominant explanation as thermoelastic: a short RF pulse produces a minute transient temperature rise, tissue expands, a pressure wave propagates through the head, and the auditory system detects it. The subject does not need a loudspeaker. The air between source and ear does not need to carry the original signal as ordinary sound.

This matters because some early Havana reports involved sudden sounds or pressure sensations that seemed directional or localized. A person could move and perceive the phenomenon differently. That feature led some observers to insist the cause had to be acoustic and others to propose RF. The Frey effect shows that the categories are not clean. Electromagnetic input can produce an internally propagated acoustic event.

But the effect also disciplines the more dramatic story. Hearing clicks is not brain injury. A perceptual mechanism is not a weapon. The threshold for auditory perception does not automatically imply the energy deposition required for persistent neurological symptoms. If someone proposes that one exposure mechanism caused sound, vertigo, head pressure, nausea, cognitive problems, and long-term impairment, each step needs a plausible physiological pathway and dose.

This is where peak power and average power become politically inconvenient.

A pulse can have a high instantaneous field while keeping average deposited energy relatively low. That is why an argument based only on average heating can miss thermoelastic effects. It is also why the phrase “nonthermal” can confuse more than it clarifies. The Frey effect is often described as nonthermal in the ordinary sense that the person is not globally heated. Yet the standard mechanism involves an extremely small and rapid temperature change that launches a pressure wave. The distinction is not between heat and magic. It is between bulk temperature rise and transient thermoelastic response.

A weapon hypothesis has to exploit such distinctions quantitatively.

Suppose a device is outside a room. The signal must reach the target with enough peak field after distance, antenna pattern, atmospheric loss, wall attenuation, reflections, and body orientation. If the source uses a frequency that penetrates building materials well, the antenna size and focusing behavior change. If it uses millimeter waves, penetration and absorption behavior change again. If it needs high peak power, switching and storage become engineering constraints. If it creates substantial broadband emissions or harmonics, detection becomes more plausible. If it is directional, misalignment matters.

None of this makes the system impossible. None makes it easy.

The danger in anomalous research is that once one piece of weirdness becomes real, it acquires gravitational pull. The Frey effect is real, so every reported sound becomes microwave hearing. Ball lightning is real, so every luminous anomaly becomes plasma. Magnetoreception is real in animals, so every human intuition becomes a magnetic sense. The disciplined move is the opposite: use the verified phenomenon to sharpen what evidence should exist if it is the cause.

A classic microwave-auditory hypothesis predicts things. The perceived sound should correlate with pulse parameters. Shielding and geometry should matter. Instrumentation should detect the responsible field if it is present during an event. A sufficiently characterized source should reproduce at least the immediate sensory component under controlled and ethical conditions or in validated physical models. Competing acoustic sources should be excluded with synchronized microphones and RF sensors rather than retrospective testimony alone.

That last point is the unforgiving one. The original Havana episodes were not captured by a purpose-built sensor array. Investigators were handed a biological report after the physical event.

The physics is therefore strongest where the historical case is weakest.

Frey’s click does not tell us who stood outside a Havana residence. It tells us that the human head is not a passive object in an electromagnetic field. It tells us that “I heard something” and “there was no audible source in the room” are not mutually exclusive. It tells us that a mechanism can be surprising without being supernatural.

And it gives us a standard.

If the next event happens and all we have afterward is a story about a sound, we have learned almost nothing from the last ten years.
