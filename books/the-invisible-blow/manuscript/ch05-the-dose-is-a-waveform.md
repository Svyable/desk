# The Dose Is a Waveform

Toxicology trained us to ask how much.

Radio-frequency problems force a more annoying question: how much of what, arranged how, for how long, and in what sequence?

A glass of whisky has a volume and concentration. Ionizing radiation has dose metrics that, while complicated, support cumulative reasoning. An electromagnetic exposure is a field evolving in space and time. Compress it too early into a single average number and the parameter that matters can disappear.

This is not an argument against dosimetry. It is an argument for better dosimetry.

The microwave auditory effect makes the point vividly. Perception depends strongly on pulsed delivery. Peak power can matter in a way that average power alone does not describe. If an AHI mechanism involved pulsed RF, investigators would need waveform capture, not merely a consumer meter that reports a broad-band average. They would want frequency, pulse width, repetition rate, duty cycle, polarization, peak field, spectral content, timing, and spatial gradients. They would want synchronized environmental sensors and a clock accurate enough to compare the emission with the onset of symptoms.

That sounds like overkill until one remembers the claim being tested: a covert technology causing acute neurological or vestibular effects at a distance.

A claim that unusual deserves good instruments.

The National Academies in 2020 recommended that if research continued to support RF energy as a possible cause, the State Department should train and equip personnel to measure and characterize RF exposure in real time. That recommendation is one of the most important sentences in the entire public record because it translates speculation into a falsifiable operational behavior. The report is at https://doi.org/10.17226/25889.

It also reveals how little value retrospective arguments can have. Years after an incident, people debate whether a weapon could fit in a van, backpack, adjacent room, or nearby building. Those are engineering questions, but they are being asked without the field measurement that would constrain them. A detector present during the event could collapse pages of speculation in milliseconds.

The absence of a detected signal would not always close the case. Detectors have bandwidth limits. A clever adversary might operate outside expected bands, use brief emissions, exploit sensor saturation, or trigger effects through a mechanism no one instrumented. But this is not a reason to avoid sensors. It is a reason to publish their detection envelopes and failure modes.

Threat science improves when “not detected” includes what the instrument could have detected.

The same discipline applies to safety arguments. A frequent rebuttal to directed-RF hypotheses is that power sufficient to cause injury would produce obvious heating. Sometimes that may be right. But the answer depends on the claimed injury mechanism, frequency, pulse structure, exposed volume, and duration. Conversely, a frequent pro-weapon argument says pulsing permits dangerous effects at harmless average power. That can also become hand-waving. The fact that pulsing changes coupling does not grant unlimited biological leverage.

The waveform has to do work.

If the hypothesized effect is auditory, thermoelastic models give measurable constraints. If vestibular structures are implicated, one needs a pathway and experimental evidence. If blood-brain barrier changes are invoked, the relevant literature must be evaluated for exposure conditions, replication, and species. If oxidative stress is invoked, it cannot be treated as a unique signature because many insults produce it. If neuronal injury is claimed, a plausible deposition pattern and temporal course are needed.

This is where the AHI debate should have migrated after 2020: away from the noun “microwave” and toward a matrix of candidate waveforms and predicted effects.

Instead, much of the public argument remained at the level of allegiance. One side heard “directed energy” as a technically literate phrase for a Russian attack. The other heard it as science fiction. Both responses skipped the engineering layer where the hypothesis lives or dies.

The title of this chapter is deliberately annoying. “The dose is a waveform” is not literally sufficient; tissue geometry, frequency-dependent absorption, and many other variables matter. But it breaks the bad habit of asking only for average intensity.

A future investigative kit should think like an oscilloscope before it thinks like a court.

Capture first. Interpret later.
