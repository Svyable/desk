# The Attribution Machine

Intelligence analysis is often described as turning secrets into knowledge.

In practice it turns incomplete observations into probability statements under deadline.

Anomalous health incidents are a hostile input for that machine.

The incidents are medically heterogeneous. The physical environment is transient. Potential adversary programs, if they exist, would be deeply concealed. Technical mechanism and geopolitical attribution are entangled. Reporting is contaminated by publicity. The workforce has legitimate expectations of care. Congress has political stakes. Agencies have reputational stakes. Different collection disciplines see different slices.

Signals intelligence may intercept foreign discussion suggesting surprise or denial. Human intelligence may report a research program. Technical intelligence may characterize a device. Counterintelligence may observe suspicious travel. Medical teams may find no common lesion. Each can be accurate while pointing in a different direction.

The 2023 Intelligence Community Assessment produced a strong majority judgment against foreign attribution. Public reporting said adversaries themselves appeared puzzled by the incidents and that investigators found no global campaign. Those are meaningful negatives. An adversary running a large covert program should leave some combination of tasking, procurement, training, communications, doctrine, logistics, technical emissions, or human reporting.

Unless it does not.

Small programs can remain hidden. Compartmentalization can isolate users from developers. Technical collection can miss a capability. Intelligence services have historical examples of strategic surprise. The absence of collection is not the same as evidence of absence, but at some point repeated absence must reduce probability or intelligence analysis becomes theology.

This is why confidence levels matter.

When two agencies shifted in 2025, they did so at low confidence. That phrase is not a decorative hedge. It means the evidence base was limited, fragmented, or questionable enough that reasonable analysts could change their minds with new reporting. Public discussion flattened “roughly even chance at low confidence” into “spy agencies now say weapon possible.” The nuance is the assessment.

The 2026 recall introduces another layer: analytic integrity.

House Intelligence chairman Rick Crawford has alleged that the earlier assessments were produced in a way that violated analytic standards and were used to discredit affected personnel. Those are serious allegations. The recall means the documents no longer carry the institutional status they once did. It does not automatically reverse their substantive conclusions. An assessment can be procedurally flawed and still accidentally right. It can be procedurally sound and wrong.

The only way forward is to expose as much analytic structure as classification permits.

What key assumptions drove the majority?

What collection gaps were decisive?

How were cases weighted?

Did agencies share the same case definition?

What new reporting caused the 2025 shifts?

Which judgments depended on lack of adversary motive versus lack of capability?

What alternatives were explicitly tested?

Were dissenting views preserved?

Those questions are not partisan. They are the machinery of intelligence.

An invisible weapon, if it exists, creates an unusually tempting failure mode: when you cannot see the device, the assessment itself becomes the battlefield.

That is dangerous.

The job of analysis is not to protect a prior verdict.

It is to remain revisable without becoming impressionable.
