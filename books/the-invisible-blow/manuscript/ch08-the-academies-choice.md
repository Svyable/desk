# The Academies’ Choice

The 2020 National Academies report became famous for one conclusion and should be remembered for its uncertainty.

The committee considered a range of possible mechanisms for the illnesses reported by U.S. government employees and families at overseas posts. It reviewed infectious disease, chemicals, psychological and social factors, acoustic mechanisms, and radio-frequency energy. It concluded that directed, pulsed RF energy appeared to be the most plausible mechanism for the distinctive acute symptoms in the cases available to it.

The phrase “most plausible” did enormous political work.

It was repeated as “the National Academies found microwave attacks.” That is not what the report established. The committee did not identify an emitter, reconstruct exposure parameters, attribute an adversary, or experimentally reproduce the syndrome. It reasoned comparatively from an imperfect clinical record and from known or reported biological effects.

This is not a trivial distinction. Comparative plausibility can be scientifically important even when absolute probability is unknown.

If every candidate explanation is weak, the least weak candidate wins a ranking without becoming proven. A detective can say one route into a locked room is more plausible than the others while having no evidence anyone used it. The National Academies’ result was a mechanism ranking under data constraints.

The report also did something more valuable: it described how badly the system had been instrumented. The committee noted variability in case timing, incomplete access to individual-level information, heterogeneity of symptoms, and delays between events and evaluation. Many chronic symptoms were nonspecific. Those limitations should reduce confidence in any simple unifying story.

Yet the committee did not retreat into “we can know nothing.” It recommended operational changes, including real-time RF measurement capability if the mechanism remained plausible.

This is scientific adulthood. Uncertainty is not an excuse for passivity.

A second important feature of the report is its treatment of psychological and social factors. The committee did not consider them sufficient to explain the distinctive sudden-onset features in the early cases, but it allowed that they could contribute to persistent nonspecific symptoms. That mixed model is harder to communicate than either “weapon” or “mass psychogenic illness,” and therefore it often disappears.

Human illness is allowed to have more than one cause.

An acute vestibular event can produce persistent postural-perceptual dizziness. Fear can amplify symptoms that began with a physical trigger. An injury can coexist with depression. A workplace rumor can change symptom monitoring without fabricating the original event. None of those statements delegitimizes the patient. The binary between “physical” and “psychological” is one of the least helpful inheritances in medicine.

The report is also a reminder that scientific committees are products of their evidence window. The National Academies worked before later NIH results and before later intelligence reporting. Its conclusion should not be frozen into scripture. New data can strengthen or weaken it. The correct question is what evidence would move the ranking.

A reproducible exposure-response experiment would move it.

A captured field during a clinical event would move it dramatically.

A foreign device with measured emissions matching predicted physiology would move it.

A large, well-matched clinical study showing a distinctive biomarker would move it.

A strong demonstration that the defining acute symptoms occur at similar rates in comparable unexposed populations would move it the other way.

The value of the Academies report is that it put pulsed RF inside the zone of serious inquiry. Its limitation is that public discourse treated entry into that zone as conviction.

The committee opened a door.

It did not tell us who walked through it.
