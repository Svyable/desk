# A Sound No Microphone Needs

There is a useful cruelty in the phrase *Havana Syndrome*. It makes a disputed cluster of events sound like a diagnosed disease and gives a city the job of carrying a mechanism nobody has established. The U.S. government eventually adopted the colder term “anomalous health incidents,” which is bureaucratically ugly and scientifically better. An incident can be real without sharing a cause with another incident. A symptom can be severe without revealing the instrument that produced it. A pattern can deserve investigation long before it deserves a name.

The book begins with one narrow fact that is stranger than most of the speculation built on top of it: human beings can sometimes perceive pulsed radio-frequency energy as sound. Allan H. Frey reported the effect in 1961. Later work usually explains the basic auditory phenomenon through tiny, rapid thermoelastic expansion in tissue: a pulse deposits a small amount of energy, the resulting pressure wave reaches the auditory apparatus, and the subject perceives a click, buzz, or knock even though no ordinary acoustic wave first traveled through the room. The phenomenon is not folklore. Nor does its existence prove that anybody in Havana was attacked with a microwave weapon.

That distinction is the spine of this book.

The public debate keeps collapsing four different questions. Can electromagnetic energy produce sensory or biological effects in people? Could a device be engineered to deliver some of those effects at useful range? Did such a device cause a subset of reported anomalous health incidents? If so, who used it and why? A yes to the first does not supply a yes to the second. A yes to the second would not identify a single historical case. Even a technically persuasive reconstruction of one case would not establish a global campaign.

The National Academies’ 2020 report is an unusually clean example of how language changes as it passes through public argument. The committee concluded that directed, pulsed radio-frequency energy was the mechanism it considered most plausible for the distinctive acute symptoms in the cases it reviewed. It also stressed data limitations, heterogeneity, incomplete access to individual information, and the possibility that persistent symptoms had multiple contributing causes. The report is freely readable at https://doi.org/10.17226/25889. It did not name an attacker. It did not demonstrate a weapon. It did not claim every person grouped under the later AHI label had encountered the same thing.

Two years later, an intelligence-community experts panel worked from a different information base. ODNI said the panel received more than a thousand classified documents, along with incident reports, scientific briefings, medical records, and sensitive intelligence. Its declassified executive summary concluded that a subset of cases could not be easily explained by known environmental or medical conditions and that pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly in the radio-frequency range, plausibly explained the core characteristics. The summary is at https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/2022_02_01_AHI_Executive_Summary_FINAL_Redacted.pdf. Again: plausible mechanism, not attribution.

Then the story bent in the other direction. In March 2023, a coordinated Intelligence Community Assessment said most agencies judged it very unlikely that a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported incidents. The assessment did not erase the physics finding. It changed the answer to a different question: attribution. In March 2024, NIH published two JAMA studies after a multi-year examination of affected personnel and matched controls. The investigators found no significant group differences in MRI measures of brain structure or function after correction for multiple comparisons, and no broad biomarker signature that explained the reported symptoms. The NIH summary is at https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-studies-find-severe-symptoms-havana-syndrome-no-evidence-mri-detectable-brain-injury-or-biological-abnormalities. That finding did not prove nothing happened. It did remove one of the easier versions of the story: a common, persistent, MRI-visible traumatic brain injury across the studied group.

In January 2025 the intelligence consensus cracked. Five of seven agencies retained the “very unlikely” foreign-adversary judgment, while two agencies shifted because of new reporting about possible foreign capability. Public accounts described one of those agencies as assigning roughly even odds that a foreign actor had developed or deployed a novel capability in some cases, at low confidence. The exact evidentiary basis remained classified. That is a maddening arrangement for public reasoning: the confidence language is visible while much of the evidence producing it is not.

Then, on June 11, 2026, House Intelligence Committee chairman Rick Crawford announced that the Director of National Intelligence had recalled the earlier AHI Intelligence Community Assessments. The committee statement is at https://intelligence.house.gov/2026/06/11/chairman-crawford-statement-on-director-gabbards-recall-of-the-flawed-intelligence-community-assessments-on-ahi/. Crawford’s accusation that the assessments were “manufactured” is his characterization, not a scientific finding and not proof of foreign attack. The recall matters because the U.S. government itself has withdrawn confidence from public assessments that had been treated as closing documents. It does not tell us what the replacement assessment will say.

This is why the story is worth a book now. Not because the mystery has been solved. Because the layers have become visible.

A human nervous system can be made to hear energy that a room does not hear. A government can know that fact, study it, classify adjacent work, and still be wrong about a specific incident. A patient can be profoundly ill and still have normal MRI findings years later. An intelligence service can be unable to find an adversary program because no program exists, because the program is well hidden, because its collection is pointed in the wrong direction, or because it has mistaken a heterogeneous set of events for a single operation. A congressional committee can expose analytic failures and still overstate the causal conclusion it prefers.

The invisible blow, if there was one, has two meanings. One is physical: energy that does not arrive like a bullet, chemical, or audible blast. The other is epistemic: an event whose evidence is distributed across medicine, physics, counterintelligence, personnel policy, and classified collection, with no institution naturally owning the whole problem.

The mistake is to ask for one verdict too soon.
