# The Russian-Shaped Hole

Russia occupies this story even when the evidence does not.

That needs careful handling.

The Soviet Union studied radio-frequency bioeffects. Soviet services illuminated the U.S. Embassy in Moscow. Russian intelligence has a documented history of aggressive overseas operations. Investigative journalists have reported associations between suspected Russian intelligence personnel and locations around some alleged AHI events. Those facts create a prior worth examining.

They do not create a chain of custody.

Attribution requires more than compatibility with character.

The strongest public case for Russian involvement has come from investigative reporting and testimony, including work by The Insider, Der Spiegel, and CBS’s *60 Minutes*. Reporters have described travel overlaps, Russian military-intelligence units, awards, and research connected to directed-energy concepts. Some affected officials and former investigators consider those patterns compelling. Critics argue that the overlaps are weak, cherry-picked, or expected given the global footprint of Russian intelligence.

The intelligence community’s public judgments have moved.

The March 2023 assessment said most agencies considered foreign involvement very unlikely. In January 2025, five of seven agencies reportedly remained there, while two shifted because of new information about possible foreign capability. In 2026, the earlier public assessments were recalled for reassessment. None of those institutional moves publicly identifies Russia as the perpetrator.

This is a Russian-shaped hole: a hypothesis with enough historical and operational fit to remain live, but without publicly available event-level evidence strong enough to fill the outline.

Intelligence analysts know this problem well. A capable adversary is often present in places where strange things happen because capable adversaries are present around embassies, intelligence officers, and senior officials as a matter of routine. Surveillance overlap is not attack attribution. Travel data can be powerful when timing, identity, access, capability, and motive converge. It can also generate false patterns in a dense operational environment.

The right question is not “does Russia seem like the kind of state that would do this?”

Many states research technologies they never deploy. Many intelligence services pursue surveillance methods that could be misread as attack. Many covert programs create incidental exposure. A beam intended to interrogate electronics is a different operation from a beam intended to injure a person, even if the hardware overlaps.

Motive also matters. A campaign that injures diplomats risks enormous escalation for ambiguous strategic gain. A targeted counterintelligence tool used against specific officers might have a more intelligible logic. A surveillance technology with unintended bioeffects would imply another. The pattern of victims and circumstances should discriminate among them.

The problem is that public case data are too sparse to do this confidently.

This is where secrecy produces mythology. The government says it cannot disclose operational details. Journalists obtain fragments. Former officials hint that classified evidence is stronger. Skeptics demand proof that cannot be published. Believers treat the absence of publication as proof of classification.

A disciplined book cannot resolve that loop.

It can insist that Russia remain a hypothesis, not a synonym for mechanism.
