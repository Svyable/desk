# When Care Becomes Evidence

A government can make a scientific mystery worse by asking sick people to solve it before they are allowed treatment.

That happened here often enough to deserve its own chapter.

Affected personnel have described difficulty obtaining recognition, specialized care, workers’ compensation, and consistent support. GAO documented gaps in the Department of Defense response. Senate and House committees criticized agency handling. Congress passed legislation to provide assistance.

None of this proves an attack.

It proves an institutional failure mode.

When care is tied to causal belief, every medical interaction becomes an argument about geopolitics. A patient who accepts a diagnosis such as persistent postural-perceptual dizziness may fear that doing so will be interpreted as conceding the event was psychological. A physician may avoid discussing stress because the patient hears dismissal. An intelligence manager may worry that validating symptoms validates a hostile-actor hypothesis. Analysts may become resented as gatekeepers to medicine.

The epistemic cost is enormous.

Medicine works better when it can say: your symptoms are real, this treatment may help, and we do not yet know what initiated them.

Intelligence works better when it can say: we are evaluating hostile attribution independently of whether you qualify for care.

The two sentences should never have been enemies.

There is a broader lesson for anomalous phenomena. People often become attached to extraordinary causal theories because institutions use mundane explanations as social demotions. If “migraine,” “functional,” “stress-related,” or “psychogenic” is delivered as “nothing happened,” patients rationally resist those labels. The scientific community then mistakes resistance to humiliation for resistance to evidence.

The opposite failure also occurs. Advocates may insist that acknowledging suffering requires endorsing an exotic mechanism. That traps patients inside an unproven narrative.

Care should be mechanism-agnostic where possible.

Evidence should be compassion-agnostic in the sense that standards do not change with sympathy.

Those positions are not contradictory. They are how a serious institution behaves under uncertainty.

The invisible blow may have injured trust more reliably than it injured tissue.

That damage is measurable.
