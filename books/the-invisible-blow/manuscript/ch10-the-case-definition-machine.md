# The Case-Definition Machine

A syndrome can be manufactured without a single patient being dishonest.

The machine is administrative.

First, an unusual cluster appears. Investigators identify several features that seem distinctive. A reporting channel opens. People in the same workforce learn the features. Physicians begin asking about them. News reports circulate. The case definition broadens so nobody important is missed. The denominator grows. Many new cases lack the features that made the original cluster unusual. Analysts then discover heterogeneity and conclude there is no single cause.

This can happen even if the first cases shared a real external trigger.

It can also happen if the first cluster was heterogeneous from the beginning.

The only defense is taxonomy.

The 2022 expert panel’s “core characteristics” were an attempt at one. Sudden audio-vestibular phenomena, near-simultaneous symptoms, strong locality or directionality, and no known environmental or medical explanation form a much narrower event class than “headache, dizziness, fatigue, cognitive complaint.” The latter are common symptoms with enormous background prevalence.

A useful investigation should maintain at least three populations: narrow core events, broader possible events, and administratively eligible affected personnel. Those groups serve different purposes and should never be silently merged.

Care eligibility should be generous.

Causal inference should be strict.

This sounds harsh until the alternative is considered. If access to treatment requires meeting a narrow research definition, sick people are abandoned. If the research definition expands to match every person who deserves care, the scientific signal dissolves. Ethical policy and valid inference require different thresholds.

The U.S. response sometimes confused them. GAO’s July 2024 report focused on access to care and found implementation problems across the Department of Defense system. That report is available at https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106593. Its existence tells us something important regardless of mechanism: a government can fail its people by making medical support contingent on the resolution of a national-security mystery.

A person with disabling vertigo does not need Vladimir Putin’s name on a lab report before vestibular therapy is appropriate.

The case-definition machine also interacts with intelligence. Analysts look for clustering by location, assignment, foreign-service contact, adversary presence, and operational role. If the event pool is noisy, those patterns can disappear. If the pool is narrowed after looking at outcomes, analysts can accidentally fit the story they expect. A robust analysis needs preregistered or at least clearly versioned criteria and sensitivity tests across definitions.

How many core cases exist under each definition?

What fraction have a contemporaneous directional sensation?

How many involve multiple people in the same place at the same time?

How many have objective vestibular findings?

How many have a plausible conventional explanation discovered later?

How do those rates compare with matched personnel who never filed AHI reports?

Those are boring questions. Boring questions are the price of extraordinary conclusions.

The phrase “Havana Syndrome” encourages the opposite. It asks the public to imagine one thing.

The data may contain several things.
