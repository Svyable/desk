# Chemistry Without a Name

There is a peculiar kind of explanation that appears whenever an instrument has clearly measured something and nobody knows the reagent.

Call it chemistry.

The word is true and almost empty.

By late 1976 the Viking results made some kind of unusual Martian surface chemistry difficult to avoid. Gas Exchange had shown that humidification could release substantial oxygen. Labeled Release had shown that adding a dilute organic solution could produce labeled gas. The molecular-analysis instrument had not supplied the expected biological context. A reactive soil was not a desperate invention. Mars had behaved reactively in several independent chambers.

The problem was identity.

Hydrogen peroxide was an obvious candidate family. Ultraviolet light reaching a dry surface in a carbon-dioxide atmosphere could drive photochemistry, and highly oxidized species might persist in ways that would be unusual in wetter terrestrial soils. Superoxide chemistry on mineral surfaces offered another family. Investigators explored whether such oxidants could account for oxygen release and nutrient oxidation.

Each candidate faced constraints from Viking itself.

The LR active agent survived some storage and temperature conditions but was damaged by modest heating. It occurred at both landing sites. Material from beneath a rock responded. The reaction had a particular time course and interacted strangely with repeated nutrient additions. Gas Exchange chemistry had its own kinetics. A candidate that merely oxidized organic molecules in a beaker was not enough.

This is where the distinction between a placeholder and a mechanism becomes visible.

"Unknown oxidant" is a placeholder. It says the nonbiological interpretation is plausible because the environment appears strongly oxidizing, but it does not tell us what substance is present, how much, how it formed, how long it lasts, what it does at 10°C versus 50°C, how it reacts with seven different organic substrates, or what products it makes.

A mechanism earns detail.

That detail took decades because Mars chemistry had to be discovered by later missions.

Phoenix's 2008 wet-chemistry experiment was transformative not because perchlorate is intrinsically exotic. Perchlorate exists on Earth. The important thing was that a spacecraft directly measured significant perchlorate in Martian soil. The chemical inventory available to retrospective Viking models changed overnight. Mars did not merely have an abstract oxidizing surface. It contained an oxychlorine reservoir.

At first this looked like only a partial answer. Perchlorate itself is comparatively unreactive at the low temperatures of the Viking biology incubations. One cannot simply pour perchlorate onto the LR nutrient under mild conditions and assume the old gas curve appears. This is a place where popular retellings often skip a necessary chemical step.

Radiation supplies that step.

The Martian surface is exposed to ultraviolet radiation and, because of the thin atmosphere and lack of a global magnetic field like Earth's, significant ionizing radiation. Oxychlorine salts subjected to radiation do not have to remain chemically identical to the parent material. Bonds break. Reactive intermediates and daughter species can form. Defects and trapped gases can accumulate in solids.

Quinn and colleagues turned this into a specific experiment. They exposed calcium perchlorate to gamma radiation in a carbon-dioxide atmosphere and reported production of hypochlorite, chlorine dioxide and trapped oxygen. They then showed how release of trapped oxygen and reaction of hypochlorite with amino acids could reproduce important aspects of Viking's biology results.

Notice what has changed from the 1970s.

The explanation no longer requires the phrase "perhaps some oxidant." It has a parent salt detected on Mars, an energy source known to operate on Mars, named reaction products and laboratory behavior connected to Viking reagents.

This is a much better abiotic hypothesis.

It is still a hypothesis about the Viking samples.

The exact calcium-perchlorate concentration at Chryse and Utopia was not measured. The radiation dose history of the sampled grains is not known. The abundance and lifetime of hypochlorite or chlorine dioxide in those samples is not known. Mineral surfaces can accelerate or suppress reactions. The soil was handled by the lander, stored, heated or incubated under conditions that differed from the open surface. A mechanism can be physically available on Mars without being the actual mechanism in one old experiment.

The forensic standard should therefore be proportional.

Laboratory oxychlorine work can establish possibility and, with sufficiently realistic conditions, plausibility. It can eliminate the claim that no known nonbiological chemistry could behave in the required broad way. It cannot, without site-specific evidence, establish historical identity.

That may sound like an unsatisfying boundary, but it is exactly what makes the later chemistry scientifically valuable. A good alternative explanation does not have to produce certainty to change the evidentiary balance. It only has to become more plausible than it was before.

The LR biological interpretation was strongest when the competing chemistry was a hand wave. As the hand wave acquired salts, radiation products and reaction pathways, the likelihood ratio changed.

The process also teaches something uncomfortable about anomaly debates.

Advocates often say an anomaly remains unexplained because no alternative reproduces every detail. Skeptics often say an anomaly is explained as soon as a broad mechanism exists. Both standards are strategically convenient. The honest position sits between them.

If one finds a mechanism that naturally generates the main signal and several control behaviors, the anomaly is less anomalous even if minor details remain. If important observations require unrelated ad hoc additions, the explanation remains incomplete. Scientific judgment consists partly in deciding which details are central enough to demand reproduction.

For Viking, the central LR details include first-injection radioactive-gas evolution, thermal sensitivity, broad similarity at both landing sites, response in under-rock material, changes with storage and the second-injection behavior. The exact hierarchy can be debated, but an explanation that addresses only "organic nutrient caused gas" is not enough.

The second-injection result is instructive because it demonstrates that one trace can contain multiple processes.

After the first nutrient injection, radioactive gas rose and later plateaued. When additional nutrient was injected days later, the headspace radioactivity did not simply rise again as one might expect if a surviving microbial population had run short of substrate. It initially dropped. Levin and Straat later reproduced a similar decrease in laboratory hardware and attributed it to a physicochemical carbon-dioxide/water/soil equilibrium. In other words, the investigators arguing for a biological origin of the first response identified a nonbiological explanation for an important feature of the same experimental run.

The signal was already hybrid.

That possibility should humble both camps. An LR curve need not have one cause from beginning to end. The first rise could arise from one process; later partitioning of carbon dioxide could reshape the trace; water could activate additional chemistry; adsorption could change gas abundance. Even if biology were present, the instrument would be measuring biology through a chemically active matrix. Even if biology were absent, more than one abiotic pathway could contribute.

We like causal purity because it makes graphs readable.

Mars was under no obligation to provide it.

The same complication appears in Gas Exchange. Oxygen release upon humidification can arise from stored or trapped oxygen, decomposition of reactive species, surface reactions and changes in adsorption. Carbon dioxide changes can include acid-base equilibria and reactions with carbonates. The term "soil reactivity" is therefore better understood as a network than as one magic compound.

This network view changes what it would mean to explain Viking.

Perhaps no single oxidant should reproduce every experiment. A radiation-processed soil could contain several reactive oxygen and chlorine species, adsorbed gases, mineral catalysts and salts. Adding water reorganizes the network. Adding amino acids consumes some species and creates products. Heating removes or transforms others. Storage lets unstable components decay. Different lander chambers perturb the same initial material in different ways.

A complex abiotic mixture begins to resemble metabolism at the level of gross gas exchange because metabolism is also a network of coupled reactions.

This is not evidence that life and geochemistry are indistinguishable in principle. It is evidence that low-dimensional measurements such as one gas count can be many-to-one with respect to cause.

The solution is higher-dimensional measurement.

Identify products, not just gas quantity. Measure chirality. Track multiple isotopes. Characterize oxidants directly. Vary substrate concentration and water activity. Measure heat-response curves rather than two temperatures. Test reaction stoichiometry. Separate mineral fractions. Look for catalytic regeneration versus finite reagent exhaustion. Search for macromolecular organization. Observe whether the active system can maintain itself, repair, grow or reproduce under changing conditions.

Viking had only fragments of that arsenal.

Later Mars missions largely moved away from direct metabolism tests toward habitability, mineralogy and organic chemistry. That shift made sense after Viking because it attacked the missing context. Before asking whether Martian dirt eats, learn what the dirt is.

The irony is that this strategy has made the Viking experiment more interesting, not less.

Phoenix and Curiosity built the geochemical world the 1976 data had been missing. Every new salt, oxidant pathway, organic-preservation mechanism and radiation reaction narrows the space of plausible interpretations.

The result may ultimately be mundane chemistry.

But it is no longer chemistry without a name.
