# Building the Oxidant

For years the nonbiological explanation for Viking had a naming problem.

The Martian soil was called oxidizing, reactive, chemically active. Hydrogen peroxide, superoxide and ultraviolet-generated species appeared in models. These were serious proposals, not hand waving, but the evidentiary structure remained awkward. Viking had measured behavior. The favored interpretation required a substance the lander had not directly identified.

A missing reagent can survive a long time in science if the environment makes it plausible.

Mars gave the oxidant hypothesis plenty of environmental support. The surface is exposed to ultraviolet radiation. The atmosphere is thin. Liquid water is scarce. Organic material delivered by meteorites appears to be efficiently destroyed or transformed near the surface. Gas Exchange had already demonstrated rapid oxygen release under humidification. A reactive surface was not an arbitrary rescue.

Still, there is a large difference between saying an unknown oxidant probably exists and showing a specific Martian chemical pathway that creates one.

Phoenix supplied the reservoir.

Its Wet Chemistry Laboratory mixed Martian soil with an aqueous solution and measured dissolved ions. The samples contained substantial perchlorate, roughly 0.4 to 0.6 percent by mass in the analyzed material, along with other salts. This was not a retrospective inference from Viking. It was a new direct measurement at a new landing site.

Perchlorate's discovery was immediately relevant to Mars habitability, resources, water activity and organic preservation. It was also awkward for simple Viking reinterpretations because perchlorate itself is not reactive enough under the LR incubation conditions to explain the whole biology response. The parent salt did not neatly fit the old placeholder.

Radiation changed the question from "is perchlorate the oxidant?" to "what does the Martian radiation environment do to perchlorate over time?"

That question is much better.

Richard Quinn and colleagues exposed calcium perchlorate to gamma radiation in a carbon-dioxide environment. The material produced hypochlorite, trapped oxygen and chlorine dioxide. Those products are chemically more aggressive than the parent perchlorate. The team showed that trapped oxygen release and hypochlorite reactions with amino acids could account for important observations from Viking's biology package.

For the first time, a retrospective abiotic model could be drawn as a chain with independently motivated links.

Mars contains perchlorate.

Mars irradiates its surface.

Irradiated perchlorate can generate reactive products.

Some of those products react with compounds Viking added or can release gases Viking observed.

This chain is not proof that it happened in a particular Viking sample. It is far stronger than an unnamed oxidant.

The distinction between parent and daughter chemistry is essential because simplified accounts often say "perchlorate fooled Viking." That phrasing makes the chemistry sound like a reagent bottle experiment. In reality, the interesting model is a planetary processing system. Salts accumulate or form. Radiation modifies them. Reactive species build up or decay. Temperature, mineralogy and atmospheric composition shape the steady state. Sampling and storage alter it again. Then Viking adds water and organics.

The false positive, if that is what LR was, was manufactured by Mars before the lander arrived and completed by the lander's protocol afterward.

This is a richer explanation than "oxidizing soil" because it predicts structure.

A radiation-produced reactive inventory should vary with exposure age, shielding depth, latitude, mineral host and perhaps grain size. It should have characteristic thermal stability. Storage away from the surface radiation environment should allow some species to decay. Heating should accelerate decomposition. Water should mobilize or consume some components. Under-rock material may differ depending on penetration depth and mixing.

These predictions can be compared with Viking's peculiar controls.

The under-rock result is not fatal because ionizing radiation penetrates rock far more effectively than ultraviolet light. The mild-heat sensitivity becomes plausible if the active daughter species is thermally labile even while parent perchlorate remains stable. Long storage can reduce activity if the reactive products are not replenished. The first nutrient injection can consume a finite reservoir, weakening response to the second.

This coherence is why oxychlorine chemistry deserves more weight than many earlier generic oxidant proposals.

But coherence can seduce us into retrospective certainty.

The Viking landers did not measure perchlorate. Phoenix did, at a different site more than three decades later. Other Mars data support widespread chlorine and oxychlorine chemistry, but the relevant concentrations at Chryse and Utopia remain inferred. Quinn's gamma irradiation experiments used controlled materials and doses; the exact natural radiation history, mineral mixtures and timescales at the Viking sites were not replicated molecule by molecule. Reactive products are short-lived by definition relative to stable salts, and their abundance in real regolith depends on competing formation and destruction pathways.

A plausible planetary mechanism is not an archived sample.

There is another complication. Different Viking observations may require different species. Oxygen release in Gas Exchange, oxidation of labeled organics in LR and destruction of organic molecules during high-temperature GCMS analysis are not necessarily one reaction. Parent perchlorate can matter greatly during pyrolysis while radiation products matter under low-temperature wet chemistry. The Martian soil may have contained a suite of chlorine and oxygen species, not one master oxidant.

This multiplicity is scientifically reasonable and rhetorically dangerous.

If every inconvenient observation is assigned to a different unmeasured species, the model becomes difficult to falsify. A strong reconstruction therefore needs mass balance. Begin with measured or plausible concentrations of parent phases. Apply known radiation yields. Calculate expected steady-state daughter abundances. Then ask whether those abundances can supply the oxygen and oxidation capacity observed without exceeding the chemical budget.

The literature has made pieces of this calculation possible. The book's publication-ready version needs to make them visible rather than hiding behind adjectives such as *strong* and *reactive*.

How many moles of labeled substrate were added? How much radioactive gas evolved? What minimum oxidizing capacity does that imply if the reaction is abiotic? What concentration of hypochlorite or equivalent species would be required in the sample mass? How does that compare with laboratory yields from irradiated perchlorate and with plausible Martian chlorine inventories?

This is where the argument should live.

A biological model also has a material budget. How many cells, with what metabolic turnover, are needed to generate the observed product? How much biomass carbon should accompany them? Would Viking GCMS have detected it under the relevant chemistry? Does the required abundance fit plausible water and energy availability?

The dispute becomes less ideological when both sides have to pay their stoichiometric bills.

One reason "life versus oxidant" persisted so long is that the terms are at different explanatory scales. *Life* names an organized causal system containing thousands of reactions. *Oxidant* names a chemical property. Comparing them invites asymmetry. The abiotic model should be expanded into its actual system: mineral phases, salts, radiation, reactive intermediates, water, organics, adsorption and gas partitioning. The biological model should be specified into organisms, abundance, physiology, substrate use and stress response.

Then the systems can be compared.

There is an unexpected beauty in the oxychlorine story even if it finally buries the life claim.

The reactive soil would not be a boring background contaminant. It would be a planetary chemical battery charged by radiation and discharged by water and organics. Viking's biology experiments would have been among the first instruments to probe that battery accidentally. Gas Exchange, LR and the later organic-analysis controversy would become different windows on the same surface-processing regime.

That is a substantial scientific discovery, even if no organism participated.

It also suggests why direct life detection is hard in places where free energy and strong gradients exist. Life occupies reactive environments precisely because chemistry is happening there. The more chemically interesting a world is, the more nonliving pathways can imitate broad metabolic observables.

A dead world can breathe when wetted.

A dead world can consume organics.

A dead world can release oxygen.

A dead world can erase molecules during analysis.

Those sentences do not make life detection impossible. They make specificity expensive.

The oxidant hypothesis became scientifically mature when Mars missions and laboratory chemistry began paying that price.

The final payment is still due: a full flight-trace reconstruction from a quantitatively justified Martian soil.

Until then, the oxidant has a name.

It does not yet have a confession.
