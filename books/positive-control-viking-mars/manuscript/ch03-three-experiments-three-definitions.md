# Three Experiments, Three Definitions

Viking did not carry a jury. It carried three witnesses who had been taught different languages.

The Labeled Release experiment spoke in radioactive gas. Gas Exchange spoke in oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen and the changing composition of a tiny atmosphere above wet Martian soil. Pyrolytic Release spoke in radioactive carbon retained after exposure to labeled carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide. All three were called biology experiments, but that administrative category can hide how different their notions of living activity really were.

The differences are easier to see if we imagine three hypothetical Martian organisms.

The first is a heterotroph that consumes small organic compounds when liquid water appears and releases carbon dioxide. Labeled Release is built for it. The second uses atmospheric carbon as raw material, perhaps driven by light or chemical energy, and builds organic matter from it. Pyrolytic Release is closer to its world. The third changes its gaseous environment when hydrated or fed, consuming one gas or releasing another. Gas Exchange has a chance to notice.

Now imagine a fourth organism that does none of those things under Viking's incubation conditions. Perhaps it is dormant, poisoned by the nutrient concentrations, adapted to brines rather than pure water, dependent on a substrate not supplied, or metabolically so slow in the cold that the experiment ends before the organism wakes. Viking could land directly on it and call the soil inactive.

This was not a bug unique to 1970s astrobiology. It is what an assay is.

An assay is an invitation phrased narrowly enough to measure a reply.

The multiple-experiment strategy was NASA's way of avoiding dependence on one invitation. If all three experiments returned coherent biological behavior, and if organic analysis supported them, the case would be formidable. If one failed because its particular assumptions were wrong, another might still succeed. Redundancy was not duplication. It was diversification of epistemic risk.

Then Mars produced the most inconvenient outcome: each experiment found activity, but the activities did not cohere into an organism.

Gas Exchange deserves more attention than it usually receives because its oxygen result changed the emotional weather around LR. In the preliminary Viking paper, the investigators reported that when Martian soil was exposed to water vapor, a substantial amount of oxygen appeared in the chamber atmosphere. Carbon dioxide also increased. This happened quickly enough and strongly enough that a reactive chemical surface became hard to ignore.

One can see the interpretive pressure immediately. If the Martian soil was capable of releasing oxygen through an abiotic reaction when merely humidified, then the surface was not a passive mineral background. It was chemically loaded. Add water and hidden capacity became gas.

That observation did not explain the radioactive gas in LR. It changed the prior probability that Mars could produce surprising gases without life.

These distinctions matter because scientific arguments often move through analogy. Gas Exchange shows water-triggered nontrivial chemistry; therefore perhaps LR's water-bearing nutrient solution also triggers chemistry. The analogy is reasonable but incomplete. The responsible reactants, products, kinetics and thermal sensitivities still have to match. A planet being chemically strange does not mean every strange result is chemical. Yet once Gas Exchange established a reactive surface, the burden on the biological interpretation rose.

Pyrolytic Release produced a different complication. The experiment was designed to test whether Martian material could incorporate carbon from a simulated atmosphere. A small amount of radioactive carbon became associated with the sample in a way affected by heating. Again the outline resembled biology without delivering exclusivity.

This is where a voting model breaks down.

If LR is "positive," PR "weakly positive" and GEx "chemical," one cannot count two votes to one. The experiments are conditionally dependent. A chemically reactive soil revealed by Gas Exchange provides an alternative background mechanism for interpreting the other assays. Conversely, heat sensitivity shared across experiments may indicate a common fragile reactive species or may strengthen a biological hypothesis, depending on the mechanistic details. A result from one chamber changes the meaning of another because all chambers sampled variants of the same environment.

The Viking biology package therefore behaved less like parallel replication and more like a network of constraints.

This can be frustrating to readers trained on the clean logic of controlled experiments. We want one variable changed at a time. Spacecraft rarely allow that luxury. The mission itself is a coupled experiment in which different instruments supply context for each other. Mineralogy changes biological interpretation. Atmospheric chemistry changes the significance of gas release. Organic analysis changes priors about biomass. Surface temperature and radiation history change what a heat control means.

The most consequential fourth witness was the gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer.

Strictly speaking, it belonged to the molecular-analysis side of Viking rather than the trio of biology experiments. In the argument over life it became unavoidable. Living systems on Earth are made of organic carbon, and even dead organisms leave organic molecules. If a Martian soil sample contains enough cells to produce the LR gas signal, one would expect some organic material to be present. The GCMS searched heated soil samples for volatile or thermally released organic compounds. It did not provide the convincing indigenous organic inventory that a straightforward biological interpretation wanted.

This was not merely another negative instrument. It threatened the coherence of the positive one.

There are ways to answer that threat. Perhaps the organisms were present at low biomass. Perhaps the relevant organic compounds were below detection limits, poorly volatilized, destroyed during heating or chemically transformed. Perhaps the active process involved a biological agent distributed differently from bulk organic matter. Each answer adds assumptions.

The chemical interpretation had assumptions too. It required a reactive species or ensemble of species capable of oxidizing the labeled nutrients, producing the observed gas, displaying the observed thermal sensitivity and doing so similarly at both sites. In 1976 the phrase "strong oxidant" did a lot of work because the exact oxidant was unknown.

Viking therefore produced an asymmetry that shaped the next several decades. The biological hypothesis pointed to a familiar causal class, microorganisms, but lacked expected molecular support. The chemical hypothesis pointed to an unfamiliar causal class, reactive Martian soil chemistry, but had broad support from Gas Exchange and did not require an ecosystem in an apparently hostile surface environment. Scientific conservatism favored chemistry even before the chemistry was fully named.

That was not unreasonable.

It is worth saying this plainly because the mythology around Viking often treats consensus as either cowardice or conspiracy. If one instrument suggests metabolism while another fails to find the expected material substrate of organisms and a third demonstrates strong water-triggered chemical reactivity, the cautious conclusion is not "life." Extraordinary claims language is almost unnecessary; ordinary cross-instrument consistency is enough.

But caution can harden beyond its evidence.

The cautious 1976 conclusion was that the observations did not permit a decision about life. The stronger cultural conclusion that later emerged was that Viking had shown Mars to be lifeless at its landing sites, perhaps on the surface generally. That stronger statement inherited authority from the mission without being equivalent to the mission team's early wording.

This is how evidence becomes history. Ambiguity is expensive to preserve.

The distinction matters especially after Phoenix and Curiosity. Phoenix discovered perchlorate in Martian soil. That did not retroactively make the LR result biological; if anything, later work on radiation-processed oxychlorine chemistry strengthened concrete abiotic mechanisms. Yet perchlorate also complicated thermal organic analysis because heating oxychlorine-bearing material can destroy organics and produce chlorinated products. Curiosity later demonstrated that reduced organic carbon can indeed persist in Martian rocks and that chlorinated organic compounds can emerge during instrument heating from interactions among Martian chlorine and carbon sources.

One of Viking's strongest anti-biological contextual facts therefore became less simple, while one of its strongest pro-chemical contextual facts became more mechanistically grounded.

Both sides got something.

That is why the argument did not die.

If later discoveries had moved uniformly in one direction, Viking would now be an uncomplicated historical footnote. Instead, Mars kept adding detail to both models. The planet contains reactive oxychlorine chemistry capable of confusing biological assays. It also contains organic carbon that 1970s investigators did not convincingly identify at the surface. Radiation changes the chemistry. Heating changes the chemistry. Water changes the chemistry. Sampling depth matters. Geological age matters. The Martian surface is not the simple oxidized desert required by one old narrative, and it is not the demonstrated microbial habitat required by the other.

The three biology experiments are useful precisely because they stop us from asking whether one graph "looked biological."

Consider the requirements for a serious extant-life claim from Viking. We would want a mechanism connecting LR substrate consumption to organisms. We would want the heat behavior to correspond to biological viability more naturally than to reactive chemistry. We would want some orthogonal structural or molecular evidence of biomass. We would want Gas Exchange results to make sense in the same ecology rather than merely coexist. We would want the Pyrolytic Release behavior to connect or at least not contradict the proposed metabolism. We would want site-to-site similarities and differences explained. We would want a model of how organisms persist under the radiation, dryness, salts and temperature regime of the sampled surface.

Viking could not supply all of that.

Now consider the requirements for a serious abiotic claim. We would want a specific reagent or family of reagents in plausible Martian abundance. We would want laboratory simulations under relevant pressure, temperature, atmosphere, humidity, radiation history and nutrient concentrations. We would want the reaction to produce labeled gas with approximately the observed kinetics. We would want its activity to decline under the specific thermal treatments used on Mars. We would want it distributed at both landing sites and under rocks. We would want to understand its evolution during months of storage and after repeated wetting.

The 1970s did not supply all of that either.

This is the difference between a preferred explanation and a closed explanation.

Science needs preferred explanations. Missions have to end; papers have to summarize; subsequent hardware has to be designed. The error comes when preference is remembered as direct detection. Viking did not directly detect an oxidant that explained LR any more than it directly imaged a microorganism. It detected a set of chemical behaviors and interpreted them in the context of other measurements.

The later oxychlorine work is powerful because it narrows the chemical side from category to mechanism. Richard Quinn and colleagues, for example, did not merely say Mars is oxidizing. They irradiated calcium perchlorate under carbon-dioxide conditions relevant to Mars and identified products including hypochlorite, trapped oxygen and chlorine dioxide. They showed that trapped oxygen release and hypochlorite reactions with amino acids could account for important Viking observations. That is what explanatory progress looks like: the word *oxidant* acquires chemical names, pathways and testable properties.

Yet even this does not convert Viking into a solved forensic case. Demonstrating a mechanism in the laboratory is not measuring its abundance in a 1976 sample. Mars varies spatially. Radiation doses and exposure ages vary. Oxychlorine phases vary. Soil mineralogy affects adsorption and kinetics. The laboratory can show that the door exists without proving the old data walked through it.

The same rule disciplines later organic detections. Curiosity's chlorobenzene and other organic results show that reduced carbon survives in Martian materials despite oxidants and radiation, and that thermal analysis can create reaction products involving chlorine. This undermines the lazy equation "Viking GCMS null equals no Martian organics." It does not supply a cell to Viking's sample cup.

An instrument suite earns its value partly by making lazy equations difficult.

The Viking team understood this more than the shorthand history suggests. The 1976 preliminary biology paper is cautious because the instruments are disagreeing productively. Gas Exchange reveals reactive soil. LR produces a metabolism-like signal. PR produces carbon incorporation. Thermal controls alter responses. The molecular analyzer does not deliver the expected organic context. No single observation is granted dictatorial power.

The mission becomes more modern, not less, when read this way.

Contemporary astrobiology talks about biosignature frameworks, multiple working hypotheses, environmental context, false positives, false negatives and suites of orthogonal measurements. Viking was already living the reason those ideas are necessary. It had multiple assays. What it lacked was enough characterization of the abiotic background and enough molecular specificity to make the assays converge.

There is a practical lesson hidden here for every future life-detection mission.

Do not simply diversify the ways you search for life. Diversify the ways you search for things that can fool each search.

A metabolism assay should travel with reagents or measurements designed to identify abiotic oxidants and reductants. An organic analyzer should characterize what its own heating step does to local salts. A microscopy claim should be challenged by mineral morphologies. Isotope fractionation should be tested against plausible abiotic pathways. Molecular complexity metrics should be calibrated against local geochemistry, radiation and preservation.

The control set needs as much imagination as the biosignature set.

Viking's three witnesses were good witnesses. The problem was that the defense had not yet met all the suspects.
