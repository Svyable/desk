# The Organic Veto

A microorganism is chemistry with ambitions, but it is still chemistry.

This was the blunt problem facing the biological interpretation of Viking's Labeled Release result. If organisms in the Martian soil were numerous or active enough to transform the supplied radioactive substrates into gas, their bodies ought to contain organic molecules. The Viking gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer searched for organic compounds in Martian samples and did not establish the indigenous organic inventory the biology story seemed to require.

For many investigators, this was the result that broke the tie.

The logic is powerful because it is orthogonal to metabolism. LR could be fooled by a chemical reaction that happens to oxidize labeled nutrients. An organic analyzer asks a different question. Are the carbon-rich molecular materials associated with organisms actually there?

If the answer is no, a metabolic signal becomes much harder to call biological.

The mistake is not in the logic. It is in forgetting how the answer "no" was produced.

Viking did not have a magical sensor for organic matter. Its molecular analysis depended on an instrument and a protocol. Soil was heated so volatile compounds and thermal products could be analyzed by gas chromatography and mass spectrometry. Molecules had to survive sampling, survive storage, be released or transformed in ways the instrument could detect, travel through the analytical pathway and rise above backgrounds and detection limits. Every negative result in analytical chemistry is conditional on that chain.

In 1976 the chain was reasonable. The GCMS was a formidable instrument for a Mars lander. Its failure to find a convincing suite of indigenous organics was genuinely damaging to the simplest life interpretation. The proper historical correction is not to sneer that the instrument "missed" organics. Scientists can only interpret with chemistry they have.

The proper correction is to specify what later discoveries changed about the chain.

Phoenix found perchlorate in Martian soil.

Perchlorate is a chlorine oxyanion with a reputation for chemical stability under many conditions. That stability can make its later role in the Viking story sound paradoxical. The key is temperature. Heated strongly in the presence of organic matter, oxychlorine salts can participate in reactions that oxidize organic carbon and form chlorinated products. Viking's GCMS heated soil. The instrument was not merely observing whatever volatile organics happened to be waiting intact. It was creating a high-temperature chemical environment inside a sample containing, as we now know Mars can contain, reactive chlorine phases.

Rafael Navarro-González and colleagues turned this into an experiment rather than a speculation. They took Mars-analog soils from the Atacama Desert containing low levels of organic carbon, added magnesium perchlorate and heated the samples under analytical conditions. Much of the organic matter was destroyed; small chlorinated molecules were produced. They argued that Viking's trace chloromethane and dichloromethane detections, originally treated as terrestrial contaminants, could instead be products of Martian organics interacting with perchlorate during pyrolysis.

The paper was not the final word.

Its authors later corrected aspects of their compound quantitation, acknowledging that using a carbon-dioxide calibration response for multiple chemical species was an oversimplification. They said the correction did not alter the central interpretation. Klaus Biemann, principal investigator of Viking's molecular-analysis experiment, published a substantive comment disputing the inference from the analog experiments to the Viking data.

This disagreement is exactly what the book needs because it prevents the later chemistry from becoming a retroactive miracle.

The weak version of the story says Viking saw no organics because perchlorate burned them all. That is too clean. The actual question depends on perchlorate abundance at the Viking sites, the chemical form of the chlorine, heating profiles, sample mineralogy, organic concentration, instrument backgrounds and the identities of the trace products. Retrospective interpretation is constrained by what the 1976 instrument actually recorded.

But the old veto was weakened.

Before Phoenix, one could reasonably treat Viking's thermal analysis as evidence that accessible organic material was extremely scarce. After Phoenix and laboratory pyrolysis work, one also had to ask whether the analytical method itself could erase part of the evidence it was designed to reveal.

Curiosity made that question unavoidable.

The Sample Analysis at Mars instrument suite on Curiosity uses evolved-gas and gas-chromatographic methods far more capable and better informed than Viking's. In drilled Sheepbed mudstone, Caroline Freissinet and colleagues identified chlorobenzene and several dichloroalkanes. Their analysis concluded that the chlorinated products could not be explained solely by known instrument background and involved Martian chlorine reacting with reduced organic carbon derived from Martian or exogenous sources.

The source of the carbon was not declared biological. That point matters. Meteorites, interplanetary dust, ancient abiotic chemistry and geological processes can all supply organics. The result established survival of reduced carbon on Mars and demonstrated the analytical relevance of oxychlorine chemistry. It did not discover cells.

It also showed why "organic" is a dangerous synonym for "biological."

Organic chemistry is carbon chemistry. Life uses it extravagantly, but the Solar System manufactures organic molecules without organisms. A meteorite is not evidence of metabolism because it contains amino acids. A Martian mudstone is not a fossil simply because it preserves macromolecular carbon. Conversely, absence of a few detectable organic compounds in a thermal analyzer is not proof of absence of biology when the sample chemistry can transform those compounds during measurement.

The evidentiary value lies in combinations.

If LR had shown metabolism-like behavior and the GCMS had found abundant complex indigenous organics with distributions difficult to explain by abiotic processes, the case would have been stronger. If the GCMS had demonstrated a truly organic-free sample under a method known not to destroy organics, the biological case would have been weaker. Viking occupied the uncomfortable middle: LR produced a striking response while molecular analysis did not provide the expected support, and later science complicated the meaning of the molecular null.

That does not mean the GCMS result should be demoted to irrelevance.

Suppose the Martian soil contained enough living cells to generate LR's radioactive-gas curve. Even if perchlorate destroyed some compounds during heating, what total biomass would be required? How much cellular carbon would that imply? What fraction would survive or transform into detectable products? Would all expected fragments be chlorinated or oxidized below detection? Could microorganisms be metabolically active at abundances low enough to evade the molecular instrument? These are quantitative questions.

The biological interpretation often suffers when it is allowed to remain qualitative. "The GCMS may have destroyed organics" is a mechanism, not a calculation. The mechanism must be run against the amount and type of biomass needed to produce the LR response.

The chemical interpretation has the same obligation. "Perchlorate destroys organics" cannot be used as a universal solvent for every negative molecular result. Different perchlorates and chlorates decompose at different temperatures. Mineral matrices alter reactions. Organic compounds differ dramatically in stability. Instrument blanks and internal carbon sources matter. Curiosity's own SAM team has had to investigate whether instrument materials such as Tenax traps can contribute carbon to chlorinated products. Analytical chemistry is a crime scene where the laboratory apparatus can become one of the suspects.

Viking's organic veto is therefore not abolished. It becomes conditional evidence rather than a categorical absence.

This change in status is important because historical scientific arguments often preserve old conclusions after the premises beneath them have softened. A sentence from 1976 can remain technically true while its implication changes.

"No indigenous organics were convincingly identified by the Viking GCMS" remains a fair summary.

"Therefore the sampled Martian soil contained no organic carbon" is stronger.

"Therefore LR could not have been biological" is stronger still.

Later Mars missions did not prove those stronger sentences false at the Viking sites. They showed why each inferential step needs additional assumptions.

The distinction between measurement and implication is the most important habit in this entire subject.

Viking measured a set of thermal-analysis products and backgrounds. Scientists inferred low organic abundance. They then used that inference to evaluate biology. Phoenix measured perchlorate at another site. Laboratory teams inferred that thermal analysis of Martian soil can be chemically destructive. Curiosity measured chlorinated compounds and organic carbon in another geological context. Scientists inferred that reduced carbon can persist and that Martian chlorine chemistry matters during analysis.

The data do not jump across forty years by themselves. We build the bridge.

A good bridge states its load limits.

This is why the phrase *organic veto* is deliberately a little unfair. The GCMS result did not possess constitutional authority to overrule the biology package. It acquired veto-like status because the underlying chemical expectation was compelling: no organic matter, no organisms. The later story does not reveal that scientists foolishly ignored life. It reveals that "no organic matter" was always an instrument-mediated conclusion.

The strongest skeptical position today is subtler than the old veto.

One can concede that Mars contains organic carbon, concede that Viking's heating protocol may have destroyed or transformed some organics, concede that its trace chlorinated compounds deserve reconsideration, and still reject the biological interpretation of LR. The later oxychlorine work supplies concrete abiotic reactions for the biology signals themselves. The lack of orthogonal evidence remains serious. No cell, polymer, growth curve or uniquely biological molecular distribution emerged from Viking. The surface environment remains hostile to known terrestrial-style active life.

That skeptical case is stronger precisely because it no longer depends on an organic desert.

The strongest biological case also improves when it stops pretending Curiosity is retroactive proof. Its narrower argument is that one major 1976 objection has lost some force: Mars is not devoid of organics, and Viking's analytical method was operating in a chemistry that can modify organics during heating. Therefore the LR evidence should be judged on its own controls and on modern abiotic mechanisms, not dismissed simply because an old molecular assay did not deliver a clean organic inventory.

That is a meaningful correction.

It is not vindication.

The difference matters because science advances by changing the weight of evidence, not only by flipping verdicts.

Viking's molecular analyzer still sits in the case file. It just no longer gets to speak last.
