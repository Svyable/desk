# Designing an Adversarial Life Test

A future Mars life-detection experiment should assume the planet is trying to fool it.

Not because Mars has intentions. Because chemistry has options.

Viking's most important design lesson is not that metabolism assays are bad or that direct life detection was premature. It is that a biosignature experiment should carry an explicit model of its own false positives, with hardware devoted to making those false positives fail.

This is different from ordinary controls.

A negative control verifies that the apparatus does not produce the signal under a chosen condition. A positive control verifies that the apparatus can produce the signal when the target process is present. An adversarial control asks a harsher question: what non-target processes are physically plausible in this environment, and what experiment would make them look maximally similar to the target?

For Viking, one of those adversaries was unknown reactive soil chemistry. The lander had controls for heat and several different biology experiments, but it could not identify the reactive species before perturbing the sample. A modern version should make the alternative hypothesis a first-class instrument requirement.

Suppose the experiment again begins with labeled organic substrates.

Do not mix them all at once. Separate them. An oxidant may react differently with formate, lactate, glycine or alanine than a metabolic network does. Measure concentration-response curves rather than one nutrient dose. Vary water activity deliberately. Include water-only and salt-matched hydration steps. Identify the gas species with high-resolution mass spectrometry instead of counting total radioactivity alone. Measure oxygen, carbon dioxide, chlorine species and pH simultaneously.

Characterize the soil before feeding it.

Measure perchlorate, chlorate, chloride, peroxide-like capacity, iron oxidation states, mineralogy and radiation-sensitive defect populations. The phrase "oxidizing soil" should never again have to carry an interpretation by itself. If a sample has enough independently measured chemical oxidizing capacity to account stoichiometrically for the entire substrate response, that matters. If it does not, that matters more.

Then challenge regeneration.

A finite chemical reagent can imitate one burst of metabolism by consuming supplied organic matter. It has a harder time imitating a system that restores its catalytic capacity, increases in abundance and continues functioning after serial dilution into fresh substrate. Serial transfer is one of microbiology's old powers because nonliving reactants usually dilute away while replicators can recover.

Usually is not always.

Autocatalytic chemistry, mineral catalysts and chain reactions complicate the distinction. That is why no single adversarial test should inherit the burden Viking gave to heat.

Replication-like behavior should be paired with molecular organization.

Microscopy can search for compartments or repeated structures, but mineral morphology must be treated as an adversary. Molecular analysis can look for families of compounds with distributions that chemistry struggles to produce randomly. Chirality can be informative if strong enantiomeric excess appears in a coherent biological context, but abiotic asymmetric processes exist. Stable-isotope patterns can reveal kinetic fractionation, but geology also fractionates. Polymer length distributions can be measured, but nonbiological polymerization exists.

The point is not to find an unforgeable signature.

The point is to make forgery expensive across independent dimensions.

This is how authentication systems work outside astrobiology. A bank does not trust one number merely because it is difficult to guess. Identity is established through multiple factors whose failure modes differ. A scientific biosignature suite should be built the same way: not as a checklist of exciting observations, but as a set of measurements chosen because the best abiotic explanation for one creates tension with another.

Viking's three biology experiments were an early version of this idea. The next version needs a more explicit causal graph.

Before launch, list the leading abiotic mechanisms for every proposed biosignature. Do not merely name them. Build samples that maximize them. Irradiate oxychlorine salts. Create mineral surfaces with trapped gases. Add meteoritic organics. Cycle them through Mars temperatures. Expose them to ultraviolet radiation. Store them dry for months. Hydrate them rapidly and slowly. Run the exact flight protocols.

Then use the failures to redesign the assay.

This approach is less glamorous than demonstrating that terrestrial bacteria trigger the instrument. Terrestrial bacteria are the easy positive control. The hard work is teaching the instrument how dead Mars might lie.

The word *lie* is metaphorical, but useful.

Every environment has a repertoire of false positives. Hydrothermal systems can generate abiotic methane and complex organics. Serpentinization creates hydrogen. Photochemistry can maintain atmospheric disequilibria. Mineral surfaces can organize molecules. Ice can concentrate solutes. Radiation can manufacture reactive species. Clouds can host chemistry that looks metabolic from a distance. An instrument that leaves Earth without rehearsing these adversaries is carrying an untested ontology.

There is also a case for deliberately preserving ambiguity rather than forcing onboard classification.

Spacecraft software often compresses measurements for telemetry and operational simplicity. A future life-detection mission should preserve as much raw time-series, spectral and calibration information as feasible. Viking's data remain reanalyzable partly because the instrument records survived well enough for later scientists to ask new questions. The next unexpected chemistry may not be imagined until decades after landing.

Archival design is part of life detection.

So is sample preservation.

The strongest response to Viking's permanent ambiguity may be the ability to bring material home under conditions that preserve relevant chemistry. Mars Sample Return architectures have faced enormous technical and programmatic challenges, but the scientific principle is hard to dispute: a terrestrial laboratory can apply methods that no lander can carry and can revise experiments after seeing results.

Returned samples create their own interpretive risks. Earth contamination becomes existential to a life claim. Pressure, temperature and radiation conditions change. Volatile or metastable species may disappear during storage and transit. Planetary-protection requirements constrain handling. A reactive Martian surface sample could arrive chemically older than it was when collected.

The adversarial philosophy therefore applies to return as well. Archive witnesses to the original state: in situ chemistry, temperature, radiation dose, imaging, mineralogy and blanks. Preserve replicate aliquots differently. Include witness materials that record contamination and handling. If a signal appears on Earth, ask what the journey itself could have made.

This way of thinking changes how missions should define success.

A direct life-detection mission is often imagined as successful if it announces life. That creates a dangerous incentive structure, even when scientists resist it consciously. The instrument is treated as a verdict machine.

A better success criterion is discrimination.

How much of the plausible causal space did the mission eliminate?

If a lander discovers a new abiotic reaction that defeats a beloved biosignature, that is a successful life-detection mission. It has improved our ability to know life when we eventually see it. Viking may belong in this category. Its biology package did not produce a consensus life detection, but it exposed the inadequacy of interpreting broad gas exchange without deep environmental chemistry.

The future instrument should be proud to falsify itself.

This requires predeclared evidentiary thresholds.

What combination of observations would the team call evidence for metabolism? What would remain merely "consistent with" biology? Which abiotic mechanisms must be independently bounded? What result would count against the life hypothesis even if the primary assay is positive? Which analyses will be blind? Which control failures invalidate a run?

Precommitment is not a cure for interpretation, but it makes retrospective goalpost movement visible.

Viking benefited from having a preflight logic. Heat-treated samples were supposed to discriminate biological activity. Because that logic existed before the data, the later heat response deserves evidentiary weight. The mistake would be to treat the predeclared discriminator as infallible after discovering a new class of heat-sensitive chemistry.

A modern framework can preserve both truths.

Predeclared tests prevent us from rewriting success after the fact.

Mechanistic updating prevents old tests from becoming dogma.

There is one more adversary worth designing against: the desire for one answer.

Mars may contain heterogeneous microenvironments. One sample could contain ancient organics, reactive oxychlorines and no life. Another, centimeters away, could contain protected brine chemistry. A third could preserve dead cellular material without active metabolism. A fourth could be sterile. A mission that compresses these possibilities into "life/no life" before it has mapped spatial variability can manufacture contradictions that are really sampling differences.

Replication should therefore be geographic and microenvironmental, not merely repeated within one cup.

Sample sunlit and shielded material. Surface and depth. Salt-rich and salt-poor grains. Ice interfaces where accessible. Sedimentary and volcanic contexts. Make hypotheses predict the pattern across those environments.

Biology, if present, should have ecology.

Chemistry certainly does.

The best discriminator may not be any single molecular signature but the way a signal follows habitat variables.

Viking gave us two landing sites and an under-rock sample. It hinted at this strategy without enough resolution to exploit it.

The next direct life experiment should be designed by people whose job is not merely to detect life, but to defeat the detection claim.

Put the skeptic into the hardware.

Then let Mars try again.
