# How to Ask Dirt if It Is Alive

A life-detection experiment has to commit an act of philosophy before it can become hardware.

It has to decide what life does.

That decision is usually hidden behind the instrument. We remember microscopes, stains, sequencing machines and incubators as if they merely reveal what is already there. In practice each one is tuned to a feature of terrestrial biology that someone has chosen to treat as diagnostic. A microscope privileges morphology. A culture plate privileges growth under the conditions supplied. A DNA sequencer privileges a particular hereditary chemistry. A metabolic assay privileges transformation of matter at rates and in patterns that sterile chemistry is not expected to match.

Viking had to make those commitments before anyone had touched Martian soil.

The mission planners were not naïve about the difficulty. The decision to fly multiple biology experiments is evidence of the opposite. Mars in the early 1970s was poorly characterized by modern standards. Its surface pressure, temperature cycles and atmospheric composition were known in broad terms, but the mineralogy of the soil, abundance and identity of strong oxidants, near-surface radiation chemistry, salt inventory and availability of liquid water were not mapped in the way later orbiters and landers would begin to map them. A test designed around one terrestrial definition of life risked missing an organism that had adapted to something Mars did differently.

The solution was not to avoid assumptions. There is no assumption-free experiment. The solution was to diversify them.

The Labeled Release experiment asked whether something in Martian soil would transform supplied organic substrates into radioactive gas. The Gas Exchange experiment asked whether humidifying and later wetting soil with a nutrient solution would cause measurable changes in atmospheric gases. The Pyrolytic Release experiment exposed Martian material to radioactive carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide under simulated environmental conditions, with and without light, and asked whether radioactive carbon became incorporated into material retained after the chamber was purged and processed. These were not three copies of the same test. They were three bets on different behaviors associated with life.

That mattered immediately after landing because the results were not a clean vote.

The Labeled Release signal was dramatic enough to attract attention. Gas Exchange produced a substantial oxygen release after humidification, a phenomenon difficult to assimilate to a comfortable microbial story because the speed and magnitude pointed strongly toward reactive surface chemistry. Pyrolytic Release produced a smaller incorporation signal with heat sensitivity, but its interpretation was also ambiguous. The biology team therefore faced a result more interesting than three positives or three negatives: Mars was active in every chamber, but activity did not line up with a single terrestrial template.

One way to tell this story is that the experiments failed to find life.

A better way is that they found the cost of operational definitions.

Suppose you are forced to search for living things in an unknown environment using one instrument. You could look for molecules. The problem is that molecules familiar from biology can be made abiotically, delivered by meteorites or altered after death. You could look for organized shapes. Crystals organize. Minerals grow patterned forms. You could look for disequilibrium. Geology also produces disequilibrium. You could look for metabolism. Chemistry reacts. You could look for replication. Complex autocatalytic systems can blur the boundary. Every candidate biosignature moves the burden rather than eliminating it.

Terrestrial laboratories escape the regress by layering evidence. A suspicious colony can be imaged, cultured, stained, sequenced, chemically analyzed and challenged with antibiotics or changes in nutrient conditions. Viking could not layer evidence freely. It had a finite set of prebuilt questions and no chance to send a graduate student across the room for a different reagent.

That is why the phrase "designed to detect life" needs care. The instruments were designed to detect behaviors and compositions that, under the team's prior understanding of Mars and terrestrial controls, would make life a reasonable explanation. They were not machines into which Mars could insert a binary answer.

The preflight LR literature makes this plain in an almost charming way. The experiment's nutrient mixture was selected to elicit responses from a broad range of terrestrial organisms. The designers tested flight-like hardware with terrestrial soils and considered factors that might produce labeled gas without biology. Heat sterilization was central because it supplied a practical discriminator. If an active soil response disappeared after a treatment expected to kill microorganisms, the biological hypothesis gained weight.

But even on Earth, the control was not philosophically universal. It was empirical. The relevant question was whether known sterile soils, minerals and contaminants could produce a similar response under the assay conditions and survive or fail the same controls.

Mars expanded the space of possible sterile soils.

This point can sound trivial in retrospect, but it is the entire book in miniature. An alien environment is not simply a familiar laboratory with different organisms. The nonliving background is alien too. If life is strange but chemistry is assumed familiar, the experiment has only done half the imaginative work.

Viking's problem therefore began before the first scoop. The mission had to model both Martian life and Martian nonlife. Life received most of the conceptual romance. Nonlife turned out to have more tricks.

The Gas Exchange experiment is a good example. Humidification caused the release of molecular oxygen. If one begins with a biological expectation, oxygen production can sound encouraging. Photosynthetic organisms make oxygen on Earth, after all. But the timing and conditions matter. Gas Exchange investigators rapidly considered reactive chemical models involving the Martian surface. A dry, radiation-exposed soil could contain oxidized or metastable compounds that release gas when water arrives. Wetting was not merely feeding hypothetical organisms. It was perturbing a geochemical system.

This was the same hidden intervention inside LR. The labeled nutrient solution supplied carbon sources, but it also supplied liquid water. On Mars, water is not a neutral carrier. A soil that has equilibrated for long periods under extreme dryness, ultraviolet radiation, cosmic radiation, low pressure and cold can undergo rapid chemistry when hydrated. Dissolution exposes ions. Adsorption equilibria shift. Carbon dioxide partitions differently. Reactive oxygen or chlorine species can encounter organic molecules they could not previously reach.

The experiment's most life-like act, feeding, was simultaneously one of its strongest geochemical disturbances.

Pyrolytic Release inverted the problem. Instead of feeding organics, it supplied radioactive atmospheric carbon and looked for incorporation. This reduced dependence on the assumption that Martian organisms would eat the particular nutrients LR offered. It was closer to asking whether soil could fix carbon. Yet abiotic chemistry could also trap or transform carbon. The experiment used heat treatment as part of its interpretive logic, but again, heating did not belong exclusively to biology.

The brilliance of the Viking package was that these assumptions were different enough that agreement would have been powerful.

The frustration was that Mars activated them differently.

There is a tendency in historical accounts to search for the moment when the mission should have known the answer. Was it the first LR curve? The sterile control? The GCMS result? The second injection? That search imposes a courtroom structure on an experimental campaign. Science rarely offers a single evidentiary gavel. The Viking biology team was watching an inference network change in real time. Each new run altered the weight of earlier runs because the mechanisms were not independent.

An LR response looked more biological if there were organics. It looked less biological if surface chemistry released large amounts of oxygen on wetting. A heat-sensitive Pyrolytic Release signal looked more interesting if the same heating pattern appeared in LR, but less decisive if a class of reactive Martian compounds could also be thermally destroyed. A GCMS failure to find organics made both biology and some organic-mediated chemistry harder to explain. Later discovery that heating oxychlorine-bearing samples can destroy organics changed the meaning of that failure without changing the old chromatograms.

No data point lived alone.

This is why operational definitions are most dangerous when they are forgotten after an experiment succeeds technically. Viking's instruments worked extraordinarily well. The landers delivered samples, reagents moved, detectors counted, ovens heated, valves operated and data crossed interplanetary space. A successful instrument can create confidence that the category it was built to test is equally well engineered.

But the category is not metal.

The category is a model.

Consider the phrase "sterilized control." It sounds stronger than "heated control" because the first wording includes the conclusion. On Earth, heating a soil sample at 160°C for hours is a reasonable sterilization procedure for the organisms an assay designer expects. On Mars, when the question is whether the original activity was biological, "heat-treated control" is the more disciplined phrase. Heating has definitely happened. Sterilization has happened if there was something biological to sterilize. The distinction is small enough to vanish in ordinary conversation and large enough to carry the entire inference.

The same caution applies to "nutrient." LR injected a mixture selected because terrestrial microorganisms can metabolize its components. If no organisms are present, the liquid remains a chemical reagent. Calling it nutrient solution can smuggle the biological interpretation into the description of the chemistry.

Scientific language is full of these compressed assumptions because compression is useful. The mistake is not using the words. The mistake is forgetting what has been compressed.

Viking's designers were aware of many such problems. Preflight testing examined sterile backgrounds. Lunar material offered another nonbiological comparison. The LR team evaluated ultraviolet-driven surface chemistry and oxidants. The mission did not wander innocently into the idea that chemistry might react with food. The difficulty was that terrestrial and lunar controls could not span the full chemical state space of Mars.

This is one reason the later perchlorate story matters even if it never closes LR. Phoenix expanded the known state space. A soil containing oxychlorine salts exposed to radiation is not just another dry mineral powder. It can accumulate reactive products. Laboratory studies decades after Viking showed that irradiated calcium perchlorate under Mars-relevant conditions can generate hypochlorite, trapped oxygen and chlorine dioxide. Hypochlorite can react with amino acids. Trapped oxygen can be released. Suddenly an abstract "oxidant" proposed in the 1970s becomes a family of experimentally tractable mechanisms.

That is scientific progress, even when it does not produce a verdict.

The important distinction is between explaining a category and explaining a trace. "Reactive oxychlorine chemistry" can explain why Martian soil might fool a metabolism assay in principle. To explain Viking, it must account for the kinetics and controls: initial gas evolution, site-to-site similarity, response under rocks, thermal suppression, storage effects, second injections and any difference among nutrient components. A candidate mechanism earns authority observation by observation.

Biology faces the same demand. "Microbes" is not an explanation of a trace. A biological model should explain why the response plateaus, why a second nutrient addition does not revive it in the expected way, why long storage diminishes activity, how organisms tolerate the surface environment, and why orthogonal evidence was weak or absent. The biological interpretation cannot use life's flexibility as permission to absorb every anomaly.

The symmetry here is worth preserving because the Viking controversy has often been narrated by asymmetrical standards. Skeptics sometimes accept a generic oxidant as sufficient while demanding a complete microbial ecology from the biological side. Advocates sometimes treat every failure of a specific oxidant model as positive evidence for organisms while allowing biology broad freedom to explain inconvenient details. Both moves are cheap.

The right question is comparative: which model explains which observations with which independently supported assumptions?

That sounds like an ordinary scientific standard. On Mars it becomes harder because the cost of obtaining independent support is enormous. The two Viking landing sites were not laboratories where the soil could be fractionated, imaged at cellular resolution, assayed for polymers, exposed to a dozen temperatures and fed one substrate at a time. The data had to live for decades while later missions slowly filled in missing chemistry.

This delay changes the sociology of evidence. A provisional interpretation can harden into common knowledge because the experiment cannot be repeated. Textbooks need sentences. Mission summaries need outcomes. Funding programs need to decide whether to repeat an approach or move on. "Ambiguous metabolism-like response in a poorly characterized reactive soil with no confirmed indigenous organics" is accurate but does not travel well. "Viking found no life" does.

So does "NASA suppressed a positive result."

Both are information-compression products.

The raw story is more useful. Viking deliberately asked Mars several definitions of life. Mars answered each through a chemical environment the designers only partly understood. The mission then had to decide whether disagreement among operational definitions meant absence of life, failure of the definitions, or an unknown background process.

That is still the problem facing astrobiology.

The instruments are better. The molecule lists are longer. We can measure isotopes more precisely, image finer structures, sequence terrestrial contamination, characterize mineralogy, map radiation environments and perform more sophisticated blank runs. Yet any mission that claims a biosignature still has to answer the Viking question: what can dead matter do under these conditions?

The answer cannot be imported from Earth.

It has to be measured alongside the search for life.
