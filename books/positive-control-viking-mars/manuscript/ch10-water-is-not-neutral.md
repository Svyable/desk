# Water Is Not Neutral

The Viking biology experiments carried one terrestrial assumption so deeply embedded that it could masquerade as plumbing.

Water wakes life.

On Earth this is usually a safe wager. Dry soil can hold dormant microbes for long periods; add moisture and metabolism rises. Water dissolves nutrients, restores molecular motion and makes the chemistry of cells possible. A life-detection experiment aimed at a dry planet could reasonably use water as the switch that turns biology on.

But water is also one of the most aggressive perturbations you can apply to a dry salt-rich mineral system.

This was visible immediately in Viking's Gas Exchange experiment. Exposing Martian material to water vapor produced a substantial release of oxygen. Whatever the exact mechanism, the observation announced that hydration alone could reorganize the chemistry of the sampled surface. Water was not an inert delivery medium waiting for organisms to use it. It was a reagent.

Labeled Release delivered its radioactive organic substrates in water.

That means the experiment changed two variables at once: it supplied potential food and it hydrated an alien regolith.

On Earth the two acts are so naturally linked that separating them feels artificial. On Mars the distinction becomes essential. A dry soil containing soluble salts, adsorbed gases, radiation products and metastable oxidants may behave dramatically when wet even if no metabolism exists. Water dissolves ions that were physically separated in the dry matrix. It changes pH. It allows diffusion. It changes adsorption of carbon dioxide. It can mobilize perchlorate and chlorate. It can expose organics to oxidants and mineral catalysts. It can alter the electric double layers on grain surfaces. It can release trapped gases.

The first LR response therefore began at the exact moment the experiment most strongly disturbed the nonbiological system.

This does not make the result abiotic. It makes the control problem harder.

A better experiment would like to separate hydration from feeding. Add water without organic substrates and monitor the same isotopic and chemical products. Add nutrients in a way that changes water activity minimally. Vary humidity before nutrient addition. Use different solvents or vapor-phase substrates if scientifically defensible. Measure oxidant concentrations before and after hydration. Viking's Gas Exchange experiment provided some cross-instrument information, but the chambers and protocols were not designed as a full factorial decomposition of LR.

The problem becomes sharper when we consider Martian habitability itself.

Modern Mars is cold and dry at the surface, with liquid water generally unstable for long periods under present pressure and temperature. Salts can lower freezing points and create brines under some conditions. Thin films and transient liquid phases are discussed in the literature. None of that means the Viking sites supported active surface ecosystems. It means any putative organisms would have evolved under water availability very different from a terrestrial laboratory's nutrient droplet.

An assay can fail by being too generous.

This is counterintuitive. We usually worry that an experiment fails to support life enough: wrong food, too little water, too cold. But organisms adapted to extreme dryness or hypersaline microenvironments can be damaged by sudden exposure to low-salinity water. Osmotic shock is real. Rapid rehydration can injure some desiccated cells. A Viking nutrient droplet designed as hospitality could have been environmental violence.

Levin and Straat's interpretation of the second-injection behavior sometimes invokes the possibility that the first wetting damaged or killed a putative biological population. That explanation cannot be dismissed merely because "water is good for life." Nor can it be accepted without evidence because it conveniently explains why the population failed to respond normally to more substrate.

The important point is that water destroys the simplicity of both models.

For biology, water may activate or injure.

For chemistry, water may dissolve, mobilize, react, adsorb, desorb and repartition.

A single nutrient injection therefore starts several clocks at once.

The Gas Exchange oxygen result is the strongest contemporary evidence that these clocks mattered. The Martian soil released oxygen quickly after humidification. Investigators sought chemical explanations because the response did not look like an ordinary biological gas-exchange pattern. If water alone can provoke major gas chemistry, then LR's water-bearing nutrient pulse cannot be interpreted as if the soil were a passive culture medium.

Later oxychlorine chemistry makes the argument more concrete. Phoenix's wet chemistry laboratory literally dissolved Martian soil to identify soluble ions, including perchlorate. The act that Viking biology used to wake organisms is precisely the act later instrumentation used to reveal hidden salts.

This is one of those historical alignments that looks obvious only afterward.

Viking knew the surface was chemically active under hydration, but it did not know the soluble inventory. Phoenix knew the inventory better because it was designed to perform aqueous chemistry. The missions asked different questions, and forty years later their data can be placed in the same reaction diagram.

There is a broader lesson for life-detection experiments in hostile environments.

The medium used to reveal a biosignature must be treated as an environmental intervention, not merely a carrier.

In an icy-moon sample, melting ice changes pressure, temperature and redox interfaces. In a hydrothermal mineral sample, bringing it to surface pressure can degas and precipitate phases. In a Venus-cloud droplet, changing acidity to make an Earth-compatible assay may destroy the chemistry one wants to measure. In a returned Mars sample, exposure to oxygen, humidity or room temperature could create products absent in situ.

The closer an environment is to Earth, the more laboratory defaults can remain invisible. The farther away it is, the more defaults become experiments.

Water is the most familiar default of all.

This familiarity shaped not only the apparatus but the interpretation. A reaction that begins immediately after nutrient solution is added feels like feeding. The temporal sequence invites the causal story. But from the soil's perspective, "food arrived" and "liquid water arrived" are simultaneous descriptions of the same event.

One of the most useful modern reconstructions would therefore repeat LR while decoupling those variables.

Prepare Mars analog soils with defined perchlorate, chlorate, iron phases and trace organics. Expose them to radiation until reactive steady states develop. Then run a matrix of conditions: dry labeled substrates if feasible; water alone; unlabeled nutrients; labeled nutrients; varied water activity; brines matching plausible Martian salts; humidity steps below bulk wetting. Monitor not only radioactive gas but oxygen, chlorine species, dissolved carbon, pH and redox potential.

Run microbial controls too, including desiccation-adapted and perchlorate-tolerant organisms. The point is not to recreate an imaginary Martian microbe. It is to map how much discriminatory power the Viking protocol retains when the alternative space is broadened.

If sterile oxychlorine soils reproduce the full first-injection response across realistic hydration regimes and thermal controls, the abiotic interpretation strengthens dramatically. If they cannot reproduce the signal without chemically implausible concentrations or conditions, the biological residual grows.

Either outcome would be better than arguing from the generic proposition that Mars is dry.

The dryness matters because it stores potential.

A dry reactive surface can accumulate species that water rapidly consumes. In that sense the LR nutrient pulse may have functioned like opening a valve on chemical energy stored over long environmental timescales. A finite reservoir naturally produces a burst followed by decline. Heating can destroy the reservoir. Storage can let it decay. A second wetting can have less to release.

That family of behavior resembles the LR trace enough to deserve serious testing.

It also exposes a weakness in the word *metabolism*. Metabolism is sustained network chemistry maintained by a living system, not merely one exergonic reaction between a stored oxidant and a supplied organic compound. A one-time gas pulse after wetting is less diagnostic than a process that can regenerate, grow, adapt or persist through repeated substrate cycles.

Viking's second injection was therefore more important than it first appeared. It was not only more food. It was a crude test of whether the active system had biological-style continuity.

The answer was messy.

The first water pulse had already changed the world inside the chamber.

This is why no single Viking run can be understood as a snapshot of undisturbed Mars. The experiments were perturbation machines. They learned by changing the sample. That is normal experimental science. The interpretive requirement is to model the change.

Mars did not fail to answer Viking's question.

Viking changed the soil, and the soil answered the change.
