# What the Later Chemistry Can and Cannot Do

By now the temptation is obvious.

Take Phoenix perchlorate, add radiation chemistry, add Curiosity organics, revisit Viking's oven, and declare the old mystery solved.

Solved in which direction depends on taste.

One version says the later chemistry vindicated the skeptics. Mars contains oxychlorine salts; radiation creates reactive daughter species; those species can release oxygen and oxidize amino acids; therefore Viking's biology experiments were abiotic.

Another says the later chemistry vindicated Levin. Mars contains organics after all; Viking's oven could have destroyed them; several LR controls behaved biologically; therefore Viking detected life.

Both stories are overfitted to the ending their authors wanted before they assembled the evidence.

The later chemistry does something less satisfying and more scientifically valuable. It changes the weight of particular inferences.

Start with perchlorate.

Phoenix established that Martian soil can contain substantial oxychlorine salts. This strengthens the plausibility of chlorine-centered surface chemistry. It does not identify the LR active agent. Radiation studies then demonstrate pathways from relatively stable parent perchlorate to more reactive species, including hypochlorite and trapped oxygen. Those experiments make a low-temperature abiotic explanation of some Viking observations much more credible than it was in 1976.

This is real movement against a biological interpretation.

But the movement has boundaries. The relevant daughter species were not measured in Viking's sample cups. Their natural concentrations at Chryse and Utopia are not known from the flight record. Laboratory production under chosen radiation doses establishes chemistry, not historical abundance. Any complete model still has to reproduce the timing, thermal sensitivity, storage behavior, under-rock response, cross-site similarity and second-injection behavior with a physically justified inventory.

Now take the organics.

Curiosity established that reduced organic carbon can persist in Martian geological materials. Laboratory and flight studies show that heating chlorine-bearing samples can transform organic compounds during analysis. These results weaken the old inference that Viking's GCMS result meant Mars, or even necessarily the sampled material before heating, was devoid of relevant organic carbon.

This is real movement toward keeping the biological interpretation open.

Again, the boundaries matter. Curiosity's organic matter came from different materials at a different site. Organic carbon is not itself evidence of organisms. The amount of biomass required for LR, the Viking detection limits and the degree of thermal destruction remain quantitative questions. No later mission has tied an organic molecule to the cause of Viking's radioactive gas.

The clean way to handle the evidence is not to ask whether a later discovery supports "life" or "no life." Ask which old premise it modifies.

Old premise: a strong low-temperature oxidant on Mars was chemically mysterious.

Later result: radiation-processed oxychlorine chemistry supplies plausible named mechanisms.

Effect: chemical interpretation strengthened.

Old premise: Viking's failure to establish organics means the Martian surface lacks organic carbon needed for biology.

Later result: Martian organics exist, and oxychlorine-bearing thermal analysis can transform them.

Effect: categorical organic absence weakened.

Old premise: heat sensitivity strongly discriminates life from chemistry.

Later result: candidate reactive species can also be thermally labile.

Effect: heat control remains evidence for fragility but loses some biological specificity.

Old premise: a repeated response at two sites favors biology because a peculiar chemical false positive should be local.

Later result: chlorine chemistry, radiation and oxidized regolith processes are planetary in scope.

Effect: cross-site repeatability remains important but is compatible with widespread chemistry.

Old premise: the second nutrient injection refutes chemistry or biology in a simple way.

Later result: chamber carbon partitioning explains part of the immediate decline; finite reactive inventories make nonrepeatability natural.

Effect: trace decomposition becomes necessary; neither whole-curve slogan survives.

This ledger produces an uncomfortable result.

The later evidence has made the abiotic model much better while also removing one of the crudest reasons the biological model was dismissed.

That is why the dispute remains alive among a small number of serious researchers without requiring us to pretend the probabilities are equal. A hypothesis can remain possible while losing the comparative judgment. Scientific openness is not numerical agnosticism.

The mainstream position today can reasonably assign low probability to extant life as the cause of Viking LR while acknowledging that the flight experiment was not retrospectively closed by one identified reaction. The burden on a biological interpretation is high because no orthogonal biological evidence emerged, because the surface environment is severe, because later chemistry provides credible false-positive pathways, and because the experiment has never been independently repeated with modern discriminatory instrumentation.

The minority position can reasonably point out that the original LR observations were reproducible and structured, that thermal controls remain nontrivial, that some simple chemical models failed, that Viking's molecular null is less decisive than once thought, and that no single abiotic reconstruction has become a direct measurement of the 1976 cause.

Those positions are asymmetrical but not mutually unintelligible.

The argument becomes dishonest when either side converts the other's missing final step into proof of its own.

Failure to prove an oxidant caused LR is not proof of life.

Failure to prove life caused LR is not proof of a particular oxidant.

The observations can be underdetermined.

Underdetermination is sometimes treated as philosophical fog, but here it has engineering consequences. If multiple causal systems can produce the same low-dimensional signal, the next instrument should measure dimensions on which those systems diverge.

For LR, product identity is an obvious one. A radiation counter measuring total labeled gas cannot distinguish carbon dioxide from every other radioactive gaseous product unless the system includes separation or identification. Modern mass spectrometry can do far more.

Stoichiometry is another. A finite oxidant should have a chemical capacity related to its abundance. A metabolic system can regenerate catalytic machinery and, under suitable conditions, grow. Repeated perturbations designed around depletion versus regeneration could separate them better than one second feeding.

Temperature can become a curve rather than two points. If activity falls according to an Arrhenius-like chemical decomposition regime, that may differ from microbial viability patterns, though real biological thermal responses are complex. Water activity can be varied instead of switching abruptly from dry to wet. Individual substrates can replace a mixture whose components blur mechanisms.

Molecular organization can provide orthogonal evidence. Chirality, polymer distributions, lipid-like structures, isotopic fractionations, cell-like compartments and replication are not individually infallible, but a coherent package can become difficult for geochemistry to mimic simultaneously.

The word *simultaneously* is doing the work.

Viking's central problem was not that each life criterion was foolish. It was that the measurements did not converge tightly enough on one cause.

The future of life detection therefore should not be a hunt for the perfect biosignature. It should be a deliberate collision of imperfect signatures whose false positives are as independent as possible.

This is harder than simply adding instruments. Two instruments that depend on the same unrecognized chemistry are not independent. Thermal organic analysis and a thermal gas-release assay may share oxychlorine artifacts. Multiple optical methods may share mineral fluorescence. Several molecular indicators may all be produced by meteorite-delivered organics.

Independence has to be causal, not administrative.

Viking's three biology experiments were already an attempt at this. The next generation can do better because Mars is less chemically anonymous.

That is the greatest contribution of the later chemistry to the old dispute.

It does not tell us what Viking found.

It tells us what the next Viking must be built to rule out.
