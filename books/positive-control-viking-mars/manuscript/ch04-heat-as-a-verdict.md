# Heat as a Verdict

The argument over Viking can be reduced to a thermometer if you are willing to be careless.

Untreated Martian soil produced radioactive gas after the Labeled Release nutrient was added. Soil heated for three hours at 160 degrees Celsius did not. Soil heated at roughly 50 degrees produced much less. A later sample heated for three hours at 46 degrees was also substantially inhibited. Heat kills organisms. Therefore the active agent was alive.

It is a good argument until the word *therefore*.

The heat experiments are among the most interesting observations Viking made because they did exactly what a biological control had been designed to do. They are also a clean lesson in why a control can increase the probability of a hypothesis without uniquely identifying it.

When Levin and Straat described the LR experiment before and during Viking operations, heating a duplicate sample was not decorative. It was meant to destroy biological activity. The instrument could not culture whatever was in the Martian sample in the usual microbiological sense, and it could not image cells. Thermal treatment supplied a blunt intervention. If an untreated sample reacted to nutrients and a heated sample did not, the response would behave like metabolism in one important respect.

At 160°C, the result looked almost too good. The active response disappeared.

The lower-temperature results were more provocative because 50°C is not an exotic sterilization temperature by terrestrial standards. Many microorganisms tolerate temperatures above that; many others do not. A three-hour exposure can be damaging to mesophilic organisms even if it is not a universal sterilizer. In the Viking 1976 interim report, the LR team emphasized that the active agent was stable at 18°C, substantially inactivated by three hours around 50°C and completely inactivated by 160°C. The authors wrote that this was what one would anticipate if microorganisms caused the response.

That statement is correct as far as it goes.

The question is what else one would anticipate.

A nonliving reactive species can be thermally unstable. A mineral surface can lose adsorbed components. Hydrated phases can dehydrate. Peroxides decompose. Radical populations can change. Oxychlorine species have temperature-dependent stability. The accessibility of reactants can change when a soil is heated even if the bulk mineral remains. At 160°C, a three-hour treatment is not a narrow biological intervention; it is a geochemical event.

The 46–50°C behavior is harder. It rules out some robust inorganic explanations because many common minerals will not dramatically change after a few hours at such modest temperature. That is why the lower-temperature control has remained central to the biological case. But "common minerals" is not the relevant universe. Viking had already indicated that the Martian surface contained unusual reactive chemistry. The active agent did not need to be a rock in the ordinary sense. It could be a metastable surface species generated by ultraviolet light or ionizing radiation, a compound adsorbed on mineral grains, or part of a coupled oxidant system whose abundance depends on temperature and storage.

This is where later Mars chemistry stops being historical decoration.

Phoenix's perchlorate discovery did not explain the LR control by itself. Perchlorate is relatively stable under the conditions of the biology experiment. But subsequent work showed that irradiation of perchlorate salts can produce more reactive oxychlorine species. Quinn and colleagues reported that gamma irradiation of calcium perchlorate in a carbon-dioxide atmosphere produced hypochlorite, trapped oxygen and chlorine dioxide. They showed that hypochlorite could react with amino acids like those added in the Viking nutrient and that trapped oxygen release could address features of the biology experiments.

Later work by Georgiou and colleagues explored radiation-driven reactive oxygen species in oxychlorine-bearing Mars analogues and noted thermal behavior relevant to the old thermally labile Viking oxidant problem. This is exactly the kind of chemistry the original LR control was not designed against because nobody knew to include it.

A skeptic can therefore say: the heat result is no longer mysterious. Mars has radiation, oxychlorine salts and reactive products. Biology is unnecessary.

An advocate can answer: demonstrate the whole curve.

That is the right reply, but it has to be applied fairly. A mechanism that explains thermal suppression but not gas kinetics, second injection behavior, storage, site distribution or under-rock activity is incomplete. A biological model that explains heat sensitivity but not the same features is also incomplete. Neither side gets to use a single successful correspondence as total explanation.

The heat experiments become especially interesting when paired with storage.

Viking did not test every soil sample immediately. Some material was retained in the instrument and examined later. The LR investigators reported diminished activity in samples held for months under lander conditions. This could resemble loss of viability. It could also resemble decay of a reactive chemical species. A metastable oxidant generated at the surface would be expected to change with time, temperature and shielding. A dormant organism might do the same for entirely different reasons.

Time and heat were therefore not orthogonal controls. They may have been probing the same hidden variable: stability of the active agent.

The under-rock sample adds another constraint. Viking 2 acquired material from beneath a rock, reducing its direct exposure to ultraviolet radiation compared with open surface grains. That sample produced an LR response essentially similar to others, according to Levin and Straat's 1977 report. This weakens a simple model in which a highly transient UV-created oxidant exists only on the directly illuminated skin of the soil.

It does not eliminate radiation chemistry.

Ionizing cosmic rays and secondary particles penetrate. Reactive species can persist or migrate. Rocks do not create perfect radiation shelters at shallow depth. Soil can be mixed. The relevant exposure history was not measured molecule by molecule. Again, one control kills an easy version of a hypothesis without killing the family.

The vocabulary of "sterilization" has done too much work in this debate.

If we call the 160°C run a sterilized control, the sentence already assumes a biological target. The hardware heated soil. Whether it sterilized life is conditional on life being present. For experimental bookkeeping, "heat-treated control" is cleaner. This may sound pedantic, but the wording matters because the public story is built from nouns that quietly contain conclusions.

Something similar happened with "positive." LR met important preflight criteria for an active biological response. It is reasonable to say the assay returned a positive result in the operational sense. It is not equivalent to saying Mars tested positive for life. The experiment was a proxy, and proxies inherit the ambiguity of everything else that can move them.

Medical diagnostics make this distinction familiar. A patient can test positive for an assay target while not having the disease the assay is used to infer. The test's sensitivity and specificity depend on the population and on alternative processes that produce the same marker. Transfer the assay into a population with an unknown confounder and its predictive value changes.

Viking transferred a biological assay to a planet.

The population of alternative chemistries was unknown.

That is why the control problem is more profound than the usual false-positive story. LR did not merely register a random background fluctuation. The signal was structured. It occurred after reagent addition. It was repeatable across landing sites. It changed with heat in the predicted direction. In other words, the possible false positive survived several layers of control.

A false positive that survives controls is not evidence that controls are useless. It is evidence that the control model was incomplete.

This distinction matters far beyond Mars. Experimental science often treats controls as a way to remove nuisance explanations. But a control only removes mechanisms that respond differently to the intervention. If two causal classes share the same vulnerability, the control cannot separate them. Heat can injure cells and destroy reactive chemicals. Antibiotics can affect mitochondria as well as bacteria. Filtration can remove organisms and also remove catalytic particles. Light can activate phototrophs and photochemistry. A negative control is an intervention plus a theory of selectivity.

Viking had the intervention.

Mars challenged the selectivity.

The obvious response is to add more controls. That sounds simple until one remembers that every control on a planetary mission costs mass, volume, time, reagents, valves, sample handling and operational complexity. A laboratory can branch an experiment after seeing the first result. A spacecraft has to launch most of its branches before the result exists.

The Viking designers did include multiple thermal treatments and repeated cycles. They were not operating with one crude sterilization run. Yet once the strange pattern appeared, the most informative next experiments were unavailable. One might want to fractionate the soil, measure specific oxidants directly, add individual LR substrates one at a time, vary water activity, alter pH, repeat at many temperatures, expose samples to defined radiation doses, use isotope-resolved product identification, search for catalytic metals, image cells, extract polymers and compare reaction rates after mineral separation.

The landers could not become those instruments.

This is where mission design becomes a form of prediction. You do not merely predict what signal life will produce. You predict what ambiguity will need resolving after the signal arrives.

Viking's thermal logic was based on a good biological prediction: organisms are heat-sensitive. It underpredicted a different ambiguity: the Martian reactive agent, even if nonliving, might also be heat-sensitive in the relevant range.

Later oxychlorine chemistry makes that ambiguity easier to imagine, but we should resist the reverse error of making it inevitable. There are many years between a demonstrated laboratory pathway and a forensic reconstruction of a specific 1976 soil cup. The composition at the Viking sites was not measured with Phoenix's wet chemistry laboratory. The radiation history of the exact grains is unknown. The concentration of reactive daughter products was not measured. Some experiments use conditions chosen to probe plausibility rather than to duplicate every feature of Viking's surface environment.

A mechanism can be credible without being proven historical fact.

The biological argument lives in the same category. The LR response remains consistent with some biological processes. Heat behavior remains one of its strongest features. But the absence of an independent biological marker means the cause is not identified. Life is a credible interpretation advanced by the LR investigators, not a documented fact hidden behind bureaucratic language.

The most useful way to read the heat data is therefore as a narrowing device.

Whatever caused the LR response had a thermal history.

It survived the unheated protocol. It was substantially damaged by hours near 50°C. It was eliminated by the high-temperature treatment. It could occur at both sites. It could occur in material sampled beneath a rock. Its apparent activity could decline with storage. Any adequate model, biological or chemical, has to walk through those constraints without changing the rules for whichever observation becomes inconvenient.

This turns the old debate into an experimental program rather than a referendum.

Take candidate A: a microbial population. Specify its abundance, substrate utilization, water response, temperature tolerance, gas products and dormancy. Run it through the flight protocol. Take candidate B: radiation-processed oxychlorine chemistry. Specify concentrations, mineral hosts, exposure history, reactive products, decay rates, thermal thresholds and reactions with each labeled substrate. Run that through the same protocol. Do not compare the words *microbe* and *oxidant*. Compare traces.

That level of model confrontation was difficult in 1976 because the chemical candidates were poorly characterized. It is more possible now, though not complete.

The heat control still matters because it refuses to become trivial.

If Mars had produced labeled gas and the response survived 160°C unchanged, the biological interpretation would have suffered badly. If the heated sample had produced a larger response, the experiment would have pointed elsewhere. The observed suppression genuinely moved the evidence toward a fragile active agent. What changed over the following decades was not the observation but the population of plausible fragile agents.

This is the pattern to remember.

A control does not have a permanent meaning independent of background knowledge. Its evidentiary weight changes when science discovers a new mechanism that responds to the control in the same way as the target hypothesis.

The Viking heat experiment worked.

What failed was the hope that only life would answer it that way.
