# Constants as Local History

A constant is the most successful disguise history can wear.

Measure the speed of light in vacuum today and tomorrow and, within the framework of relativity and our measurement precision, you expect the same value. Measure the electron charge in Chicago and Geneva and you do not expect geography to matter. Physics depends on such invariance. Without it, the project of universal law collapses into local bookkeeping.

So this chapter begins by defending constants against its own title.

There is no accepted evidence that the fundamental dimensionless constants of the Standard Model vary across ordinary space and time. Searches for variation in the fine-structure constant, proton-electron mass ratio, and other quantities use atomic clocks, quasar spectra, nuclear phenomena, and cosmological observations. Results are consistent with remarkable constancy, with bounds depending on the quantity, epoch, and model. Claims of spatial variation have remained controversial and unconfirmed.

A serious book should not turn “constants may be history” into “physics has discovered changing constants.” It has not.

The interesting question is different.

Some quantities that behave as constants within a low-energy phase can be determined by the state of fields rather than by an explicit immutable number written naked into the deepest laws.

The Higgs vacuum expectation value is the cleanest established example of the structure, though calling it a “constant of nature” requires care. In our vacuum it has a fixed nonzero value that sets the electroweak scale and contributes to particle masses through couplings. At high temperatures the effective Higgs field state changes. The low-energy value is therefore not a universal state-independent number in the same sense as a purely mathematical constant.

A field value can be stable enough to function as background furniture while remaining phase-dependent.

This opens a dangerous and fertile question: how many apparently fixed low-energy parameters might ultimately be properties of a vacuum or phase rather than uniquely dictated by the deepest theory?

We do not know.

High-energy physics contains serious frameworks in which scalar fields set effective couplings or masses. The QCD axion, if it exists, dynamically relaxes an effective strong-CP angle toward a small value. Moduli fields in theories with extra dimensions can determine effective coupling constants depending on how compact dimensions are stabilized. Inflation uses scalar fields whose background values control expansion in many models. Dark-energy theories sometimes introduce dynamical scalar fields such as quintessence. None of these establishes that the constants we measure are historical accidents.

They show that field-dependent parameters are not an incoherent idea.

The difference between “coherent” and “observed” must remain visible.

This is where cosmology’s vacuum problem becomes philosophically explosive and empirically frustrating.

Quantum field theories can possess multiple minima of a potential. In simple toy models, different minima can correspond to distinct vacua with different symmetry properties and particle spectra. In more ambitious theories, especially string-theory discussions of a landscape of vacua, different compactifications or field configurations can yield different low-energy constants.

The landscape idea is influential and controversial. It has not been experimentally established. Counting possible vacua, defining measures, explaining selection, and deriving testable predictions remain difficult. Anthropic reasoning enters because observers can exist only in some regions of parameter space. The entire subject can turn into metaphysics wearing equations if its empirical boundaries are not stated.

This book does not need the landscape.

The modest point survives without it: a parameter can be locally constant because the field that determines it has settled.

A crystal lattice spacing is a simple material analogy. Under fixed temperature and pressure, a crystal has a characteristic spacing. It behaves like a structural constant of that phase. Heat the material, compress it, or change the phase and the spacing changes. No one confuses the lattice spacing with a mathematical constant of the microscopic interaction.

The analogy should not be pushed onto the fine-structure constant directly. We have no evidence that α is just the lattice spacing of a cosmic medium. The lesson is epistemic: local invariance does not identify explanatory layer by itself.

A measurement tells you constancy over the domain measured.

Theory tells you what kind of constancy it is.

This distinction matters enormously when physicists ask whether parameters are “fundamental.”

The Standard Model contains parameters that must be measured: gauge couplings, Yukawa couplings, masses encoded through those couplings and the Higgs scale, mixing angles, CP phases, and more. The model organizes them but does not derive all their numerical values. The cosmological model adds parameters such as dark-matter density, baryon density, primordial fluctuation amplitude, and the cosmological constant or dark-energy density.

Some are initial conditions. Some are couplings. Some may be emergent from deeper theory. Some may reflect vacuum selection. We do not know the full classification.

The list of constants is therefore partly a list of unfinished explanations.

This does not diminish their reality. It identifies where explanation currently stops.

The history of physics contains several quantities that looked fundamental until theory related them to something deeper. Kepler’s empirical laws became consequences of Newtonian gravitation under appropriate conditions. The speed of light shifted from a property of electromagnetic waves in Maxwell’s theory to a fundamental invariant speed in relativity. Atomic spectral constants became consequences of quantum mechanics, electron mass, charge, and Planck’s constant. Nuclear properties became consequences of QCD and quark masses to varying degrees.

Explanation changes which numbers are primitive.

A future theory could do the same for Standard Model parameters.

Or it could tell us that some are irreducibly environmental within a larger theory.

These possibilities have different philosophical flavors and currently insufficient evidence.

The human temptation is to prefer one because it feels cleaner.

Physicists have historically valued explanations that reduce arbitrary parameters. Naturalness arguments grew partly from this instinct: dimensionless ratios that are extremely small or large without a protecting symmetry or mechanism invite explanation. The hierarchy problem asks why the electroweak scale is so much smaller than the Planck scale without enormous quantum corrections destabilizing it under certain formulations. Supersymmetry, composite Higgs models, extra dimensions, relaxion mechanisms, and other ideas have been proposed.

The LHC has not found the simple new physics many naturalness arguments led people to hope for.

This has not killed the hierarchy problem, but it has forced a reassessment of what kinds of explanation nature may provide and how much aesthetic intuition should count.

A constant can embarrass a theory simply by being the wrong size.

The cosmological constant is the most brutal example.

Observed cosmic acceleration is consistent with a positive cosmological constant whose energy density is tiny compared with naive quantum field theory estimates of vacuum contributions. The discrepancy between observed dark-energy scale and straightforward theoretical expectations is often described as one of the worst prediction problems in physics.

No accepted solution exists.

Anthropic selection in a multiverse, dynamical dark energy, modified gravity, sequestering mechanisms, symmetry arguments, and other proposals have been explored. Current observations remain broadly consistent with a cosmological constant, though surveys such as DESI continue tightening constraints on the dark-energy equation of state and possible evolution.

The number sits there, stable in the equations, radiating ignorance.

This chapter’s thesis is that such numbers should be treated as questions with different possible genealogies.

Some may be law.

Some may be phase.

Some may be initial condition.

Some may be effective summary of deeper dynamics.

Some may be selection effects.

Some may be artifacts of our current parameterization.

The task is classification, not premature metaphysics.

Symmetry is one of the tools for classification because a parameter can become technically natural when setting it to zero increases a symmetry. In that case quantum corrections are constrained. The smallness is protected by structure. This is a different explanation from a parameter being environmentally selected or dynamically relaxed.

The universe can make a number small in more than one way.

Humans should resist treating all unexplained numbers as the same mystery.

This restraint also matters for varying-constant searches.

Suppose a scalar field couples to electromagnetism so that the effective fine-structure constant changes as the field evolves. Then distant astronomical spectra could, in principle, reveal a different α in the past. Atomic clocks can search for present-day drift. Equivalence-principle tests constrain related couplings. Cosmology constrains the field’s energy density and evolution.

A detected variation would be revolutionary because it would turn a “constant” into a dynamical observable and reveal a new field or interaction.

No robust detection has occurred.

The search is still valuable because constancy itself becomes a measured range rather than a metaphysical assumption.

This is one of science’s most underappreciated moves: take the word *constant* and attach error bars to it.

The same happened with Lorentz invariance, CPT symmetry, equivalence principle, charge neutrality, photon mass, and many other foundational properties. Physics does not merely assume its principles. It constructs experiments designed to catch them failing.

A principle becomes stronger when it survives betrayal attempts.

Humans are unusual in this way. We can inhabit a stable world and systematically search for instability.

An animal benefits from regularity. If gravity works today as yesterday, the branch holds. A scientist benefits from the same regularity and then builds an experiment to detect a one-part-in-10^18 deviation.

We weaponize trust against itself.

This is the correct human response to apparent constants.

Not to declare them arbitrary because a fashionable theory contains a landscape.

Not to declare them eternally necessary because every measurement agrees locally.

Measure wider in space.

Measure longer in time.

Measure at different energies.

Derive what can be derived.

Search for fields that could make the value dynamical.

Ask what symmetry protects it.

Ask whether a phase transition could have selected it.

The possibility that constants are local history becomes scientific only when it generates such questions.

The Higgs field provides a concrete case where the vacuum state is definitely part of the answer. The electron’s mass is not a raw standalone constant; it is the product of its Yukawa coupling and the Higgs vacuum scale up to conventional factors. This explains the mechanism but not the small Yukawa coupling.

The decomposition itself is progress.

What looked like one number becomes two pieces with different conceptual roles: coupling and background state.

Future physics may decompose further.

Perhaps flavor symmetries explain Yukawa hierarchies. Perhaps masses arise from compositeness. Perhaps extra dimensions or other structures determine couplings. Perhaps some parameters remain input.

We should want the decomposition more than the preferred story.

This is where esoteric theses usually go bad. The author starts with a legitimate frontier possibility — variable constants, vacuum selection, hidden fields — and then speaks as if uncertainty is evidence for the boldest interpretation.

Uncertainty is room, not support.

The strongest version of this book does not need to claim that our constants are accidents.

It claims that physics has already taught us a category error to avoid: properties that are constant inside a phase can owe their existence to the state of that phase.

This category is real.

How far it extends is open.

That boundary is enough to change how we think about human certainty.

We are born after the constants have been measured by nature for us in the only local phase available to ordinary life. We inherit electron mass, proton stability over enormous timescales, electromagnetic coupling, the speed of light, and the cosmological expansion rate as conditions.

We then build metrology to distinguish condition from principle.

The history of metrology is an attempt to escape artifacts. The meter was once tied to a physical bar. The second was tied to Earth’s rotation and later ephemeris time. Today SI base units are defined through fixed numerical values of fundamental constants such as the speed of light, Planck constant, elementary charge, Boltzmann constant, and cesium hyperfine frequency relation.

This looks like the ultimate declaration that constants are not history.

It is actually a declaration of operational dependence.

We choose definitions that make measurements reproducible by anchoring units to invariants nature appears to provide. If future physics discovered a relevant variation under conditions that affect the realization, metrology would have to adapt its interpretation. The definitions are human conventions built around empirical stability.

A constant becomes civilization’s ruler because it has not bent.

This is one of the most beautiful relationships between fundamental physics and ordinary coordination.

GPS, telecommunications, semiconductor fabrication, spectroscopy, medicine, power grids, financial timestamps, and scientific experiments depend on standards traceable through metrology. The more stable the physical invariant, the more precisely humans can synchronize distant institutions.

We turn universal regularity into social interoperability.

This is the opposite of the book’s earlier defect story. There, local choices create boundaries. Here, invariants let distant systems agree.

Civilization needs both capacities: preserve distinctions that matter and share standards that should not vary.

The art is knowing which is which.

Treat a convention as a constant and you freeze history into necessity.

Treat a constant as a convention and you destroy coordination with reality.

Science exists partly to tell the difference.

The universe chose directions in many broken phases.

It also kept some quantities stubbornly unchanged.

Our job is not to prefer one kind of world.

It is to find out which kind we have.
