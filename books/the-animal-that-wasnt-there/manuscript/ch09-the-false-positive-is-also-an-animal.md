# 9. The False Positive Is Also an Animal

A false positive is not nothing.

Usually it is something else.

This matters because investigators often treat `false` as if the instrument hallucinated. In biology, the alternative cause has its own ecology: a related species cross-amplified by primers, DNA transported from elsewhere, contamination from a laboratory control, a blue jay mimicking a call, a pileated woodpecker producing the wrong silhouette, a bear leaving a print distorted by substrate.

The best skeptical explanation is therefore not `mistake`. It is a competing organism plus a mechanism.

Lahoz-Monfort and colleagues showed in 2016 that even low false-positive rates can bias eDNA occupancy estimates if they are ignored. This is mathematically obvious and operationally profound. When the target is very rare, false positives can dominate the positive dataset.

Imagine a species present at one site in a thousand. Imagine a method with a one-percent false-positive rate. Even an apparently excellent assay can produce many more false detections than true ones across a large survey unless confirmation is strong.

Rare-target science is backwards from everyday intuition. The more extraordinary the scarcity, the more a positive result needs specificity.

This is why `we got a hit` is not the end of a search. It is the beginning of an audit.

A serious cryptid program would maintain a false-positive zoo: known species, artifacts, environmental transport pathways and human behaviors capable of generating each claimed signal class. It would deliberately try to make the instrument lie.

If your protocol has never been attacked by its confounders, you do not know what a positive means.
