# DNA Leaves the Body

An environmental DNA sample begins with shedding.

That sounds trivial until you try to build an inference on it. Fish do not shed at a constant rate. Amphibians do not shed like mammals. Breeding, stress, temperature, body size, feeding, disease and life stage can change how much genetic material enters the environment. Cells and extracellular fragments leave through mucus, feces, urine, gametes, skin and decomposition. The environment then starts dismantling the evidence.

Sunlight breaks molecules. Microbes consume them. Temperature changes degradation. Currents dilute and transport. Sediments can trap material and preserve it longer than the overlying water. A sample is therefore a mixture of biology and physics before a laboratory touches it.

This makes the phrase DNA detection deceptively clean. What we detect is the residue of a process.

For rare-species work, the distinction is not academic. Suppose an animal occupies a stream but sheds very little outside the breeding season. A negative survey in the wrong month may tell us almost nothing. Suppose a carcass upstream releases enormous amounts of DNA. A downstream positive may overstate the location of living animals. Suppose DNA persists in sediment. A positive core may be historical evidence rather than a census of the present.

The right question is not simply how sensitive the assay is. It is sensitive to what, across what time and space?

This is why eDNA methods matured from proof-of-concept into a discipline of controls. The 2016 synthesis by Goldberg and colleagues emphasized field and laboratory contamination, assay validation, inhibition, temporal and spatial processes, uncertainty around positive and negative results, and allochthonous DNA—genetic material arriving from somewhere else.

That list is the book in miniature.

A molecular trace can be more sensitive than a net and less local than a footprint.

The analogy to forensic science is useful if we keep its limitations. A hair at a crime scene may indicate a person was present. It may also have been transferred. A DNA profile can be authentic while the narrative built around how it arrived is false. Chain of custody does not solve every interpretive problem, but without chain of custody interpretation becomes theater.

Wildlife sampling has the same burden. Who opened the bottle? Was it sterile? Was the sampler upstream or downstream of known captive animals? Were field blanks exposed? Did the laboratory process high-copy positive controls in the same room? Was amplification replicated? Was the assay tested against close relatives? Were results confirmed by a second locus or laboratory?

The extraordinary animal raises the standard because the prior probability is lower and the social reward for a positive can be enormous.

A common mistake is to treat that higher standard as hostility. It is the opposite. A rare claim deserves a protocol strong enough to survive excitement.
