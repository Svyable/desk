# Contamination Is a Witness Too

The blank sample is one of the least glamorous objects in science. It may be the most honest.

A field blank asks whether target DNA entered a sterile sample through collection. An extraction blank asks whether the laboratory introduced it during processing. A no-template control asks whether amplification reagents carry the signal. Positive controls verify that the assay can work, while simultaneously becoming the most dangerous source of target molecules in the room.

A serious extraordinary-species search should publish the blanks with the excitement.

This sounds bureaucratic until one remembers what amplification does. PCR is designed to turn vanishingly small quantities of target DNA into detectable quantities. That is why eDNA works. It is also why contamination can become convincing.

Chain of custody should therefore be designed before anyone knows which samples are positive. Sample identifiers can be blinded. Replicates can be split between independent laboratories. Positive-control DNA can be synthetic and designed so accidental carryover is distinguishable from wild sequence. Sampling order can move from low-risk to high-risk sites. Equipment can be single-use or rigorously decontaminated.

None of these guarantees truth. Together they make certain errors harder.

The cultural problem is that controls are emotionally asymmetrical. A positive target sample is a story. A contaminated blank is an embarrassment. Teams invested in discovery can therefore experience a control failure as something to explain away rather than as data about the pipeline.

But the contaminated blank is a witness too. It is telling you that the experiment permitted target material to travel.

In a field obsessed with elusive animals, we should be at least as interested in elusive molecules.
