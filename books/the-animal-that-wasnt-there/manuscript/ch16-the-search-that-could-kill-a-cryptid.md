# 16. The Search That Could Kill a Cryptid

Most cryptid searches are designed to succeed.

This is their deepest methodological flaw.

They define success as any new ambiguous signal and failure as merely the need for more searching. The hypothesis has no death condition.

A scientific search should be capable of making the target less plausible.

Take a hypothetical surviving thylacine population. Before deployment, specify a demographic model: minimum breeding population, plausible home range, habitat use, road interaction, shedding into waterways and camera encounter rates based on comparable marsupial carnivores. Then design a multi-method survey with enough power that, if the population existed under those assumptions, the probability of getting zero confirmed detections would be very low.

Use camera traps, scat-detection dogs, water and soil eDNA, roadkill surveillance, acoustic arrays if justified, and local reporting. Blind molecular labs to site status. Duplicate samples across independent labs. Publish every negative.

Then decide in advance what zero means.

Perhaps it does not prove extinction. But it should eliminate a region of parameter space: `a population of at least N animals using habitat H at rate R is inconsistent with the data at confidence C.`

That sentence is more useful than `no evidence found`.

The same design can test other claims. A large North American primate should produce a different trace ecology than a rare woodpecker. An aquatic reptile in a small lake should be easier to constrain with eDNA than a pelagic deep-sea animal in an ocean basin.

There is no universal cryptid protocol.

There is only the obligation to design a search that can lose.
