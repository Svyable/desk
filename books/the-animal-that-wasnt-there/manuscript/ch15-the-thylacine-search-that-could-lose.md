# The Thylacine Search That Could Lose

The last known captive thylacine died at Hobart Zoo in 1936. Since then Tasmania has produced what extinction produces when an animal was large, distinctive and culturally unresolved: sightings.

Some are sincere. Sincerity does not identify an animal.

The thylacine is unusually attractive for a rigorous molecular search because museum specimens and subfossil material provide genetic reference data. Researchers have reconstructed mitochondrial genomes from historical and ancient samples. We know what to look for at the sequence level far better than we would for a wholly hypothetical creature.

That does not mean eDNA can casually settle the case.

A surviving thylacine population would be terrestrial, mobile and presumably sparse. Water samples might work only where catchments integrate material from animals crossing or drinking. Soil, scat and carrion sampling could be useful. Camera traps cover limited geometry. Roads create both detection opportunities and an argument from absence: a medium-sized predator persisting for many generations in a roaded landscape without an accepted carcass is not impossible, but the silence has evidentiary weight.

A good search should begin by writing the result that would lower belief.

For example: define high-priority habitat from historical ecology and modern land cover; sample water, soil and suspected scat across multiple seasons; use assays validated against close marsupial relatives; split blinded positives between independent laboratories; deploy cameras in the same catchments; publish all sampling locations at a resolution that protects sensitive wildlife but permits design evaluation; predefine how repeated negative seasons update occupancy estimates.

Then stop moving the goalposts.

This last instruction matters more than any sequencing platform. A search that can always migrate to the next valley is not a test.

The surviving-thylacine hypothesis should be allowed to become less plausible.

If it cannot, the project has become heritage, not zoology.
