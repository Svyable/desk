# 15. How Legends Preserve Biology

Legends are bad field guides and excellent compression algorithms.

They preserve salience, not measurement.

A dangerous animal becomes larger. A rare call becomes a voice. A nocturnal encounter gains motive. Generations of retelling can keep an ecological fact alive while destroying the variables a scientist would want.

This does not make folklore useless. It makes it lossy data.

Historical reports of sea monsters sometimes preserve recognizable encounters with giant squid, oarfish, basking sharks or whale remains. Indigenous and local traditions can encode distributions, migrations and species interactions. But retrospective matching is dangerously easy. Once you know the animal, old stories become magnets for resemblance.

A rigorous approach asks whether a tradition contains specific features that were recorded before the modern candidate was proposed, whether those features distinguish the candidate from known alternatives, and whether geography and season align.

The standard should be predictive surprise.

If a legend tells you to look in a particular habitat at a particular time and you then find a biological trace no one expected, the legend has earned evidentiary weight. If the match emerges only after flexible interpretation, it remains cultural evidence, not zoological confirmation.

The point is not to bleach mystery out of stories.

It is to keep stories from being forced to testify to things they never actually said.
