# The False Negative Has a Geography

The absence of DNA is not the DNA of absence.

That sentence is easy to turn into a slogan and then abuse forever. So it needs conditions.

A negative becomes informative when the survey had a known chance of detecting the organism if it were present. That chance is never one. The work is to make it high enough, measured enough and repeated enough that continued non-detection matters.

Geography is one reason it may not.

A pond is not a lake. A headwater is not a braided river. A shallow coastal lagoon is not the open ocean. DNA distribution follows mixing and source location. Sampling ten bottles from one accessible shore can look like replication while repeatedly interrogating the same bad piece of habitat.

The correct design begins with the animal's expected use of space. Breeding sites may concentrate DNA. Narrow channels may integrate upstream sources. Thermal layers may separate water masses. Seasonal runoff can increase or erase signals.

Rare terrestrial animals create an even harder problem. Waterholes can act as biological funnels, collecting DNA from many visitors. Soil samples can be hyperlocal. Air filters may integrate larger areas but complicate source localization.

This is why negative surveys should be drawn on maps before they are translated into conclusions.

For an extinction search, the strongest design targets places where a surviving population would have difficulty avoiding detection over repeated seasons. It samples independent locations, not merely independent tubes. It uses more than one evidence channel.

If the animal is aquatic, water may be paired with sediment, cameras, hydroacoustics and conventional nets. If terrestrial, eDNA may be paired with scat dogs, camera traps, acoustic recorders and community interviews whose evidentiary status is kept separate.

A monster hunt that samples where tourists stand has measured tourism geography.

A serious search samples where the animal's biology says it should leak into the world.
