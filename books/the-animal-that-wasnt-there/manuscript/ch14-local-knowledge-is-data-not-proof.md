# 14. Local Knowledge Is Data, Not Proof

Science has a recurring bad habit: arriving late and calling itself first.

The coelacanth, saola and many less famous species were known to local people before they were integrated into formal taxonomy. Hunters, fishers and forest communities accumulate encounter data at temporal scales academic field projects cannot afford.

Cryptozoology is right to notice this.

It often goes wrong in the next sentence.

Local names do not map one-to-one onto biological species. A single folk category may cover several animals. Several names may refer to age classes, sexes or seasonal forms of one species. Stories can absorb moral and cosmological functions. Interviewees can adapt responses to researchers' expectations.

The solution is ethnobiology, not romanticism.

Collect names independently across communities. Ask for free descriptions before showing photographs. Map habitat and season. Examine material culture. Test whether informants agree on diagnostic features. Compare reported distribution with known species. Where possible, let local experts guide sampling design.

The saola discovery is powerful because local knowledge led to material evidence.

That is the bridge we need: testimony generating predictions, predictions generating samples, samples surviving independent analysis.

Respect is not exemption from verification.

Verification is how knowledge travels.
