# 3. The Fossil That Wasn't Dead

On 22 December 1938, a trawler brought an unfamiliar fish into East London, South Africa. Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, curator of the local museum, recognized that it was unusual. J. L. B. Smith recognized what kind of impossible it was.

A coelacanth.

The story is often told as resurrection: a lineage known from fossils, absent from the record for tens of millions of years, suddenly alive on a dock. The rhetorical version is irresistible and slightly dangerous.

Fossil absence is not continuous monitoring. The geological record is a filter with biases so severe that entire habitats can become nearly silent. Soft tissues disappear. Deposition is patchy. Exposure is patchy. Collection is patchy. Taxonomic assignment changes. A lineage missing from younger rocks is not the same kind of absence as a terrestrial mammal missing from thousands of camera traps, roads, hunter harvests and genetic surveys.

The coelacanth survives because its environment and behavior were badly sampled by the methods that had generated the prior belief.

This is the recurring move of the book: ask what kind of absence you have.

There is no universal unit called `no evidence`.

No fossil is one thing. No roadkill is another. No camera-trap image after ten nights is almost nothing. No image after a rigorously placed network representing a million camera-nights in suitable habitat can be quite a lot. No DNA in one litre of river water says little. No DNA across replicated seasonal samples with validated primers, positive controls, occupancy modeling and known shedding characteristics says more.

The coelacanth also complicates the phrase `living fossil`. It was not literally unchanged. The term compresses evolutionary history into a visual joke: old-looking animal survives. For our purposes, the more important lesson is institutional. Scientists can be surprised not because science is uniquely closed-minded but because evidence arrives through channels no one had weighted properly.

Before 1938, fishers in the western Indian Ocean already encountered coelacanths. As with the saola, local encounter preceded formal integration.

This pattern can flatter cryptozoology. `They laughed at the coelacanth too` is a familiar maneuver even when nobody involved in the current claim has produced anything comparable to a coelacanth on a dock.

A real precedent has costs. If you invoke the coelacanth, you inherit the coelacanth's evidence: a whole body, available for anatomy, later specimens, a stable population distribution and eventually genetics.

That is the standard worth stealing.

Not ridicule resistance. Specimen resistance.
