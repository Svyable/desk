# 4. Absence Has a Denominator

Ecologists have a useful embarrassment built into their statistics: seeing nothing does not mean nothing was there.

This sounds obvious until you try to build a population estimate from field data. Suppose you visit a wetland and fail to detect a rare frog. There are at least two explanations. The frog is absent. Or the frog is present and you missed it.

Occupancy models were built to keep those states separate.

Let ψ be the probability that a site is occupied and p the probability of detecting the organism on one survey given that it is present. After repeated independent surveys, the probability of missing a present organism falls roughly as (1-p)^n. The equation is elementary. The consequences are not.

If p is 0.1, ten failures still leave a 35 percent chance of missing a present animal. If p is 0.8, ten failures are devastating. The same number of empty surveys can mean almost nothing or almost everything depending on detection probability.

Cryptid debate rarely has a p.

People count expeditions, years or witnesses. But `fifty years of searching` is not a sampling design. Search effort must be mapped onto habitat, season, method, target behavior and false-positive structure. Ten thousand tourist-hours beside a road may be weaker evidence than one month of targeted genetic sampling in a drainage where the animal should shed DNA.

The denominator also prevents a second mistake: treating every ambiguous detection as independent evidence. A thousand social-media photographs may all originate from the same misidentification process. If the false-positive rate is high and the observations are not independent, volume creates confidence theater.

This is where Bayesian language becomes useful without becoming mystical.

Start with prior odds. Add evidence according to how much more likely that evidence would be if the animal existed than if it did not. A sharp image with scale, metadata, multiple independent cameras and recoverable tissue may have an enormous likelihood ratio. A witness saying `large dark animal crossed the road at night` may have a ratio barely above one, especially where bears, dogs, deer and expectation effects are common.

Negative evidence works the same way. If a breeding population of hypothetical apes in a road-dense temperate forest should generate carcasses, feces, hair, nests, game-camera images and genomic traces at high rates, decades without verified material should lower the probability. If a small population of deep-sea cephalopods occupies an enormous dark volume rarely sampled by quiet low-light cameras, failure matters less.

The phrase `absence of evidence is not evidence of absence` survives because it is rhetorically perfect and mathematically unfinished.

Sometimes absence of evidence is strong evidence of absence.

You just have to earn the denominator.
