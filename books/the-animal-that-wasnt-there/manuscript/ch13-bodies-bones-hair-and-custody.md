# 13. Bodies, Bones, Hair and Custody

A specimen is powerful because it can disappoint everyone repeatedly.

You can measure it, section it, image it, date it, sequence it, hand it to another laboratory and discover that your first interpretation was wrong. Good evidence has an afterlife.

Cryptid material usually dies young.

A hair arrives without collection metadata. A bone has no excavation context. Tissue sits in preservative of unknown origin. A footprint cast has no photographs of the trackway before casting. Each missing link expands the space of ordinary explanations.

Chain of custody sounds like police bureaucracy because it was designed for exactly this problem: objects do not carry their histories inside them.

For biological anomaly work, a defensible chain should record collector, location, time, method, container, storage conditions, transfers, subsampling, laboratory accession and destructive tests. Ideally, multiple aliquots are sealed before anyone knows what the first lab reports.

The goal is adversarial reproducibility.

If a sample is truly extraordinary, its value rises when hostile laboratories can touch it.

This is one reason old museum collections are so scientifically rich. Their labels can be imperfect, but institutional custody often preserves enough context for new methods to revisit old material. The thylacine genome and historical RNA work are examples of evidence compounding over time.

A blurry photograph decays under scrutiny.

A good specimen gets better.
