# Presence Is Not a Population

One living animal is not a future.

This matters most in extinction searches. A confirmed individual can overturn the sentence extinct while doing almost nothing to overturn functionally extinct. A species can persist as a handful of aging animals, reproductively isolated fragments or one sex.

The public drama of rediscovery tends to hide this. We love Lazarus species because the story has a resurrection shape. Biology is less generous. Rediscovery can arrive five minutes before the end.

Environmental DNA complicates the story further because it can detect a taxon without revealing demographic structure. A repeated positive may indicate multiple animals or one highly shedding animal repeatedly sampled. Genetic variation across recovered fragments can help, but short amplicons often provide limited individual resolution.

A survival claim must therefore grow into a population claim.

Where are juveniles? Is reproduction occurring? Are detections spatially distributed in a way compatible with home ranges? Is genetic diversity plausible? Are there enough individuals to persist through ordinary stochastic events?

This is where many cryptid narratives become biologically expensive. A large vertebrate allegedly surviving for centuries in a bounded habitat requires not one remarkable animal but generations. Generations require breeding, food, mortality, bodies and genetic continuity. The larger the claim, the more ecological bookkeeping it owes.

That bookkeeping is not a debunking trick. It is how conservation biologists keep real species alive.

The same logic applies to the ivory-billed woodpecker. Even if one disputed twentieth- or twenty-first-century observation were eventually confirmed, conservation would immediately ask whether a breeding population survives, where its habitat is and whether reproduction can continue. The existence question would close and the harder demographic question would begin.

For a thylacine survival hypothesis the burden is similar. One late-surviving individual would be historically astonishing but would not imply that a hidden Tasmanian population persisted to the present. A modern population has to pass through every decade between the last accepted captive animal and now.

That produces opportunities for evidence. More generations mean more bodies, more scat, more hair, more road crossings, more interactions with prey and more DNA.

The absence of those traces does not prove extinction with mathematical certainty. It changes probability.

This is the uncomfortable work of extinction science: converting repeated failure to detect into an inference without pretending nature signs certificates.
