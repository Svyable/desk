# Detection Is Not Presence

A positive eDNA result is often described in ordinary language as presence. That compression is useful until it isn't.

Imagine a river. A fish sheds cells upstream. Current carries fragments past a bridge. A sampler filters water five kilometers downstream and detects the sequence. Where was the fish?

The answer may be upstream. It may be closer if other individuals contribute DNA. It may be impossible to resolve with one sample.

Now add human infrastructure. Fish are transported. Water is pumped. Boats move material. Sewage contains food DNA. Anglers carry bait. Predators eat in one place and defecate in another. A positive remains a real molecular observation, but the organism and the sample acquire separate biographies.

This was central to the Asian-carp debate around Chicago. Early eDNA positives above barriers generated understandable alarm because the management stakes were high. Yet officials repeatedly noted the gap between DNA detections and physical fish. Subsequent work did not make eDNA useless; it forced a more careful vocabulary about transport pathways, sensitivity and inference.

The public hears this as backtracking because public language prefers binary categories. Fish present. Fish absent. Monster present. Monster absent.

Ecology rarely offers those clean handles.

The distinction becomes even sharper in marine systems. Currents can carry DNA away from its source. How far depends on degradation, flow, particle association, depth, temperature and sampling design. A sequence recovered from seawater is not a GPS fix.

The same problem appears in air. Airborne eDNA opens extraordinary possibilities for terrestrial biodiversity monitoring, but wind is a transport system. Detection volume can be a feature if the question is regional surveillance and a bug if the question is whether an animal stood beside one tree.

For cryptozoology this is liberating. We do not need to pretend a first positive proves the creature. A positive can instead be used to redesign the search.

If a claimed aquatic animal produces repeated taxonomically diagnostic signals concentrated in one basin, the next move is denser sampling against hydrological flow. If detections cluster near tourist docks and vanish under controls elsewhere, human transport moves up the list. If multiple loci recover the same unknown lineage and independent laboratories reproduce it, the case strengthens.

The point is not to demand impossible certainty. It is to preserve the difference between what the instrument measured and what we want the measurement to mean.

Detection is an event in a pipeline. Presence is an ecological inference.
