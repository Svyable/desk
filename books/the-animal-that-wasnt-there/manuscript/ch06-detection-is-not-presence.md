# Detection Is Not Presence

A positive eDNA result is often translated into ordinary language as presence.

That translation is sometimes useful. It is never free.

A molecule has a location. An animal has a location. Those locations are not necessarily the same.

Imagine a river. A fish sheds mucus, cells, scales, feces, gametes. Current carries fragments past a bridge. A sampler filters water five kilometers downstream and amplifies a species-specific sequence. The laboratory result can be completely correct while the ecological sentence built from it is wrong.

The sequence was in the bottle.

Where was the fish?

That is a second question.

The Chicago bigheaded-carp episode is one of the best demonstrations because the management stakes were high enough to force everyone to learn the distinction in public.

The Chicago Area Waterway System joins two continental drainage basins through a heavily engineered network of canals, locks, treatment-plant discharges, navigation traffic, storm inputs, and occasionally reversed flows. Electric barriers were installed to reduce the chance that bighead and silver carp moving up the Illinois River could reach the Great Lakes. Traditional monitoring depended on nets, electrofishing, commercial crews, and eventually very large organized searches.

Then eDNA arrived.

Beginning in 2009, researchers began finding bigheaded-carp DNA closer to Lake Michigan than capture records suggested the invasion front had reached. The result was politically explosive because a water sample seemed to see a fish that nets could not.

It is tempting, with hindsight, to divide the episode into believers and skeptics.

The data are more interesting than that.

David Lodge's 2024 retrospective describes more than 3,000 water samples taken from July 2009 through May 2010, with 66 samples positive for bighead carp and 72 positive for silver carp across the Chicago system and selected southern Lake Michigan tributaries. The detections helped trigger the rapid-response structure that became today's Invasive Carp Regional Coordinating Committee.

On December 2, 2009, agencies treated roughly ten kilometers of canal with rotenone during barrier maintenance. Before that operation, eDNA had been positive in the treated section while conventional gears had not captured bigheaded carp there. The poisoning recovered one bighead carp upstream of the electric barrier.

That fish matters.

It means the early eDNA alarm cannot fairly be retold as a laboratory scare detached from organisms. At least one physical bighead carp had made it farther upstream than the accepted front suggested. The molecular method was detecting a management problem that ordinary capture methods had incompletely described.

But one fish is not every positive.

That is where the story becomes useful.

From 2009 through 2012, one later synthesis reports 43 bighead-positive samples out of 5,522 tested and 236 silver-carp-positive samples out of 5,503. Those detections were sparse, spatially complicated, and often unaccompanied by captured fish at the same locations. Managers therefore had to ask a question that early public arguments sometimes treated as evasive: what else can carry carp DNA?

Quite a lot, in principle.

Fish can be dead.

Slime can remain on a hull or deck.

A bird can eat a carp downstream and defecate upstream.

Fishing gear can move material between waters.

Storm drains and wastewater inputs can move biological material from markets, kitchens, or other human sources.

Barges travel from waters where bigheaded carp are abundant into waters where they are rare or absent. They can move water, residue, and, under some conditions, small fish.

The important word is *can*.

An alternative pathway becomes scientifically useful only when it survives an experiment.

USGS researchers did that work. In a 2014 study, carcass material, slime residue, and predator feces were deliberately tested as secondary sources. All three could produce detectable silver-carp eDNA after being moved into water without living target fish; detectable signal could persist for at least a month in some treatments.

That result did not prove that Chicago's positives came from birds, barges, or dead fish.

It changed the inference structure.

Before the experiment, `DNA detected → live fish nearby` might look like the only realistic causal chain. After the experiment, there were verified competing chains. A positive could still come from a living carp. It could also come from biological material that had acquired a different transport history.

Hydrology adds another layer because water itself does not honor management boundaries.

The Chicago system usually carries water away from Lake Michigan, but not always. A later analysis of 2009–2012 eDNA records found that the volume of flow reversal toward the lake had a statistically significant positive relationship with silver-carp eDNA detection probability. Several other environmental variables in the model did not show the same relationship.

That does not identify the original source of each molecule.

It shows that detection probability depends partly on where water has been.

This is the exact point at which the word *presence* starts doing too much work.

There are at least four different propositions hiding inside it:

1. target DNA is present in the sample;
2. target DNA entered the local water from a living organism;
3. a living organism is presently within some biologically meaningful distance of the sampling site;
4. a reproducing population occupies the management unit.

PCR can establish the first under a validated assay and contamination controls.

The other three require additional evidence.

Notice that this does not make eDNA weak. It makes it sensitive.

A highly sensitive detector finds traces conventional sampling misses. That advantage necessarily creates a source-attribution problem because the detector can see material after the organism, water parcel, predator, carcass, boat, or waste stream has moved.

The trade is not unique to molecular ecology.

A smoke detector is more sensitive than waiting to see flames. It therefore detects burnt toast.

A seismic network is more sensitive than human perception. It therefore detects quarry blasts.

A radar is more sensitive than eyesight. It therefore detects birds and clutter.

Better sensing usually enlarges the classifier problem before it shrinks it.

The Chicago program learned to use eDNA as one channel in a surveillance system rather than a molecular verdict machine. Agencies intensified conventional monitoring after detections. They calibrated assays. They studied alternative vectors. They moved sampling locations as hydrologic understanding improved. They compared molecular signal against fish-density gradients. They did not need every positive to mean a live fish sat beneath the bottle for the method to remain useful.

That is the management distinction the book should preserve.

Evidence can be **actionable** without being **identifying**.

If a species capable of major ecological damage produces repeated DNA detections above a barrier, a manager may rationally increase surveillance even when the posterior probability of a live local fish is far below one. The cost of checking can be much smaller than the cost of waiting for a captured breeding pair.

That policy threshold is not the same thing as a scientific claim that a population is established.

Confusing the two produces predictable arguments. Critics say agencies acted on DNA that did not prove fish. Agencies respond that surveillance tools are supposed to trigger investigation before proof arrives. Both statements can be true.

For extraordinary-animal searches, this matters even more.

Suppose a lake associated with a famous cryptid produces one sequence assigned to an unexpected vertebrate lineage. The interesting result is not `monster found`.

The interesting result is a branching investigation.

Was the locus actually diagnostic?

Does the reference library contain close relatives?

Was the sequence reproduced from a second extraction?

Does a second locus recover the same lineage?

Do independent laboratories obtain it?

Does signal recur spatially and temporally?

Does its distribution follow hydrology?

Does it concentrate around inflows, harbors, fish markets, tourist docks, sewage, bird roosts, or boat traffic?

Can the inferred organism plausibly shed enough DNA to generate the observed concentration?

Can any conventional method find tissue, a photograph, a track, a carcass, prey remains, or a living animal where the molecular map says to look?

A good positive does not end the search.

It tells you how to make the next search less stupid.

Negative eDNA has the mirror problem. Failure to detect a sequence is not absence unless the assay had enough probability of encountering material under the site's flow, shedding, degradation, and sampling conditions. A fish can be present and invisible to the bottle. A fish can be absent and its DNA visible in the bottle.

That symmetry is not a reason to give up.

It is a reason to model the pipeline.

The sample has a biography before it reaches the sequencer: organism, shedding event, transport, decay, deposition, resuspension, capture, filtration, extraction, amplification, classification. Every arrow can alter what the final read means.

Chicago's bigheaded-carp episode became scientifically valuable because managers were forced to stop treating those arrows as invisible.

The result is a better sentence than *fish present*.

**Target DNA was detected here. These source and transport models remain plausible. This is what we should measure next.**

That sounds less decisive.

It is more useful.

Detection is an event in a pipeline.

Presence is an ecological inference.
