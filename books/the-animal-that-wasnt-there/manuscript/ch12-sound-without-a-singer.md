# 12. Sound Without a Singer

Acoustic monitoring solves one problem by creating another.

A recorder can listen for months without getting tired. It can sample at night, in rain, far from roads. It timestamps signals and lets analysts replay them. For rare vocal species, this is a gift.

But a microphone records sound, not species.

The ivory-bill search demonstrated the ambiguity. Double knocks could be biologically meaningful, but branches, gunshots, machinery and other sources can produce impulsive pairs. Kent-like calls can be mimicked by blue jays or confused with other birds. Automated classifiers make scale possible while also creating an industrial supply of candidates requiring validation.

The same issue now appears in cetacean surveys, bat monitoring and gunshot detection. Classification performance measured on curated datasets can collapse in the wild because the real soundscape contains unknown negatives.

For cryptids, acoustics should be treated like forensic audio.

Preserve raw files. Publish detection thresholds. Maintain negative libraries. Blind expert classification. Use arrays to localize sources. Pair acoustic events with cameras, radar or thermal sensors. Most important, estimate how often the classifier produces target-like events when the target is known absent.

A sound without a singer is evidence of a sound.

Everything after that is inference.
