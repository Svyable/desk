# 10. Camera Traps and the Geometry of Rarity

A camera trap is a rectangle of probability strapped to a tree.

Popular imagination turns it into an all-seeing witness: forests are full of cameras now, therefore any large unknown mammal should have appeared. Field ecology is less convenient.

The camera sees a narrow cone. Trigger sensitivity varies with temperature, speed and body size. Placement favors trails, salt licks, roads, water crossings or whatever the researcher believes animals use. Some species investigate cameras. Others avoid them. Vegetation produces false triggers. Batteries die. Memory cards fill. Theft happens.

Survey design converts these imperfections into estimates.

Work on rare terrestrial mammals shows the basic tradeoff: rare species often demand more sites and more sampling occasions because occupancy and detectability are separate constraints. More days on one bad site may accomplish less than spatial expansion. More cameras can also become redundant if all are placed according to the same mistaken habitat model.

For a hypothetical cryptid, this creates a requirement believers rarely volunteer: predict habitat before looking at the data.

If every new sighting changes where the animal is supposed to live, the model cannot fail. If absence from camera networks is dismissed by saying the animal avoids cameras, then camera avoidance must generate independent predictions: approach trajectories, detection deficits relative to similar species, perhaps tracks entering but not crossing detection zones.

`It avoids cameras` is not an explanation until it can itself be detected.

The saola remains a powerful caution. A confirmed large mammal can be missed for years even in landscapes under active conservation surveillance. The correct lesson is not that cameras are useless. It is that camera-nights acquire meaning only through a species-specific detection model.

We need to stop counting cameras and start measuring the geometry of encounter.
