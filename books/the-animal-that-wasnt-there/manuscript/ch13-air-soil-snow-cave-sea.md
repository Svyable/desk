# Air, Soil, Snow, Cave, Sea

Water made environmental DNA famous because water is easy to imagine as a biological soup. The field has since escaped the bottle.

DNA can be recovered from soil, sediment, snow and air. Each medium changes the question.

Soil is local and persistent. Sediment can become an archive. Snow can capture tracks and biological material across a season. Air can sample animals that never touch the collection device. Cave sediments can preserve biological histories far older than any conservation survey.

This expansion changes what a hidden-species search can look like.

A terrestrial mammal need not walk into a camera's frame if it leaves hair, feces, saliva or airborne particles in a place that can be sampled. A cave-roosting organism may contribute DNA to guano deposits. A snow track can be sampled rather than merely photographed.

But every medium carries a different clock. Water may integrate hours to days under some conditions. Sediment may preserve older signals. Air may move rapidly. Soil can hold DNA after the organism has left.

The first question after a positive should therefore be temporal: what interval of animal activity could plausibly have generated this signal?

Ancient DNA makes the warning vivid. A molecule can outlive a population by thousands of years. The existence of old environmental DNA is scientifically wonderful and devastating to any rule that says DNA equals a living organism now.

The medium is part of the evidence.

For extinction searches, this allows a useful two-layer design. Historical environmental archives can establish where the species used to occur and help refine habitat models. Contemporary samples can then test persistence in those same systems.

The boundary between archaeology and ecology begins to blur.

A species can survive as sequence after it stops surviving as animal.
