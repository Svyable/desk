# The Ivory-Bill Evidence War

In 2005 a team led by John Fitzpatrick announced in Science that the ivory-billed woodpecker survived in Arkansas. The evidence included sightings, audio and a brief video. The announcement triggered conservation spending, intense public attention and immediate scientific dispute.

The dispute is valuable because almost everyone involved cared about the bird and still disagreed about what the evidence showed.

Critics argued that the video and other observations could be explained by pileated woodpeckers. Defenders argued that plumage pattern, flight behavior, reports and acoustic evidence fit the ivory-bill. Years of further searching failed to produce the kind of photograph or specimen that would end the argument.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed delisting the species as extinct in 2021, reopened comment and extended review in 2022, then in 2023 explicitly held the ivory-bill out of a final rule that delisted 21 other species because it wanted to continue analyzing information. As of the current Service species page, the last commonly agreed upon sighting remains the 1944 Singer Tract observation.

That institutional history is more interesting than a victory declaration for either side.

The ivory-bill problem is a perfect demonstration of correlated evidence. Several observers can increase confidence, but observers may search the same habitat after the same initial report. Audio detections can accumulate, but similar sounds have alternative sources. A video can be replayed thousands of times without becoming higher resolution.

What would eDNA add?

Potentially a lot, if reference sequences are diagnostic and sampling targets biologically plausible sites. Repeated independent molecular detections could redirect search effort. But swamp hydrology, low population density and unknown contemporary occupancy would make negative results difficult to interpret without carefully modeled detection probability.

A single positive would create its own war. Was it contamination from museum tissue? Could closely related woodpeckers cross-amplify? Did the sample come from a feather handled by a researcher? Was the sequence independently replicated?

The bird therefore provides a blueprint for how not to let one ambiguous channel carry an extinction decision.

A modern search should integrate cameras, autonomous audio, eDNA, habitat models and traditional fieldcraft. Each method should be asked to fail differently.

The goal is not more evidence in the abstract. It is evidence whose errors do not all point in the same direction.
