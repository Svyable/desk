# What Would Count as Rediscovery?

A rediscovery is not one thing.

There is legal rediscovery, when an agency must decide whether a species remains extant. There is scientific rediscovery, when evidence persuades specialists. There is public rediscovery, which usually requires a photograph. There is conservation rediscovery, which is not complete until managers know enough to act.

These thresholds can diverge.

A clean diagnostic eDNA sequence from a species believed extinct would be a major result. If independently replicated from multiple field samples with uncontaminated controls, it could justify intensive protection and search even before an animal was seen. That is sensible because conservation cannot always wait for courtroom-grade closure.

But calling the species rediscovered would still require care. DNA can persist after death. It can move. Historical material can contaminate. The claim should specify what has been established: recent environmental presence of diagnostic genetic material under a validated assay.

Then go find the animal.

The ideal rediscovery is boring in the best way. A molecular detection leads to a camera image. The image leads to a locality. The locality leads to repeated observation, scat, hair, perhaps a nonlethal tissue sample. Multiple laboratories sequence it. Taxonomists compare it. Ecologists find juveniles. A population replaces a rumor.

The great promise of environmental DNA is not that it lets us lower the bar for extraordinary animals. It lets us build stairs to the bar.

The larger philosophical change is about absence.

We used to talk about not seeing an animal as though the world had failed to present it. Now absence can be instrumented. Detection probability can be estimated. Water can be archived. Replicates can be designed. Negative evidence can accumulate without pretending to become certainty.

This is a better way to handle extinction because extinction is one of the few scientific conclusions whose object cannot appear in front of you if you are right.

The search ends asymmetrically. Presence can be proved by one extraordinary body. Absence must be earned by repeated failure under methods that should have worked.

That asymmetry will never disappear.

Environmental DNA does something subtler. It makes the failure measurable.
