# The Library Can Be Wrong

A sequence is identified by comparison. Comparison requires a library. Libraries are built by people.

This sounds obvious until an algorithm returns a species name with nine decimal places of confidence.

Reference databases contain extraordinary biological wealth and ordinary human error: mislabeled specimens, contaminated submissions, outdated taxonomy, short regions unable to discriminate close relatives, uneven geographic sampling and entire lineages that have never been sequenced.

For known rare species, assay design can target diagnostic regions and test them against close relatives. For truly unknown species the problem is harsher. How do you recognize DNA from an animal that has no reference sequence?

Metabarcoding can recover sequences that fail to match named taxa closely. But an unmatched sequence is not automatically an unknown vertebrate, much less a cryptid. It may belong to an unsequenced common species, degraded material, sequencing error, microbial life, food, or a taxon represented poorly in the database.

The giant claim begins only after the boring explanations survive attack.

Museum genomics offers one route for alleged recently extinct animals. Historical specimens can provide reference sequence. That makes targeted eDNA searches for species such as the thylacine conceptually possible. It also creates a contamination hazard: the same historical DNA used to design and validate an assay can contaminate the workflow.

Separation is therefore physical as well as statistical. Ancient-DNA laboratories learned this lesson painfully. Pre-amplification and post-amplification spaces, protective clothing, directional workflow, blanks and replication exist because amplification is very good at amplifying mistakes.

The reference library carries another philosophical trap. A method can only name what its taxonomy allows it to name.

Nature does not know GenBank accession numbers.

When environmental sequencing discovers an unexpected lineage, the responsible response is curiosity plus restraint. Recover more sequence. Test alternative primers. Sample again. Seek a body. The molecular anomaly should become the beginning of field biology, not the end.
