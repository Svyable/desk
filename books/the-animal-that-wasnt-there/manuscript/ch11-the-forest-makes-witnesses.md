# 11. The Forest Makes Witnesses

Eyewitness testimony is biological instrumentation made of meat.

That is not an insult. Human vision is extraordinary. Experienced observers extract ecological detail from tiny cues. Hunters can identify movement patterns before consciously seeing an animal. Birders distinguish species by flight silhouette at distances where a phone camera records only pixels.

Human perception is also reconstructive, expectation-sensitive and terrible at preserving measurement metadata.

A witness rarely reports retinal input. The witness reports an interpretation assembled after the event: `large cat`, `upright ape`, `striped dog`, `woodpecker with white wings`. Distance estimates depend on assumed size. Size estimates depend on assumed distance. Memory consolidates a category and then repaints detail around it.

The correct scientific posture toward witnesses is therefore neither belief nor disbelief.

Interview them before exposing them to candidate images. Separate free recall from forced choice. Record confidence before feedback. Map sight lines. Return to the location. Reconstruct illumination, weather, distance and duration. Ask what known species were considered and why rejected. Preserve the first account, because later accounts are contaminated by retelling.

Witness quality can be modeled.

An experienced local observer with a ten-second daylight view at twenty metres is not equivalent to a motorist seeing an animal in headlights for half a second. Cryptid databases that count both as `sightings` destroy information.

The forest does not merely contain animals. It produces observation conditions.

And those conditions are part of the evidence.
