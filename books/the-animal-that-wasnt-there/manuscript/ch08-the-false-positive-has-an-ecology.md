# The False Positive Has an Ecology

A laboratory false positive is easy to imagine. Someone contaminates a tube. The wrong sample splashes. An amplicon aerosol lands where it should not.

The more interesting false positives happen before the laboratory and are not false in the molecular sense at all.

The DNA is genuinely there. The organism is not there in the way the researcher hoped.

Call this ecological mislocalization.

An osprey eats a fish and moves. A heron defecates. A boat carries slime. Market waste enters sewage. Frozen bait melts into a river. DNA from a dead animal is transported through a system. Every pathway can produce an authentic target sequence with a misleading ecological interpretation.

The Asian-carp work forced these possibilities into management discussions because the stakes were unusually visible. Positive eDNA detections in waterways near the Great Lakes raised the prospect that invasive carp had crossed barriers. Yet direct captures did not always track the molecular signal.

The right response was neither to dismiss the DNA nor to call every positive a fish.

It was to map pathways.

That idea should be imported into extraordinary-animal searches immediately. Before sampling begins, investigators should write down every plausible route by which target DNA could reach the environment without a living target animal being present.

For an alleged surviving species known from museum tissue, one route is the investigators themselves. Positive-control material becomes dangerous precisely because it is rich. A few molecules from a control can imitate the discovery of the century.

For a famous lake monster, tourist commerce creates stranger pathways. If shops sell fish products, taxidermy, bait or biological material from candidate species, those sources belong in the contamination model. If researchers handle reference specimens before fieldwork, that belongs there too.

The language of false positive makes all this sound like statistics. It is natural history.

A good contamination audit asks what organisms people eat, what predators move, how water flows, where boats came from and what the field crew touched.

The false positive has an ecology because molecules inherit routes from bodies, infrastructure and behavior.
