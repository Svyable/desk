# The Animal That Wasn't There

## Cryptids, eDNA, and the Mathematics of Looking

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a lazy way to discuss cryptids. Put the believer on one side, the skeptic on the other, then let them throw blurry photographs at each other until everyone goes home feeling confirmed.

That is not this book.

The giant squid was a monster before it was a specimen, a specimen before it was photographed alive, and a photographed animal before anyone filmed it behaving normally in its own habitat. The saola entered science not by walking into a laboratory but by leaving horns in a hunter's house. The coelacanth was dead for tens of millions of years in the scientific imagination until a fish landed on a dock in 1938. These are not arguments for Bigfoot. They are arguments against a childish theory of knowledge in which anything not yet photographed is impossible.

But the opposite mistake is just as childish. If a creature is claimed for fifty years, searched for intensively, and never produces a carcass, a genome, an unambiguous image, a scat sample, a roadkill, a nest, a hair with provenance, a kill site, or any other repeatable biological trace, that long failure matters. It does not matter because absence magically becomes proof. It matters because absence becomes evidence once you know how often the animal should have been detected if it were there.

That sentence is the spine of this book.

The central variable is not belief. It is **detectability**.

A blue whale and a shrew can both be rare. They are not equally easy to miss. A nocturnal forest mammal and an eight-metre squid living hundreds of metres down are not equally observable. An animal that sheds DNA into a river is not equally detectable by eDNA at every flow rate, season and distance. An acoustic recorder does not hear an ivory-bill; it records pressure fluctuations that may or may not have been produced by one. Every method has a detection function. Every method also has a false-positive function. The interesting science begins when both are admitted at once.

I have tried to keep four bins visible throughout: what is documented; what is credible but incomplete; what is disputed; and what I am inferring. The bins leak. That is why the labels matter.

This book is sympathetic to strange animals and hostile to weak evidence. Those positions are not opposites.
