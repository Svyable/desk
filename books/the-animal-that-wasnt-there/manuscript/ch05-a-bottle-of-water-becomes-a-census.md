# A Bottle of Water Becomes a Census

There is something indecently efficient about environmental DNA. A field crew can stand on a dock, lower a bottle, filter water and recover evidence of animals they never saw.

The power becomes clearer when the target is rare. Conventional capture methods may require nets, electrofishing, trapping or repeated visual surveys. Those methods can disturb habitat and sometimes harm the species being sought. eDNA can increase detection probability while reducing contact.

But the word census should make us nervous.

For many applications eDNA is better at occupancy than abundance. Even occupancy needs translation. A positive means target DNA was detected under the assay's decision rule. It does not automatically mean one animal, ten animals or a reproducing population. DNA concentration can correlate with biomass under some controlled conditions and fail to scale neatly in the field because shedding, hydrology and degradation vary.

The temptation to turn molecular counts into animal counts is understandable. Numbers invite numbers. A qPCR cycle threshold looks quantitative. Sequence reads look quantitative. The instrument has decimal places. Ecology is under no obligation to respect them.

Still, the practical achievement is considerable. eDNA has been used for invasive-species surveillance, endangered-species monitoring and biodiversity inventories. It is particularly attractive near invasion fronts, where managers care about an organism while it is still rare enough to stop.

That is exactly where classical surveys are weakest.

The Chicago waterways became an early public demonstration. DNA from bighead and silver carp was detected in places where live fish were difficult or impossible to confirm by conventional capture. Managers faced an uncomfortable decision problem: the molecular test might be warning them earlier than nets could, but acting on every positive as proof of a local live population risked overinterpretation.

This is not a flaw unique to eDNA. Radar, medical screening and intelligence collection all confront the same structure. A sensitive early-warning system lives in the interval before corroboration.

The system becomes valuable when the response is proportional to uncertainty. A positive may justify intensified sampling, not a declaration. Repeated positives across time and space may justify a stronger inference. An independently confirmed sequence, followed by capture or image, changes the case again.

The problem with hidden-species culture is that it often skips these intermediate states. A single anomaly is asked to carry the weight of a zoological conclusion.

eDNA offers a more disciplined staircase. Detection. Replication. Spatial pattern. Orthogonal evidence. Specimen.

Nothing requires the final step in every conservation application. A rare salamander should not have to die to satisfy our philosophy of science. But extraordinary survival claims benefit from evidence that accumulates across independent methods.

A bottle of water can become a census only after we know what the bottle can miss.
