# The Animal That Wasn't There

The easiest animal to believe in is the one standing in front of you. The second easiest is the one in a museum drawer with a date, locality, collector, label, skin, bone and a curator who can tell you who misidentified it in 1912. Everything after that gets difficult.

For most of natural history the gold standard was a body. A specimen could be remeasured after the discoverer died. Teeth could be compared. DNA could be extracted decades later. The body carried its own correction mechanism because other people could come back to it.

But bodies are expensive evidence. Rare animals are rare partly because they are hard to encounter, and the act of collecting them can be grotesquely counterproductive when the population is small. Modern field biology therefore learned to accept indirect traces: tracks, hair, scat, calls, camera traps, feeding marks, nests. Environmental DNA is the most radical extension of that habit because the trace may be invisible even while it is sampled.

In 2008, Ficetola and colleagues reported a simple experiment that helped open the modern field: water samples could detect bullfrog DNA in controlled and natural freshwater settings. The proposition now sounds obvious. At the time it contained a strange inversion. Instead of finding the organism and taking DNA from it, the investigators took the environment and asked whether the organism was inside it in molecular fragments.

The inversion matters because evidence now has two locations. There is the location where the organism lived, and the location where its DNA was recovered. Those may overlap. They need not.

A water sample positive for a fish sequence can mean a fish was nearby. It can also mean DNA arrived from upstream, from a predator's feces, from a boat, from wastewater, from bait, from dead tissue, or from another pathway that is biologically real but spatially misleading. A negative sample can mean absence. It can also mean low shedding, dilution, degradation, bad timing, assay inhibition, poor primer performance, patchy water movement, or plain bad luck.

The technology therefore makes a peculiar promise. It can detect less animal than the human eye can see while demanding more humility about what detection means.

That is why it belongs in the same room as the thylacine, the ivory-billed woodpecker and every lake monster ever promoted with a blurry photograph. Not because the claims are equally plausible. They are not. The useful collision is methodological. An extraordinary-animal claim becomes scientifically interesting when it can be converted into a sampling design that has a nontrivial chance of returning a negative result that the claimant agrees to respect.

That last clause is where most monster hunts die.

If every failed survey is explained by saying the animal moved, hid, stopped shedding, migrated, lives deeper, lives elsewhere or is too intelligent to be detected, the hypothesis has acquired perfect camouflage. It no longer describes an animal. It describes an escape hatch.

A real search needs enough biological specificity to lose. Where does the animal feed? What water does it cross? What tissue does it shed? What season raises detection probability? Which sequence would discriminate it from its nearest relatives? What sample blanks would expose contamination? What independent laboratory would replicate a positive? What physical evidence should follow if a breeding population exists?

The remarkable thing about eDNA is not that it can prove a monster. Usually it cannot. The remarkable thing is that it can make the argument less theatrical.

The animal may not be there. The DNA may be. The DNA may not be there. The animal may be. Between those four states is the entire book.
