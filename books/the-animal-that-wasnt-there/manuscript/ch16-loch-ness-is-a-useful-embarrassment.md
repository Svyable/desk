# Loch Ness Is a Useful Embarrassment

Scientists often avoid famous monster claims because the reputational cost is asymmetric. A dull negative result looks like wasted time. Any ambiguity gets turned into a headline.

That is exactly why Loch Ness is useful.

In 2018 a University of Otago-led team sampled the loch for environmental DNA and later announced that it found no evidence supporting several popular biological candidates, while detecting substantial eel DNA. The project was widely reported as a monster test, although its scientific value was broader: a deep freshwater system became a public demonstration of metabarcoding and biodiversity survey.

The result did not prove that no unknown large animal could exist in the loch. No finite environmental survey can prove universal absence without assumptions about shedding, detection and coverage.

But the project constrained stories.

That is what good science often does before it solves anything.

If a proposed animal is a reptile, fish or marine mammal with sequences sufficiently represented in reference databases and if it sheds DNA into sampled water at detectable rates, broad repeated metabarcoding should recover related signals. Failure does not close every loophole. It makes some loopholes narrower.

The eel result created an instructive public distortion. Newspapers could turn abundant eel DNA into giant-eel speculation. The molecular finding itself did not establish gigantic eels. It established eels.

This is the recurring problem: a disciplined measurement enters a narrative economy that rewards the least disciplined interpretation.

A cryptozoological protocol therefore needs communication controls almost as much as laboratory controls. Results should be reported at the level measured. Unknown sequence is unknown sequence. Eel DNA is eel DNA. Absence of detected reptile DNA is not proof of no reptile under all imaginable conditions.

Loch Ness is a useful embarrassment because it forces scientists to practice uncertainty in front of an audience that wants a monster or a debunking.

Neither is usually what the water contains.
