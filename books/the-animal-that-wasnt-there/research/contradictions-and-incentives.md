# Contradictions and Incentives Audit

## Conservation hope versus evidentiary inflation

Rediscovery can unlock money, habitat attention and public excitement. Those incentives do not imply dishonesty, but they can make ambiguous positives institutionally sticky. Counterpressure also exists: declaring extinction can redirect scarce conservation resources and simplify administrative obligations.

## Skeptical overreach

Skeptics can overgeneralize surveillance coverage. `There are cameras everywhere` is not a detection model. Dismissing local ecological knowledge because it is non-institutional can repeat the exact blind spot visible in saola and coelacanth history.

## Believer overreach

Believers often import late-discovered species as generic precedents while omitting their physical evidence. Giant squid and coelacanth histories support incomplete inventory, not evidence-free persistence.

## Media incentives

Ambiguous rediscovery outperforms careful non-detection. `Maybe alive` can be refreshed indefinitely; a null survey is harder to sell. Image enhancement can also create false confidence by making pictures more legible without adding information.

## Laboratory incentives

A surprising molecular hit is publishable. Contamination audits are less glamorous. Rare-target studies therefore require independent replication, blinding and negative controls that are structurally capable of embarrassing the first result.

## Local incentives

Witnesses may have no incentive to deceive and still be wrong. Tourism, prestige, fear of regulation, hunting restrictions or researcher expectations can also shape reporting. Incentive analysis changes weight, not truth value.

## Core contradiction

The same history that proves science can miss large animals also proves that real animals eventually produce converging, inspectable traces. The book must preserve both facts.
