# Chapter 14 primary-source audit — the ivory-bill evidence war

## Scope

Chapter 14 currently has the right methodological instinct but compresses twenty years of argument into generic categories: sightings, audio, video, then “years of searching.” The stronger chapter is available in the evidence itself. The Arkansas claim produced one unusually information-poor physical record that could be argued over frame by frame; later searches accumulated extraordinary amounts of sensor effort without producing a universally accepted modern photograph, specimen, feather with secure recent provenance, nest, or diagnostic genetic sample. At the same time, a 2023 Louisiana team argues that long-term visual, acoustic, camera, and drone records collectively indicate intermittent persistence.

The editorial opportunity is not to declare a winner. It is to make the reader feel why the case remains difficult: evidence quantity, evidence independence, and evidence diagnosticity are different things.

## 1. Keep the 2005 announcement and the later audio evidence separate

Fitzpatrick et al. (2005) reported the Arkansas persistence claim in *Science*. The public case centered on repeated visual reports and the Luneau video, a fleeting record that became the main piece of physical evidence available for repeated scrutiny.

The acoustic case should not be folded casually into that original publication. Cornell presented the autonomous-recording-unit evidence publicly after the *Science* announcement. In August 2005 its researchers described kent-like calls and double knocks recorded by ARUs deployed at more than 150 sites. Cornell’s own account also preserved the ambiguity: branches, gunshots, raindrops, machinery and other birds could complicate double-knock classification, and blue jay mimicry was described as the largest obstacle to assigning some kent-like calls to ivory-bills.

**Claim boundary:** sightings, the Luneau video, and later ARU sounds are distinct evidentiary channels with different error structures and dates. They should not be bundled into one apparently simultaneous “rediscovery package.”

## 2. The four-second video is one channel, however many times it is reanalyzed

The Luneau video became the center of the dispute precisely because the same few seconds could support incompatible visual interpretations. Cornell’s defense emphasized white-wing pattern, flight style, and later wingbeat comparisons. Sibley, Bevier, Patten, and Elphick’s 2006 *Science* comment reanalyzed the record and concluded that none of the proposed diagnostic features eliminated a normal Pileated Woodpecker.

That disagreement is more useful than a generic sentence about “critics.” It demonstrates a central problem in hidden-species evidence: multiple measurements extracted from one ambiguous recording are not necessarily independent confirmations. Plumage interpretation, apparent wing position, flight path, and wingbeat rate can all inherit the same image-quality and identification uncertainty.

**Strongest persistence-side counterargument:** an ambiguous video does not become evidence of absence merely because a common species can be made to fit it. The Arkansas team argued that the combined morphology and flight behavior were more consistent with ivory-bill, and an elusive bird need not cooperate with documentary standards.

**Strongest skeptical counterargument:** the survival claim needs evidence that discriminates between hypotheses. If ordinary Pileated Woodpecker behavior remains compatible with the video, repeated analysis of that video cannot supply the missing discrimination by itself.

## 3. Search effort is enormous — and raw hours are not a detection probability

Two 2023 peer-reviewed papers make a productive pair.

Latta et al. report a ten-year Louisiana effort from 2012–2022 using visual observations, roughly 70,000 hours of recordings from 80–100 acoustic units, about 472,550 camera-hours from as many as 34 trail cameras, and roughly 1,089 hours of video from about 3,265 drone flights. The authors conclude that several evidence types suggest intermittent repeated presence of multiple birds consistent with ivory-bills. Crucially, the paper also states that post-1944 reports have not reached a quality threshold for general acceptance by ornithologists or the birdwatching public.

Troy’s 2023 *Ibis* review reaches the problem from the other direction. Its compiled table estimates 578,935 search hours from 2004–2021 across field observation, autonomous audio, cameras, video, and review effort, and argues that the continuing lack of indisputable evidence weighs against the rediscovery narrative.

These totals should not be converted into a rhetorical referendum. A camera-hour in a poorly placed patch, an hour of audio later screened by an algorithm, a human search hour, and a drone-video hour do not have the same detection probability. Many hours also share geography, seasons, target assumptions, equipment, observers, and classification rules. Conversely, low density, movement through huge swamp systems, and imperfect sensor placement can make even very large effort surprisingly weak against a one- or few-bird hypothesis.

**Editorial rule:** whenever the chapter uses “hundreds of thousands of hours,” immediately ask what survival state those hours were capable of detecting. One terminal bird, a nonbreeding remnant, and a viable breeding population predict different rates of photographs, calls, nests, feathers, juveniles, and repeated occupancy.

## 4. The strongest survival and extinction arguments are asymmetric

The strongest case for continued caution is not that every ambiguous sighting should be believed. It is that rarity plus difficult habitat makes non-detection an imperfect extinction test, and a peer-reviewed long-duration Louisiana project continues to report convergent but disputed observations. This is enough to keep the probability above literal zero and enough to justify asking whether the search design could miss the proposed survival state.

The strongest case against persistence is also not that one video looks like a Pileated Woodpecker. It is cumulative evidence production. More than two decades after the Arkansas announcement, intensive searches have still not yielded a modern record that has achieved broad agreement comparable to a clear diagnostic photograph, a recently shed feather with secure chain of custody and diagnostic DNA, an occupied nest, a carcass, or a specimen. A viable breeding population should be expected to generate more and more diverse traces than a single transient individual.

The chapter should therefore separate two hypotheses that public argument often mixes:

- **at least one ivory-bill survived unusually late**;
- **a self-sustaining population persists now**.

Evidence capable of keeping the first hypothesis alive may be much too weak for the second.

## 5. The federal chronology is not a declaration of either survival or extinction

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s 2019 five-year review recommended delisting due to extinction. The Service proposed delisting the ivory-bill in September 2021, reopened the public-comment period and held a hearing in 2022, then declined to include the species in the October 2023 final rule that delisted 21 other species. The Service said it would continue to analyze and review information before deciding whether to delist the ivory-bill.

The current FWS species page still states that the last commonly agreed upon sighting was in April 1944 on the Singer Tract in Louisiana.

**Claim boundary:** postponement is not official confirmation of survival. The 2021 proposal is not a completed extinction determination. The institutional history records unresolved evidentiary judgment under an irreversible management decision.

Fresh source checks through September 2026 did not locate a later FWS final rule resolving the ivory-bill as extinct or a post-2023 physical confirmation that has achieved broad scientific acceptance. Treat that statement as literature/status-search context, not proof of absence.

## 6. What eDNA could add — and what it could not

A molecular detection would be valuable because its main error modes differ from blurry flight video and eyewitness identification. But the chapter should not treat DNA as a magical adjudicator.

A serious ivory-bill eDNA claim should, at minimum, freeze these requirements in advance:

- reference sequence(s) demonstrated to discriminate *Campephilus principalis* from plausible sympatric and laboratory contaminants;
- field blanks, extraction blanks, PCR negatives, and explicit handling controls for museum or archival ivory-bill material;
- independent primer or locus confirmation rather than one amplicon carrying the entire claim;
- replicate extraction and sequencing, ideally with independent-laboratory confirmation for a first modern detection;
- chain of custody tying the sample to a place and time without contact from people or equipment recently exposed to reference material;
- a transport/persistence model appropriate to the sampled medium, so “DNA here” is not silently promoted into “living bird occupied this exact point now.”

A positive result could sharply redirect cameras, audio arrays, nest searches, and field teams. By itself it would not establish population size, breeding, or viability.

A negative result is even easier to overread. It only meaningfully updates extinction probability if the study can estimate the chance that a surviving bird or breeding population would shed recoverable DNA into the sampled medium, that sampling would intercept it, and that the assay would detect it. Without that denominator, “no ivory-bill DNA” is an observation whose evidentiary weight is unknown.

## 7. Recommended Chapter 14 architecture

Open with the physical constraint rather than a moral: a few seconds of 2004 video became an evidentiary object large enough to sustain a scientific argument for years.

Then move in four acts:

1. **Arkansas:** sightings and the Luneau video, followed by the 2006 *Science* dispute over whether the record excludes Pileated Woodpecker.
2. **Sound:** later ARU kent calls and double knocks, including Cornell’s own stated confounds and blue-jay problem.
3. **The denominator arrives:** the extraordinary later search effort, with Latta 2023 and Troy 2023 placed directly against each other. Do not let a huge number of sensor-hours masquerade as a huge number of independent trials.
4. **The new forensic test:** eDNA as a deliberately different error channel, with a protocol strict enough that a positive can survive museum-contamination and cross-amplification objections and a negative has a quantified chance of having found the hypothesized population.

The chapter can then end on a narrower point than its current generic “errors should point in different directions” line: the surviving question is not how much evidence has been collected, but whether the evidence would look substantially different if the bird were a Pileated Woodpecker, one lingering ivory-bill, or a reproducing population.

## What this audit should prevent

Do not write “the 2005 evidence included audio” as though all channels arrived in the same publication. Do not count different measurements extracted from one blurry video as independent confirmations. Do not treat raw search hours as identical Bernoulli trials. Do not treat FWS’s delayed decision as survival confirmation, or its 2021 proposal as a completed extinction ruling. Do not let one positive eDNA result imply a breeding population, and do not let one negative imply extinction without a detection model.
