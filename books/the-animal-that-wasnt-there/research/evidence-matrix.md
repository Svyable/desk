# Evidence matrix

| Claim | Class | Current posture |
|---|---:|---|
| Aquatic species can be detected from environmental DNA in water | 1 | Established by foundational experiments and extensive subsequent literature. |
| eDNA can improve detection of rare aquatic species | 1 | Strongly supported, conditional on assay and sampling design. |
| A positive eDNA sample proves a living animal occupied the exact sample point | 3 | Not supported as a general rule; transport and allochthonous DNA matter. |
| eDNA concentration can be converted directly into animal abundance | 3 | Not as a general rule; shedding and environmental processes intervene. |
| Asian-carp eDNA detections in Chicago waterways demonstrated live carp at every positive site | 3 | Officials explicitly distinguished molecular detections from captured fish. |
| The 2005 Arkansas evidence established surviving ivory-billed woodpeckers beyond dispute | 3 | Remains disputed; FWS continued review and the last commonly agreed sighting remains 1944. |
| Thylacines survive today | 3 | No independently confirmed contemporary population evidence established here. |
| Historical thylacine specimens provide usable genetic references | 1 | Supported by published mitochondrial-genome work. |
| Loch Ness eDNA disproved every possible unknown large animal | 3 | Too strong; finite survey and database limits remain. |
| Pre-registered multi-method eDNA protocols could improve extraordinary-animal searches | 4 | Methodological synthesis proposed by this book. |
