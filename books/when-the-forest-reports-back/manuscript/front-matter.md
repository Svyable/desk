# When the Forest Reports Back

## Biology as Military Infrastructure

Sven Hardy Benson

There is an old engineering fantasy that a sensor is a thing you place in the world. It has a battery, a housing, a specification sheet, perhaps a camouflage pattern. Somebody installs it. Somebody replaces the battery. Somebody eventually retrieves the dead box or leaves it to become archaeology.

Biology suggests a stranger arrangement.

A sensor can grow.

It can feed itself. Repair damage. Occupy a coastline before the engineer arrives. Notice chemistry at concentrations an ordinary instrument finds inconvenient. It can be a plant whose physiology changes when its roots encounter something of interest, a microbial consortium designed to convert an environmental molecule into a readable signal, or an oyster whose normal response to disturbance becomes one layer of an undersea surveillance system.

The Department of Defense has funded all of these ideas in recognizable form. DARPA's Persistent Aquatic Living Sensors program proposed using marine organisms to detect and help characterize underwater vehicles. Advanced Plant Technologies pursued plants as persistent ground sensors for chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear and explosive threats. Quantum Effects in Biological Environments asked whether some biological sensing advantages arise from quantum mechanisms worth understanding and exploiting. More recent programs push engineered microbes toward fieldable environmental sensing.

None of that means the forest is secretly spying on you. It means something more interesting and much better documented: the boundary between an instrument and an ecosystem has become an engineering variable.

This book follows that boundary. It is not a catalog of DARPA oddities. The question is what changes when the machine's useful properties include metabolism, adaptation, reproduction, ecological embedding and evolved perception. Hardware fails. Organisms fail differently.

The evidence rules are strict. Documented programs and peer-reviewed measurements are facts about what was funded, built or observed. Program goals are not achievements. Contractor claims are not field performance. Proposed military applications are not evidence of deployment. Patents establish filing, not efficacy. Speculation about future self-sustaining surveillance networks remains speculation.

The wager is narrower: biology is becoming infrastructure, and infrastructure that is alive changes the mathematics of persistence, maintenance, concealment, false alarms, control and failure.
