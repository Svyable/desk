# False Positives in a Living Ocean

The ocean is trying to frame the submarine.

A magnetic detector sees geology and infrastructure. A wake detector sees ships, fronts and internal waves. Infrared sees weather. Optical systems see plankton and turbidity. Electric sensors see natural induction. Gravity sensors see moving water. Chemical systems see biology and industry.

This is why a detector's most important specification may be false alarms per square kilometer per hour, not sensitivity.

Wide-area sensing multiplies the problem. Suppose an algorithm has an impressive specificity in a small test. Scan millions of resolution cells and rare errors become a daily flood. If human analysts then inspect only the interesting images, confirmation bias completes the trap.

The countermeasure is experimental design. Known target and no-target passes must be randomized. Environmental conditions must vary. Analysts should be blinded. Algorithms should be locked before final trials. Performance should be reported as receiver-operating characteristics, not curated examples.

The hardest controls are look-alikes. Surface ships, fishing activity, internal solitary waves, marine mammals, fronts and weather should be deliberately included. A detector that distinguishes submarine wake from blank water but fails against a tanker wake is not ready.

The sea is alive, moving and adversarial even when no enemy is present.
