# Why Sonar Became the Baseline

Sound won because seawater is kind to it.

Light is rapidly absorbed and scattered. Radio frequency energy fares badly in conductive salt water except at very low frequencies, where antennas and bandwidth become awkward. But acoustic energy can propagate over extraordinary distances when the ocean's temperature, pressure and salinity structure cooperate. The discovery and exploitation of deep sound channels made this physical fact strategic.

The Cold War built institutions around it. SOSUS, later folded into the Integrated Undersea Surveillance System, turned fixed hydrophone arrays and oceanography into a listening architecture. Submarines answered with quieting: machinery mounts, turbine and pump improvements, propeller and later propulsor design, hull coatings, operating doctrine. Anti-submarine forces answered with better arrays, processing, sonobuoys, active sources and environmental models. Detection became a duel between noise production, propagation and signal processing.

That history matters because it explains both the strength and the blind spot of current thinking. Acoustic methods are not obsolete. They remain indispensable. The U.S. Navy still describes IUSS in explicitly acoustic terms: detection, classification and reporting of submarine contacts, paired with long-term acoustic, oceanographic and hydrographic collection. It would be foolish to write a book about the end of sonar.

But a mature sensor family can make adjacent signals look secondary even when technology changes their economics. Magnetic sensors become smaller. Satellite revisit improves. Synthetic aperture radar processing grows cheaper. Optical detectors improve. Autonomous vehicles can carry instruments for days without risking crews. Machine learning can search enormous backgrounds for patterns, though it can just as easily manufacture confidence from badly curated data.

The relevant comparison is not sonar versus a magical successor. It is sonar plus everything else versus sonar alone.

This is a recurring pattern in sensing history. A new channel first appears too weak to matter. It becomes useful not because physics changes but because platforms, calibration, computation and deployment density do. A magnetic anomaly detector does not need to search the Atlantic independently if another system can get it close. A wake detector does not need to name a submarine if it can say, with disciplined probabilities, that a patch of ocean deserves attention.

The strategic unit is therefore not the sensor. It is the kill chain, or more precisely the evidence chain: cue, narrow, classify, localize, maintain contact. Non-acoustic methods become interesting wherever they can remove uncertainty from one of those steps.
