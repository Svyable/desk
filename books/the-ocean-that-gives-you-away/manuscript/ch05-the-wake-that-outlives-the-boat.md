# The Wake That Outlives the Boat

A submarine is smaller than its wake.

That sentence is the seduction behind non-acoustic detection. A hull may be tens or hundreds of meters long. Its hydrodynamic disturbance can extend much farther, interact with the thermocline, alter surface roughness under some conditions, entrain bubbles and carry turbulent structures long after the vehicle has passed.

The wake is attractive because it offers leverage. Instead of finding the submarine, find the damage the submarine did to order.

But wakes are not fingerprints by default. The ocean is an industrial-scale wake generator. Surface ships, internal waves, currents, fronts, fish schools, wind streaks and natural surfactants can create linear or quasi-linear features. A submerged body's turbulent wake evolves with depth, speed, stratification and ambient shear. Some signatures never reach the surface in a useful form. Others do but are too weak to distinguish from clutter.

Recent technical reviews treat wake detection as plausible and potentially useful for wide-area cueing, while emphasizing sensitivity to environment and difficulty of real-time localization. That is a sensible middle position. The physics of a wake is not in dispute. The operational performance of a specific remote-sensing architecture is.

One recurring candidate is the internal-wave wake. A moving body crossing stratified water perturbs density layers. Under favorable conditions those disturbances can propagate and modulate the surface through interactions that radar may see indirectly. This chain contains several opportunities for exaggeration. A modeled surface modulation is not a detection. A detected line is not a submarine. A submarine-like wake is not a track until it persists and predicts future position.

The research problem is beautiful because it is adversarial oceanography. To detect the boat, one must understand the sea well enough to know what the sea would have looked like without it.
