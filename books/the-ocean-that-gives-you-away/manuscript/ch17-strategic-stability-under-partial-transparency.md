# Strategic Stability Under Partial Transparency

Ballistic-missile submarines matter because they are difficult to find before they can retaliate. Their survivability supports second-strike confidence.

What happens if the ocean becomes only partly more transparent?

The dramatic answer is that deterrence collapses. That is too fast. An adversary must do more than occasionally detect a submarine. It must maintain track on enough boats, with enough confidence and localization quality, under wartime conditions, to believe a disarming attack could succeed. That is a much higher bar.

Partial transparency can still be destabilizing. If leaders believe detection has improved, they may move submarines differently, concentrate them in bastions, increase escort activity, alter patrol tempo or adopt launch-on-warning assumptions. Perception can outrun capability.

It can also be stabilizing in narrow contexts. Better tracking of conventional submarines may reduce surprise or improve maritime safety. The strategic effect depends on which boats are vulnerable, where, and with what confidence.

This is why exaggerated claims are dangerous even when false. A state that believes its rival has a secret wide-area detector may redesign forces around an imagined capability. Conversely, public skepticism can create complacency if real incremental sensing has quietly improved.

The most plausible future is not a transparent ocean. It is an ocean with fewer truly empty search boxes.
