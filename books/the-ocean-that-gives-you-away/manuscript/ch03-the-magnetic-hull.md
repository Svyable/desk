# The Magnetic Hull

Steel is inconveniently honest.

Put a large ferromagnetic object inside Earth's magnetic field and the field changes around it. A submarine hull carries permanent and induced magnetization. The result is not a luminous beacon but a local anomaly. Magnetic anomaly detection, MAD, exploits that perturbation.

This is the least speculative chapter in the book. The technique is operational history, not fringe physics. The U.S. Navy's own public description of the MK 30 anti-submarine warfare training target says the device provides a submarine-like magnetic anomaly signature for ASW aircraft equipped with MAD gear. Modern systems continue the line. In 2025, public reporting on the CAE MAD-XR integration for MH-60R helicopters described a compact passive sensor intended to detect changes in Earth's field caused by large metallic objects in the water.

MAD's limitation is geometric cruelty. Magnetic dipole fields fall rapidly with distance. Background geology matters. The aircraft itself is a magnetic contaminant. Maneuver, heading, electrical systems and platform vibration can complicate extraction. A sensor that is excellent for confirmation may be poor for continental-scale search.

This is precisely why it is useful as a control case for more exotic claims. MAD demonstrates the difference between physical detectability and operational searchability. The submarine perturbs the field. That does not mean an arbitrary sensor can find it from orbit. Range, noise, platform stability and background subtraction decide whether the perturbation becomes actionable information.

Navies respond by managing magnetic signature. Degaussing coils generate compensating fields. Deperming reduces remanent magnetization. Magnetic ranges measure what the vessel is leaking into the environment. None of this makes the hull cease to be steel. It lowers contrast.

The deeper lesson is that stealth is active housekeeping. Every detection channel creates a maintenance regime. Acoustic stealth requires quiet machinery. Magnetic stealth requires degaussing and calibration. Infrared stealth would require heat management. Wake stealth would require hydrodynamic and tactical discipline. A submarine that defeats one sensor may become more conspicuous to another if the countermeasure has physical costs.

The ocean gives you away one compromise at a time.
