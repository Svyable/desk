# Light Into the Sea

The ocean is not opaque. It is selectively unwilling.

Blue-green wavelengths penetrate farther than most visible light, which is why airborne laser systems can detect objects in the upper water column. The U.S. Navy's Airborne Laser Mine Detection System is a public operational example: lidar from an MH-60S detects and localizes near-surface moored mines. That does not make it a submarine detector at arbitrary depth. It proves the narrower proposition that active optical sensing from the air can interrogate useful volumes of seawater under some conditions.

Water clarity, depth, surface state, suspended particles, solar background and platform geometry control performance. A submarine that stays deep under turbid or rough water is a different problem from a mine near the surface.

The strategic temptation is to treat improvements in laser power, detectors and processing as a simple depth multiplier. They are not. Exponential attenuation is stubborn. More photons help until scattering, background and safety or platform limits take over.

Yet lidar belongs in the book because submarine operations are not always deep. Periscopes, masts, shallow transits and chokepoints create moments when optical methods become relevant. Detection architectures exploit vulnerability windows, not metaphysical permanence.

There is also an epistemic lesson. A real technology adjacent to a more dramatic claim can lend it undeserved credibility. Airborne laser mine detection is real. Therefore, someone says, deep submarine lidar must be secretly solved. The inference does not follow. Capability scales are exactly where classified-technology mythology hides.
