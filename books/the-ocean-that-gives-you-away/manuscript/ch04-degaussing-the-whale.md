# Degaussing the Whale

A useful way to understand a secret sensor is to study the public countermeasure.

Degaussing is an admission written in copper. Naval vessels carry coils because magnetic signature matters. Deperming facilities exist because steel remembers fields. Multi-influence ranges measure magnetic, electric, acoustic and pressure signatures because a warship presents more than one kind of target to the sea.

The engineering is less romantic than detection mythology. A vessel moves through different geomagnetic latitudes and headings. Its induced magnetization changes with orientation. Permanent components drift with repairs, shocks and material history. Compensation is therefore not a one-time spell. It is calibration.

That word should be kept close. Calibration is where many extraordinary sensing claims go to die. If a system purports to detect a submarine through an indirect field, the first question is not whether a dramatic trace exists. It is whether the instrument can separate target signal from platform signal, geological field, diurnal variation, storms, nearby infrastructure and processing artifacts. The second is whether the operator knew the target's position in advance. The third is whether blind trials exist.

Magnetic warfare has had enough decades to learn these humiliations. That maturity is valuable. It provides a template for evaluating younger channels: define the expected field; characterize environmental covariance; move the sensor through blank water; use known targets; withhold truth data; measure false alarms; repeat under different sea states and latitudes.

The whale in the title is intentionally unfair to submarines. A whale is magnetically subtle compared with thousands of tons of steel. Yet the analogy captures the practical problem: both are moving bodies inside a huge and variable environment. You do not detect the ocean object in isolation. You detect a deviation from a model of the ocean.

The better the model, the more interesting weak channels become.
