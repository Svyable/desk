# Editorial brief and falsification map

**Working title:** Ideas From The Future & Other Odd Things In My Head  
**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson  
**Genre:** linked narrative-essay nonfiction with clearly marked thought experiments; popular science, design, civic imagination, technological culture.

## Distinct contribution

This book is a cabinet of inconvenient future artifacts, each encountered as if it were an ordinary object from a world that took a different turn. The story lies in the distance between a technically plausible proposal and a society able or willing to accommodate it. Its apparent looseness is a feature: curiosity moves across ecology, medicine, electricity, memory, social architecture, law, public safety, astronomy, and failure.

It must not recycle the central theses of Desk's *Wild Speculation*, *The World Beneath the World*, *The Unobserved World*, *Keep Cooking*, *The Half-Life of Now*, or AI-institution books. These chapters are object-first and self-questioning, with humor and consequential real cases.

**Strongest competing view:** Interesting notions are cheap. Real progress comes from mundane implementation, regulation, financing, and adoption, so a cabinet of imaginings risks romanticizing the least valuable part of innovation. The manuscript must grant that argument full force rather than treating resistance as stupidity.

## Voice and narrative rules

- Do not invent personal travels, interviews, witnessed scenes, exact dialogue, or experts' motives.
- Hypothetical future scenes must be identifiable as constructed examples.
- Every chapter moves an idea through a different obstacle: water, tariffs, repair tools, biological evidence, a clinical limit, privacy, public space, time, incentives, public procurement, attribution, rights, political representation, perceptual translation, debris, detection, invention history, or failure itself.
- Less sermon, more object, person, document, institution, and awkward cost.
- Any factual assertion that gives a chapter its hinge needs a source in the research ledger and, where useful, a reader-facing link.
- Do not equate an analogy or theoretical possibility with an observed result.

## Progression

Chapters 1–5 concern ordinary physical systems made strange; 6–10 the human body, memory, solitude, and care; 11–15 institutions that might care for near-misses, alternatives, weather, rivers, and people not yet born; 16–20 the edges of perception, space, extraterrestrial life, unbuilt inventions, and the need to leave ideas open to correction.

## Falsification questions

Could urban shade increase displacement? Can vehicle-to-grid's economics survive battery wear and bidirectional charger costs? Do product passports produce repair or surveillance? What does the forest-network evidence actually support? Are transplantable organoids clinically ready? Can fMRI research be generalized to real-world dream-reading? Is perfect memory desirable? Do time banks make coercive care work visible or exploit it? Can near-miss reporting remain confidential under litigation? Are civic futures more than a planning theater? How do extreme-event attributions communicate uncertainty? Can legal personhood change ecological outcomes? Who represents future people without using them as an unaccountable veto? Does sonification convey information or manufacture emotional certainty? Can debris removal be financed and licensed? What observations could actually falsify a technosignature interpretation? Does archiving abandoned ideas distort failure into prophecy?
