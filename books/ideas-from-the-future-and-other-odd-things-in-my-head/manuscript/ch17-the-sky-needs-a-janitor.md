# The Sky Needs a Janitor

The job advertisement is not promising.

Wanted: operator of a machine that will chase an object through orbit, approach it without damaging anything, grasp it despite uncertain motion, and change its trajectory safely. The workplace is remote, the target may be tumbling, and the customer is not entirely sure who has legal authority to authorize the work. Previous experience with garbage collection is welcome but unlikely to help.

Someone has to clean up the sky.

The statement sounds ridiculous because people imagine space as the definition of emptiness. Earth orbit is immense compared with a city street, and most of it is not occupied. But particular orbital regions are increasingly crowded with working spacecraft, defunct satellites, rocket bodies, and fragments created by past missions and collisions. The problem is not that a few pieces of junk spoil the view. Objects move at enormous relative speeds, and a collision can damage a satellite or create additional fragments. The debris can persist depending on altitude and atmospheric drag.

The European Space Agency's [2026 Space Environment Report](https://www.esa.int/articles/esa-space-environment-report-2026) makes the scale and urgency unusually concrete. According to the report, more than 300 launches took place in 2025, placing over 4,000 new payloads into orbit. ESA describes continued growth in large constellations, expanding debris populations, and a long-term danger from fragmentation and cascading collisions. It argues for more effective debris prevention and active removal. The report is not a proclamation that space is already unusable. It is a warning about an environment whose future depends on what operators do now.

The apparent contradiction is interesting. Satellites provide services many people use every day: communications, navigation, weather observations, Earth science, and other capabilities. The industry is not simply a collection of reckless people making trash. It is producing valuable infrastructure while accumulating risks that individual operators cannot fully control. A satellite owner may benefit from launching today, while some future collision costs are shared across others who use the same orbital environment.

This is a familiar economic problem in an unfamiliar location.

The first task is prevention. Spacecraft can be designed to reduce debris creation, avoid collisions where possible, and leave useful orbits at the end of their lives under applicable rules and technical capabilities. Operators can share information and coordinate maneuvers. A design can include mechanisms to passivate stored energy or facilitate disposal. Prevention is more attractive than trying to retrieve fragments later because catching a fast-moving object in orbit is exceptionally difficult.

Yet prevention does not remove all debris already there. A failed satellite may be unable to maneuver. A rocket body may have been abandoned years ago. Fragments may be too small or too numerous for any practical removal mission. Orbital decay can eventually bring some objects into the atmosphere, but the timescale varies greatly with altitude and other conditions.

The janitor cannot sweep the sky with a broom. The tools have to be specialized.

A removal vehicle might rendezvous with a target, match its motion, and alter its orbit so it reenters safely or otherwise reaches a permitted disposal state. This requires navigation, control, sensors, propulsion, and a method of interacting with the target. The target may be uncooperative. It may tumble, contain residual energy, or have structures that are unsuitable for grappling. A failed operation could create new fragments, worsening the problem it set out to solve.

There is a wonderfully unfair requirement here: the cleanup machine must be more careful than many of the objects it is cleaning up.

Now imagine the contract. Who pays to remove a defunct spacecraft? The owner that launched it? A government that authorized the activity? A consortium of current operators threatened by it? An international fund financed by space users? The original operator may no longer exist. The object may date from a period with different expectations. The benefits of removal may be spread across many future missions, making it difficult for any individual company to justify the expense.

There are also legal and security concerns. Under international space law, jurisdiction and control over registered space objects and obligations concerning them matter. A private company cannot simply select somebody else's satellite and drag it away like an abandoned shopping cart. A machine capable of approaching and moving a spacecraft could also be used in ways that threaten operational satellites. The technology has implications for trust, authorization, and military security. Good intentions do not remove those concerns.

The janitor needs a permission slip. In some circumstances the permission slip may be harder to obtain than the spacecraft.

This raises the possibility of a new design standard. Spacecraft could include accessible interfaces for servicing or removal, allowing an authorized operator to approach and grapple them more reliably. Such an arrangement would need interoperability, security, and safeguards against misuse. A removal interface is simultaneously a helpful maintenance feature and a potential point of vulnerability. The engineering team must balance those risks in the context of the vehicle and mission.

It reminds me of the repair tag on the washing machine. Design for maintenance is easiest to discuss before a product is built. The space version is more demanding because the repairer must travel at orbital velocity and may never physically meet the original manufacturer.

There is a second complication: what counts as removal? Sending an object into the atmosphere may reduce orbital hazard, but reentry has implications for ground casualty risk and atmospheric effects. ESA's report notes increasing attention to the environmental consequences of reentries, including the need for better scientific understanding. A cleanup system should not declare the problem solved merely by moving the unwanted material from a place where humans can see it to one where monitoring is more difficult.

The full life cycle matters even when the object spends most of its existence above our heads.

A responsible strategy might combine stricter end-of-life disposal, better tracking, coordination, and targeted removal of objects whose collision risk justifies the expense. This would require modeling risk and evaluating interventions, not collecting the largest object simply because it looks dramatic in a publicity video. Some fragments may be impossible to catch individually. The goal would be to reduce future risk, not produce a perfect photograph of a spotless orbit.

A metric is needed, but the metric can also mislead. The number of objects removed may be less important than the expected reduction in dangerous collisions. One large object in a critical orbit might pose a greater long-term concern than several small objects in locations where they will reenter soon. Estimates depend on models and uncertainties. A successful removal program should publish its assumptions and compare outcomes with the alternatives.

The imagined janitor could be rewarded for reducing risk rather than hauling away mass.

There is a business opportunity here, but it is not guaranteed to be profitable. Governments might purchase cleanup services. Satellite operators might cooperate on risk reduction. Insurers could have interests in safer orbital environments. In-orbit servicing could make spacecraft more durable or adaptable. Each proposal must confront costs, legal authority, liability, technical reliability, and the question of who receives the benefit.

The danger is to assume that because debris removal is socially valuable, a market will automatically pay someone to perform it. Markets require a customer with a reason and ability to pay. Many public problems remain unsolved precisely because the beneficiaries are numerous and dispersed.

Perhaps the future needs a cleanup fee assessed at launch, designed to fund responsible end-of-life operations and, where justified, removal of legacy risks. Such a fee would require careful rules, international cooperation, and avoidance of perverse incentives. It might make new entrants pay for old debris, or encourage operators to shift jurisdiction. A badly designed levy could punish useful missions without changing the behavior creating risk. The administrative problem is not an afterthought.

A more direct approach might strengthen disposal requirements and enforcement. That too depends on national licensing regimes, international coordination, and the ability to verify compliance. If rules are strict in one jurisdiction but weak elsewhere, the shared orbital environment remains exposed. A global resource resists governance by a single country, even when that country launches a large share of the spacecraft.

This is the moment when an ordinary-sounding public service becomes international law.

I imagine the future janitor completing one successful mission. A defunct object is removed from a dangerous orbit under an authorized agreement. There is no stirring view of an astronaut hauling garbage into a truck. The operation is executed by robots and ground teams, with small adjustments and frequent checks. The public hears little about it. The risk of future collisions is reduced by some uncertain amount. Nobody can point to the particular collision that was prevented.

The Ministry of Almost would approve.

The sky will never be literally clean. Meteoroids exist. Old objects decay on different schedules. New missions introduce new risks. Space activity will continue to produce complicated trade-offs, and even excellent mitigation cannot abolish all hazards. The objective is to keep useful orbits safe enough for generations to benefit from them without treating the environment as an infinite free disposal service.

In another century, an orbital cleanup contractor might be as unremarkable as a municipal waste service. Technicians would complain about budgets. Engineers would argue about standards. Insurance adjusters would question claims. The occasional failure would be investigated. The operating company would send an invoice.

I hope the invoice includes the price of doing nothing.
