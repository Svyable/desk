# The Envelope in the Wrong Century

The envelope has no return address. It is made of the thick, unpleasant paper that official notices use when they want the contents to feel expensive. Across the front, in the handwriting of a person who is apparently in a hurry, somebody has written: OPEN BEFORE IT IS OBVIOUS.

There is no envelope, of course. This is how I imagine the book beginning because the actual delivery system for ideas is so disappointing. Usually an idea arrives while you are doing something else, refuses to introduce itself, and takes up residence in a part of your mind that was meant for remembering where you parked. The more peculiar ones do not announce their subject. They say something like, What if a street could move? Or, Why does a house pay for electricity if its car has a larger battery than its furnace? Or, What would happen if a government collected all the disasters that nearly happened and paid as much attention to them as the disasters that did?

A sensible person asks whether the questions can be answered. Another kind of sensible person asks whether they should be answered at all. I tend to wonder who would get angry first if they were.

That is a surprisingly efficient way to locate the interesting part.

Consider a city on a day when the pavement is too hot to make a reasonable invitation to walk. The municipal government could issue an advisory and tell residents to remain indoors. It could put cooling centers on a map. It could ask people to check on older neighbors. These are worthwhile responses to dangerous heat. But imagine somebody in the public works department asking a less responsible question: if a road must occasionally be closed to cars for construction, a parade, or a visiting dignitary, why can't it occasionally be closed to sunlight?

The obvious difficulty is that sunlight has never applied for a permit.

The worker means shade. Not a beautiful drawing of a park in a climate plan. Actual shade on the route between a bus stop and the place people need to go. Shade for a person carrying groceries who cannot solve the heat problem by buying an expensive car with a better air conditioner. Shade that appears at the right hour and can be maintained, financed, and measured. A heat map turned into a municipal schedule.

The moment the idea becomes practical, the trouble begins. Which streets? Which residents are entitled to the first installations? Who owns the awnings? Will they survive wind? Does the utility company have equipment in the way? Will businesses complain that the shading changes their storefronts? Does the sidewalk owner become liable when a mechanism fails? Will the city plant trees instead, knowing that trees take time, water, and care and may someday lift the pavement? A summer intervention has acquired a procurement process, an insurance question, and a budget.

By the time a committee finishes the preliminary hearing, the future is sitting outside, sweating.

I find this more interesting than a city rendered in chrome and covered with flying vehicles. A shaded sidewalk is a much smaller miracle. It is also much closer to the point at which an idea becomes a change in ordinary life. The chrome city asks us to admire its appearance. The sidewalk wants to know who has the ladder.

Future writing has always had a taste for finished objects. The flying car is the grand champion. Every drawing gives it a clean underside, generous windows, and a family whose hair remains undisturbed while the vehicle glides past a skyscraper. The drawing rarely includes a noise complaint, an air-traffic controller, a battery inspection, a collision report, or the problem of getting five thousand people to land at the same shopping center on Saturday. A flying car is an idea in the way that a perfectly fried egg is an idea about breakfast. The actual breakfast requires a stove, a pan, some heat, and the possibility that something sticks.

I am fond of the flying car as a drawing. I would be interested in one that came with a zoning dispute.

Every plausible future contains somebody who has to service it. That may be the most dependable prediction in this book. Bridges are maintained. Sewer pipes are replaced. Software needs patching. Machines break at the points where marketing illustrations remove the fasteners. The glamorous part of an invention can be spectacularly short compared with the long, mostly invisible period in which somebody has to keep it working.

There is a peculiar injustice in the history of progress: the person who dreams up a device may be remembered, while the engineer who made it safe enough to use, the technician who learned its failure modes, the worker who manufactured it in quantity, and the clerk who created the right place for it on a form may disappear from the story. We have trained ourselves to imagine innovation as a spark. The spark is sometimes real. It is not a power station.

I am not proposing that imagination should be discouraged until it submits a feasibility study. That would be a terrible arrangement, and the people most qualified to conduct the studies would soon run out of things to study. Odd ideas are valuable partly because they arrive before their purpose has been agreed upon. They can cross from one discipline into another without a professional license. They can be funny in a way an established system cannot afford to be funny. Their first advantage is that they do not yet know what the rules are.

Their first danger is the same.

A great deal of contemporary forecasting has learned to counterfeit the future's authority. The forecast appears as a timeline, sometimes with a precision that should embarrass its author. By a particular year, a technology reaches a particular capability; by another year, an industry disappears; a profession ends; a nation becomes dominant; a threshold is crossed. There are graphs. The curves are persuasive, especially when they have been taken from a very real phenomenon and carried several years beyond the measurements. The future itself is then expected to uphold the extension.

The trick is that a real trend is not a guaranteed destination. Power grids, factories, hospitals, courts, households, and ecosystems are not waiting patiently to be interpolated. A technical capability can improve while the cost of deployment stalls. A thing can become cheap to make and remain expensive to own. An invention can satisfy every engineer in the room and offend almost everybody who must live near it.

A forecast ought to be a claim that can be wrong. A story can explore a possibility without pretending to know its probability. Confusing those two activities has done strange things to public conversation. People start fighting about whether an imaginary device will exist in a named year rather than asking why anyone would want it or which hidden assumption makes it possible.

The envelope is my attempt to preserve the more useful activity. It contains an invitation to examine a possibility. It does not contain tomorrow's newspaper.

The distinction matters most when the possibilities are attractive. I can imagine a machine that records a dream, converts it into moving images, and sends the result to your phone before you have finished brushing your teeth. That is an extraordinary idea. I would like to see a good demonstration. I would also like to know what the machine does when it is wrong, because dreams are not stored in the brain like video files waiting for an export command. The scientific work on decoding aspects of brain activity is fascinating and constrained. Those constraints belong in the story. In some cases the most important invention is a reliable way to say that the model has no idea.

Once a machine could construct a convincing picture of something you may or may not have dreamed, a more uncomfortable invention would be needed: a way to keep somebody else from claiming that picture is evidence of what you thought.

The second invention has no romantic rendering. It looks like a right, a procedure, an audit, an unexciting legal argument. It may prove more consequential than the first.

That is a recurrent pattern in what follows. A physical ability suggests an institutional problem. The institutional problem suggests a different technology. The technology produces a new form of dependency. If the idea survives those encounters, it begins to resemble the future. If it does not, the failure might still teach us something.

Sometimes a good idea is a mistake we need to make cheaply.

Think about the first person who suggested that a vehicle battery might be useful when a house loses power. The thought is almost embarrassingly obvious once the capacities are placed beside each other. A car may carry enough stored electricity to supply important household loads for a meaningful period, depending on the house and the equipment. Why should all that energy sit in the driveway while the freezer thaws?

Then the obvious thought meets the less obvious details. The vehicle must support bidirectional power. The installation has to isolate safely from the grid during an outage. The transfer equipment must be approved and compatible. A battery's owner may worry about wear, warranty, and arriving at work with insufficient charge. The utility may have rules about exporting energy and may place little financial value on a household's contribution. A car is transportation first; its owner may not want an electricity cooperative in the parking space.

Nothing about those objections makes the original thought foolish. They are the contents of the thought after it has grown up.

I want to put the grown-up objections in the book alongside the childish excitement. Both are necessary. Without excitement, progress becomes a maintenance schedule. Without objections, it becomes a trade show with a remarkable rate of structural collapse.

There is another problem with the envelope: who is it addressed to? A future may be desirable to one person and a nuisance or danger to another. A driver who enjoys a private, climate-controlled trip may feel no urgency to shade a bus stop. A homeowner with solar panels and a new electric vehicle has a different idea of an energy revolution from a renter whose building has inadequate wiring. A company producing replaceable parts may welcome a right to repair; the company selling devices engineered to be opened only by its own technicians may develop a sophisticated philosophy of why opening them would be unsafe.

The future does not arrive as a referendum in which every participant receives an equal share of the vote.

Many ideas are already available in some places. Their distribution is the unsolved invention. The shade exists on the campus with a landscape budget but not at the bus stop. The resilient house exists for the household that can afford its equipment. The organ replacement exists in a research paper but cannot yet be grown safely, reproducibly, and at scale for the person waiting in a clinic. A technology reaches the world in a different order from the order in which the world needs it.

There is no reason to assume this difference will become smaller as inventions become cleverer. It may grow. New capacities often accumulate around older advantages. A person with capital can adopt equipment that reduces future expenses; a person without capital continues paying the high expenses that make saving for equipment difficult. A wealthy town can purchase improved infrastructure before its existing systems fail. A poorer town must spend money responding to the failures. A technical future that ignores those accounting relationships is really a proposal for a much smaller audience than its author admits.

At this point the reader may suspect that I intend to ruin every attractive idea with a bill. Not every idea needs to be a policy recommendation. Some should remain playful for a while. There is room for a machine that turns the nighttime sky into music, for an archive of inventions that never found a buyer, for a letter addressed to people who cannot possibly answer, and for a detective story in which the main clue is the continued absence of a signal from another civilization. Wonder is an adequate reason to investigate something.

But wonder lasts longer when it survives questions.

One useful test is to remove the device and leave the problem. If the dream recorder never works, why do people want to revisit dreams? Perhaps they want to know themselves more accurately. Perhaps they want proof of an experience they cannot describe. Perhaps they fear the ordinary loss of the night. If the machine cannot be built, the desire remains. It may send us toward sleep research, art, memory, medicine, or simply better ways of talking about things that disappear.

Another test is to remove the problem and keep the device. Suppose the recording mechanism eventually becomes cheap and precise. Does everybody then need it? A new capacity creates temptation to use it whether or not the use improves a life. Technology is especially good at transforming an optional activity into something difficult to decline. The first person to record a dream for amusement will have a different relationship to the device from the employee asked to prove that a stressful night did not interfere with concentration. The invention acquires a management department. Nobody put that in the original sketch.

The third test is to ask whether the idea requires the future to become morally better before it can work. I distrust ideas that succeed only after selfishness, inequality, conflict, bureaucratic delay, and fatigue have been cured. Those are demanding prerequisites. If we discover a procedure for eliminating them, it deserves its own book and should probably be tried before the rest.

A practical future is one in which ordinary people continue to have ordinary motives. They want convenience. They want to be left alone. They want to know who is paying. They defend things they own and habits they understand. Some are generous; some are exploitative; most are neither consistently. A well-designed institution makes good behavior easier without requiring saints to occupy every office. A well-designed invention keeps working on a Thursday afternoon when the exciting launch ceremony is over and the person who knows the password is out sick.

This is why I am drawn to the overlooked maintenance worker in every speculative drawing. Where does that person live? What does the job pay? Can the device be repaired while the business remains open? Do replacement parts arrive in time? Is the failure visible before it harms someone? Does the person have the authority to stop the machine?

An idea from the future might be the machine. Another, much more difficult one might be the arrangement that allows its repairer to say no.

I also want the book to make an honest place for the absurd. An idea can sound ridiculous because it is ridiculous, and the distinction is usually easier to make after someone has tried it. A city government might announce a department that buys shade by the hour. It may be an administrative comedy. It may fail because measuring shade is too complicated, or because the city would be better off planting and maintaining trees, or because a neighborhood has more urgent needs. Yet the attempt forces a useful question into public view: can someone without private property claim access to a tolerable outdoor temperature as part of an ordinary trip through the city?

The administrative form may die. The question may survive for generations.

This is different from the mythology of the rejected genius. The fact that somebody laughs at an invention does not establish that the inventor is right. Most bad ideas deserve to be laughed at, although ridicule is not a substitute for an experiment. Many good ideas also fail for reasons that have nothing to do with timid officials. Physics can be inconvenient. Biology is an exceptionally thorough reviewer. Markets are inconsistent judges of long-term worth, but they are very effective at demonstrating that people are unwilling to pay the required price. Institutions can protect incumbents and still identify genuine risks. The adult response is to discover which objection is real.

A reliable notebook should record the failed experiment as carefully as the intoxicating hypothesis. Mine is likely to collect too many intoxicating hypotheses. That is one reason to invite the reader in.

Somewhere in the next chapters a river will receive legal representation. This is no pure fantasy: law already has examples. The interesting question is how the representative speaks for an ecological system while living among people with competing claims. A forest will appear to communicate, though the science behind popular stories about underground networks is contested. A medical laboratory will grow tissue, leaving the harder problem of building something safe, vascularized, and durable inside a body. The sky will fill with objects that nobody wants to own after they stop working. The future will require garbage collection in a place where it is very difficult to pick up the garbage.

There are no instructions for building all these things in the envelope. Some are observations of beginnings already underway. Some are proposals that would need trials. A few are provocations meant to expose the assumptions inside arrangements we take for granted. The latter are allowed to fail. In fact I hope some of them fail in illuminating ways.

The envelope has one more sheet of paper. It says: DO NOT CONFUSE AN UNFINISHED IDEA WITH AN UNIMPORTANT ONE.

Then, underneath, as if somebody had added it later and with slightly less confidence: DO NOT CONFUSE AN INTERESTING IDEA WITH A GOOD ONE EITHER.

There. That is an instruction set I can live with.

The first item inside the envelope is a receipt for shade. It is dated on a summer afternoon several decades from now. The amount is small. The payer is a city. The recipient is a street. The person who benefited never learns that a transaction took place.

I want to know whether that would be a sign of progress or a sign that a very basic human need had finally been turned into a bill. There is enough material in the argument to walk down the street and find out.
