# The Quietest Signal

The most exciting line on the screen is the one the researcher refuses to celebrate.

An instrument has detected something unusual. Perhaps a narrow radio signal appears from a direction in the sky where an observing campaign was looking for evidence of technology. Perhaps a spectrum includes a feature that could have several explanations, one of them extraordinary. The raw display is a small achievement of measurement. The interpretation could change the history of the species.

The researcher asks whether anyone has checked the equipment.

This is the part of the search for extraterrestrial intelligence I find most moving. An observation that might mean something staggering has to pass through the humblest questions. Was there interference? Did the signal come from a satellite or a terrestrial transmitter? Did the instrument behave properly? Can another observation recover the pattern? Does a natural physical explanation fit? The researchers know the stakes. That is why they should be difficult to impress.

An extraordinary event in science is often surrounded by very ordinary work.

On April 29, 2019, the Breakthrough Listen project used the Parkes radio telescope in Australia to observe the direction of Proxima Centauri, the closest star to the Sun. The observing campaign recorded a narrow radio signal near 982 megahertz. The signal's frequency shifted slightly over time, a feature researchers pay attention to because apparent motion between a transmitter and receiver can change an observed frequency. It was detected while the telescope pointed toward the target and not in the immediate comparison observations pointing elsewhere. These characteristics were interesting enough to survive initial filtering.

The candidate acquired the dry designation BLC1. It could have acquired a much grander name. Proxima Centauri is a neighbor in astronomical terms, and it is associated with known planets. A persistent, narrowband signal apparently coming from that direction offers exactly the kind of story a popular audience wants: a nearby system, an unusual observation, and an apparent technological clue. But the signal's arrival in a telescope's records did not establish that a civilization had sent it. It created a testable problem for the research team.

Two papers in *Nature Astronomy* published in October 2021 provide the evidence and the rejection. [The first describes the original search and candidate](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-021-01479-w): the frequency near 982.002571 megahertz, the drift rate, the on-source and off-source observing pattern, and why the feature passed initial candidate filters. [The companion analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-021-01508-8) investigates whether the apparent signature could be produced by radio interference. The researchers identified dozens of related interference events and concluded that BLC1 was an electronically drifting intermodulation product of local, time-varying human-generated signals. A candidate that initially resembled a technosignature was traced to a much more ordinary origin.

This is an excellent story about a failed discovery because the failure was produced by successful method. The observing strategy had identified something unusual. The subsequent analysis found a better explanation. The researchers did not have to erase the original observation or pretend that it had never been interesting. They published the data and the steps by which the promising interpretation fell apart. The result improved the procedure for evaluating other candidates.

Intermodulation is an annoying physical reminder that instruments and electronics do not merely transmit the universe to human observers. When signals interact in nonlinear electronic components, they can generate products at frequencies not present as ordinary independent sources. Time-varying local interference can mimic features that an astronomer hopes will identify a remote transmitter. The instrument is part of the experiment and must be investigated alongside the apparent astronomical source.

This kind of false positive is more dangerous than an obviously local broadcast because it can arrive dressed in the properties the search is designed to find. A narrow signal with a changing frequency is precisely the sort of observation that invites curiosity about an engineered transmission. If human electronics can reproduce those properties, then the criteria need more layers: repeat observations, alternate pointing, inspection of neighboring frequencies, scrutiny of the equipment and comparison with other human-generated signals.

It would be easy to turn BLC1 into a moral tale about scientists who were almost fooled by an attractive story. That would be unfair. A well-designed search has to discover interesting candidates before it can reject them. If a filter discards everything that resembles known interference, it might also reject something genuinely unusual. If it accepts everything unusual as extraterrestrial, it abandons the distinction between search and discovery. The difficult work is constructing procedures that can allow a candidate to advance while preserving a clear path for it to fail.

The [Breakthrough Initiatives' public explanation](https://breakthroughinitiatives.org/news/33) of the 2021 papers stressed the importance of the analysis for treating technosignature searches as rigorous experimental science. There is a commercial and public-relations temptation in such a statement. It is more compelling when the research is available and the conclusion is disappointing. Publishing a false positive with a credible explanation may do more for public trust than announcing an unverified marvel every few years.

The observing system also teaches a lesson about absence. After BLC1 was rejected as interference, the question was not whether Proxima Centauri had been proven devoid of technology. The telescope had examined selected signals during a limited observing period with particular sensitivity and processing criteria. A non-detection of a credible extraterrestrial signal constrains a class of possibilities, not the entire future of the universe. A civilization might use different transmission methods, frequencies or times, or produce no emissions within the experiment's reach.

The episode made the idea of a public absence ledger much more concrete. For each observation the archive could record the target, frequency coverage, effective sensitivity, timing, instrumental conditions, data-processing methods, candidate events, reasons for rejecting them and the limitations remaining after the search. The rejection of BLC1 would be a valuable entry because it contains the anatomy of a misleading candidate. Future investigators could compare unusual new signals with the interference patterns that defeated the earlier interpretation.

A particularly honest museum exhibit could place the two papers side by side. On one wall, the original detection and reasons for suspicion; on the other, the tests that revealed the interference. Visitors would be invited to guess which pieces of evidence mattered. The exhibit would end not with a voice from another civilization but with a better understanding of why an apparently celestial signal can arise from ordinary electronics.



The search for technosignatures asks whether remote observations could reveal traces of technology beyond Earth. NASA's [overview of technosignatures](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/search-for-life/searching-for-signs-of-intelligent-life-technosignatures/) discusses possibilities such as radio or laser signals, unusual atmospheric chemicals, and evidence of large-scale engineering. These are proposed targets for investigation, not verified discoveries of extraterrestrial civilizations. The search remains a scientific endeavor with severe observational limits and substantial uncertainty.

An intelligent civilization may be difficult to detect for reasons that have little to do with its intelligence. Its technologies might not produce signals that our instruments can recognize. It might exist at enormous distance. A particular kind of emission might occur only briefly. Our searches cover selected frequencies, regions, times, and signal types. A telescope's field of view is not a referendum on the universe.

The other famous radio observation makes an instructive pair with BLC1. On August 15, 1977, the Ohio State Radio Observatory's Big Ear telescope detected a strong narrowband radio signal during a sky survey. The recorded sequence of characters, 6EQUJ5, indicated intensity levels in a system used to print the data. Astronomer Jerry Ehman later wrote Wow! beside the relevant part of the printout, giving the observation its enduring name. The [SETI Institute's history of the Wow! signal](https://www.seti.org/research/seti-101/the-wow-signal) explains that the pattern rose and fell in a way consistent with the telescope's beam sweeping past a source in the sky.

The observation remains interesting because it was striking, but it has never been recovered in the same way despite searches. Its origin is still uncertain. It has inspired proposals involving natural astrophysical processes, human interference and other explanations. A single unexplained observation is not a confirmed signal from technological life. It is evidence that a particular instrument recorded a particular unusual event at a particular time. The failure to repeat it is central to the scientific assessment.

The contrast is useful. BLC1 was investigated in enough surrounding data that researchers could find a persuasive terrestrial explanation. The Wow! event survives as an unresolved observation in part because the same signal was not observed again and independent discriminating evidence is limited. One case became less mysterious through detailed analysis. The other remains uncertain because a convincing interpretation has not won the necessary tests. Neither justifies a declaration that a civilization has been found.

It is tempting to think an unexplained signal becomes more likely to be extraordinary the longer it remains unexplained. That intuition can be badly wrong. A scarcity of information can preserve many explanations simultaneously without strengthening any one of them. If a detection cannot be repeated, the original evidence may remain fixed while the surrounding stories multiply. The absence of a resolution is a condition of the evidence, not positive proof of the most exciting hypothesis.

The public archive I imagine should include this distinction explicitly. An unresolved case would be labeled INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE TO DISTINGUISH EXPLANATIONS. It would not be presented as a failed experiment or as a concealed discovery. A conclusively traced interference event would be labeled with the mechanism and the supporting tests. A verified, repeatable but still unexplained observation would occupy a separate category. The categories make it harder for the same word mysterious to do the work of several different scientific judgments.

The institution should resist the pressure to convert each new observation into the next chapter of a cosmic detective serial. Scientific understanding can improve through long periods when nothing dramatic is announced. A better receiver, a more carefully characterized interference environment, or an improved method of rejecting instrumental artifacts may be worth more than a year of speculative headlines. Those improvements make future discoveries more credible, even if they also make the candidate list shorter.

I would place BLC1 and Wow! in the same display case but give them separate labels. Visitors could see the underlying observations, the follow-up attempts and which questions were settled. The empty space between them would show why the researcher's first obligation is not to supply an answer. It is to preserve the difference between what the instrument measured and what a human being hopes the measurement means.



The absence of a detected signal can be informative about what was searched and the sensitivity of the search. It is not automatically proof that no one is there.

This is a difficult idea for human beings who enjoy conclusive answers. The universe contains a vast number of potential places to investigate, and the observational space is even larger when possible signal types, durations, repetition patterns, and technological behaviors are considered. A null result can eliminate or constrain some possibilities under the assumptions of a search. It generally leaves many others untouched.

I imagine a future observatory creating a public ledger of absences. Each entry specifies where the instrument looked, when it looked, what it could detect, what it could not, and what was found. A person might open the archive and see millions of carefully described non-events. It would be a museum of the things science knows it has not observed.

This would not make a thrilling poster. It could be extraordinary scientific infrastructure.

Without records of non-detections, it is difficult to distinguish a genuinely neglected region of the search from a region studied repeatedly. An absence documented with a sensitivity threshold is different from an absence assumed because no headline announced a discovery. The ledger could help researchers plan observations and avoid claiming broad conclusions from narrow evidence.

The telescope's observing time also has an opportunity cost that belongs in a credible search proposal. An hour spent checking one intriguing direction is an hour unavailable for a different target or frequency range. A candidate with a very low prior probability can still be worth following up if the possible information gain is large and the cost is reasonable. A proposed search should therefore explain its selection rule and the circumstances under which further observation would no longer be justified. Curiosity has to coexist with scheduling.

An archive containing rejected candidates could help estimate how many false alarms a particular procedure produces and which kinds consume disproportionate follow-up time. A high false-alarm rate can make investigators less responsive to genuine anomalies; a filter made too restrictive might eliminate the rare event it was intended to detect. This is not a simple numerical optimization, because the true population of extraterrestrial technological signals is unknown. But recording the criteria before looking at results would reduce the freedom to change the standards after something exciting appears.

Even the expression we found nothing should be used with care. An observation may rule out a signal above a stated sensitivity in a defined band during a defined period. That is useful information. It does not establish that a distant system lacks technology. The careful statement may be longer and less dramatic, but it gives the next observer a record they can build upon instead of another story they must first untangle.



There is another challenge. A signal can be real and its origin uncertain. A radio telescope measures electromagnetic activity. The instrument does not label a signal extraterrestrial or technological. A narrowband pattern may be suspicious in one context and mundane in another. Human technology surrounds observing instruments with potential interference. Satellite constellations, ground transmitters, aircraft, electronic equipment, and the observing system itself can complicate analysis. An apparent source in the sky may need careful localization and repeated observation.

The more surprising the interpretation, the longer the list of alternative explanations should become.

This principle can sound humorless. It is actually the only way to protect the possibility of a genuine discovery. If every unusual signal is celebrated as an alien message, careful researchers will spend their lives correcting the headlines. If the field becomes known for premature announcements, a truly interesting observation may arrive to an audience already exhausted by false alarms. Credibility is a resource that must be preserved before it is needed.

The future telescope will require a public relations department that occasionally declines to issue a press release.

I am drawn to one especially strange possibility: perhaps the most important technosignature will be an absence in an otherwise ordinary astronomical pattern. A star system may exhibit an unusual distribution of energy, an atmospheric feature, or some structure that does not fit a simple model. Such a finding would require stringent natural explanations to be explored before invoking technology. Many physical phenomena are poorly understood. A civilization is a hypothesis with enormous explanatory freedom, which is precisely why it can become a trap.

An explanation that can account for almost anything without producing a discriminating prediction has limited scientific value.

A useful technosignature claim would identify observations that distinguish the proposed technological origin from plausible natural and instrumental alternatives. It would specify what additional data are needed. It would admit when the available evidence cannot decide. A spectacular story is not a substitute for a test.

There is an interesting contrast with the way popular culture imagines first contact. A spacecraft arrives, a door opens, or a message appears in comprehensible language. The scientific version may begin with a statistical anomaly buried in a large dataset. Years may pass between the first observation and a secure interpretation. Instruments in different locations may be needed. Researchers might disagree about models. A candidate may disappear on follow-up, leaving everyone with a useful lesson about interference.

The universe is under no obligation to arrange an announcement for human dramatic satisfaction.

The costs of false claims are not the only reason for caution. A genuine discovery could carry social, political, and philosophical implications that extend far beyond the observing team. Public confidence would depend partly on transparent evidence, independent verification where feasible, and communication that distinguishes observation from interpretation. It would be irresponsible to present a speculative candidate as confirmed because the resulting excitement might attract funding or attention.

At the same time, secrecy and excessive control could foster suspicion. A careful process should make data and analysis available when appropriate, while respecting legitimate technical or operational constraints. Independent investigators should be able to challenge the conclusion. Science works better when extraordinary claims can be checked by people who did not invent them.

The imagined observatory has one wall devoted to instruments that found nothing. Each entry records years of work, the range of sensitivities, and the questions that remained. The display is less dramatic than a recovered alien artifact. It tells a story about patience and a willingness to use expensive equipment without being guaranteed a reward.

There is something almost heroic in that restraint.

The search also changes how I think about humanity. People often ask whether finding another civilization would make us feel insignificant. That is plausible, but not inevitable. The discovery of another technological species could make the human achievement of building instruments, organizing research, and understanding physical laws seem more remarkable, not less. We would have learned to recognize a distant form of activity through signals crossing enormous distances. The same physics would connect very different lives.

Or perhaps the search will continue producing careful absences for generations. That would remain a scientific activity worth evaluating on its evidence, methods, and opportunity costs. Public money is finite. Projects must justify themselves. The value of a search cannot rest solely on the most spectacular possible outcome. It can also include instrument development, scientific knowledge, data methods, and the disciplined exploration of an important question.

The ledger should make those benefits and costs visible rather than relying on cosmic romance.

I imagine an investigator returning to the unusual line on the screen. An instrument test reveals a mundane source. The candidate is rejected. There is disappointment, perhaps relief, and the results become part of a database that helps the next search. The public never hears of it.

I would like to read that report. It would tell me how serious the researchers were.

Somewhere, there may be a signal whose origin no existing instrument can yet recognize. We may never observe it. We may observe it and misunderstand it. We may discover that the universe is far stranger than the question we originally thought to ask. Those are possibilities, not promises.

For now, the most valuable sentence in the observatory may be the quiet one: we need to check.
