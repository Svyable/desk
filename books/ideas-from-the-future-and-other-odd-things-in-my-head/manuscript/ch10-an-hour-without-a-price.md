# An Hour Without a Price

The note is stamped with a number one and a date. It says that the bearer has earned an hour.

The bearer might have helped a neighbor set up a new telephone, collected groceries for someone recovering from surgery, or taught another member of the community how to use a spreadsheet. In return, a local exchange has credited the person with time that can later be used to request help from someone else. An hour of assistance is exchanged for an hour of assistance, even when the activities have different market prices.

This sounds like a new social invention. Versions of it have been tried for decades. Timebanking is a real practice, and its history is much less tidy than the attractive idea suggests.

The principle has immediate charm. Money measures time and skill very unevenly. A highly compensated professional may earn in an hour what a care worker earns in several days. The market prices reflect many forces, including scarcity, bargaining power, institutions, training, productivity, and the ability of a buyer to pay. They are not moral grades assigned to the people doing the work. A time bank offers an alternative accounting convention for a defined community: an hour is an hour.

A person with useful skills but limited money could contribute and receive help. Someone who has retired might teach a skill or assist a neighbor. The exchange could encourage relationships across age or income. It might recognize forms of support that ordinary markets undervalue. The whole thing has the comforting appearance of a small rebellion against the invoice.

Then somebody has to arrange the exchanges.

The difficulty is not incidental. A study of six timebank cases in England, published in *Health & Social Care in the Community*, found that the practice was substantially harder than the theory. Person-to-person exchanges were limited in some programs. Managing risk, safeguarding, organizational culture, and the mechanism itself required resources. Some projects moved away from the exchange model originally envisioned. The study is worth reading precisely because the idea remains appealing after the evidence has made it inconvenient: [Timebanking and the co-production of preventive social care with adults](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/hsc.13166).

If one person offers computer advice and needs help with a difficult plumbing problem, the local exchange may not contain a qualified plumber willing to trade an hour for an hour. Even if it does, the plumber might be working during the available times. A person who needs a ride to an appointment cannot wait three weeks for an exchange to match. Some needs are urgent. Some require licenses, specialist knowledge, insurance, or equipment. Others are intimate and should not be assigned casually to strangers.

Time is equal on the ledger. Needs are not.

This is the point where the idea becomes honest. A time bank can facilitate some forms of mutual aid. It cannot replace the labor market, professional services, healthcare, or the systems that support people with intensive care needs. Treating it as an inexpensive substitute for properly funded services may be dangerous. A well-meaning volunteer can provide company or practical assistance within an appropriate scope. They should not be expected to perform work that requires clinical training, specialized qualifications, or legal responsibility merely because the community has run out of money.

An exchange that makes unpaid labor visible is useful. One that gives public authorities an excuse to stop paying for essential work is something else.

I imagine a future time bank with surprisingly strict rules. It refuses some transactions. It has clear boundaries for activities involving medical needs, financial information, transportation, children, and vulnerable participants. It explains insurance and safeguarding arrangements. It trains coordinators. It handles disputes and cancellations. It does not advertise its participants as a free workforce. It openly records the costs of managing the program.

The administration may cost more than enthusiasts expect. That does not necessarily make the program a failure. A public library has administrative costs too. The relevant question is whether the benefits justify the resources compared with other ways of helping people.

There is some evidence of beneficial effects in particular settings. Reviews have reported promising social and personal outcomes but also limitations in study quality and the need for stronger evaluation. A 2025 quasi-experimental study of older participants in Hong Kong found increased volunteering within the program it examined, without evidence of crowding out other volunteer activity in that setting. Its results are specific to the participants and design; they cannot establish that every time bank will produce the same effects. [The study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12578508/) is interesting because it measures something more concrete than a general promise to build community.

I would like the future program to be equally specific about what it hopes to improve. Is the goal to help people complete small tasks? Reduce isolation? Build relationships across generations? Provide opportunities for volunteering? Recognize unpaid work? Improve disaster resilience? A program that claims to accomplish all of these at once may struggle to evaluate any of them properly.

The contrast between England and Hong Kong gives the experiment a useful shape. In the English study, Ruth Naughton-Doe, Ailsa Cameron and John Carpenter examined six time banks, drawing on interviews with eighty-four members and thirteen staff, as well as observations and activity records. Their [published evaluation](https://doi.org/10.1111/hsc.13166) found that the number of person-to-person exchanges was often small and that some programs had moved away from the direct exchange model. It is a strangely revealing outcome. The central idea was built around reciprocal transactions, but the organizations that adopted it sometimes found the transactions difficult to make happen.

Consider what a coordinator has to know before approving a seemingly simple exchange. One participant can help carry groceries but has no car. Another can drive but is available only after six. A third wants assistance with medication, which falls outside what a volunteer should responsibly provide. The people may live on opposite sides of a town with poor bus service. They may have legitimate reasons not to enter one another's homes. A platform can match categories and schedules; somebody still has to resolve questions of trust, safety and suitability. The time credit is the smallest part of the job.

The English research is valuable because the researchers did not stop at the stated purpose of the schemes. They examined the systems in operation. They found tensions with professional cultures, safeguarding and bureaucracy. The work suggests that a community-service ledger can depend on an expensive human brokerage function that the idealized model makes almost invisible. It would be unfair to ask a volunteer-based organization to behave like a professionally staffed care service without providing the resources and authority that such a service requires. When an evaluation shows that the original form of reciprocity is rare, the correct response is to examine the real work being done before deciding whether the program is effective.

The Hong Kong study, published in *The Gerontologist* in 2025, used a different design. Researchers followed a timebanking intervention involving adults aged fifty and over from 2021 to 2022. The [analysis by Shiyu Lu, Cheryl Chui and Terry Lum](https://doi.org/10.1093/geront/gnaf200) compared 116 participants in the timebank group with 114 in a comparison group. Both groups had opportunities to volunteer through community centers. Participants in the timebank group could exchange time credits for rewards, while the comparison group did not receive that credit-based incentive. The study found more volunteering within the supported program among the timebank participants, without evidence in that sample that the credits reduced volunteering elsewhere.

It is a promising result with a complication tucked inside it. Rewards in the Hong Kong program included goods or vouchers, rather than a perfect one-hour-for-one-hour exchange between neighbors. The measured success was increased participation in volunteering through the program. It does not demonstrate that participants accumulated a dependable reserve of future care or that a community-wide reciprocal labor market can clear every request. The practice called timebanking had been adapted into a system that rewarded activity without requiring every participant's future needs to match another person's offer.

This may be a reasonable adaptation. It also alters what the institution is. A program offering incentives for volunteering can be useful even when it no longer resembles the exchange of comparable personal services that gave the original idea its moral force. It might be more transparent to describe the activity it actually organizes than to insist on the prestige of the term bank.

Imagine two people inspecting their time-credit statements. The first has earned credits through organized volunteering and exchanged them for a small reward. The second has earned credits helping several neighbors and expects to redeem them someday for specialized assistance. Both possess numbers called hours. Their claims are different. The second depends on another person with a relevant skill being available and willing to participate. No reserve requirement or financial insurance automatically guarantees that obligation. The first is closer to a program reward whose redemption rules are set by the sponsoring organization. A good scheme would make those differences legible before anyone begins accumulating credits.

This matters especially if time credits are presented as a form of retirement security. The image is attractive: people help while they are able, accumulate hours, and draw on the community later. But a future community may have a different population, different services, fewer volunteers, and more people needing assistance. Credits could accumulate much faster than the supply of redeemable help. A time bank might deliberately impose limits, periodically expire balances, or ask for contributions from institutions that can provide reliable services. Each choice would alter the promise. None should be hidden behind the language of an hour remaining an hour.

The public-policy version of the idea would need to distinguish the objectives. If a city wishes to encourage volunteering, it can evaluate whether time credits are more effective and cost-efficient than other forms of recruitment, recognition and support. If it wants to address unmet care needs, it must study the needs, qualifications, safeguarding and actual availability of services. If it wants to reduce loneliness, it has to measure social outcomes without assuming that recorded transactions automatically produce friendship. If it wants to acknowledge unpaid labor, it can make that labor visible while still asking whether households need wages, paid leave or professional support more urgently.

An institution that tries to accomplish every aim with one currency may end by pleasing the people who like the currency and neglecting the people who need the service.

I imagine a future program returning to its members with difficult survey results. People enjoy volunteering. They appreciate recognition. But most have not redeemed their credits for personal assistance, and a small group of coordinators carries a large administrative burden. The board could respond by doubling down on publicity about a coming age of reciprocal abundance. Or it could acknowledge that the community has built a promising volunteer organization rather than a bank. It could redesign the scheme around what its members actually use, retaining credits only where they improve participation.

That would not be a defeat of the future idea. It would be a more serious version of it. The purpose of a tool should survive changes to the tool. The ledger is expendable if the human relationships and practical assistance can be supported better without it.

The choice of accounting unit also contains a moral argument. By assigning equal credit to every hour, the exchange says that different participants' contributions have equal standing within its rules. This can be socially valuable. But the rule can also obscure differences in effort, qualifications, risk, and responsibility. A complicated repair that exposes the volunteer to liability is not equivalent in every respect to watering plants. A coordinator may need to decide which services are permitted and how exchanges are organized, even if the time credits remain equal.

Equality in one dimension does not eliminate inequality in the others.

There is a risk that time banks will attract people who already have spare hours, reliable transportation, and enough financial security to volunteer. People working several jobs or providing intensive unpaid care may have the least capacity to participate, even though they might benefit greatly from assistance. An institution that rewards available time could unintentionally widen gaps unless it attends to those barriers. Access to an account is not access to a spare hour.

The OECD's [Time Use Database](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/datasets/time-use-database.html) illustrates why the distribution of time deserves careful measurement. It distinguishes unpaid work, paid work or study, personal care, leisure, and other activities, though comparisons across countries require attention to differences in surveys. A society that sees only paid hours has an incomplete account of the labor required to maintain households and communities. But a society that turns every unpaid hour into a credit also risks reducing relationships to transactions.

I do not want to receive a bill because somebody listened to me complain about the weather.

Friendship, family care, and neighborly kindness occupy a space where money and reciprocity already interact, sometimes awkwardly. People help each other for affection, duty, solidarity, gratitude, or the ordinary pleasure of being useful. Turning assistance into a ledger may encourage people who feel uncomfortable asking for favors, because the exchange gives them a way to reciprocate. It may also make generosity feel contractual. Different people will respond differently, and the effect may depend on how a particular community designs the program.

I suspect the most successful exchange would understand when not to count.

Consider a person who earns ten credits over a year and then becomes ill. If the program has sufficient participants and can match the needed services, those credits may provide a meaningful form of support. But what happens if many people need help simultaneously? During a disaster, the demand for certain tasks can surge while volunteers themselves are affected. Credits are claims on an uncertain supply of human time, not guaranteed reserves like coins in a vault. If the system advertises them as savings that can always be redeemed, it may create a false sense of security.

The exchange should probably be described as a network of potential mutual assistance, not a bank offering insured deposits of labor.

A future version could be designed with these limits in view. Members might list the kinds of help they can safely offer, when they are available, and the boundaries they want respected. Coordinators could match requests with appropriate participants. Credits could be earned and used within clear rules. The program might combine formal exchanges with ordinary volunteer projects that do not require strict reciprocity. It could partner with professional services without implying that volunteers can replace them.

The coordinator would be important. So would the funding.

It is easy to imagine software making the matching effortless. A request appears; an algorithm identifies available members; a person receives help. Such a tool might reduce some administrative work. It cannot verify trust, competence, safety, or suitability by itself. It may create unfair advantages for people whose schedules are flexible or whose services are highly sought. It may mishandle sensitive personal information. An app can organize a community; it does not automatically create one.

A useful pilot could compare the program's actual exchanges, costs, access patterns, and outcomes with what members expected. It would examine who participates and who does not. It would track whether participants receive help when needed and whether volunteers feel supported. It would not mistake the number of credits issued for the amount of social value created. If a large ledger contains credits that nobody can redeem, the system has created paperwork rather than assistance.

That sounds like an obvious distinction. Entire economies sometimes struggle to make it.

What I like most about timebanking is that it reveals how much of everyday life rests on work that the market does not price directly. Care for children, support for older relatives, household maintenance, volunteering, organizing, listening, and ordinary mutual help make other economic activity possible. Some of this labor is paid; much is not. The distribution of unpaid work can be unequal and exhausting. Recognition matters. So do paid leave, access to services, fair wages in professional care, and the resources needed to reduce avoidable burdens.

A time bank might support a community and still leave the large structural problem intact. That is not a moral failure if the program is honest about its purpose. It becomes a failure when its small achievements are advertised as a replacement for investment in care.

The imaginary hour note has one advantage over money. It can start a conversation. When a person receives help from someone outside their ordinary circle, they may learn a name, a skill, a story, or a need that would otherwise remain invisible. The exchange may make it easier to ask for assistance without shame. It may produce relationships that continue without the ledger.

There is no guarantee that it will. That is why the evidence and the local design matter.

I imagine a final experiment in which the program allows members to give away unused credits to a communal pool. The pool supports people who have little capacity to earn hours but genuine needs within the program's safe scope. The exchange becomes partly reciprocal and partly charitable. Some members may object that the pool undermines the rule that everyone contributes. Others may recognize that equal capacity to contribute is a fantasy. The conflict would force the organization to decide what it believes about fairness.

Perhaps the value of the time bank lies in making that debate visible at a human scale.

A member holds a credit earned by helping someone learn to use a computer. Months later, another member accompanies the person to a local event. They talk. The credit is spent. The organization records an hour of service. The people involved might remember something else.

If their relationship continues after the account reaches zero, I would consider the experiment worth examining.
