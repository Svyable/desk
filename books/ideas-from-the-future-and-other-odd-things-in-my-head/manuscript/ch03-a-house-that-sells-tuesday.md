# A House That Sells Tuesday

The house has earned seventeen dollars.

In the imagined version of this story, an electric utility sends a statement to the occupant of an ordinary home. The household supplied a little power to the neighborhood during an expensive summer afternoon, when air conditioners were running, the grid was strained, and the electricity stored in a car parked outside was more useful than it would have been sitting quietly in the battery. The household bought that energy earlier, at a cheaper time. It returned some to the grid, kept enough to drive, and received payment for helping the system through a difficult hour.

Seventeen dollars is a number invented for this thought experiment. The actual economic outcome would depend on equipment, tariffs, energy prices, compensation rules, the vehicle, the battery, and the size of the transaction. I chose the number because it is pleasingly unheroic. The future should sometimes pay for groceries rather than announce the transformation of civilization.

For a very long time, a house's relationship with a utility was easy to describe. A generating plant made electricity. Wires carried it. A meter measured consumption. The household paid the bill. This picture was never complete, even before distributed solar, home batteries, and sophisticated demand response programs complicated it, but it was complete enough to teach a child. Electricity went one way. Money went the other.

A home with a substantial battery is capable of a different arrangement. It can consume power when doing so is useful or cheap, store some, and supply some later. That battery might be bolted to a wall. It might be a vehicle that spends the majority of its time parked. It might be connected to rooftop solar or to an ordinary grid supply. The house can become a participant in the electrical system rather than only an endpoint.

The arithmetic is seductive. Batteries in electric vehicles can be far larger than the stationary systems many households install for backup. If millions of vehicles exist, the aggregate storage capacity is enormous. Why build an entirely separate fleet of batteries if a large fleet already rolls down the roads?

The answer begins with a sentence that is less pleasing: because the vehicle owners have other plans.

The car is not a battery installed for public service. It is transportation. The owner expects it to work when the keys are in hand. A family might need to leave unexpectedly. A tradesperson may have an early appointment. A worker on a night shift may charge at hours that coincide with the system's demands. A household may own the vehicle but lack off-street parking or control over the building's electrical equipment. Millions of theoretical batteries do not automatically become millions of available resources.

That limitation is not a failure of imagination. It is the first term in a realistic design.

The International Energy Agency's 2026 discussion of [vehicle-to-grid technology](https://www.iea.org/reports/vehicle-to-grid-technology) describes the promise and the barriers. Smart charging can shift load without sending electricity back to the grid. Bidirectional systems go further, allowing power to flow from the vehicle to a house, building, or wider grid where the hardware, software, regulatory arrangements, and grid connection permit it. The benefits can include lower peaks and grid support, but deployment depends on compatible vehicles and chargers, communications standards, testing, regulation, and compensation.

The distinctions get lost when the idea is summarized as your car can power your house. It may be able to do that under particular conditions. Supplying a house during an outage, supplying a building during expensive hours, and exporting electricity to a local distribution network are related but different activities. A device approved for one may not be allowed to do another. The electricity system has reasons to be cautious about what connects to its wires.

An electrical grid is a fragile achievement dressed up as a wall socket. At a large scale, the amount of power generated and consumed must remain closely balanced. Voltage and frequency need to stay within acceptable ranges. Utilities plan for demand that changes by hour, season, weather, and economic behavior. They maintain reserves and networks designed around probable peaks. Equipment has capacity limits. A transformer serving a street does not care how enthusiastic its residents are about becoming virtual power plants. It can carry only what it was designed to carry.

People sometimes talk about distributed batteries as if the batteries themselves dissolve those constraints. They can help, but their location matters. Electricity must travel through particular parts of a network. A battery behind one transformer cannot magically relieve every overloaded cable elsewhere. The timing and shape of discharge matter. So does the possibility that thousands of devices respond simultaneously to the same price signal, creating a new problem while solving an old one.

There is an engineering literature behind this, and it is less cinematic than the advertisement with a car illuminating a dark house. It involves protection systems, interoperability, communications failures, capacity forecasting, and tests in which something is deliberately disconnected to make sure the right part of the installation stops supplying power. The person responsible for those tests deserves more attention than the person who chooses the app icon.

In the hypothetical house, the owner has a dashboard. It shows a green number, the day's earnings, and a cheerful illustration of the car sending lightning into a neighborhood. I am generally suspicious of such dashboards. They create the feeling that a relationship has become simpler because the difficult variables have been hidden. A positive number does not tell the owner how much the installation cost, what maintenance will be required, or whether using the battery for grid services has accelerated degradation in a measurable way.

A proper household calculation would need to consider the cost of energy purchased, the value of exports, capital expenditure for bidirectional equipment, installation and permitting, any tariff charges, battery wear, warranty terms, taxes where applicable, and the value of retaining enough charge for driving or emergency use. It would also need to account for the hassle. An hour spent resolving a utility billing dispute has a price even when no accountant records it.

Battery degradation is particularly interesting because it is neither the simple disaster opponents sometimes imply nor an imaginary cost enthusiasts can ignore. Battery aging depends on chemistry, temperature, state of charge, charge and discharge patterns, calendar time, and other conditions. Depending on use and control, managed charging may have benefits or costs. A credible program should specify what is being measured rather than advertising a universal effect.

The household will also want to know who is allowed to make decisions. If the utility can discharge a privately owned battery, under what conditions? How much charge is reserved? Can the owner override the instruction? Can a commercial aggregator combine hundreds or thousands of vehicles into a resource and bid their collective behavior into a market? Who is responsible if a communications error causes a vehicle to arrive at its owner's first shift with too little energy to complete the drive?

This is an ancient question wearing electrical clothes: when you share ownership of a resource with a system, who has the final right to say no?

The owner might sign a contract that reserves a minimum state of charge. That makes good sense, but it also reduces the amount of power available. A generous override system protects the household and reduces an aggregator's certainty. If many owners override during the hottest day of the year, the promised grid resource may not appear when it is needed most. A system designed to work only when participants behave as forecast will eventually encounter participants who have not read the forecast.

There is a temptation to blame the owners. I would blame the design.

An elegant program should treat human behavior as one of its physical constraints. It should offer payment appropriate to the commitment being made and allow the vehicle to remain a vehicle. Contracts may distinguish between occasional emergency participation and more reliable scheduled service. A household could choose an aggressive earning mode, a conservative savings mode, or an emergency-only arrangement. The choices should be comprehensible without a degree in power systems engineering. And a person who does not participate should not be made to feel that owning a car has become a moral failure.

The money question leads somewhere unexpected. A wealthy household may have the capital and property control to install the necessary equipment. It may earn incentives or avoid high electricity prices. A renter with a modest income may have no place to connect a vehicle even if one is owned. A household without a car has no vehicle battery to sell. If grid-service revenues ultimately reduce system costs, everyone may benefit through rates, but the pattern depends on regulatory design. If incentives are badly structured, people who can afford hardware may collect payments financed by the broader customer base.

That is a problem worth taking seriously before mailing out the first cheerful earnings statement.

Utility rates are political artifacts as well as prices. Regulators attempt to allocate the costs of generation, transmission, distribution, maintenance, reliability, and public obligations across different customers. The system contains fixed costs that do not vanish when one household consumes less electricity from the grid. If a customer offsets a large share of a bill while still relying on the network for backup and export, other customers may end up paying more unless the rate structure accounts fairly for those services. The exact answer varies by jurisdiction and tariff; there is no honest universal slogan.

The resulting debate can sound like a quarrel between innovators and utilities, but both parties have interests worth understanding. Utilities must maintain networks and safety. They may also have financial incentives that discourage certain kinds of distributed generation or competition. Vehicle owners want savings and control. Aggregators want enough devices and predictable behavior to make a business. Regulators want reliability and prices that are defensible to people with and without batteries. The future here is a negotiated settlement, not a victorious gadget.

I would like to see the imagined earnings statement include a second column. The first would show the owner's compensation. The second would estimate, with stated assumptions, whether the contribution reduced the system's cost of serving that hour. The numbers might differ. A subsidy can pay an individual more than the value the system receives. An efficient program can deliver value that is not fully paid to its participants. A useful public debate would ask why the discrepancy exists instead of hiding it behind the attractive language of community energy.

If the program is designed well, another possibility appears. Instead of only rewarding households with new private batteries, the utility or a community organization might support shared storage, bidirectional equipment in fleet depots, school buses, apartment complexes, and public buildings. A school bus has a known schedule during much of the year, although special events, summer programs, weather emergencies, and operational requirements complicate that predictability. A municipal fleet may be easier to coordinate than thousands of private cars, but public budgets and procurement make it no more magical.

A cooperative could operate storage that benefits a neighborhood directly. A utility could pursue lower-cost grid improvements instead of installing batteries where they are unnecessary. A community program might pay participants for flexibility rather than for the equipment they own. The objective should be to obtain an actual grid service at a reasonable total cost, not to manufacture a new class of household entrepreneur.

The phrase household entrepreneur is revealing. The future sometimes arrives with the insistence that every person become a manager of an ever-growing collection of assets. The home must sell electricity, rent out its garage, arbitrage appliances, license personal data, monetize idle processing power, and cultivate an audience while the residents are trying to eat dinner. A clever technology can impose a peculiar kind of cognitive rent. It transfers decisions from institutions to households and celebrates the transfer as empowerment.

Some people will enjoy the control. Others may reasonably prefer an electricity bill that is predictable and an appliance that does not ask to be part of a market.

The private car's battery is not the only asset that can help a grid. Smart thermostats, water heaters, commercial refrigeration, industrial operations, and other flexible loads can sometimes adjust demand. The most cost-effective measure may be to reduce or shift consumption rather than export stored energy. It is entirely possible that a particular neighborhood would be better served by improving insulation, weatherizing homes, repairing equipment, or investing in distribution infrastructure. The technically sophisticated choice is not automatically the best value.

That fact makes the subject more interesting. The future is not a single winning machine. It is a competition among methods of providing reliability, comfort, mobility, and reasonable cost.

The hypothetical household has one further use for the battery: surviving an outage. It is a different argument from buying and selling electricity. During a power failure, the capacity to keep essential loads running can be valuable even when it produces no revenue. But the design must be safe. An installation must prevent electricity from being exported into lines where utility workers may expect them to be de-energized. Protective equipment and approved transfer arrangements matter enormously. An improvised connection is not ingenuity; it is a danger.

The household must also decide which loads matter. Refrigeration, medical equipment, communications, lighting, heating or cooling within the limitations of the system, and perhaps the ability to charge a phone are not equivalent to running every appliance at once. Battery capacity measures stored energy; power ratings limit what can be supplied at a given time. The two concepts are often confused, especially in advertising. A large amount of stored energy cannot rescue a design that lacks enough instantaneous power for the equipment someone expects to run.

Every time I read an advertisement promising that a house can operate from a vehicle, I want to see the list of what remained unplugged during the demonstration. The list would be useful. It might help someone make a good purchase. It would also be an effective antidote to the fantasy that energy resilience is simply a capacity number.

There is a deeper social question. Reliability is a property of networks as much as of individual homes. One household can build expensive resilience around itself; the family next door may still struggle. If critical infrastructure becomes a contest in which wealthier residents protect their own property while collective systems deteriorate, the future has become a more luxurious form of abandonment. Distributed systems can support public resilience, but only if their benefits and obligations are designed beyond the private driveway.

A neighborhood could, for example, prioritize critical public loads during an emergency rather than allowing every battery to behave entirely independently. Such arrangements require careful technical design, legal authorization, and consent. No one should simply commandeer a private vehicle because the need is genuine. Yet it is reasonable to ask whether a community with many batteries could become more resilient together than a collection of perfectly isolated owners. The answer may be yes in some configurations and no in others. The practical pilot would be more valuable than the grand claim.

I imagine the first version of the future house failing in an ordinary way. The earnings statement arrives. The owner is pleased. Then the electricity bill reveals a fee that was not explained clearly, or the battery management app loses its connection, or an override fails to register before a journey. No disaster occurs. The person is irritated, calls customer service, and turns the program off.

This is the kind of event that disappears from the heroic accounts of innovation. It is also the event on which the whole business model may depend. A system serving millions of households cannot require heroic patience from each of them. It needs sensible defaults, reliable communication, transparent bills, good support, and a graceful way to refuse participation. Small frictions can defeat a technically sound proposal more efficiently than an ideological campaign.

Perhaps the most consequential invention would be a simple contract. It would state what equipment is required, what electricity may be used, how the household is compensated, the expected range of financial outcomes, what happens if the vehicle or grid is unavailable, and who bears each category of risk. It would not hide the unattractive terms behind a diagram of a smiling family and a blue lightning bolt.

The contract might be harder to design than the battery.

There is a future in which the street's transformers, vehicles, buildings, and appliances cooperate through networks of carefully tested equipment. The system uses energy more efficiently, reduces some investments in capacity, and makes certain outages less disruptive. It is a serious possibility. The IEA describes reasons to explore it. But the value will vary with the local power mix, network constraints, driving patterns, hardware, rate structures, and the weather. The proposal deserves demonstration rather than worship.

There is also a future in which an enthusiastic fleet of incompatible vehicles, proprietary apps, inadequate incentives, and confusing tariffs produces little grid value while making electricity bills harder to understand. Some customers prosper. Others pay for complexity they never requested. Both futures begin from the same ingenious observation: a parked electric car contains a useful battery.

This is why the seventeen dollars matters. The amount asks the proposal to account for itself. It interrupts the grand language with a household question: was the transaction worth doing after everything was counted?

At the bottom of the imaginary statement there is a note: the owner may opt out at any time. I want that sentence to remain visible. It acknowledges that the car belongs to a person with a life, and the life has priority over the market in which the car might profitably participate.

Perhaps a later generation will find the distinction quaint. Homes may be built with storage integrated from the beginning, and the decisions may be nearly invisible. Electricity may flow back and forth with none of the drama once attached to a utility bill. But the people will still need to leave the house in a hurry now and then. Someone will still forget to charge the car. Somewhere a customer service representative will still have to explain why Tuesday's earnings were smaller than expected.

I would take that as a sign the future had arrived.
