# Ideas From The Future & Other Odd Things In My Head

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

## A Note from the Wrong End of Time

There are people who have an idea and immediately make a company out of it. I have always been more interested in what happens during the dangerous interval before that. The idea is still funny. Nobody knows where it belongs. It might be a useful invention, a misunderstanding of something already invented, or a terrible solution to a problem that has not had the decency to arrive yet.

It is a nice interval. Unprofitable, frequently embarrassing, and free of pitch decks.

The title of this book is a small confession with a much larger escape clause. I do not receive messages from the future. If I did, I would ask for the exact date on which somebody finally makes the plastic packaging around a pair of scissors easy to open. By ideas from the future I mean proposals that look less ridiculous after you find out what the present has already been doing. Some are attached to real technologies. Others depend on governments changing how they count, houses changing what they sell, human beings changing their minds about what may be owned, or scientists learning to hear what a telescope already sees.

There will be trees here, but no claim that the trees have a secret Wi-Fi password. There will be dream machines, but not a promise that anybody can read yours. A river gets a lawyer, a household tries to sell part of its electricity, the sky acquires a sanitation problem, and an entire office is devoted to events that almost happened. These things do not all belong to the same industry. That is part of the pleasure.

I wanted a place to keep the thoughts that would not behave themselves when arranged into ordinary categories. Some are probably wrong. In fact, a book about ideas that has no wrong ones is a suspicious document. If everything inside it is sensible, the author has already allowed the present to edit the future.

I also wanted to resist a particular kind of future writing. It begins with a miraculous invention, jumps over manufacturing and politics, and ends with a civilization transformed. The difficult middle is missing. It is a little like selling a bridge without discussing the river. Most inventions are defeated or changed in that middle. A battery has to meet a utility rate. A medical breakthrough has to survive an immune system and a clinical trial. A beautiful vision for a city has to meet the person whose rent goes up. A government created for future generations must first persuade the present generation to keep funding it.

My interest is in the middle. The people with objections often have a point. They are not always enemies of progress. Sometimes they are the only people who have read the instructions.

The chapters do not claim to disclose what is coming. Their occasional future scenes are explicitly imagined, made to test an idea from another angle. Reported history, current research, and speculation should remain distinguishable even when the prose invites them to sit together at the same table. Links in the research notes are starting points for checking, not decorations that confer certainty.

I hope a reader leaves with several thoughts that cannot be put away neatly, along with a useful intolerance for glamorous nonsense. The future will be made partly by people with tools, budgets, permits, bad timing, and ordinary human limitations. It may also be made by the person who has an idea so peculiar that nobody yet knows which department should receive it.

There are worse reasons to keep a notebook.

Sven Hardy Benson
