# People Who Are Not Here Yet

A seat at the council table is reserved for a person who has not been born.

The chair remains empty. The voting rules are complicated. A clerk records the proceedings so that someone alive decades from now could understand which promises were made on their behalf. The current members find the arrangement irritating because the empty chair appears to oppose almost every proposal that would shift a cost into the future.

The institution is imaginary. The underlying problem is not.

Governments make decisions with consequences extending beyond their officeholders and voters. Bridges and power stations may last for generations. Debt, environmental change, land use, education, public health, and the durability of institutions can shape lives that do not yet exist. Future people cannot vote today, write letters to present legislators, or take their interests to the local town hall. The political system contains an asymmetry so obvious that it often disappears into the ordinary machinery of elections.

People who are alive can organize. People who are not alive have to trust us to imagine them fairly.

In September 2024, United Nations member states adopted the Pact for the Future, including a [Declaration on Future Generations](https://www.un.org/pact-for-the-future/en/declaration-future-generations). The declaration recognizes that decisions made now affect those who will inherit the planet. It emphasizes intergenerational responsibility and the importance of representing long-term interests. A declaration is not a functioning electoral constituency, nor does it specify a single institution that solves the problem. It establishes a commitment that can be evaluated against future action.

The commitment is more difficult than it sounds. A current generation may sacrifice something valuable to provide a benefit in the future. But what counts as a benefit to people whose preferences and circumstances are unknown? A government might preserve a particular industrial capacity that later becomes obsolete. It might build an infrastructure project that future residents would rather replace. It might protect a landscape while limiting housing in a region where later generations need places to live. A policy can be sincerely motivated by concern for the future and still make the future worse.

The unborn cannot correct our guesses.

This creates a particular temptation for people who enjoy declaring themselves guardians of history. If I claim to speak for future generations, who can challenge me? Living citizens can disagree with my priorities. I might dismiss their objections as shortsighted, selfish, or morally inferior. The farther into the future I place the supposed beneficiary, the harder it becomes for anybody to test the claim. The office can become a shelter for present preferences wearing the costume of posterity.

Wales has created a less theatrical version of the empty chair. Its [Well-being of Future Generations Act of 2015](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/anaw/2015/2/enacted) established duties for specified public bodies, set out seven national well-being goals, and created a Future Generations Commissioner. This is a working legal arrangement rather than a symbolic experiment. Public bodies are asked to consider long-term consequences and to work in ways that include prevention, collaboration, integration and the involvement of affected people.

The commissioner's general duty under section 18 is especially careful. The office is to promote the sustainable-development principle, act as a guardian of the ability of future generations to meet their needs, encourage public bodies to take greater account of long-term effects, and monitor and assess progress against well-being objectives. The statute does not authorize the commissioner to take over the legislature or veto any decision they dislike. It gives the office a role inside a constitutional system where elected officials retain their own responsibilities.

That distinction resolves part of the imaginary chair's problem. An official can serve future interests without claiming to possess an unborn citizen's exact preferences. The office can ask decision-makers to explain how a plan treats long-term effects, can recommend better practice and can publish assessments that the public may use to judge results. The future is represented through a duty to examine consequences rather than a fictional ballot cast by a single person.

The arrangement is also more ambitious than a narrow environmental office. Wales's [government explanation](https://www.gov.wales/well-being-future-generations-act-essentials-html) identifies seven goals that include prosperity, resilience, health, equality, cohesive communities, cultural vitality and global responsibility. A project that protects a landscape while damaging housing access or employment cannot simply point to the environmental goal and claim total compliance. The law asks public bodies to consider how the objectives interact. A long-term school investment can matter for economic and cultural well-being; a transport decision can affect health, equality and access; an energy decision can alter jobs and environmental conditions.

The complexity is a virtue only if the institutions can make it manageable. Seven goals can become seven headings in a report, each filled with reassuring sentences and no change to the decision. A government can learn to speak the language of integration without altering which projects receive money. The law needs a route from broad principles to actual choices, and that route can be difficult to audit.

Wales has attempted to create a record against which such claims can be judged. Its [Wellbeing of Wales 2026 report](https://www.gov.wales/wellbeing-wales-2026), published on October 1, 2026, assesses national progress using fifty national indicators and other evidence. The indicators cover a range of experiences from economic conditions and health to environmental quality and social outcomes. They create a partial answer to a difficult question: if the commissioner's duty is to speak for the future, what measurable present evidence tells citizens whether institutions are honoring that duty?

The answer is necessarily incomplete. A country may improve one indicator while worsening another. A current improvement in health may reflect policies enacted many years earlier, while a new policy's effects appear after the present government has left office. National averages can conceal people and places with very different circumstances. Some forms of long-term harm are difficult to observe before the harm becomes widespread. Indicators help discipline the discussion; they do not convert an ethical judgment into an automatic calculation.

A more immediate example appears in the Welsh Government's [2026–2030 well-being statement](https://www.gov.wales/programme-for-government-2026-to-2030-well-being-statement-html). The statement sets out government objectives concerning health care, living costs, the economy, schools, child poverty and governance. It explicitly describes how the government considered the long-term, integrated and preventive duties of the Act. This is a record that officials can be asked to defend. It is not proof, by itself, that the policies will produce the desired results.

The distinction matters because future-oriented institutions can become very good at writing future-oriented documents. Their credibility will depend on whether the documents change resource allocation, reveal otherwise hidden trade-offs, and survive an adverse result. If a policy fails, does the institution have the authority and willingness to say so? When a proposed project benefits one goal and damages another, does its analysis identify the conflict or simply describe the proposal as contributing to everything?

One way to make a future-generation assessment less ceremonial is to ask officials to identify decisions that would change if the long-term analysis were taken seriously. For a hospital service, perhaps the analysis shows that preventive care deserves a larger share of funding. For a public building, perhaps it favors a design that is more expensive initially but cheaper to maintain over its useful life. For a school, perhaps it reveals that deferred repairs are shifting costs and risks onto children who cannot vote. The office need not win every argument. It should be able to point to specific arguments it made and the reasons they were accepted or rejected.



The empty chair must therefore have strict limits.

I imagine an institution that reviews major long-term decisions but does not possess an unlimited veto. Its task would be to examine evidence about consequences that ordinary budget and election cycles neglect. It might ask a transport agency for the projected maintenance costs of a new bridge; ask a utility how an asset behaves under different demand scenarios; ask a government to show how a debt commitment is expected to be serviced; or ask whether an environmental risk can be reversed if the forecast is wrong.

The questions are not necessarily ideological. They are questions of time.

A representative would need to distinguish the interests of future people from the interests of the children living today. The latter are real citizens and human beings with present needs. Policies that invest in their education, health, safety, and opportunities can also have long-term effects, but they need not be justified exclusively through speculative descendants. Future generations are not a reason to neglect people already facing hardship.

A government that refuses to repair a dangerous school today while saving for an imagined ideal city a century from now has misunderstood the assignment.

This is where the issue becomes an argument about investment and distribution. A country may inherit assets and liabilities from its predecessors: infrastructure, knowledge, institutions, public obligations, depleted resources, pollution, and debt. Every generation receives something it did not create and leaves something it cannot fully control. A simplistic ledger of dollars would fail to capture the inheritance. A well-maintained water system may be worth more than a monetary reserve in a region struggling with drought. A robust legal institution may protect rights long after the officials who built it disappear.

Yet long-term language can excuse very poor decisions. A government could borrow to build an unproductive monument and call it an investment in descendants. A company could describe its environmentally harmful operation as necessary for future prosperity. A politician could avoid practical near-term questions by invoking civilization over centuries. There must be a way to examine the mechanisms connecting today's action to tomorrow's benefit.

The imagined representative should be required to say where the uncertainty begins.

One useful practice would be to produce an intergenerational impact statement for decisions with unusually long-lived effects. It would identify different plausible futures, major dependencies, risks of irreversible harm, and options that preserve flexibility. It should not assign a single exact value to every consequence several centuries ahead. Such apparent precision would be an invitation to hide assumptions inside a spreadsheet.

Forecasting is useful when it reveals what a decision depends upon. It becomes dangerous when the forecast is presented as an appointment with destiny.

Consider a coastal city deciding whether to build a new protective barrier. The project may require substantial investment today. Its benefits depend on future sea levels, storm patterns, land use, engineering performance, maintenance, and whether people continue to live in the protected area. The alternative might involve different kinds of defenses, restoration, changes in zoning, or managed relocation in particular places. None of these options is painless. A future-generation review might improve the decision by forcing officials to examine the consequences across longer periods and acknowledge which costs would be passed on.

It could also make decisions harder, as more plausible futures enter the room.

That is not necessarily an argument against it. The world contains uncertainty whether or not the meeting agenda accommodates it. A public institution earns trust by making important disagreements visible rather than keeping the alternatives in a drawer. The representative's role should be to improve the quality of the choice, not promise that the choice will be correct.

There is a deeper philosophical difficulty. Future generations are not a single person with a stable set of interests. A century from now, different populations may value competing things. Technological and economic change may alter what is scarce. Cultural priorities may differ from ours. In some choices, the identities of people who exist in the future depend on the policy chosen today. This makes comparisons even more difficult: we cannot always point to a fixed person who would be better off under one scenario than another.

The philosophy has a name for some of these problems, but the point can be understood without it. We are deciding for people whose lives may be partly determined by our decisions and whose preferences we cannot know.

That should make representatives humble. It should not make them inactive.

There are interests likely to be important across many futures: the ability to breathe reasonably clean air, access safe water, inherit functioning institutions, avoid preventable catastrophic risks, and retain choices rather than face irreversible damage. Even here, specific trade-offs are disputed. But a government can examine whether it is narrowing future options for benefits that are short-lived and concentrated. It can make those judgments transparent.

I would ask the imaginary office to keep an archive of predictions. A future visitor could open the files and see what today's officials believed, which risks they considered, and where their forecasts failed. The institution should not erase embarrassing errors. It should learn from them. Long-term planning needs an honest memory of how difficult prediction is.

There is also a representational question that may matter more than the empty seat. Young people alive now will experience some of the long-term consequences of current policies. Their participation in public decision-making can improve the conversation, though no one young person automatically speaks for all future generations. People living with environmental or economic harm today can also reveal risks that abstract long-term models miss. A future-oriented institution should not replace those voices with an expert committee claiming superior moral vision.

The emptiness of the chair must not become an excuse to silence the occupied chairs.

I imagine a council proposing a new industrial facility. The future representative asks about the projected life of the asset, disposal plans, environmental effects, and obligations that remain if the company fails. A worker asks about employment. A nearby resident asks about pollution. A business owner asks about energy costs. A scientist describes uncertainty. An elected official weighs them under the law. No single speaker owns the future.

The representative may advise approval with conditions. They may advise refusal. They may ask for a reversible pilot. A useful institution would be able to explain why, and later evaluation would examine its effects.

The most important practice might be to make some decisions reversible when reversal is possible. A pilot project can reveal consequences before a large commitment. A contract can include review points. A building can be designed for adaptation. A government can preserve the data needed to understand an old decision. Flexibility has value when the world is uncertain, though delay also has costs. A permanent refusal to commit can be as damaging as a reckless commitment.

Long-term judgment is the art of deciding what may change and what should be protected from change.

The imagined chair remains empty at the close of the session. That is appropriate. We should not mistake the institution for the people it represents. They will arrive with their own arguments, faults, ambitions, and ideas. They may be grateful for a preserved river or furious about a transport system that cannot be maintained. They may inherit tools that make our most difficult problems appear small and discover others that we cannot imagine.

All the representative can do is leave them a record and as many genuine options as the present can reasonably afford.

The chair is empty. The responsibility is not.
