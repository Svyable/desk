# The Spare Organ Problem

The future hospital has a peculiar room. It is neither an operating theater nor a pharmacy. Along one wall are carefully monitored containers, each carrying living tissue that may someday belong inside someone else's body. A label identifies a patient, a batch, a date, a protocol, and a sequence of tests. A technician checks the conditions, records a deviation, and rejects one container that no longer meets the requirements.

This room is imaginary. Its procedures are meant to make a fantasy more difficult.

The fantasy is simple and powerful. A patient needs a new organ. Instead of waiting for the complex circumstances that make a donated organ available, medicine grows the replacement from cells, perhaps genetically matched to the recipient, and implants it when ready. The original body's failure becomes a manufacturing problem. The spare part arrives in time. The patient goes home.

There are few medical dreams more attractive. They are also among the easiest to oversell.

The human body contains organs with specialized structures, multiple cell types, blood vessels, nerves, immune interactions, and functions that must operate under demanding conditions. A liver is not a bag of liver cells. A kidney is not a smooth object whose silhouette can be reproduced and then trusted to filter blood. The organization of tissue matters. So does its connection to everything around it. The body is an integrated system with an extraordinary ability to punish poorly understood substitutions.

Researchers have made remarkable progress in growing organoids: three-dimensional structures derived from stem cells that reproduce aspects of the form and function of tissues. Organoids have enabled new approaches to studying disease, development, and possible therapies. They can give researchers human-derived models that differ from conventional cell cultures, and in certain settings they may help explore individual responses to treatment. But a miniature tissue model is not necessarily a replacement organ, and success in a laboratory dish is not success in a human body.

A 2025 review of [organoids in disease modeling and regenerative medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011692/) describes both the promise and the practical obstacles, including variability between batches, standardization, vascularization, long-term viability, and missing biological components in some models. Those are not small footnotes. A transplanted tissue must receive oxygen and nutrients, integrate with the surrounding environment where required, avoid dangerous growth, and perform a useful function long enough to justify the risks. The laboratory achievement and the clinical treatment occupy different stages of the journey.

That distinction matters whenever a headline announces that scientists have grown an organ. What exactly was grown? How closely does it resemble the mature tissue? What function has been demonstrated? In what model? For how long? Was it transplanted, and if so under what circumstances? Was it a research demonstration, a clinical trial, or an established treatment? A responsible reader can be excited by a result and still insist on the answers.

In the imaginary hospital the label on each container would be astonishingly important. It would not simply describe the tissue. It would be part of the chain of custody and quality assurance for a living medical product. If the wrong cells, contamination, or a manufacturing error entered the process, the consequences could be severe. The more personalized the therapy, the more complex the tracking might become. A large factory making one standardized product faces different challenges from a facility producing many patient-specific batches with small volumes.

This is the place where regenerative medicine becomes unexpectedly industrial.

Modern manufacturing has learned how to make identical objects in vast numbers. Living tissue resists that kind of identity. Cells vary. Differentiation protocols can produce mixtures of cell states. The behavior of a structure may change as it matures. A product that looks correct under one measurement may fail another. The manufacturing process may itself change the biological outcome. The quality-control system needs to determine which differences are harmless and which make a product unsuitable.

Biologists and manufacturing engineers will have to become good neighbors.

A person hearing about organs grown from a patient's own cells may assume that immune rejection is solved. Matching cells can offer potential advantages, but the immune system is not so easily dismissed. Reprogramming, genetic changes, culture conditions, transplantation procedures, and the underlying disease can affect how tissue behaves. Depending on the approach, immunological risks may remain. Some therapies use donor-derived material; others investigate patient-specific methods. Every proposed treatment needs evidence for its actual risk profile.

The idea of avoiding rejection is worth pursuing. It is not a substitute for demonstrating safety.

There are also anatomical questions that make my imaginary hospital room feel less orderly. If a replacement tissue must be connected to blood vessels, how are those connections made? How quickly can blood supply reach the necessary cells? How does a large structure develop adequate circulation during growth? Can it be monitored for abnormal proliferation? If the tissue is implanted into a patient with a chronic disease, will the same disease process affect the replacement? Some organs perform several functions at once; a successful replacement may need to reproduce only one to help a patient, while a complete substitute would face a far higher bar.

These differences should change the engineering strategy. A partial tissue graft that restores a limited function may arrive before the science can build a whole organ. A support device may do some of the work of an organ without reproducing its biology. A drug may prevent the damage that would otherwise require transplantation. In a particular case, the most useful breakthrough may be a way to preserve the patient's existing tissue rather than replace it.

A real clinical experiment gives this distinction more force than any imaginary tissue bank. In June 2025, researchers published results from a phase 1–2 study of a therapy called zimislecel, developed from donor-derived stem cells into pancreatic islet cells. Islets perform an essential part of the pancreas's job: they produce hormones, including insulin, involved in regulating blood glucose. This was not a laboratory-grown replacement pancreas. It was an attempt to restore a specific missing function in people with type 1 diabetes, delivered through an infusion into the portal vein. The organ remained in the body. The experiment sought to supply some of the cells doing the work.

The [study, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2506549), reported interim results from fourteen participants who had completed at least twelve months of follow-up. Twelve received a full dose. Among those twelve, ten were insulin-independent at day 365, and all twelve met the study's reported criteria of no severe hypoglycemic events over the specified period and a glycated hemoglobin level below seven percent. That is an extraordinary result to investigate. It is also a small, early clinical study with specific eligibility criteria and an important limitation: every participant received immunosuppressive therapy.

The distinction between function and structure is central. The investigators did not have to build an entire pancreas, recreate every duct and vessel, or supply each of its functions. They needed enough viable islet cells to survive in a body and produce clinically useful insulin under the conditions of the study. The apparent simplicity is deceiving. The cells had to be manufactured and differentiated reproducibly, delivered through a medical procedure, survive, and function in people whose immune systems could threaten the transplant. The evidence was promising because a meaningful clinical outcome was observed, not because the resulting cells resembled a miniature organ in a photograph.

The study also reported serious adverse events, including neutropenia, and two deaths during the broader follow-up described by the investigators. Those events demand context rather than an automatic verdict. The report describes the circumstances and assessments of causality; it does not establish that the islet product itself caused those deaths. Immunosuppression, however, has real risks. It can increase vulnerability to infection and requires clinical monitoring. For a patient considering a treatment, the possibility of reduced reliance on injected insulin has to be weighed against the burdens and hazards of the procedure and long-term treatment. A headline containing the words insulin independence is not the whole consent conversation.

This case also clarifies a terminological trap. Stem-cell-derived islet transplantation and laboratory organoids are related areas of regenerative science, but they are not interchangeable terms for the same product. The clinical study concerned differentiated islet cells. A research organoid may be useful for studying pancreatic disease without being an appropriate transplant. If a book calls everything made from stem cells a grown organ, it conceals the actual engineering work and misrepresents how close different lines of research are to patients.

The commercial history around the science contains its own useful counterexample. In March 2025, Vertex, the company developing zimislecel, [reported that a separate investigational approach](https://news.vrtx.com/news-releases/news-release-details/vertex-announces-program-updates-type-1-diabetes-portfolio) involving islet cells inside an immunoprotective device had produced efficacy data insufficient to justify further clinical advancement. The aim of such encapsulation work is compelling: protect the cells from immune attack without placing the recipient under the same burden of systemic immunosuppression. But a protective barrier can also make it difficult for transplanted cells to obtain nutrients, oxygen, and the other conditions necessary to function. A device may solve an immunological problem while worsening a physiological one.

Two programs aimed at a related disease can therefore reach very different points. One produces striking early clinical results while retaining an important treatment burden. Another seeks to remove that burden and confronts a difficult trade-off in performance. This is the opposite of a simple story in which biotechnology climbs a staircase of inevitable successes. The science can advance by showing which elegant solution does not work as intended.

A properly labeled future tissue room would need to preserve that history. A technician checking a batch would not be deciding merely whether a glowing sample looks healthy. They would be testing a product against manufacturing specifications developed through evidence. A clinician would not ask whether the product is sufficiently futuristic. They would ask what functional improvement has been demonstrated in people like the patient under consideration, what monitoring is required, and what remains unknown beyond the available follow-up. A payer would ask how a highly specialized treatment compares with alternatives over a relevant period, without using uncertain future savings to justify any current price at all.

The new room, if built, would sit within an older and less photogenic institution: clinical medicine. It would depend on trial design, adverse-event reporting, long-term surveillance, quality standards, and the willingness to withdraw an approach when results are not good enough. Those obligations do not become less serious when the tissue is derived from cells rather than donated as an intact organ.

The race to grow a complete organ can obscure smaller advances that matter more immediately.

Imagine a treatment that replaces a damaged patch of tissue rather than an entire structure. It may be technically more attainable. Clinical trials could establish whether the graft survives, integrates, performs its intended task, and improves patient outcomes without unacceptable harms. If successful, it could reduce some need for larger interventions. It would not fulfill the dream of a warehouse of spare organs, but it might be the route by which that dream becomes a disciplined research program.

The supply chain would be different as well. The material may need to be produced close to the patient or transported under tightly controlled conditions. Some steps might be automated; others might require highly skilled personnel. If manufacturing takes weeks or months, treatment planning becomes a scheduling problem as much as a surgical one. A critically ill patient cannot necessarily wait for a personalized production cycle. A therapy that is extraordinary in principle may be unavailable when needed.

Timing is a biological constraint that no inspiring rendering can remove.

There is a reason human organ transplantation depends on intricate systems of allocation, compatibility, logistics, surgery, and lifelong care. The need is urgent, the resource is scarce, and the consequences of error are immense. Regenerative approaches may eventually change some of those trade-offs. But new medical capacity does not automatically abolish scarcity. It can create new bottlenecks in facilities, personnel, intellectual property, insurance coverage, trial participation, regulatory review, specialized equipment, and the clinical ability to deliver the treatment safely.

A rich patient and a poor patient may inhabit entirely different regenerative futures even when the same scientific article belongs to both of them.

I worry about this most when a technology is described as a personal backup organ service. The image invites a world in which the fortunate have replacement tissue prepared in advance. There might be legitimate reasons to store cells or prepare personalized therapies under specific clinical circumstances. But the commercial language could become intoxicating before the medical benefit is established. People might pay for storage or preparation that has no demonstrated use in their situation. Companies could acquire samples and future rights whose value is uncertain. A medical frontier is especially vulnerable to products that sell hope faster than evidence.

Hope deserves protection from its sales department.

Medical innovation also confronts unusually difficult ethical questions because patients may be desperate. A person with severe disease can reasonably consider participating in a trial that offers uncertain benefit. That does not relieve researchers, clinicians, and regulators of the duty to describe the risks and the state of evidence accurately. An experimental treatment is not made effective by the intensity of the patient's need. A compassionate motive can coexist with poor science. The solution is not to prohibit research; it is to design trials and oversight that can distinguish real benefits from hopeful interpretations.

The future hospital must therefore have another room. In it, people argue over the results.

They compare patients with appropriate controls. They examine adverse events. They ask whether the treatment changed a meaningful outcome rather than merely a laboratory measurement. They investigate whether a positive result occurred because of the therapy or because the participants differed in an unrecognized way. They consider long-term follow-up. A tissue product that performs adequately at the moment of transplantation may still raise questions about durability years later. The record must remain open long enough for those questions to be answered.

A reliable spare part has to outlast the demonstration that introduced it.

There is a tempting analogy between organs and manufactured components. It can be useful if kept within its limits. A machine needs replacement parts. Bodies can sometimes benefit from replacement tissue. Yet treating the body as a machine with interchangeable pieces hides the complexity of development, adaptation, and the continual conversation between tissues and the immune, nervous, endocrine, and circulatory systems. A successful graft does not become a solution merely because its dimensions fit an anatomical space.

The body is the final test environment, and the body is not a controlled factory.

That is why the living containers in my imagined hospital are so important. They transform a miraculous headline into an accountable process. A protocol must identify the input cells, the steps used to create the tissue, the criteria for release, and the conditions for rejecting a batch. A clinical team must explain what the product can and cannot do. The patient must know whether it is experimental. Long-term information must be collected without turning the person into an involuntary permanent research subject.

All of this could make the treatment more expensive at first. A therapy may be scientifically possible and economically inaccessible. Health systems will have to evaluate whether its benefits justify costs relative to existing care. Those evaluations can be frustrating for developers and patients. They can also protect resources needed to treat other people. Scarcity does not disappear because the science becomes spectacular.

There is another version of the spare organ problem that begins before the organ fails. What if researchers could use tissue models derived from a person to better understand disease or test candidate treatments? There is substantial interest in these approaches, though their reliability and suitability vary by disease and setting. A laboratory model may reveal mechanisms that are difficult to study directly in patients. It may help narrow research directions. It can be a valuable intermediate tool even if it never becomes a transplant.

That possibility reminds me that the most important future of a technology may be the future its original marketing never mentioned.

A research platform could change drug development. Improved models might reduce some experiments that are expensive, poorly predictive, or ethically difficult. They might reveal why a treatment helps one subgroup but not another. These are possibilities that need validation against real-world outcomes. The existence of a human-derived organoid does not make every result more faithful to human disease than other models. Its limitations must be part of its specification.

Scientists have to choose models for the questions they can answer, not for how human the model looks in a photograph.

The photographs themselves can be misleading. An organoid may appear as a small translucent sphere or tissue structure. The image is captivating partly because it resembles a beginning. The viewer supplies the rest of the organ and the surrounding body from imagination. There is nothing wrong with that response. It is one way science attracts curiosity. The difficulty is that a picture has no obligation to show the missing blood supply, incomplete maturation, variability, or functional limitations. Without explanation, beauty can become a false warranty.

A medical paper should be judged by its methods and results, not by the resemblance of a laboratory object to an organ in a schoolbook.

There are broader questions about ownership of biological material. Patient-derived cells may have enormous scientific value. Research may involve consent, biobanking, privacy, commercial partnerships, and potentially the creation of products that benefit many people. Laws and practices differ by jurisdiction and research context. Who gets to use a cell line? What did the donor consent to? Can the data associated with a sample be de-identified adequately? What obligations follow if a line yields valuable discoveries? There is no single rule that settles all these questions.

The imaginative version of the future hospital would need a consent department as carefully designed as its tissue laboratory.

Some people may be uncomfortable with creating biological structures that mimic aspects of human development, especially as models become more complex. Research ethics must address what kinds of capacities are being created, what evidence exists about them, and which precautions are proportionate. Speculation about consciousness should not be presented as a finding, but dismissing serious ethical inquiry because it is inconvenient would be equally poor practice. The science will evolve. Some questions will need to be revisited rather than answered once.

These subjects are difficult in a way that makes simple ideological positions unreliable. A promising method may offer medical benefit and raise genuine new responsibilities. A precaution may protect research participants and also slow useful work. Oversight can be both necessary and improvable. Progress is rarely achieved by declaring one side wholly enlightened.

The imagined hospital must contain a patient, although I do not want to invent one and tell a false story about suffering. Real transplantation and regenerative-medicine patients exist, with histories that deserve accuracy. For this thought experiment it is enough to acknowledge a person waiting for a treatment whose success matters beyond the laboratory. There is a family planning around uncertainty, a clinician balancing options, and a health system allocating finite resources. A failed batch is not merely a manufacturing statistic if it delays care. An exaggerated claim can alter difficult personal decisions.

The patient's interests should set the standard by which the miracle is judged.

Suppose researchers eventually solve the technical problems for one useful application. An organoid-derived tissue is produced reproducibly, transplanted under an established protocol, and shown through clinical evidence to improve outcomes with acceptable risks. The next challenge may be scale. Facilities need to make it reliably in many places. Staff need training. Regulators and clinicians need data. Insurers and public programs need ways to pay. Supply chains need redundancy. Hospitals must know what to do when a batch fails inspection.

This would be a genuine triumph, although the patient might remember it as a scheduled appointment followed by a long recovery and a series of follow-up visits.

I prefer that kind of triumph to a press release promising the end of organ failure.

In the fantasy hospital the technician rejects a container because its measurements fall outside the specifications. The product will not be used. That small act deserves a place in the story. It means the system has learned to refuse its own creation when the evidence does not support the next step. It also means that somebody has authority to interrupt a process that took time and money.

A good future needs more people with that authority, not fewer.

The room may exist someday, though not in the perfect form I first imagined. It may produce patches, grafts, support tissues, or disease models rather than entire organs. Some applications may work; others may fail. A medicine that once sounded impossible may become unremarkable treatment. The essential achievement will be the transition from an extraordinary biological object to a safe, effective, accessible clinical practice.

The spare organ will have arrived only when the person who needs it can receive it on terms that make medical sense. The tissue in the container is the beginning of that story. The hospital has to write the rest.
