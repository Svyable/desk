# The Ministry of Almost

A government office has received a report that nothing happened.

The driver did not strike the pedestrian. The train did not collide with the maintenance vehicle. The laboratory sample was not switched. The nurse caught the dosage discrepancy before the medicine reached the patient. A worker noticed that the safety guard was missing and stopped the machine. The event ended without the injury that would have made it important to the public.

In the imaginary office, these reports are treated as valuable evidence. A clerk does not stamp them NO INCIDENT and send them to a basement archive. The reports are examined for patterns, shared in appropriate de-identified form, and used to identify failures before the failures acquire victims.

I would call the office the Ministry of Almost. The name is ridiculous enough to deserve consideration.

It is also important not to pretend the underlying practice is new. Aviation has a particularly instructive example. The National Aeronautics and Space Administration's Aviation Safety Reporting System has collected and analyzed voluntary, confidential safety reports since 1976. The reports describe near misses, hazardous situations, unsafe occurrences, and other safety-relevant experiences. NASA's overview describes a process in which reports are de-identified and shared to help identify hazards and learn from incidents that may not have produced a catastrophe. The system has received millions of reports. [NASA's ASRS overview](https://www.nasa.gov/human-systems-integration-division/aviation-safety-reporting-system-overview/) is a useful reminder that an institution can be built around the practical value of things that nearly went wrong.

A near collision at Austin-Bergstrom International Airport illustrates why a close call can be more informative than its outcome suggests. On February 4, 2023, a Southwest Airlines jet was preparing to depart from a runway also being approached by a Federal Express cargo aircraft. Dense fog limited visibility. The FedEx crew executed a go-around after recognizing the danger. Everyone survived and neither airplane was damaged. A headline could have reported a frightening episode in which two planes ultimately went on their way.

The [National Transportation Safety Board's investigation](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/pages/DCA23FA149.aspx) found a chain of interacting conditions. The controller had assumed that the departing aircraft would be airborne before the arriving aircraft reached the runway. Low visibility prevented the tower from seeing the aircraft clearly. Surface detection equipment that could have assisted in identifying the conflict was not available at the airport. Communication and awareness among the crews and controller were inadequate to the evolving situation. The NTSB examined the roles of expectations, procedure, technology and training rather than settling for a verdict that one person should have been more careful.

The outcome was not the measure of the system's safety. Had the geometry, timing or decisions differed by a small amount, the same underlying weaknesses could have produced far worse consequences. In June 2024, the NTSB issued safety recommendations addressing detection and alerting technology, low-visibility operations, and related problems. The investigation produced a set of correctable vulnerabilities from an event in which nobody was injured.

It would be wrong to imply that this particular incident was an anonymous Aviation Safety Reporting System submission. The NTSB conducted a formal investigation into a publicly known event. That difference helps explain why a mature safety ecosystem needs more than one route for learning. Some hazards require an official investigation with records, findings and recommendations. Others may be recognized by a mechanic, a pilot or an operator before an incident becomes visible enough to generate an investigation. A confidential reporting system can draw attention to patterns that otherwise remain hidden.

The Austin case also shows why the phrase human error can be so misleading when treated as the end of the inquiry. A controller's assumption mattered. So did the availability of detection technology and the preparation for low visibility. If investigators stop at the individual who made the assumption, the next controller may inherit the same environment with a different chance of making a similar mistake. Human decisions occur inside systems that make some errors more likely and make some errors harder to detect or recover from.

The imagined ministry should study recovery as seriously as failure. The FedEx crew's decision to discontinue the approach prevented the dangerous situation from becoming a collision. A reporting culture focused entirely on blame might miss the value of the action that reduced harm. A useful review asks both what permitted the hazardous condition and what prevented it from producing the worst outcome. The answers lead to different improvements. One may reduce the chance of recurrence; the other may increase the system's ability to cope when a failure begins.

The central insight seems obvious. A serious accident is often preceded by conditions that existed before the accident. Some of those conditions may produce close calls. Studying those cases can reveal weaknesses at lower cost than waiting for the worst outcome.

But collecting them is difficult because a near miss is often somebody's potential confession.

If a worker reports a mistake that did not injure anyone, management may see evidence of incompetence. A supervisor may fear litigation or a damaged performance metric. A company may worry about reputation. A public agency may hesitate to produce records that can be used against it. A person who reports a hazard may become known as difficult. The work of reporting may fall on people who already have too much paperwork. It is entirely rational for individuals to remain silent when the institution rewards silence.

A culture that wants honest information must change those incentives, not merely install a button labeled REPORT.

The actual protections used by NASA's aviation reporting system are an instructive lesson in limited promises. [ASRS explains](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/confidentiality.html) that submitted reports are de-identified before the information enters its database. Names and details that might identify an individual or organization are removed or generalized. The Federal Aviation Administration, which supports the program, offers incentives for reporting, including the possibility of waiving certain penalties for qualifying inadvertent violations. The distinction between the report and enforcement evidence matters. A voluntary safety system would gather little useful material if submitting a report simply made it easier for an employer or regulator to punish the person who disclosed a problem.

The protections have boundaries that the [program itself states plainly](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/caveats.html). Reports of accidents and criminal conduct are not covered by the usual assurances in the same way, and the availability of a penalty waiver depends on conditions. Among them are the inadvertent nature of an eligible violation and timely reporting. These details would not fit neatly on a poster proclaiming a culture of openness. They are part of what makes the promise credible. A program that says nothing bad will ever happen to anybody who reports anything would become untrustworthy as soon as it encountered a serious case.

I imagine a manager wanting to copy the aviation system into a municipal transport department. They announce a protected near-miss portal. Employees can report a dangerous crossing, malfunctioning equipment or an incident narrowly avoided. The manager then discovers that every useful report raises further obligations. Some contain information that could identify a colleague. Some concern a condition that still exists and may require urgent action. Some allege misconduct. Some are based on misunderstandings. The program needs trained people to screen the reports, a protocol for emergencies, legal advice about confidentiality and a route for changing procedures.

The arrangement also needs the confidence of people who have no authority to design it. A worker might have watched a previous colleague disciplined after raising a concern through an ordinary management channel. A new logo and a promise of anonymity will not necessarily change their behavior. Trust has a history. If supervisors quietly identify reporters and retaliate, the dashboard may register fewer incidents and management may celebrate its apparent improvement. The institution would be lying to itself.

There is a mathematical problem beneath the social one. Reports are observations generated by a process that changes with culture, staffing, publicity and experience. Suppose one unit files twelve near misses in a year and another files two. Which is safer? If the first unit has many more operations, higher reporting trust or a broader definition of a report, the raw comparison tells us very little. A credible analysis should account for exposure and reporting behavior. It should not treat a reported close call as identical to an injury or assume that zero reports means zero hazardous conditions.

The question becomes more difficult after an intervention. Suppose a new safety manager encourages disclosure and the number of reports doubles. The manager may be succeeding. Suppose an equipment upgrade removes a hazard and reports decline. That too may be success. The same metric moves in opposite directions under two desirable changes. Evaluation needs multiple signals and a willingness to inspect the reports rather than reduce safety to the count.

My imaginary ministry would devote a portion of its staff to closing the loop. A report would be categorized, checked where possible, and routed to someone with authority to address the underlying condition. The system would record whether the concern was investigated, what was found, what action followed, and whether there was evidence the risk changed. Not every report deserves a major redesign; some will be incorrect or unactionable. The reporter should still be able to see that the institution did more than collect a confession.

The useful institutional artifact might be a short notice sent back months later: a recurring confusion at an intersection was documented, the signs were altered, drivers were retrained, and subsequent observations have or have not shown a difference. There is no need to turn the worker into a hero, and no reason to invent a disaster that was supposedly prevented. The result is a record of institutional learning.

The aviation example includes confidentiality and certain protections intended to encourage reporting within defined limits. Those limits matter. A non-punitive system cannot simply grant immunity for every reckless or unlawful act. It needs rules about what is protected, what is excluded, and how information is handled. A worker has to trust that the stated protections will be applied in practice. A lawyer can draft a reassuring policy, but the person whose career depends on it will look for evidence in what happened to previous reporters.

The credibility of the ministry is established by its first uncomfortable case.

Imagine a bus driver who reports that a confusing intersection creates repeated near collisions with cyclists. The driver may have made an error, or the road geometry may be difficult, or the traffic signals may encourage conflicting expectations. The report by itself cannot settle the explanation. It can identify a situation worth investigating. If the system responds by automatically punishing the driver, future reports may disappear. If it excuses every driver and ignores the conditions, the information will become another archive of unresolved concerns.

A competent office must distinguish accountability from the learning process without pretending they never conflict.

The data itself will be biased. People are more likely to report certain incidents than others. Some organizations encourage reporting; others discourage it. Highly visible errors may produce many submissions while subtle systemic problems remain unrecorded. A rising number of near-miss reports could mean that safety has deteriorated, that trust in reporting has improved, or both. A manager who rewards a lower report count may be rewarding silence.

This is why a dashboard showing an annual decline in incidents can be less comforting than it appears. Without information about exposure, reporting practices, and underlying hazards, the number is ambiguous. The ministry should be suspicious of improvements that seem to arrive just after reporting becomes unpleasant.

It should also be suspicious of the opposite temptation: treating every report as proof of a hidden catastrophe. Near misses can signal risk, but the relationship between near misses and severe incidents differs across systems. A report may describe a genuine vulnerability or a one-time anomaly that is unlikely to recur. Investigators need expertise, context, and methods for prioritizing work. A mountain of low-quality reports can overwhelm an organization and obscure the most important cases.

The ability to collect information is not the same as the ability to learn from it.

I imagine the future office receiving reports from many sectors. A school district describes a transportation arrangement that nearly left a child at the wrong stop. A water utility reports a backup procedure that failed during a test. A hospital documents a labeling problem caught before treatment. A local government records a flood barrier that almost could not be installed on time. The cases are not identical, and some contain confidential or legally protected information. The idea is not to pour everything into a public database. It is to establish the principle that a prevented failure has informational value and deserves a route into better practice.

Different domains would need different reporting systems. Medical privacy rules and clinical governance are not the same as traffic engineering. Security-sensitive infrastructure cannot publish every detail. A regulator may have authority over one sector and none over another. A ministry that claims to solve every class of near miss from a central office might become a dangerous bureaucratic fantasy.

A good version would make existing institutions better at learning rather than require them to surrender all judgment to a new one.

The most interesting cases are those in which a person notices a problem and the organization quietly absorbs the warning. A mechanic improvises a workaround that keeps a machine running. A nurse double-checks an ambiguous label. A clerk knows that a form repeatedly causes errors and corrects them before they enter the permanent record. A software operator intervenes when an automated process behaves unexpectedly. The institution may record the final success without noticing the human effort that prevented failure.

Such organizations can appear more reliable than they actually are. Their apparent smoothness depends on people compensating for bad design.

A Ministry of Almost would want to study those compensations. Why did the mechanic need a workaround? Why was the label ambiguous? Why did the clerk have to correct the same error repeatedly? A system that runs safely only because attentive workers continually rescue it may be vulnerable to fatigue, turnover, or a change in staffing. The future failure is already present, but it is being held away from view by ordinary competence.

This may be the best argument for paying attention to the unremarkable save.

There is a danger in the idea, however. If the institution records every near miss, workers may begin to experience the reporting system as another surveillance tool. A manager could use patterns of reports to rank employees, identify alleged troublemakers, or impose productivity penalties. Even a de-identified report may contain enough contextual detail to reveal a person in a small workplace. Confidentiality is a practice with technical and organizational limits, not a magic property attached to a checkbox.

The reporting system's governance would need to specify who sees what, why, for how long, and with what safeguards. It might separate safety analysis from routine performance management. It might protect reporters while allowing narrowly defined exceptions for serious misconduct. It should communicate those boundaries clearly. Otherwise the ministry will gather carefully edited stories of heroism while the uncomfortable facts remain in private conversations.

The future office could also become a source of perverse incentives. An organization might reward employees for submitting reports, producing a flood of trivial material. It might penalize departments for hazards identified, causing those departments to discourage investigation. It might set quotas for corrective actions, encouraging superficial fixes that look good on paper. A report is a means of discovering risk; it is not itself a unit of safety.

I would want the office to publish something rarer than a total count: examples in which a report actually changed a procedure, design, or decision, accompanied by evidence of the outcome where it exists.

That would make the institution accountable for learning, not merely for collecting.

There is a strong connection between near misses and the way people remember history. A catastrophe creates a date, a headline, an inquiry, a monument, sometimes a new law. The same defect might have existed for years before the event and produced several escapes that nobody later documents. The final accident becomes the story because it is the visible proof. But from the perspective of prevention, the earlier signals may be more important. They show how a system behaved when harm was still avoidable.

The counterfactual is difficult to measure. If a corrective action succeeds, the accident does not happen. There is no definitive photograph of the tragedy that was prevented. This makes prevention politically vulnerable. A funding request can be challenged because the disaster is hypothetical; a funded prevention program can later be challenged because no disaster occurred. Success removes some of the evidence that justified the expenditure.

The ministry would be responsible for making that invisible work intelligible without inventing certainty about what would otherwise have happened.

This requires a kind of statistical modesty. The office could estimate risk reductions from validated models, incident patterns, or controlled interventions where feasible. It could compare outcomes across similar operations, while attending to confounding factors. It could examine whether known hazards were actually corrected. It should not count every reported near miss as a disaster prevented. That would be as dishonest as counting no harm as proof that no risk existed.

I imagine a display in the lobby labeled DISASTERS WE CANNOT CLAIM TO HAVE PREVENTED. It would list the hazards addressed, the evidence behind the actions, and the limitations of the evaluation. The display would make a terrible election advertisement. It might make a great safety culture.

There is another virtue in studying near misses: it can reveal what people knew before an event changed the meaning of earlier evidence. After an accident, warning signs often look obvious. Investigators and commentators can arrange them into a narrative of inevitability. People working at the time may have faced conflicting information, limited authority, uncertain probabilities, and many ordinary tasks. A fair analysis should examine what was actually knowable and actionable before the outcome.

The future office should resist using hindsight as a weapon.

I would want the Ministry of Almost to have a small team whose job is to investigate the reports that everyone else finds boring. The repeated confusing label. The temporary repair that becomes permanent. The software warning dismissed because it fires too often. The door that almost closes at the wrong time. The person who always knows whom to call and whose retirement will leave a gap nobody has identified. These may be insignificant. They may be symptoms of an organization held together by unrecorded knowledge.

The boring cases should be allowed to earn attention through evidence, not drama.

There is also a social benefit to making near misses discussable. An employee who reports a mistake without being humiliated may be more likely to participate in improvement. An organization that admits vulnerabilities can become more trustworthy than one that claims perfection. Yet trust is fragile. The public may misread an increase in reports as evidence of worsening performance. Political opponents may use a safety database as a source of alarming fragments. The institution must communicate openly enough to be accountable without making disclosure so punishing that it destroys the incentive to disclose.

It is a difficult balance. The alternative is to learn only when something breaks badly enough that nobody can deny it.

The Ministry of Almost belongs to a future in which public agencies and companies make prevention visible as serious work. It does not require a new form of intelligence or an exotic machine. It requires a reporting culture, a secure process, skilled analysis, and the willingness to spend resources on hazards that have not yet caused harm. Some sectors have already developed versions of that practice. Others could learn from them while respecting their different constraints.

The imagined clerk receives another report. A worker noticed a bolt loosening, isolated the equipment, and prevented operation until it was repaired. No injury. No property damage. No public alarm. The report is brief and unremarkable.

The clerk files it where investigators can find it, not in a cabinet labeled NOTHING HAPPENED.
