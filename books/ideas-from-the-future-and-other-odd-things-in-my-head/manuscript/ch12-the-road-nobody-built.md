# The Road Nobody Built

The ribbon-cutting has been canceled because there is no road.

This is a difficult achievement to photograph. The mayor stands beside an intersection where traffic moves much as it did last year. No new pavement has been poured. No bridge has acquired the mayor's name. The money originally reserved for a highway widening has been divided among bus service, intersection redesign, bridge maintenance, and repairs to sidewalks leading to a hospital.

The people responsible must now explain why the missing road represents progress.

I imagine this scene because public institutions are usually better at announcing construction than at celebrating a decision not to build. A project can be described with engineering drawings, employment numbers, concrete volumes, and an opening date. The alternative is a set of things that never happen: a family doesn't have to buy a second car; a bridge doesn't deteriorate into closure; a bus passenger doesn't lose another connection; a neighborhood isn't divided by an expanded right of way. The evidence is dispersed. The ceremonial shovel has nowhere to go.

There are real reasons to build roads. Freight must move. Emergency services need access. Rural communities can depend on reliable highways. Congestion wastes time and money, though its costs and causes vary. Safety improvements can prevent serious injuries. A proposal should not be rejected because pavement is unfashionable. The interesting question is why a large project so often gets to define the alternatives as if its own existence had already been decided.

An economic evaluation should compare a proposal with a credible baseline and with other plausible ways to address the problem. The U.S. Department of Transportation's [benefit-cost analysis guidance](https://www.transportation.gov/grants/dot-navigator/what-is-a-benefit-cost-analysis) describes a disciplined attempt to measure benefits and costs across a project's lifetime. The actual exercise is not just adding a set of attractive numbers. Analysts have to identify the problem, define what happens without the project, consider costs and effects, and explain assumptions. An analysis is only as useful as the alternatives and evidence permitted to enter it.

Imagine a highway project that promises to save commuters twenty minutes during the evening peak. The projected benefit looks tremendous when multiplied across vehicles and years. Yet the forecast depends on how many people drive, where they drive, how long the improved conditions last, and whether households and businesses change location or travel choices in response. Adding road capacity can change travel behavior. Some congestion may return as drivers change routes or make trips that the previous conditions discouraged. The magnitude depends on the setting, and an improvement may still produce benefits. But a calculation that assumes travel demand remains fixed after the road changes the cost of travel is leaving out part of the mechanism.

This is a problem of cause and response. The solution affects the problem being measured.

The alternative might be a bus corridor. It too can fail. Service may be infrequent, inconvenient, or poorly connected. A dedicated lane may reduce space for other traffic, creating genuine trade-offs. A system that works on paper may be unpleasant at night or inaccessible to the people whose journeys take place outside office hours. Operating budgets matter; building a station is different from funding a dependable timetable. A transport proposal should be tested against the actual journeys it seeks to improve.

The third alternative may be maintenance. A bridge with deteriorating supports is not photogenic as an investment until something goes wrong. Repairing it may prevent closures and expensive replacement later. Yet deferred maintenance competes in budget hearings with new projects that promise to transform a region. The future sometimes consists of allowing an existing asset to remain boring.

I like that possibility because it gives accountants a heroic role they have not asked for.

Los Angeles has a real example of the ceremonial shovel being put away. For years the proposed widening of Interstate 710 along the corridor between the ports and inland communities sat inside a much larger fight about freight, pollution, mobility and the neighborhoods beside the freeway. The corridor carries essential goods movement. It also passes through communities that bear the effects of heavy vehicle traffic and the land-use decisions of earlier generations. Proposals to expand capacity were met with concerns about air quality, displacement and the consequences of treating the roadway as the only transportation problem in the room.

In May 2022, the Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority's board acted to end the previous widening proposal. According to a [subsequent board report](https://metro.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?G=A5FAA737-A54D-4A6C-B1E8-FF70F765FA94&GUID=BEDCE3EF-A791-4ACD-AA1D-DB2C13CD61BB&ID=5844793&Options=&Search=), the board substituted a no-build alternative for the earlier locally preferred expansion and directed that the corridor effort move toward a broader investment plan. A related [May 2022 motion](https://metro.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?FullText=1&G=A5FAA737-A54D-4A6C-B1E8-FF70F765FA94&GUID=D46F3FB4-129D-487F-AB48-0F3E9AB443D8&ID=5652969&Options=&Search=) stated that capacity-expanding freeway widening would no longer be part of the project. In September the board approved the new name Long Beach–East LA Corridor Mobility Investment Plan and recommendations for vision, goals and initial investment opportunities.

A decision like that becomes a test of whether rejecting a project is the beginning of serious work or an excuse to stop doing it. The ports still move freight. Trucks and commuters still use the corridor. Nearby residents still live with the current road, its emissions and its hazards. Abandoning a widening proposal does not remove the need to maintain infrastructure or improve mobility. It changes which answers officials are allowed to pursue. Their obligation becomes designing a credible package of alternatives, selecting priorities, securing funding and showing whether the conditions that motivated the plan actually improve.

The history also prevents an easy ideological victory. People who depend on reliable freight routes have genuine interests, including workers whose jobs are tied to the movement of goods. Residents alongside the road have genuine interests in health, housing stability and access. Transit advocates may see an opportunity to invest differently; a logistics operator may worry that an alternative leaves congestion unresolved. The no-build decision does not dissolve the conflicting purposes of the corridor. It brings the conflict out from behind the assumption that widening is inevitable.

One might imagine a public plaque beside the freeway recording the canceled project. Yet I would be more interested in a budget showing what followed. Did the agency fund improvements that reduced conflicts between freight and local travel? Were safer crossings or transit investments delivered? Were environmental concerns taken seriously in project design? Did funding reach the communities that had fought the original proposal, or did the argument end with an announcement and little practical change? Those questions require later evidence, and the board's 2022 decision cannot answer them by itself.

The physical road remains, which is why the phrase no-build can be deceiving. The alternative to expansion may involve maintaining what exists, changing operations, improving nearby streets, offering other modes and sometimes building different infrastructure. A corridor can change significantly without adding lanes to the particular highway that once defined the planning conversation. The absence of widening is not the absence of investment.

The case also exposes a disagreement about time. A road capacity project promises benefits that can be modeled decades ahead; construction itself may take years. The neighborhoods adjoining the corridor experience health, noise, travel and access conditions now. A replacement plan can fail if it consists entirely of distant promises. That makes early investment opportunities more than political decoration. They allow the agency to demonstrate whether the new philosophy has a route into actual improvements.

A skeptical reader should reserve judgment on the success of the decision until there is evidence about the delivered projects and their effects. That skepticism should apply equally to the canceled widening and to the replacement plan. A proposal is not successful because its advocates have persuasive language about community. It is successful, in the dimensions it claims, when people can see a change that matters and when the costs of getting there are defensible.

The road nobody built could be a decision made for bad reasons as well. A government might cancel a necessary connection because the budget is exhausted or a politically powerful neighborhood refuses change. Officials could call the cancellation environmental stewardship while communities remain isolated. Avoiding construction does not automatically deliver justice or efficiency. The decision needs the same scrutiny as a project approval.

A good evaluation would ask who benefits and who bears the costs, not merely whether the aggregate numbers are favorable. Travel time may be particularly valuable to a worker managing a tight shift or childcare schedule. A new road might remove homes from a low-income neighborhood. A transit investment might help people who do not own cars. A freight delay might change the economics of a business. Some effects are difficult to price; difficulty is a reason to disclose them, not to pretend they have no value.

The imagined mayor cannot simply say that the project saves money. The public deserves to know where the money went, which outcomes were expected, and whether the new arrangement performed as promised.

Suppose the city begins with the intersection rather than the highway. It measures crashes and near misses, turning movements, pedestrian exposure, transit delay, and access to nearby destinations. Engineers change signal timing and crossing geometry, improve sidewalks, and reallocate some funds to more frequent buses. They monitor the results. Some congestion remains. The city discovers that one small improvement increased a queue elsewhere. Another works better than expected. A transit route becomes popular enough to require additional service.

This is not a triumphant story in which one clever change solves traffic. It is how a transport system learns.

The difficulty is political durability. A mayor may leave office. A new council may prefer a visible construction project. A bus route that requires annual operating funds can be more vulnerable than a road whose concrete was paid for in a single capital budget, though roads also carry lifelong maintenance costs. The design of public finance can reward the creation of assets and underfund the service needed to make them useful.

A system that pays generously for a new station while hesitating to fund buses will produce attractive places to wait.

There is a parallel to medical care. A dramatic treatment receives attention, while preventing the illness may produce no visible event. A disaster prevented is hard to prove. A road never built can look like an absence rather than a choice. If an institution wants to make the latter decision well, it must learn to explain counterfactuals: what would have happened under plausible alternatives, how uncertain the forecasts are, and what changes as new evidence arrives.

Counterfactuals are uncomfortable because no one gets to observe the same city twice.

In my future ribbon-cutting, the officials make a curious compromise. They install a small plaque where the proposed interchange would have stood. It records the original project, its estimated cost, the alternatives selected, and the indicators the city promised to review. A visitor can read the case for cancellation and the evidence on which it relied. If the alternative fails, the plaque becomes an embarrassing record of a wrong decision. That is a feature.

Institutions should leave enough information behind for later generations to discover whether they were clever or merely lucky.

Some projects will fail the test and should be built after all. Some should be smaller, safer, or differently located. Others may be replaced by service, maintenance, pricing, or changes in land use. The decision cannot be made by an ideology about concrete. It has to survive the details of a place.

The road nobody built has another advantage: people can keep asking whether it should exist. Once the government takes land, divides a neighborhood, and builds a large structure, reversing the decision becomes expensive. Not building preserves certain options, though delay can also be costly. There is value in avoiding irreversible commitments when information is uncertain, but the value must be weighed against the harm of waiting.

The most futuristic object on the missing interchange might be a revised project ledger. It would include every proposal that was seriously considered, the evidence for rejection, and the observed results of the choice eventually made. Instead of a memorial to grand ambition, it would be an archive of judgment.

The ribbon-cutting ends without photographs of a highway. People still need to get home. Some take the bus. Some drive. A bridge farther along the route is less likely to close because maintenance was funded. No one knows whether the canceled project would have performed better in every respect. The city continues measuring.

The absent road has finally become something that can be evaluated.
