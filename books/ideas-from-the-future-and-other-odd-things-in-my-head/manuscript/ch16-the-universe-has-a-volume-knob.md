# The Universe Has a Volume Knob

The person at the exhibit puts on headphones and hears a nebula.

High notes climb and fall as an image advances across a screen. An arrangement of light becomes a sequence of pitches. The sound has the odd authority of a field recording: someone has apparently placed a microphone in the universe and brought home a piece of it.

Except no one put a microphone there, and the noise did not travel through interstellar space to a pair of headphones.

NASA has published a fascinating collection of [data sonifications](https://www.nasa.gov/data-sonifications/) that translate astronomical observations into audible patterns. Scientists, musicians, and accessibility specialists have worked on projects using observations from telescopes including Chandra, Hubble, and the James Webb Space Telescope. Features in images or measured data can be assigned sound attributes such as pitch, volume, or timing. These are ways of exploring data through a different sensory channel. NASA explains that sound does not propagate through empty space as it does through air; the audio is a constructed representation of measurements.

That is important because the result can be beautiful without being a literal recording.

I have always been suspicious of the idea that beauty and technical accuracy must compete. A good chart can reveal something that a table conceals. A musical rendering of data might draw attention to a pattern that is difficult to perceive visually. It can also create the emotional impression that the universe has chosen a melody. That impression may be affecting, but the choice of notes belongs to the people who designed the mapping.

The galaxies did not hire a composer.

Sonification is a broad technique with applications beyond astronomy. Signals and data can be represented through sound in scientific analysis, accessibility, monitoring, and art. A change in pitch or rhythm can communicate variation in an underlying measurement. Auditory displays may help people notice events while their eyes are occupied or give access to data that would otherwise be presented visually. But the effectiveness depends on the data, the task, the listener, and the mapping. Some sounds are easier to distinguish than others. An attractive composition may obscure quantitative relationships.

An honest exhibit should offer both the sound and the explanation of how it was made.

Consider a telescope image in which brightness varies across a region. A designer could assign higher pitches to brighter features, or louder sounds, or a different instrumental timbre. Each choice yields a different experience from the same measurements. If position on the image maps to time, the scan direction changes what the listener hears first. If colors correspond to observations at different wavelengths, the mapping may combine signals from instruments sensitive to different physical phenomena. The sound can be informative, but it is not the uniquely correct voice of the object.

The representation has an author.

That raises an interesting question about scientific trust. When researchers publish a chart, they are expected to label axes, units, scales, and sometimes uncertainty. A sonification should disclose comparable information. What is mapped to pitch? Which quantities affect loudness? Has the signal been normalized? What range of frequencies is audible? Are multiple data sources combined? Does the sound include musical accompaniment not derived from measurements? The answers do not spoil the art. They allow the listener to appreciate what is measured and what is interpreted.

A good exhibit may invite people to alter the mapping themselves. One version might emphasize intensity, another spatial position, another changes over time. A visitor could discover that the same data produce quite different auditory impressions. This is an unusually direct lesson in scientific representation: a model or visualization can be useful and contingent at once.

The issue is especially important for accessibility. Sonification can give blind or low-vision users an alternative way to explore data that are typically displayed as images. That goal deserves more than a decorative audio track. Design should involve intended users, evaluate comprehension, and avoid assuming that one auditory experience serves everybody. Some users may prefer descriptions, tactile materials, or interactive formats. Others may find complex sonifications compelling. Choice and usability matter.

It would be embarrassing to advertise an accessibility triumph without asking the people it is meant to serve whether it helps.

The Perseus galaxy cluster offers a wonderful exception to the lazy sentence there is no sound in space. In most of the enormous distances between astronomical objects, the vacuum does not provide an ordinary medium for acoustic waves. But the gas filling a galaxy cluster is not empty. Observations of hot gas in the Perseus cluster revealed pressure waves associated with activity near its central supermassive black hole. Those waves corresponded to frequencies far below the range of human hearing. Their existence was inferred from observations, not from a microphone drifting through the cluster.

The [Chandra X-ray Center's sonification of Perseus](https://www.chandra.cfa.harvard.edu/sound/perseus.html) begins with that real physical phenomenon. Scientists extracted the pattern of pressure waves from observational data and shifted the frequencies upward by approximately fifty-seven to fifty-eight octaves to bring them into the range humans can hear. The resulting audio can be eerie. It also provides a wonderful argument against careless language about scientific representation. These are related to actual pressure waves in gas, but the audio is not what an unaided human ear would hear while floating near the black hole. The frequency translation is astonishingly large, and the circumstances of listening are invented by the researchers.

That distinction has a counterpart in a very different sonification. In [Cassiopeia A](https://www.chandra.cfa.harvard.edu/sound/casa_multi.html), the remains of an exploded star have been observed using several kinds of telescopes. A Chandra project translates data from X-ray and infrared observations and optical images into different sounds. In a multiwavelength version, features in Chandra's X-ray data are assigned modified piano sounds, infrared observations from Webb and Spitzer are associated with strings and brass, and stars represented by Hubble data with small cymbal sounds. The scan begins at the central neutron star and moves outward. Brighter regions are louder or higher pitched according to the mapping.

Here the construction is impossible to overlook once it is explained. An infrared observation is not inherently a trumpet. X-ray photons did not arrive at the telescope with a little piano melody attached. The mapping uses sound as a display. Different instruments and frequency ranges have been assigned different audible identities so that listeners can perceive relationships across the image. It is a creative, potentially informative representation whose authority depends on the underlying observations and the clear explanation of the transformation.

The contrast between Perseus and Cassiopeia A could fill an entire exhibition. In one, a physical pressure-wave phenomenon is shifted into an audible range. In the other, different kinds of electromagnetic data are mapped into sound according to a designed scheme. The two projects can both be called sonifications, but the word does not settle what the sound means. A listener who knows the mechanism gains an additional pleasure from the audio. They can distinguish the ingenuity of the researchers from the physical process they are representing.

I would put a large knob on the exhibit labeled ASSUMPTIONS. Turning it would change the mapping while leaving the observational data fixed. In Cassiopeia A, the visitor might hear the same X-ray structures rendered with shorter or longer notes, higher or lower registers, or a different scan speed. The image would not have changed. The experience would. The demonstration could explain why a choice of auditory scale can make a faint variation more apparent or bury it under other sounds.

The next knob would be labeled ORIGINAL DATA. It would not play a pure, authentic sound because no such sound exists for some mappings. Instead it would show the observations, how the instruments detected them, and the processing steps used before the sonification. A visitor could trace each prominent sound back to the relevant measurement. This is what transparency looks like when the representation is auditory: not a demand that scientists remove every artistic decision, but an opportunity to understand which decisions they made.

The projects were also built with an accessibility ambition. [NASA's description](https://www.nasa.gov/data-sonifications/) credits collaboration among scientists, musicians and members of the blind and visually impaired community, with audio resources developed to support blind and low-vision listeners. This is a stronger foundation than making an astronomical soundtrack and declaring it accessible because it has sound. A person who cannot use a visual graph may need an auditory form that permits detailed exploration, repeated listening, orientation and comparison. The useful design depends on the questions the user wants to answer.

A museum could test two very different goals. One is whether a visitor enjoys a sonic experience of astronomical data and becomes curious about astronomy. The other is whether a listener can accurately identify a specified pattern or relationship that the sound was designed to communicate. Both goals are legitimate. The first concerns public engagement; the second concerns information access. A beautiful composition may succeed wonderfully at the first while providing little help with the second. A less musical, more systematic display might succeed at the second while offering less emotional satisfaction.

The ideal should not require every sonic representation to satisfy every purpose. A data sonification for a research instrument might need to distinguish narrow anomalies, changes in a sensor's operating state and uncertainty. A museum exhibit may prioritize inviting people to explore something distant and unfamiliar. An accessibility tool must be designed with its users and tasks in mind. Confusing these purposes can make the evaluation either unfair or meaningless.



The imagined future museum has an entire wall of volume controls. A visitor can listen to a chart of changes in global temperature, the rhythm of traffic at an intersection, the activity of a distant star, or variations in a sensor network. These sounds are translations from data, not direct recordings. The wall allows listeners to hear patterns they might not notice visually, and it lets them inspect the rules producing each sound.

Then a curator makes a mistake. One dataset is assigned to a musical scale that sounds cheerful even when the underlying phenomenon is dangerous. Another produces ominous low notes from data that are entirely benign. Visitors infer emotions from the soundtrack. The instrument has created a narrative bias.

This is not a reason to ban music from science museums. It is a reason to distinguish sonic design from the emotional properties of the phenomena being measured. Data have numerical and structural relationships; music also carries cultural expectations and individual associations. A falling note may feel like decline, but the underlying number could represent a reduction in disease or an increase in safety. A warning sound can be effective, yet it should correspond to an identified condition rather than the designer's taste for drama.

We already know that sound can influence behavior. Alarm systems depend on it. The difficulty is assigning an audible signal to a meaningful state and testing whether the people who hear it respond appropriately. A factory alarm that is ignored or misunderstood can be dangerous. An astronomical sonification that is misunderstood usually has less immediate consequence, but the underlying lesson is the same. Sound should communicate something true about the data's intended use.

There is a more mischievous idea hiding here. What if people could hear the operations of institutions? A municipal water system might publish an auditory display of usage and leakage patterns. A city's public transport could turn delays into sound. A building might allow residents to explore energy demand by ear. These applications would need careful attention to confidentiality, noise, accessibility, and whether sonification actually improves decisions. Some would be useless. A few might turn a complicated system into something people can understand without reading a dense report.

I like the thought of an orchestra for public infrastructure, but I would not want it to become a substitute for fixing the pipes.

The temptation to confuse the representation with reality grows as the technology becomes more sophisticated. A generative model could turn the same data into a lush symphony, adding musical continuity, harmony, and dynamics that never existed in the source. The result might make extraordinary art. It could also conceal which parts of the experience are data-driven. The responsible label would say so.

There is no shame in an artwork inspired by scientific measurements. There is a problem when the art is described as evidence.

The future exhibit might have two buttons. The first says HEAR THE DATA. It plays a carefully documented mapping whose design has been evaluated for the stated task. The second says HEAR WHAT THE DATA INSPIRED. It plays a composition made by a musician. Visitors can enjoy both. They can also learn something about the difference between measurement and imagination.

I would use the second button often.

There is a third button I want to add. It says HEAR THE UNCERTAINTY. When a measurement is imprecise, the sonification might convey the uncertainty through an audible range or other carefully designed cue. This would be a difficult problem. It could easily make sounds confusing. But scientific measurements are rarely exact, and a representation that strips uncertainty away may communicate greater confidence than the evidence permits. The design should be tested with actual listeners rather than assumed effective because it is clever.

The larger question is how many ways the world can be made perceptible. Human senses occupy a narrow range of possible signals. Instruments extend those senses into wavelengths, scales, and conditions we cannot directly experience. Data visualization translates the results into color and shape; sonification into pitch, rhythm, and timbre. Each translation makes something available while inevitably leaving other things out.

I find that deeply satisfying. The universe does not need to resemble a human experience to be studied. Human ingenuity can build a bridge toward parts of it.

The danger begins when the bridge becomes a myth about the destination. A sonified black hole is not evidence that it sings in a way recognizable to a human listener. An image made from infrared measurements is not simply what an eye would see from a nearby spaceship. A simulation of a galaxy collision is not a time-lapse camera recording of billions of years. These representations can be accurate about specific relationships and still be acts of construction.

Their limits make them more interesting.

At the end of the imagined exhibit, the visitor removes the headphones. The room is quiet. The nebula remains on the screen, light from a distant event measured by an instrument and translated through several layers of human design. Someone asks whether the sound was real.

The curator could give a complicated answer. A good one might begin: the measurements are real, and the music is one way of making them available to you.

That seems enough to me. The universe has no volume knob of its own, but people have built one. There is reason to be astonished by both the sky and the ingenuity of the translation, provided we remember which part belongs to which.
