# Naming the Weather

The storm has been given a name before it arrives.

The name helps television anchors, emergency managers, insurers, and ordinary people refer to the same moving system. It fits neatly in a headline. A person can tell a friend to take precautions before the storm reaches the coast. International arrangements for naming tropical cyclones have existed for decades, with lists and regional procedures developed through the World Meteorological Organization and its partners. The details vary by basin. A name is useful communication equipment attached to a physical danger.

Now imagine the city naming an afternoon.

The temperature is forecast to become dangerously high. The municipal bulletin says that Thursday is Eleanor. The next extreme-heat episode will be another name. Residents learn to recognize the warning. Employers review outdoor schedules. Transit operators prepare cooling measures. Schools consider adjustments. Local services reach out to people most at risk.

It sounds silly until one remembers that heat can harm people without the spectacle of a storm. A hurricane announces itself with wind, rain, and a path that can be drawn across a map. Dangerous heat can look like a perfectly ordinary summer day in a photograph. There may be no ruined storefront and no dramatic nighttime video. The consequences can develop in rooms with inadequate cooling, on job sites, at bus stops, and in the bodies of people whose vulnerabilities are invisible to the person reading the forecast.

Seville, Spain, put the theory into practice during the summer of 2022. A program called proMETEO Sevilla created a system for categorizing dangerous heat events based on their expected health impact and named one of them Zoe. It was a striking change in how the public might be asked to experience a day. The city had not discovered a new atmospheric phenomenon. Researchers and public-health partners were trying to give an old hazard some of the public urgency that people associate with a named storm. The first names of most technological inventions are chosen to help people buy them. This name was chosen to help people avoid a danger.

The experiment produced evidence, and the evidence is much less triumphant than a publicity campaign would like. Researchers later surveyed 2,022 adults in the Seville area about awareness, beliefs and heat-safety behavior. In a [2024 report in *Scientific Reports*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59430-8), only 123 respondents, about six percent of the sample, recalled Zoe's name without prompting. Another 526, about twenty-six percent, said they recognized it when asked directly. The two figures are not interchangeable: recognition after hearing a name is less demanding than spontaneous recall, and it may be subject to different reporting errors. Most respondents did not spontaneously remember the name at all.

The minority who could recall Zoe without help also reported greater engagement in heat-safety practices and more favorable views of the local government's heat response. That association was consistent with the idea that naming could be useful. It did not establish that the name caused the behavior. People who already paid closer attention to heat warnings, followed local news, had more resources to act, or cared more about public health might have been more likely to remember Zoe. The authors recognized that the post-survey was correlational and that there was no equivalent comparison group exposed to precisely the same warning campaign without the name. The difference is decisive for any city considering what to copy.

The results tell a more interesting story than either champions or skeptics might prefer. Something got through to some people. The communication apparently passed many others by. The naming component may have helped, but the study cannot isolate its effect from the surrounding campaign. A city that spends money assigning names should ask what additional behavior the names achieve compared with an equally well-funded system that sends clear instructions without them.

The researchers also found that unaided recall was more common among respondents with higher monthly incomes. That is a warning hidden in the marketing results. A message may reach people who already have the time, technology, safety and social connections to respond. It could become popular among journalists, officials and affluent residents while failing to reach the worker whose employer will not change a shift or the older person whose apartment remains dangerously warm. A memorable name that spreads unevenly might make a city feel better prepared without changing the distribution of risk.

Consider how the effect would look inside an actual municipal budget. A communications department can purchase advertisements, develop a branding system, and monitor online discussions. A public-health team can identify high-risk groups and offer targeted information. A transit agency can provide access to cooling facilities; an employer can modify outdoor work schedules; housing agencies can address overheated residences. These expenditures compete for money and attention. If naming improves recognition, it may be worth funding. If providing accessible services changes the number of people who can actually take protective action, a city should not confuse the inexpensive delight of a catchy name with the more expensive work.

The way an alert is phrased can reveal hidden assumptions about the audience. A message saying stay indoors during an extreme heat event is convenient to write. It assumes that indoors is cooler, that a person has control over whether they work outside, and that the household has a safe space in which to remain. A message to visit a cooling center assumes a person can travel to it, knows its hours, and can safely return home. Naming the event may increase attention. It does nothing on its own to make the advice possible.

There was also a revealing failure in the Seville survey. Far fewer respondents could identify the program's heat categorization system than could recognize the name Zoe. A label can be remembered while the information that tells people what to do remains obscure. This is a particular danger of communication that emphasizes cultural salience. A person may remember that something important happened and still not know whether tomorrow's condition requires avoiding strenuous activity, checking on a vulnerable neighbor, or finding an accessible place to cool down.

A city should therefore test comprehension of action, not merely recall of a name. If the warning arrives in a language someone cannot read, or if it relies on a smartphone the person does not own, the name may be perfect and the communication useless. Local organizations, employers, schools, transit operators and community groups can be essential parts of a warning system because they know which people are likely to hear the instructions and which have the practical means to use them. These organizations also have limitations; the point is to design delivery as carefully as the message.

Some jurisdictions and organizations have explored naming heat waves or assigning heat alert categories. The point is to improve recognition and action. It is an intervention in communication, not in the atmosphere. A name cannot reduce a temperature. It can change what people understand about the temperature, provided the alert is credible.

That last condition deserves scrutiny. A public warning system succeeds when it helps people make decisions that reduce harm. It can fail if the words are too vague, if alerts arrive too frequently, if the danger categories are inconsistent, or if people are told to take actions they cannot afford or practically perform. A heat warning that tells people to stay home assumes home is a safe place. A warning to avoid outdoor work assumes a worker can change their schedule without losing income. A suggestion to visit a cooling center depends on transportation and hours of operation.

The weather has no obligation to match the social privileges of the person writing the advice.

Naming can create a different problem. Tropical cyclones have relatively clear meteorological definitions and well-established regional processes, though there are complexities in tracking and classifying them. A heat wave is often defined relative to local climate, duration, health risk, or a particular threshold. Two regions can experience the same temperature and different levels of danger. Nighttime cooling, humidity, exposure, acclimatization, housing, and infrastructure all matter. A national list of names may make communication easy while flattening local risk.

A warning should begin with the mechanism that harms people and the action that could help, not with branding.

The broader idea is that the future may need names for changes that are already visible in measurements but poorly represented in public language. Air quality episodes, harmful algal blooms, dangerous urban heat, prolonged drought, and other hazards can unfold slowly or across administrative boundaries. Naming can make a diffuse process easier to discuss. It can also create the illusion that a complex phenomenon has become a single event with a beginning and end.

Consider the difference between a storm and a drought. A storm may pass through a region in hours or days. Drought accumulates through shortages of precipitation and other hydrological conditions, with consequences for agriculture, ecosystems, water supply, and power generation. Its onset and recovery can be difficult to define. Giving it a memorable name may attract attention. It may also encourage the false belief that the problem is over because rainfall returned for a week.

A name can be an index. It cannot be the evidence.

The United States National Weather Service has taken another route by building a forecast of health-relevant heat risk without assigning human names. Its [HeatRisk tool](https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat-index) uses weather and climate information alongside relationships between heat and health derived in part from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention data. It considers how unusual conditions are for a location and time of year, the duration of heat, and the effect of hot nights that may offer little relief. The tool provides a color-and-number scale ranging from green, little to no elevated risk, through increasingly serious categories to an extreme level represented by magenta. It supplements rather than replaces official watches, warnings and advisories.

This is less personable than calling Thursday Eleanor. It is also trying to answer a different question. The named event creates a memorable unit for a public campaign. A risk scale tells people how a forecast relates to potential harm, with particular attention to those who face elevated risk at temperatures that may not trigger the strongest conventional warning. A vulnerable person may need to adjust an activity while most of the city continues functioning. The best system could combine a memorable message with usable risk information. The two are complementary only if the public can tell what each means.

The [CDC's clinical guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/heat-health/hcp/clinical-guidance/how-to-use-the-heatrisk-tool-and-air-quality-index.html) connects the HeatRisk categories with recommended precautions and the groups likely to be affected. This matters because a color is not a prescription. A person has to translate a forecast into decisions about work, travel, medications, housing, recreation or care. The right interpretation will depend on their circumstances. A public scale that never reaches the people facing the greatest danger may be technically impressive and operationally weak.

There is a particularly difficult public-health question buried in the map: the risk is distributed unevenly within any colored area. A high-resolution forecast can show regional patterns, but it does not know the ventilation in an individual apartment, the condition of a bus shelter or whether an employer will let a worker take a break. A local heat-risk category is a statement about conditions and possible impacts. It is not a guarantee that every resident is equally safe or unsafe. That is a limitation to explain in the warning rather than a reason to abandon the forecast.

I would like a municipal exercise in which two departments receive the same forecast but have to use it differently. The public-health department needs advice suitable for people with different levels of vulnerability. A transit agency needs to inspect stops, adjust operations where possible and provide riders with useful information. Parks staff might need to consider outdoor programming. Employers are responsible for workplace precautions under applicable rules. The name of an event could coordinate the response, but the work of responding would remain particular to each institution.

A naming scheme also has to confront the problem of novelty. Zoe attracted interest partly because it was an unusual thing to do. If heat episodes receive names every summer, does the public continue paying attention? If only the most severe episodes are named, do people interpret an unnamed but still dangerous day as safe? If a name is issued and the forecast changes, does the event keep its name? These questions affect credibility. A weather system can become famous for its name; an effective health warning should become remembered for what people understood about their options.

The test might be straightforward and unglamorous. A city could compare messaging approaches, if ethically and operationally appropriate, and record whether recipients understood the risk, remembered the practical advice and took feasible protective action. It should stratify results by factors that shape exposure and access, without assuming the study sample perfectly represents the city. It could examine missed and false warnings, and whether trust changes after a forecast turns out differently from what residents expected. The result might be that names help some audiences, a risk scale helps others, and a robust local service network helps everyone more than either communication device alone.

The future does not owe us a catchy name for every hazard. It owes people information accurate enough to use, delivered before the decision must be made and attached to services that make action possible. A government should be willing to discover that its cleverest word did not do much good.

I am interested in what a genuinely useful naming system would have to include. It might explain the criteria that triggered a warning, give the expected location and duration, identify the groups and activities facing heightened risk, and connect the public to specific services. Its categories should be evaluated against outcomes, including missed warnings and false alarms. It should be adjusted when evidence shows that a threshold failed to identify danger.

If people stop responding, the institution should investigate why before blaming the public for complacency.

A city might test whether named heat alerts improve understanding compared with ordinary warnings. That requires more than counting headlines. Researchers could measure whether people recall the advice, whether employers and public agencies take appropriate action, and whether the system reaches vulnerable populations. The effects would need to be distinguished from improvements in service, changing weather, other communications, and seasonal experience. A polished logo may be excellent at selling a public campaign while doing little for the person without air conditioning.

There is another strange consequence of making hazards legible. They can become cultural events. People might remember Eleanor as the heat wave when the library stayed open overnight. Local stories could accumulate around a name. That might help future planning. It could also encourage dramatization, comparisons, and a sense of competition among episodes. No one should need a record-setting disaster to recognize an ordinary dangerous condition.

I would prefer a warning system that is boring when it works. It tells the truth soon enough for someone to act.

The scientific part of the story is also more complicated than much public commentary allows. Meteorology predicts particular weather conditions over limited horizons with uncertainty. Climate science studies distributions, trends, mechanisms, and longer-term changes. Event attribution research can examine whether and how human-driven climate change affected the likelihood or intensity of a particular type of extreme event. The question is generally probabilistic and conditional on methods and assumptions. It is not equivalent to placing a name tag on a storm and declaring which portion of it came from a specific factory.

Public communication can be accurate and plain without pretending every uncertainty is eliminated.

I imagine the city's first naming program receiving a formal complaint. A resident points out that the named event arrived on Tuesday and the city had forecast Wednesday as the most dangerous day. The timing of the warning confused a workplace. Another resident says the name was memorable but the cooling center was inaccessible. A third says the alerts had become too frequent to distinguish urgent danger from ordinary hot weather.

These complaints should not be treated as enemies of science. They are information about whether the warning actually served its purpose.

It is possible that naming proves inferior to a simple color-coded risk scale and practical guidance. If so, the city should abandon the theatrical name. A communication device should be judged by what people understand and do, not by how imaginative its designers feel. The failure would be useful evidence.

But if the name improves recall and encourages timely action, it may be worth preserving. A city could develop a local ritual around heat preparedness that brings schools, employers, transit, public health, and neighborhood organizations into the same conversation. The name would be a small shared signal, a way of making an invisible threat socially visible.

This is a different kind of future invention. It changes language, not machinery.

We often talk as though technology transforms the world and words merely report the transformation. Yet public institutions act through words. An alert activates plans. A diagnosis changes treatment. A legal category determines which rules apply. The difference between a vague concern and a named hazard can affect funding, responsibility, and urgency. That power makes naming useful and dangerous. A label can bring attention to a neglected risk or turn a complicated person or event into a misleading category.

The future will need better names for some things, and fewer names for others.

In the imagined city, the alert arrives before dawn. Eleanor is expected to bring several dangerous afternoons and unusually warm nights. The message identifies the highest-risk hours, accessible public facilities, transit information, and practical precautions. It acknowledges uncertainty. A worker shows the warning to a supervisor. A school adjusts an outdoor event. A neighbor calls someone who cannot easily leave home. The actions, not the name, are the measure of success.

Weeks later the name has been forgotten by most residents. The emergency manager reviews what happened and what could be improved. Nobody orders commemorative merchandise.

I would count that as a good season.
