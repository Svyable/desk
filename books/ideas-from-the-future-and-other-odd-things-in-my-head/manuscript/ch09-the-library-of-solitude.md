# The Library of Solitude

The door has a sign that would seem absurd on almost any other public building: YOU ARE WELCOME TO BE HERE WITHOUT BUYING ANYTHING.

The building is a library, so the promise is familiar. What makes the imagined version unusual is what it lends. Books are available, certainly. There are desks, reference materials, public computers, and the ordinary services of a library. But one wing has been designed specifically for people who want to be alone among other people. The rooms are small. Some face a courtyard. Others have comfortable chairs and no screens. There is no compulsory program and no suggestion that a quiet visitor should improve a skill, expand a network, or complete a project.

You can sit there for an hour without producing evidence that the hour was useful.

I suspect this would be either an extraordinary public service or a municipal scandal, depending on the mood of the budget committee.

Public discussion of loneliness often assumes the remedy is more connection. That is sensible up to a point. Social isolation and loneliness are significant concerns, and institutions can help people form relationships. The United States Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on [loneliness and social connection](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) highlighted the role of social infrastructure, including libraries, parks, public transportation, housing, and community programs. The report treated connection as shaped partly by the places and institutions through which people encounter one another.

That is different from claiming every quiet person is lonely.

Solitude can be restorative. It can permit thought, reading, grief, recovery, concentration, and the unobserved experience of being oneself. A person may want others nearby without wanting a conversation. Crowded loneliness and chosen solitude can occur on the same street. A city that measures success only through social engagement may accidentally make life harder for people whose needs involve quiet, privacy, and freedom from performance.

The market offers solitude, but much of it arrives with a bill. A hotel room, a private office, a vacation, a membership space, a rented cabin, a secluded home, or a table in a quiet restaurant can create privacy for people who can afford it. Someone living in a crowded household or working in a noisy environment may have little access to space that is both safe and unclaimed by another obligation. Public parks and libraries provide alternatives, but their availability, accessibility, hours, and atmosphere vary.

It seems reasonable that a civilization capable of making sophisticated entertainment available to billions of people should also be able to offer a room where nothing is happening.

The distinction between solitude and loneliness has been studied experimentally, although the evidence does not license grand claims about what a municipal room can do. In research published in *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* in 2018, Thuy-vy Nguyen, Richard Ryan and Edward Deci examined how periods spent alone affected emotional experience. Their [experiments on solitude as affective self-regulation](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167217733073) found that being alone tended to lower the intensity of high-arousal positive and negative feelings. Some participants reported benefits in relaxation and stress when the solitude was chosen rather than imposed. This was not a clinical trial of public libraries or a finding that isolation is universally beneficial. It is evidence that the experience of being alone can depend on agency and circumstance.

The distinction matters to the architect. A person who enters a quiet room voluntarily after a crowded shift may experience the absence of demands as relief. Another person who is left in a similar room because nobody has invited them anywhere may experience the same silence differently. The building is identical. The social meaning is not. A design that counts only time alone may confuse two experiences whose human consequences diverge.

In a [2024 study using repeated assessments of people through the day](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12939), Nguyen and colleagues collected thousands of brief reports from hundreds of participants about emotion, solitude and preference. The researchers found that high-arousal negative feelings were associated with a greater preference to be alone at the time, although their lagged analyses did not support every hypothesis about predicting subsequent solitary behavior. That qualification is useful. People may desire solitude when agitated, but preference does not guarantee they will be able to obtain it, or that the setting offered by a public institution will help. The study cannot establish that building more quiet rooms will reduce loneliness across a city.

I would write the proposal around the right to choose rather than the power of silence. A citizen should be able to enter a library and join a program, consult a librarian, use a computer, meet a friend, sit quietly among others, or occupy a modest quiet space without performing a justification for every option. The building would have to accommodate conflicting needs rather than construct a moral hierarchy in which silence is always healthier than conversation.

This creates a more difficult measure of institutional success. Suppose the rooms are in heavy use. A commissioner might celebrate the occupancy rate. But full rooms could indicate that the city has successfully met a need, or that housing and working conditions have made private peace scarce, or that the library has too few other services. Suppose use is modest. The space may still be vital to a small group whose access would otherwise be poor. A room should not be judged solely by whether it resembles a busy restaurant at lunchtime.

One useful evaluation might include a record of demand at different hours, who can get there, the time people can reasonably spend, and whether the design respects accessibility and safety. Visitors could voluntarily report why they came and whether the room was suitable. The institution could compare the new quiet spaces with existing reading areas, parks and other low-cost alternatives. It should also collect complaints from people whose prior uses of the building were displaced. A program that benefits one set of visitors by taking away an essential service from another might still be defensible, but the trade-off deserves an honest account.

There is an interesting labor issue in the scene. The proposal imagines a room in which nobody must serve or entertain the visitor. Yet librarians, custodians, maintenance workers and security staff are making the quiet possible. Somebody opens the door, keeps it safe, repairs a broken fixture, manages conflicts, ensures accessible facilities and closes the building. The apparent simplicity of the room is produced by other people's work. A municipal budget may be tempted to fund a fashionable design while reducing the staff whose judgment makes the space usable. That would repeat a familiar mistake: buying the visible part of a service and treating the human part as overhead.

A room designed around the absence of demand should not become a new source of excessive demands on its workers. Visitors may have urgent needs, conflicts or distress that staff are not trained or funded to handle. Rules cannot avoid every difficult situation. Management should define the facility's purpose and support employees in applying it fairly, with appropriate referral pathways for services outside its scope. This may sound less romantic than a chair beside a window. It is what permits the chair to remain available without making the quiet room a site of neglect.

There is also a conflict around privacy and supervision. A completely enclosed room can offer quiet but make it difficult for staff to know whether someone needs help. A room with extensive glass walls can feel safe to a building manager and exposed to the occupant. Cameras may be proposed as a simple compromise, although they can undermine the very reason a person seeks the space. The architect would have to think carefully about sightlines, alarms, circulation, accessibility, and reasonable staff presence instead of borrowing the surveillance logic of an office lobby.

The imagined institution could test several arrangements in a pilot. One might be an open reading area with acoustic treatment; another a handful of booths; a third a quiet courtyard that can be used in appropriate weather. People could move among them without offering a diagnosis or becoming a participant in a forced wellness program. The library would learn which forms of quiet are actually valued and what each costs to maintain. There is no reason to expect one design to fit every neighborhood.

The most radical rule might be the simplest: do not require the visitor to explain what their time is for. The room can have ordinary rules about safety and fair use. It can close for cleaning. It can ask a person to respect others. But it should not turn every seat into a measurable productivity station. Public institutions already ask citizens to fill out enough forms. Some of the social value of a library lies in allowing people to enter without making an argument about their own worth.

The proposal runs into immediate architectural difficulties. How quiet is quiet? Can visitors bring children? What about telephone calls, mobility aids, sensory needs, safety, accessibility, ventilation, and staff supervision? Should a public facility have a time limit? How would the building remain welcoming to people who need services rather than merely a silent place to sit? Would a room designed for solitude attract the very people who are most socially isolated, and if so would the institution have obligations beyond providing a chair?

A library is not an empty building. It has employees, rules, competing uses, finite space, and a public mission. Silence can be a kindness or a tool of exclusion depending on how it is enforced.

Public libraries already negotiate these questions. Some offer quiet reading rooms; others host children's events, job-search assistance, community meetings, language programs, and services that require conversation. One visitor wants a place to concentrate; another needs help filling out a form; another needs somewhere warm. The same institution cannot satisfy every preference simultaneously. Its value often comes from balancing purposes that private businesses separate into different price categories.

The Library of Solitude is therefore a thought experiment about one additional function, not an attempt to replace everything libraries do.

I imagine an architect being handed a brief: design a place that makes it easier to be alone without making people feel abandoned. The architect cannot solve the problem by surrounding each visitor with walls. Too much enclosure could feel isolating or unsafe. Too much openness could make quiet impossible. Natural light, sightlines, sound absorption, accessible circulation, staff presence, and the ability to choose different levels of privacy would matter. The building might include solitary seats overlooking a shared space, small quiet rooms with transparent safety measures, and places where two people can sit together without being obliged to talk.

A good design would allow different relationships to other people rather than declare one arrangement ideal.

There is a deeper issue in the invitation. What does it mean for a public institution to say that a person's presence is enough? Many contemporary spaces are organized around consumption. A person becomes a customer, an employee, a student, a traveler, a patient, or an attendee. Each role confers a reason to occupy the space. Someone sitting still without purchasing or participating can become a problem for the management team.

The library is one of the few places where this expectation is less absolute. That makes it an unusually useful model for thinking about public life.

It also makes the library vulnerable. Free access requires funding, maintenance, trained staff, materials, utilities, and political support. There is no mysterious process by which a public room pays its own bills merely because the cause is admirable. When budgets tighten, services that lack an easily monetized output can look expendable. The room where nothing happens would be especially difficult to defend using conventional productivity measures.

A politician might ask how many people used it and what benefit they received. The library could count entries, estimate utilization, gather voluntary feedback, and study access patterns. But the most valuable outcome may be deliberately private. A person could spend an hour thinking through a difficult decision, recovering from a long workday, or simply escaping a noisy home. They might not want to report the experience to a dashboard.

A society may have to decide that some benefits do not require a customer satisfaction survey after every visit.

I can already see the attempts to monetize the model. A company would open a premium solitude lounge, sell memberships, add personalized lighting, publish biometric relaxation scores, and introduce a subscription tier offering freedom from notifications. There could be a market for such services. The absurdity is that the products would ask people to pay for escape from systems that spend much of their time demanding attention.

The public version has to resist an equally tempting failure: turning quiet into a compulsory wellness program. Visitors might be asked to complete guided breathing exercises, attend mindfulness sessions, or track mood changes to demonstrate the institution's value. These activities may be useful when chosen voluntarily. They are not substitutes for a place where a person can simply remain unmeasured.

I am not convinced that every human need improves when somebody invents a metric for it.

The relationship between solitude and loneliness is complicated enough to defeat a simple institutional promise. A person experiencing loneliness may seek company, but also feel exhausted or anxious about social participation. A place that permits quiet proximity to others could provide an intermediate experience. It might be easier to sit among people reading than to attend a program that requires introductions. That is a plausible design hypothesis, not an established therapeutic outcome. A city would need to evaluate the place carefully without treating it as a clinical intervention.

A social infrastructure policy can improve the conditions under which relationships form. It cannot manufacture affection or friendship on a schedule.

The same caution applies to the idea that public spaces automatically generate social cohesion. The presence of a park, library, or community center does not guarantee that everyone feels welcome. Rules, transportation, staffing, safety, cultural expectations, and local histories affect who uses a facility. A beautifully designed room may attract people who already have many alternatives while missing those with the greatest need. If the building is distant from transit, inaccessible, uncomfortable, or open only during working hours, its free admission is not the same as practical access.

The institution's ambition must survive the trip to its front door.

This makes location a serious part of the thought experiment. Should the city build one great Library of Solitude in a prominent location or many modest rooms in existing facilities? A central building can offer specialized spaces and staff. Small distributed rooms may be closer to daily life. A school, transit hub, municipal office, or neighborhood library could adapt a portion of its space. But every adaptation competes with existing uses. A room designated for silent sitting might displace a meeting space, a service desk, or an area for children. There is no morally neutral square foot in a crowded public building.

The design process should listen to the people who already depend on the institution, not just to those who would like the new service.

I would want to visit a prototype, though I cannot honestly claim to have visited one. Imagine a modest room inside a functioning neighborhood library. It has several seats separated by enough distance to be comfortable, lighting that can be adjusted without creating glare, and clear signs about noise. Staff can monitor safety without recording what visitors read or think. Access is free. There are time limits when demand is high, but no demand to explain why one came. The experiment records utilization and asks visitors, voluntarily, whether the space meets a need. It also asks people who cannot use it what prevents them from coming.

Some participants may love it. Others may find it too controlled, too silent, or emotionally uncomfortable. A few may prefer the ordinary reading room. The pilot would learn more from those differences than from a beautifully edited video of a single contented visitor.

There is a particularly important accessibility question. Quiet does not mean the same sensory condition for everyone. Some people may benefit from reduced noise; others may need specific accommodations. Mobility, vision, hearing, neurodivergence, and other differences affect the experience of a room. A design that assumes one universal body and one universal comfort level can become exclusionary while advertising serenity.

The library could provide choice rather than enforce a single aesthetic of silence. That would be more complicated. It would also be more humane.

The idea has an economic dimension that is often missed. Crowded housing and long commutes reduce the time and space available for private thought. A person with a room of their own and reliable leisure hours may not need a public retreat. Someone sharing a small apartment or working several jobs has a different relationship to quiet. A free room cannot resolve housing scarcity or labor conditions. It may, however, provide a small and meaningful public resource while larger problems remain.

It would be wrong to make the room a consolation prize for unaffordable housing. It would also be wrong to refuse a useful small intervention because it does not solve the entire economy.

I find the debate more interesting than the building. It reveals which experiences a community considers legitimate reasons to use public resources. Recreation is accepted in some forms, study in others, meetings and programs in others. A space dedicated to undirected attention may appear wasteful because its benefit is difficult to quantify. Yet a public park is not required to produce a specified number of friendships. A sidewalk does not ask pedestrians to submit evidence that the walk was necessary. Infrastructure sometimes exists so that people may decide for themselves what to do with it.

A room can be infrastructure for the mind without pretending to repair the mind.

There is an alternative design that requires almost no new construction. Instead of creating a specialized building, a city could audit existing public places for the availability of quiet, safe, accessible, noncommercial space. It could extend library hours, improve seating, reduce unnecessary noise, maintain parks, provide shade and shelter, and support staff who make people feel welcome. The most effective version might be a network of small corrections to institutions that already exist.

It would have less branding potential than a Library of Solitude. I count that as a possible advantage.

One caution remains. Institutions that appear open to everyone can become sites where marginalized people are scrutinized or excluded. Rules against sleeping, carrying belongings, remaining for long periods, or appearing disorderly may have practical justifications and unfair effects. A genuine public invitation requires attention to how rules are applied. Staff need support, visitors need safety, and decisions should be reviewable. A room cannot be generous merely in its architectural rendering. It has to be generous when a difficult visitor arrives.

There is no design so beautiful that it abolishes conflict.

I imagine a future librarian explaining the program to a council committee. She does not claim that the quiet rooms have cured loneliness. She does not promise a measurable increase in productivity or an economic multiplier that turns every chair into a civic investment miracle. She says that people use the rooms, the cost is known, the rules are workable, and the institution is serving a need that other places do not serve well. She provides evidence where the evidence exists and admits what cannot be measured with confidence.

Some council members will ask why public money should pay for a person to sit still. Another may ask why the city is paying so much to make sitting still difficult everywhere else.

Both questions belong in the hearing.

I like the thought of a public room with no obligation to explain itself every morning. It feels like an old idea that has become futuristic through neglect. A person could enter from the heat, the traffic, the telephone calls, the payments, and the messages. There would be a chair, a window, perhaps a shelf of books, and a rule against making unsolicited demands of the stranger beside you.

Eventually the person would leave. The librarian would turn off the lights. The room would have produced nothing that could be shipped, licensed, or sold.

The city would still have to pay the electricity bill. I hope it would.
