# The Repair Tag

The washing machine does not need a revolution. It needs a bearing.

There is an excellent chance that the owner cannot buy the bearing alone, cannot remove the part that holds it without destroying another component, or cannot find a person willing to perform the repair for a price that makes sense. The machine may contain remarkable electronics. It may have a beautiful interface that recognizes the kind of fabric in the drum. It may send an alert to a phone when the cycle is finished. None of those features will help when a modest piece of metal wears out and the repair procedure requires the removal of half the appliance.

For years, I have thought the real symbol of the future should be a screwdriver. Not a robot carrying one. Just a screwdriver that still fits.

The idea in this chapter is almost insulting in its modesty. Imagine that every durable product comes with a small tag capable of answering a set of questions. What is this? What parts are inside? Which parts can be replaced? Who can replace them? Which tools are needed? What instructions are available? What happens to the remaining materials when the product can no longer do its job? The tag does not have to behave like an advertisement. It should provide useful facts to the person who owns the object, the person who fixes it, and the person who eventually takes it apart.

I imagine pointing a phone at the tag on a washing machine and receiving a diagram that can be understood while standing beside an open panel. The manufacturer has made parts information available to qualified repairers where appropriate. An independent shop can order a component from a supplier. The owner can estimate whether repair is economically reasonable. A recycler can identify materials without guessing. The machine has an identity beyond the last time its warranty was sold.

This is no longer pure invention. The European Union's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, creates a framework for product-specific environmental requirements and digital product passports. The European Commission describes the passport as a means of sharing information relevant to sustainability, circularity, compliance, and potentially materials, safety, and repairability. The requirements are introduced progressively, not imposed identically on every product tomorrow. The EU also adopted a directive on common rules promoting repair, with national application from July 31, 2026. These are real policy efforts, with implementation details and contested practical consequences. They do not mean that every washing machine is now easy to fix.

The relevant primary starting points are the [European Commission's Digital Product Passport page](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/single-market/digital-product-passport_en) and its [Directive on repair of goods](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/directive-repair-goods_en). The important word is framework. A framework provides a way to set rules. It is not itself a repaired bearing.

France has already run a conspicuously down-to-earth experiment in this area. The object on display is not a passport that follows a product through every stage of its life. It is a score beside the price. Beginning in 2021, selected categories of electrical and electronic goods sold in France became subject to a repairability index, scored out of ten. The [French Ministry for Ecological Transition](https://www.ecologie.gouv.fr/politiques-publiques/indice-reparabilite) explains the ingredients: availability of technical documentation, ease of disassembly, spare-part availability, the relative price of replacement parts, and criteria specific to the product category. The detailed score must be accessible to consumers, and the manufacturer or importer is responsible for the calculation and the supporting documentation.

That creates a delightful problem for a marketing department. A product can have a beautiful finish, a sophisticated app, several useful modes and a sticker that says, in effect, a technician may hate this thing. The score does not tell a buyer whether it will break, but it can make the practical consequences of design decisions visible at the point of sale. A product with difficult fasteners or scarce replacement parts can no longer rely exclusively on being admired while it is new. At least in the categories covered, the label asks a question that the ordinary box usually leaves out.

France's rules have evolved in ways that make the original idea more interesting. In 2025 a durability index replaced the earlier repairability index for televisions and household washing machines. According to the [French government's explanation](https://www.ecologie.gouv.fr/politiques-publiques/indice-durabilite), the updated measure incorporates aspects of reliability, resistance to wear and ease of maintenance alongside repairability. This is a meaningful admission. The easiest product to repair may still fail too often. A durable object that requires little attention may be preferable to one that can be dismantled in five minutes but needs the attention every month. Repairability and durability are connected, but they are not synonyms.

There is an unsatisfying arithmetic hidden in a score out of ten. It creates the impression that seven is exactly one unit better than six, and that the numbers can be compared as casually as prices. Yet a score is built from selected criteria and weights. A product may score well because parts are available for many years but remain awkward to disassemble. Another may be easily opened but have expensive modules. How would the score treat a design whose most common failures are impossible to predict? What if spare parts remain theoretically available but routinely take weeks to arrive? A number can discipline purchasing, but the buyer needs the underlying criteria when the failure has a particular shape.

The same question becomes more consequential when public institutions buy equipment. A school or hospital may purchase hundreds of devices and expect to service them for years. A repairability score can inform procurement, but it should sit alongside real service history, contractual spare-parts commitments, security updates where applicable, maintenance staffing and the cost of downtime. A hospital should not infer from a favorable consumer label that a safety-critical device will be easy or appropriate for its own technicians to maintain. The category boundary is doing important work.

I would put a second label beside the score: WHO CALCULATED THIS? The answer in France's system is not generally an independent mechanic appointed to examine every unit. Producers and importers calculate the index under defined rules, with documentation available for control. This is a workable way to scale a system across many products, and also a reason to care about oversight, audits and enforcement. A label that consumers trust can be valuable enough to invite gaming. If a manufacturer finds a way to gain points without making common failures cheaper to fix, the formula must be capable of improvement.

We have seen this problem before in the design of energy and nutrition labels. Making a complex quality legible is useful, but the measure becomes part of the market and therefore an object of strategic behavior. A label can drive genuine redesign or teach companies how to optimize for a number. Neither outcome can be assumed from the existence of a law.

The European passport proposal takes the problem one step farther. Rather than presenting a single score at purchase, it envisages a body of information that different participants may need at different stages. The [EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/ecodesign-requirements-for-sustainable-products.html) establishes a framework; product-specific requirements and phased implementation determine what actually becomes obligatory. A [July 2026 Commission update](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/news/ecodesign-sustainable-products-regulation-moves-implementation-2026-07-02_en) makes the staged approach explicit. Nothing in those statements permits an author to write as if every appliance in the Union already carries a complete, functioning digital passport.

That matters because the passport could become an extraordinarily attractive abstraction. Imagine a tidy database field for every component, raw material and service operation. The diagram would be gorgeous. The real chain of production might contain subcontractors who have changed their software systems, suppliers reluctant to disclose commercially sensitive information, batches that differ, and organizations that do not share a common vocabulary. Even deciding whether a component is the same part after a revision can require technical judgment. If the data are incomplete, a passport may look authoritative while sending a repairer toward the wrong replacement.

The repairer needs a functioning part, not an impressive genealogy of the part.

A credible implementation would have to decide which facts matter to particular users. A consumer wants to know whether a repair is feasible and what it is likely to cost. A recycler wants information about material composition and hazards. A regulator may need evidence of compliance. A professional service technician may need diagrams and safe procedures that would be inappropriate to expose without context. These information needs overlap, but they are not identical. The temptation to put everything into a single visible record has to be balanced against security, privacy and the practical burden of maintaining accurate information.

There may even be a simpler alternative for some products: make standard spare parts, repair manuals and software support reliably available, then let the object live its ordinary life. A universal digital architecture could be unnecessary for a kettle with three easily replaced parts. It might be more valuable for a complex industrial system whose components travel through several owners. An elegant rule is not necessarily one that treats every object as a miniature airline.

I am interested in whether these policies change the decisions made before the product is manufactured. If a designer knows that dismantling, support periods and parts pricing will influence purchasing, a cheap glued seam may start to look more expensive than four screws. A manufacturer might provide a better service arrangement or choose a component that can be replaced without disturbing the rest of the assembly. Those are plausible responses to changed incentives, not claims that every score causes redesign. The effect has to be measured in actual products and repairs.

The washer's bearing is useful because it refuses to become an abstract percentage. At the moment of failure, the owner needs to know whether a technician can obtain the part, safely reach it, fit it and leave the machine functioning at a reasonable total price. If the score and passport help answer that question, they have done something worthwhile. If the paperwork is immaculate but the bearing is inaccessible, the system has become better at describing a failure than preventing it.

Why would a repair tag change anything? Because information has become part of the physical design of an object. A century ago, a mechanic could often understand a machine by inspecting its parts, measuring dimensions, and recognizing a familiar construction. Modern objects can contain software, proprietary diagnostic interfaces, glued or sealed assemblies, security systems, specialized fasteners, and components whose behavior depends on digital authorization. Many products are still quite repairable, and some are not. The difficulty varies enormously. What has changed is that possessing an object does not necessarily give the owner the practical knowledge or tools required to maintain it.

A manufacturer may have a legitimate reason to restrict certain operations. A high-voltage battery requires safety precautions. A medical device must be serviced without compromising its intended function. A connected product can contain security controls intended to prevent malicious modification. A vehicle's safety system cannot be treated as a hobbyist toy. Those concerns are real.

It is also real that safety can become an exceptionally convenient word for a business that would rather sell a complete replacement than a small component. The difficulty is telling the two uses apart.

Suppose an owner brings the broken appliance to an independent repairer. The technician discovers that a failed component can be replaced only as part of a much larger assembly. The replacement part costs more than the machine's remaining economic value. Nobody has to have designed the appliance with the intention of creating waste for the result to be wasteful. Engineers may have optimized production costs, speed of assembly, weight, sealing, or factory reliability. Procurement may have preferred a single supplier. Service systems may have been designed around modules rather than individual parts. The product can be an excellent manufacturing achievement and a poor repair proposition at the same time.

That is what makes repair interesting. It exposes the compromises hidden inside design decisions.

The tag could make the compromises visible. A buyer might compare not only price, appearance, and energy consumption but the probable cost of replacing common wear components. A professional repairer could see whether parts are reasonably available before accepting a job. A public agency purchasing hundreds of units could require service documentation and replacement-part commitments. A product might sell fewer units in the short term and develop a better reputation over a longer life.

But there is a reason manufacturers do not all rush toward that future. A long-lived product changes revenue. A company with a strong recurring business in replacement units or proprietary parts may lose some control. A company that uses a sealed design to maintain performance may face new costs or design constraints. A business that is asked to provide repair information must decide what to publish without surrendering secrets unrelated to repair or creating new security risks. Competitors, counterfeiters, and malicious actors may want access to the same information. Regulation can address some of these tensions, but no rule eliminates them.

And the information on a tag will require maintenance. An instruction manual can become inaccurate after a design revision. A supplier can disappear. A part number can be reused or superseded. A digital service can close. A product passport dependent on a company server may outlive the company. If the whole point is to support products over long lifetimes, the information infrastructure cannot expire when a subscription ends.

The product needs a future that includes the possibility that its manufacturer will not have one.

This is the most interesting design requirement of all. Can the data remain accessible when the business that created it fails, is acquired, changes its strategy, or stops supporting the product? Should there be escrowed repair information, standardized open formats, or a public-interest archive for essential documentation? What are the legitimate limits for security-sensitive materials? Who pays to maintain the archive? A durable machine with a disappearing service manual is not quite durable.

A repair tag is therefore also a proposal about institutional memory. The documentation must travel farther than the marketing campaign.

The economics become stranger with recycling. An object may contain valuable materials but be too expensive to separate into useful streams. A recycler faces labor costs, contamination, transport, safety, and volatile commodity prices. Knowing what is inside the object can help, but it does not guarantee profitable recovery. If a device contains a complicated mixture of materials bonded in difficult ways, the tag may merely give an accurate description of an expensive problem.

A better passport could influence design before recycling. If manufacturers must disclose certain material information and take account of end-of-life outcomes, they may have an incentive to choose assemblies that can be disassembled or materials that can be recovered. They may also face competing demands for durability, low cost, low weight, efficiency, and safety. The answer might be different for an aircraft component, a mattress, and a pair of jeans. The passport is a tool for asking questions that should not receive identical answers.

The environmental claims need similar discipline. Repair does not always yield the largest benefit. A highly inefficient old appliance may consume enough extra energy that replacement could be justified, depending on the age and performance of the units, energy supply, manufacturing impacts, repair cost, and expected lifespan. The embodied environmental costs of making a new product matter, but so do operational impacts. A responsible life-cycle comparison must examine the particular case instead of treating repair and replacement as moral categories.

The tag might help provide the numbers. It cannot decide how a household values cost, reliability, environmental consequences, safety, and inconvenience.

In my imagined future, the tag does something one level more surprising: it records repairs. The appliance acquires a history. A buyer of a used machine can see what has been replaced, whether a technician performed safety-critical work, and perhaps whether a known defect has been addressed. This could make secondhand products more trustworthy. It could also create an industry of forged repair histories, disputes about responsibility, and awkward questions about who owns the record.

The owner might not want the manufacturer to know every time the machine is opened. A repairer might resent a system that reports customer activity to competitors. Information about the location of a product, its use, and its service history can reveal things about the people who own it. A digital passport designed to support circularity does not automatically require surveillance, but careless implementation can create it. The architecture should separate information necessary to repair an object from information that tracks a household's life.

The original concept was a tag that told the owner what was inside the machine. It would be embarrassing if the machine used that tag to tell the manufacturer too much about the owner.

Another consequence is the labor market for repair. Technicians possess practical knowledge that is not reducible to a diagram. They know how a failure sounds, how a component wears, when a discolored contact signals a deeper problem, when a repair will not hold, and when the customer should stop spending money. Making instructions available can support those skills and open some work to new entrants, but it cannot create a skilled workforce overnight. Training, equipment, insurance, licensing where required, access to parts, and business economics all matter.

The future of repair may depend as much on the viability of a small local business as on a large international regulation.

A customer can already face a discouraging calculation. An appliance replacement is available quickly, perhaps with financing and delivery. A repair requires diagnosis, a service appointment, a part, another visit, and a charge that might approach the cost of a new unit. Repair shops can struggle with travel time, staffing, low margins, and the huge range of models. The customer is not necessarily wasteful for choosing replacement. The system has made replacement more convenient.

If a repair tag makes diagnosis and parts ordering easier, it may reduce those frictions. If manufacturers design products with accessible common-wear components, the repair may become faster. If more people choose repair, local services may find a more sustainable volume of work. These are plausible mechanisms, not guaranteed outcomes. An intervention has to change actual costs and incentives rather than simply make repair sound virtuous.

There is even a cultural issue hidden inside the price. A repaired object can carry a kind of dignity. A scratched table, a mended jacket, a machine with a replacement switch may feel more particular to its owner than a new object. It has passed through hands and years. That attachment does not fit easily into standardized environmental metrics. Nor should it be mandatory. People are allowed to be tired of their old toaster.

The more interesting possibility is choice. A consumer who wants to repair should not find the option foreclosed by missing documentation, inaccessible parts, or restrictions that cannot be justified. Another consumer may reasonably replace. A good system reveals the true options.

As the tag becomes common, a peculiar business might emerge: an independent service that tracks the repairability and remaining useful life of household objects without belonging to any one manufacturer. It could help owners choose between repairing, selling, donating, and recycling. It could help institutions manage equipment fleets. It could detect recurring component failures across products, subject to responsible data practices. The service might reveal design problems more quickly than a manufacturer's own warranty process, or it might distort the picture by collecting mostly complaints. It would need to account for that selection bias.

A company could use the same information to plan a more durable product. That is not against commerce. Businesses make money from reliable things, service contracts, replacement parts, and customer loyalty. A manufacturer whose revenue depends on confidence over decades may value repair differently from one whose earnings depend on rapid model turnover. The change is in the business model, not an abstract battle between business and nature.

The tag also opens questions that belong to trade policy. Materials and components cross borders. Documentation must be understandable to people in different countries. An appliance sold internationally may be covered by different requirements and standards. A spare part made far away may be subject to tariffs, delays, or shortages. A digital information system cannot repair a supply chain that has no available component at the other end.

Perhaps the future industry should advertise parts availability as boldly as it advertises features. A machine that can be repaired in theory but requires a six-month wait for a common part is a poor bargain. Availability must have a time dimension. So must the warranty, the service commitment, and the software support period.

In the imagined kitchen, the machine's bearing is failing. The owner scans the tag and receives a diagnosis guide. The guide says what symptoms suggest a bearing problem, how to avoid unsafe work, and what a professional inspection might cost. It offers a part number and a list of repair options. The owner does not need to decipher a private service manual of unknown origin. The repairer arrives with enough information to make an informed estimate, but still has to investigate the actual fault.

Perhaps the repair is worthwhile. Perhaps it is not. The scene ends with a choice made on better terms.

If this sounds less futuristic than a household robot, that is because the most expensive part of the future may be keeping yesterday's inventions useful. A society that constantly replaces functional assets can display extraordinary technical capacity while consuming enormous resources to solve problems it has already solved. Yet an economy that refuses to replace obsolete and inefficient equipment can also impose real costs. The purpose is not to preserve everything. It is to give people the ability to make those distinctions intelligently.

The washer's bearing becomes a small test of that ability.

I still like the screwdriver as an emblem of progress. It is a tool that assumes an object may be opened. It expresses a kind of trust in the possibility of understanding a machine. The person holding it can be a professional, a skilled amateur working within safe limits, or someone trying to discover whether repair is possible before buying a replacement.

A future worth having should leave room for that person.

The tag on the washing machine may eventually become ordinary enough that nobody calls it an innovation. That would be a success. A technician would scan it, identify a part, give an honest estimate, and complete the repair. The owner would do laundry. The machine would not file a report about the household's habits.

Somewhere a product designer would decide to leave four screws accessible instead of hiding them beneath a decorative panel.

It would be difficult to photograph the importance of the decision.
