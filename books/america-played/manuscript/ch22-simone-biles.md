# Chapter 22 — The Right to Stop

The most important American sports moment of the 2020s may be the moment the greatest gymnast in the world did not continue.

That is a strange sentence to place at the end of a book built from winners.

It is also the reason Simone Biles is the provisional selection for a decade that is not finished.

The word provisional matters. The 2020s still have years left. Shohei Ohtani has changed the imaginable shape of a baseball superstar by becoming elite as both hitter and pitcher. Caitlin Clark has accelerated the audience and economics of women’s basketball. Katie Ledecky has extended swimming dominance across an extraordinary span. Stephen Curry’s influence on basketball geometry continues. LeBron James has pushed elite longevity into territory that would have sounded implausible in earlier eras. Coco Gauff, Noah Lyles, Sydney McLaughlin-Levrone, Scottie Scheffler, Patrick Mahomes and others are still writing their cases.

A decade selected before it ends should be allowed to change its mind.

Biles leads because the events from Tokyo through Paris altered something more fundamental than a record. They changed the public argument over what an athlete owes the event.

At the postponed Tokyo Olympics in 2021, Biles arrived as the face of the U.S. team and the most accomplished gymnast of her era. She had already won four gold medals at Rio in 2016 and accumulated a world-championship record that placed her competitive greatness beyond serious dispute. The expected Tokyo story was excess victory: how many more medals, how many more skills, how far beyond everyone else could she go?

The actual story broke during the team final.

On vault, Biles intended an Amanar, a Yurchenko entry with two and a half twists. She completed fewer twists than planned and landed safely enough to walk away, but the error was not an ordinary miss. Her sense of orientation in the air had become unreliable.

Gymnasts call the condition the twisties.

The name sounds almost playful until the mechanics are considered. Elite gymnastics asks a person to leave the ground, rotate around multiple axes, locate the floor without the ordinary reference points available to someone standing upright, and complete the movement within fractions of a second. Biles performs some of the most difficult skills ever attempted in women’s gymnastics. If the internal map connecting mind and body disappears during those rotations, determination is not an adequate substitute.

A basketball player with a shooting slump can keep shooting without risking a broken neck on every attempt. A golfer with the yips can putt badly. A gymnast who cannot reliably locate herself in the air can be in physical danger.

Biles withdrew from the remainder of the team final.

Then she withdrew from the all-around and several apparatus finals while working with medical professionals, coaches, and a sports psychologist. She eventually returned for balance beam, altered the dismount to remove twisting, and won bronze.

The medals were almost beside the point.

The withdrawal produced an immediate cultural argument because sports had trained audiences to recognize only one direction of courage.

Continue.

Play hurt.

Take the shot.

Get back on the field.

Do not let the team down.

Do not let the country down.

Do not waste the moment.

Biles introduced a different possibility at the highest possible stakes: sometimes stopping is the performance of judgment.

That proposition threatened a very old sports mythology. American athletics contains countless admired stories of competitors performing through pain. Some deserve admiration. Tiger Woods winning the 2008 U.S. Open on a damaged leg is astonishing. Willis Reed walking onto the Madison Square Garden floor in the 1970 NBA Finals became legend. Athletes have played through fractures, illness, grief, blood, exhaustion, and fear.

The problem begins when an example becomes a command.

Tiger’s choice does not establish that every injured golfer should continue. Reed’s choice does not settle every basketball player’s medical decision. Courage is not a universal dosage.

Biles’s Tokyo decision separated willingness from capacity.

She wanted to compete. The problem was not that the Olympic final suddenly felt unimportant. The problem was that the physical system required to perform safely was not functioning reliably. Her coach Cecile Landi later described seeing that this was beyond ordinary fear. Biles herself explained that mind and body were not in sync.

Once the situation is described accurately, the moral arithmetic changes.

Continuing would not have proved that she cared more.

It could have proved only that public expectation was stronger than private information.

That distinction is the endpoint of the ownership story running through this book.

In the 1770s, Austin Curtis was a famous jockey whose legal status allowed another person to own him. Abe Hawkins became nationally known while enslaved. Nineteenth-century fighters and jockeys negotiated a sporting economy that could celebrate Black bodies while restricting the people attached to them. Jackie Robinson entered the major leagues under a strategic bargain that required extraordinary control of his reactions. Muhammad Ali asserted authority over his name, religion, political conscience, and military service at enormous professional cost. Billie Jean King helped women players build collective economic power. Michael Jordan transformed athletic identity into commercial property. Serena Williams insisted that a woman champion could control business, motherhood, fashion, anger, departure, and return.

Biles brings the question down to the final second before performance.

Who decides whether the body goes?

The answer can seem obvious until the athlete says no.

The Olympics complicate the pressure because the athlete is wrapped in national identity. A professional league game belongs to a team and season. An Olympic performance arrives once every four years under a flag, after public investment, qualification systems, sponsorship campaigns, television promotion, and years of anticipation. The athlete becomes the visible endpoint of an enormous machine.

That machine has legitimate interests. Teammates depend on one another. Coaches plan lineups. broadcasters pay for events. federations select teams. fans invest emotion. Sponsors invest money. National committees organize logistics. None of those interests is imaginary.

They are also not ownership.

The distinction becomes clearest in a dangerous sport. A gymnast possesses information about her own orientation that no broadcaster, sponsor, fan, or commentator possesses. The people watching can know the stakes. They cannot know the body from inside it.

Athlete autonomy therefore has an epistemic basis as well as a moral one.

Sometimes the athlete knows something the institution cannot know soon enough.

That is why the Tokyo argument mattered outside gymnastics. It was not only a mental-health awareness story, though mental health was central to Biles’s explanation and to the public response. It was also a decision-rights story.

Who holds authority when the person performing has private information about whether performance is safe?

The history of industrial work contains versions of this question. So do aviation, medicine, military command, and other high-risk systems. A healthy safety culture creates channels for the person closest to the hazard to stop the process without first winning a public debate about toughness.

Elite sport has traditionally been ambivalent about that principle because willingness to endure risk is part of competitive selection. The athlete who stops too easily will not reach the top. Coaches train people to operate under fear and fatigue. Teams need reliability.

But a culture that cannot distinguish discomfort from danger eventually rewards concealment.

Biles made the distinction public.

The context of USA Gymnastics made the question heavier.

Biles was a survivor of sexual abuse by former team doctor Larry Nassar. In September 2021, weeks after Tokyo, she testified before the U.S. Senate Judiciary Committee alongside McKayla Maroney, Maggie Nichols, and Aly Raisman about the failures surrounding the investigation of Nassar. Her written testimony explicitly connected the burden of continuing through the postponed Tokyo cycle with the unresolved institutional failures and the abuse she had endured.

This history matters because it changes the moral posture of the sporting institution.

The system asking an athlete to sacrifice for the team had already failed athletes entrusted to its care.

That does not mean every current coach, teammate, official, or federation employee bears personal responsibility for past abuse. It means the institution had lost the right to expect unquestioned deference.

Trust has to be earned in both directions.

For much of twentieth-century gymnastics, especially women’s gymnastics, the ideal champion was very young, highly controlled, obedient to coaches, and treated by outsiders as an extraordinary body before being treated as an adult worker with governance rights. Biles’s long career helped change that image simply through age, success, and public authority.

By Tokyo she was not a child prodigy waiting for adults to explain the meaning of her experience.

She was the sport’s central star saying what the experience meant.

The backlash revealed how unsettled that authority remained.

Some commentators treated withdrawal as quitting. Others argued that a champion should endure pressure because pressure is the definition of championship. The criticism often assumed that the relevant problem was emotional discomfort rather than loss of aerial awareness.

That assumption is revealing.

Sports culture is comfortable with visible injury. A torn ligament can be scanned. A broken bone can be shown. A bleeding wound fits the old script because the public can inspect the evidence and then admire the athlete for trying to continue.

The twisties were less visible.

The athlete had to be believed.

That required a different relationship between audience and performer.

The public was being asked to accept that the person inside the body held information the cameras could not validate.

This is one of the most important changes in modern athlete power.

Direct media makes athletes more capable of describing their own conditions. Sports medicine has become more sophisticated. Players’ associations and independent medical protocols can create institutional support. Fans have greater awareness of concussion, mental health, overtraining, abuse, and long-term injury.

At the same time, the economic pressure to perform has never been greater. Broadcast rights, betting markets, fantasy sports, endorsements, social media, and global fandom create more claims on availability.

The athlete has more voice inside a machine with more reasons to want the athlete on schedule.

Biles’s no tested the balance.

Then Paris changed the meaning of Tokyo.

After an extended break, Biles returned to competition in 2023. At the Paris Olympics in 2024, she won gold with the U.S. women’s team, gold in the individual all-around, gold on vault, and silver on floor exercise. Team USA records her career Olympic total through Paris at eleven medals: seven gold, two silver, and two bronze.

The comeback mattered because it destroyed the easiest criticism of autonomy.

Stopping had not made her soft.

Stopping had not ended greatness.

Stopping had not revealed that she could no longer handle elite competition.

She came back and won the all-around eight years after her Rio title, becoming the first woman to win Olympic all-around titles in nonconsecutive Games.

The sequence forces a re-evaluation of the usual sports narrative.

If she had continued in Tokyo, suffered catastrophic injury, and never returned, sports culture might have called the attempt brave.

If she stopped, recovered, and later returned to dominate, was the stop less brave because it contained self-preservation?

The question sounds strange only because the old mythology treats damage as evidence of commitment.

Paris supplied a different model: preservation can be part of performance.

This does not mean every athlete should withdraw whenever confidence falls. It does not mean a difficult day is dangerous. It does not mean mental health language should become a universal explanation for ordinary competitive failure.

The Biles case is powerful precisely because the condition was specific. She was losing air awareness while performing skills where orientation is fundamental to safety.

Generalizing carelessly would weaken the lesson.

The athlete’s right to stop is not a right to guaranteed applause.

It is a right to make the decision without public ownership of the body overriding private judgment.

Teams can still select other athletes. Contracts can still contain participation expectations. Competitive consequences can still exist. Sport requires commitment.

Autonomy is not consequence-free choice.

It is the recognition that the final decision about whether a person attempts a dangerous physical act cannot belong entirely to spectators.

Biles’s technical importance also stands independently of Tokyo.

She has expanded the difficulty frontier of women’s gymnastics repeatedly. Multiple skills bear her name in the sport’s code because she was the first woman to perform them successfully in international competition. Her combination of power, air awareness, execution, and difficulty altered what elite gymnastics could look like.

This produces a peculiar feedback loop.

The better Biles became at extraordinary aerial skills, the more dangerous a loss of aerial awareness became.

Her greatness increased the cost of pretending the twisties were ordinary nerves.

A gymnast attempting simpler movements might have more room to reduce difficulty and continue. Biles’s routines were built around skills at the edge of the sport’s technical map.

The very capacities that made the public expect invulnerability made vulnerability more consequential.

That paradox appears across elite sport. The fastest pitcher creates stresses ordinary arms do not experience. The most explosive basketball player lands from heights ordinary players do not reach. The golfer generating exceptional speed loads joints differently. The gymnast capable of unprecedented rotations operates with less margin for spatial error.

Superhuman performance can require unusually human caution.

The Paris comeback also challenges the historical model of women’s gymnastics as a sport of disappearing teenagers.

Biles won the 2024 Olympic all-around at twenty-seven. Suni Lee, Rebeca Andrade, and other adult gymnasts competed at the highest level in an era where athletes increasingly have access to improved training, medical support, professional earning opportunities, and more authority over career timing.

Biles did not cause all of those changes.

She made the possibility impossible to ignore.

Longevity shifts power because an adult champion with financial independence, public credibility, and years of experience can negotiate differently from a minor whose entire pathway is controlled by coaches and federations.

Age is governance capacity.

The modern athlete can know the sport well enough to disagree with it.

This is especially significant in a discipline whose abuse scandals revealed the dangers of obedience cultures. An athlete safety system that depends on young people never challenging authority is not safe.

Biles’s public authority became part of the repair, even though no individual survivor should be asked to fix the institutions that failed her.

Her Senate testimony in 2021 makes that boundary clear. The athlete’s voice can demand accountability. It cannot substitute for competent institutions.

That is a useful limit on the whole book.

We keep choosing one person per decade because a person gives history a shape. The danger is pretending the person produced the entire change.

Austin Curtis did not create colonial racing. Bill Richmond did not create transatlantic boxing. Jackie Robinson did not integrate baseball alone. King did not create the women’s movement. Magic did not rescue the NBA alone. Jordan did not globalize basketball alone. Tiger did not modernize golf alone. Serena did not transform women’s tennis alone.

Biles did not create athlete autonomy.

She made a particular version of it undeniable.

The decade’s strongest challenger may eventually be Shohei Ohtani.

His two-way excellence has reopened a possibility Major League Baseball had treated as nearly impossible at the highest modern level: one player operating as both elite hitter and elite pitcher. His value touches roster design, global media, U.S.-Japan sports relations, baseball strategy, free-agency economics, and the definition of a baseball superstar. If he sustains that influence through the rest of the decade, this book may need a new final chapter.

Patrick Mahomes has another claim because quarterback is the highest-leverage position in America’s most commercially powerful league, and a run of championships can shape an NFL era. Caitlin Clark’s effect on women’s basketball audiences, ticket demand, media attention, and league economics is still developing and could become one of the decade’s clearest examples of superstar acceleration. Katie Ledecky’s dominance and longevity create a different case built on sustained excellence rather than institutional rupture.

A book that calls its current-decade choice final in 2026 would be betraying its own method.

Importance is downstream consequence.

The downstream is still arriving.

Biles leads because Tokyo and Paris have already completed a rare causal sequence.

The old assumption was visible.

The athlete challenged it.

The challenge carried immediate cost and controversy.

The athlete later returned.

The return supplied evidence about the challenge.

The culture’s baseline moved.

After Biles, every major discussion about an athlete withdrawing for mental, neurological, orientation, or safety reasons exists in a different historical environment. Critics can still disagree. Teams can still make hard decisions. Fans can still feel disappointed.

But the simple equation between continuation and courage has lost some authority.

That is a change.

It also reaches beyond women’s sports. Quarterbacks discussing concussion symptoms, baseball players leaving for mental-health treatment, tennis players stepping away from media pressure, Olympic athletes discussing depression, and veterans managing dangerous pain all operate in a sports culture where human limits are more speakable than they were.

Biles is not the cause of every case.

She became one of the most visible permission structures.

Permission structures are hard to measure because their evidence appears in other people’s choices. A younger athlete hears the greatest gymnast alive say she could not safely continue and understands that stopping did not erase the rest of the career. A coach knows the public has seen an example of a champion being believed. A federation knows that dismissing the athlete’s account can become a governance issue rather than merely a performance issue.

The precedent changes the negotiation before the next crisis occurs.

That is institutional importance even without a new written rule.

The commercial system adapted too. One fear surrounding modern athlete candor is that sponsors only want invulnerability. Biles’s continued prominence after Tokyo showed that brands and audiences can value honesty, resilience, and return without requiring a perfect champion narrative.

That does not make sponsorship benevolent. Companies follow incentives. It means the market discovered another version of star value.

Vulnerability did not automatically destroy the brand.

This matters because commercial punishment has historically disciplined athlete identity. King lost endorsement support after being outed. Other stars learned to protect carefully engineered public selves. Jordan’s commercial universality created incentives for caution. Tiger’s private-life scandal showed how quickly an identity-based brand could fracture.

Biles’s era contains a different possibility: candor can be part of credibility.

Again, there is no universal formula. Some disclosures will cost money. Some audiences will react harshly. The point is that the athlete-brand can now include limits without ceasing to be valuable.

That is a subtle extension of ownership.

The athlete no longer has to sell only the fantasy that the body is endlessly available.

Paris matters because the return was not marketed as a correction of a moral failure. Biles did not need to prove Tokyo had been wrong. She returned to gymnastics under conditions she described as more centered on doing the sport for herself. The competitive result happened to be overwhelming.

The distinction should survive even if she had lost.

If the only way to vindicate self-protection is to come back and win gold, then autonomy remains conditional on extraordinary performance. Most athletes who make a correct safety decision will not later win an Olympic all-around.

Biles’s principle cannot depend on her being Biles.

That may be the hardest part of using great athletes as historical examples. Greatness creates attention but can distort the lesson. The exceptional person survives choices an ordinary worker might be punished for making. The star has money, lawyers, sponsors, and alternative opportunities that a marginal roster player lacks.

The real measure of progress is whether the athlete with less leverage can make the same safety call.

Biles opened the argument.

Institutions have to decide whether to generalize the respect.

That challenge is unfinished, like the decade.

The story also forces one final reconsideration of what “player” means in the title of this book. Gymnasts do not play a game in the ordinary sense. Neither do sprinters or boxers. “Sports player” is being used here in the broad human sense: the person who enters a bounded contest and accepts a public measurement of performance.

For two hundred and fifty years, America has loved those people partly because the contest seems to settle something cleanly.

First across the line.

Most runs.

Knockout.

Lowest score.

Highest total.

Gold medal.

The score gives society relief from ambiguity.

But the stories behind the scores have never been clean.

An enslaved jockey can win.

A free Black fighter can become famous abroad while racial hierarchy persists at home.

A baseball league can call itself major while excluding major talent.

A nation can cheer a Black Olympian against Nazi racism while maintaining segregation.

A champion can serve a segregated military.

A pioneer can integrate a roster without integrating ownership.

A heavyweight can lose his title because he refuses the draft.

A woman can win the spectacle while the important work happens in a meeting room.

A basketball player can become a global brand and still discover that commercial universality creates its own limits.

A golfer can expand a sport while representation outruns access.

A tennis champion can control her brand yet still have the public argue over her body.

A gymnast can reach the Olympic floor and decide the score is not worth the risk of taking off.

The scoreboard tells us what happened in the contest.

The athlete tells us what the contest was allowed to ask.

That may be the most important change across the entire book.

At the beginning, the athlete could be property.

At the end, the athlete can say the performance is not.
