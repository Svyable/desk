# Chapter 2 — Americans in the London Ring

In the first decade of the nineteenth century, one of the most important American athletes was making his name in England.

That sounds like a problem only if national sports history is treated as a story that must stay inside national borders. It never has. Athletes travel toward money, competition, freedom, safety, audiences, and opportunity. Their movement is part of the story.

Bill Richmond was born enslaved on Staten Island in 1763. During the Revolutionary era he came into the orbit of Hugh Percy, a British officer who took him to England. There Richmond eventually built a life that would have been almost unimaginable from the circumstances of his birth: cabinetmaker, publican, boxer, trainer, and respected figure in the London prizefighting world.

The decade belongs to him not because he fought for an American championship. There was no such stable national institution to win. It belongs to him because Richmond shows an early version of a pattern that will recur throughout this book: America produces an athlete whom America’s own institutions are not yet equipped to contain, so the athlete’s significance becomes visible somewhere else.

In London, prizefighting existed in a strange social position. It was violent, frequently illegal or quasi-legal, and deeply entangled with gambling. It could also attract aristocratic patrons and enormous public interest. Bare-knuckle fights were tests of endurance as much as punching power. Rounds ended when a fighter went down, and contests could continue through dozens of rounds. The ring mixed brutality with strategy, spectacle with patronage, and class performance with real physical danger.

Richmond became exceptional at navigating that world.

He was smaller than many of the men he faced. His reputation rested on intelligence, speed, defense, and tactical movement. Later generations of boxing fans would recognize the type immediately: the fighter who refuses to cooperate with an opponent’s preferred geometry. Richmond’s skill made raw size less decisive. He also became a teacher of the craft, which matters because sporting importance is not only what one athlete wins. It is what knowledge survives through other bodies.

The easiest way to write Richmond would be as a racial pioneer whose talent conquered prejudice. That is only partly true and therefore not good enough.

He encountered racism. He also benefited from unusual patronage. He succeeded in a British sporting culture that was not egalitarian but offered a particular kind of opening to Black fighters because boxing audiences could turn racial difference into spectacle. Admiration and objectification could occupy the same seat. A Black fighter might be praised for courage and skill while being discussed in language that denied full equality.

That tension is familiar from Austin Curtis. The difference is that Richmond had legal freedom and, over time, meaningful professional agency. He could turn fighting knowledge into a livelihood. He could cultivate patrons. He could become a trainer. He could help shape another American-born Black boxer whose fame would briefly exceed his own.

That man was Tom Molineaux.

Molineaux takes the 1810s.

He was born into slavery in Virginia and later reached England, where he entered the London prize ring and became one of the most discussed fighters of his moment. By 1810 he had earned a chance to face Tom Cribb, the English champion. The fight became something larger than two men exchanging blows. It became a national and racial drama in which an American-born Black challenger threatened the symbolic possession of British sporting masculinity.

The first Cribb-Molineaux fight, in December 1810, ran thirty-five rounds. Accounts from the period and later histories disagree about several details, as fight narratives often do. Crowds pressed close. Rules were imperfectly enforced. Molineaux at points appeared capable of winning. The contest ended with Cribb victorious, but the manner of the ending generated controversy and gave the rematch a reason to exist.

The second fight in 1811 drew a vast crowd by the standards of the era. Molineaux lost again.

If this book were ranking athletes by championships alone, those losses would end his case. He did not become the recognized champion. He did not return to the United States with a belt. He did not build a clean dynasty.

But importance is not the same as possession of the title.

Molineaux made a Black American body the center of an international sporting argument before the United States had a durable professional sports system of its own. He became news because he could plausibly beat the English champion. He showed that an athlete could cross the Atlantic and carry national meaning with him even when his own country had denied him basic human freedom.

There is an almost unbearable irony in that. Molineaux could be treated as “the American” in a British ring even though American law and custom had once treated him as property. National identity became available when it was useful to the spectacle.

Sport does this repeatedly. It can grant symbolic citizenship faster than political institutions grant actual equality.

Fans embrace “our” athlete. Newspapers claim “our” champion. Flags appear. National character is projected onto shoulders, fists, stride, composure, and pain tolerance. The athlete becomes a representative even when the represented nation has not represented the athlete.

That pattern will reach enormous scale with Jesse Owens in Berlin in 1936. The United States will celebrate a Black runner as evidence against Nazi racial ideology while maintaining segregation at home. Joe Louis will become a national symbol against Max Schmeling and then serve in a military that still segregates troops. Native American Jim Thorpe will be marketed as an American athletic ideal after being formed inside a federal boarding-school system designed to erase Indigenous cultures.

Richmond and Molineaux are early versions of the same contradiction.

Their stories also force another correction. Boxing history is often told as a chain of heavyweight champions, and sports history as a chain of institutional milestones. But the world before stable leagues was held together by people who could transfer technique.

Richmond’s later importance as a trainer is therefore not a footnote. He helped prepare Molineaux. He understood the English ring, its tactics, patrons, customs, and dangers. Migration created a knowledge bridge. One Black American-born fighter who had learned to survive and prosper in London helped another Black American-born fighter challenge the British champion.

That is a sporting network before anyone would have used the phrase.

The network did not guarantee success. Molineaux’s career after the Cribb fights was uneven. Fame did not become permanent security. Prizefighting was punishing, volatile, and dependent on the body remaining useful. Athletes could become public property in one sense and economically precarious in another.

That, too, is modern.

The sports industry has always been better at monetizing the athlete’s peak than financing the athlete’s whole life.

Richmond managed the transition better than many. His roles as trainer and publican let him remain connected to the sport after his own prime. He became part of London sporting society rather than disappearing the moment he could no longer fight at elite level.

Molineaux’s arc was harsher. The spectacular challenger became a reminder that celebrity can be intense and brief. The same public that turns an athlete into an event can move on quickly once the event ends.

Why, then, choose Richmond for the 1800s and Molineaux for the 1810s rather than treating them as one chapter of Black boxing history?

Because the distinction reveals two different kinds of importance.

Richmond represents institution-building from below. He turns individual skill into professional standing, relationships, teaching, and continuity. He is not simply a great fighter. He becomes sporting infrastructure.

Molineaux represents the event that exceeds the institution. His challenge to Cribb becomes a public drama so large that the outcome matters to people who are not studying footwork. His importance is concentrated in the confrontation itself: the possibility that the established racial and national order could be contradicted by a result everyone could see.

Sport’s political power often comes from that kind of visibility.

A law can declare hierarchy. A newspaper can repeat hierarchy. A custom can enforce hierarchy. Then two people enter a measurable contest and the wrong person, from the hierarchy’s point of view, is plainly capable of winning.

This does not destroy prejudice. Jack Johnson’s victory over Jim Jeffries in 1910 will prove the opposite; a sporting result can intensify racial violence as easily as it can dissolve stereotypes. But performance creates a stubborn form of evidence. It may be misinterpreted, suppressed, resented, or turned into myth, but it has happened in public.

Molineaux’s threat mattered because he could not be dismissed as an abstraction.

There is a danger in using modern racial categories too casually across centuries. Richmond and Molineaux lived in societies whose language, legal structures, and ideas about race were specific to their time. Their British careers do not mean that Britain had escaped the racial order of empire or slavery. Their American origins do not make them representatives of a unified Black American sporting community that did not yet exist in later institutional form.

The safest claim is narrower: both men were American-born, both had been enslaved, both became internationally important prizefighters in England, and their careers exposed the porous boundary between racial exclusion and sporting fascination.

That is enough to make them central to the national story.

The location also helps clarify what “American sports” means in a book spanning 250 years. It means sports as lived by people shaped by the United States, not only events conducted on U.S. soil. Duke Kahanamoku will later carry Hawaiian surfing around the world. American basketball will become global through players, broadcasts, shoes, and Olympic competition. Michael Jordan’s importance cannot be understood within Chicago city limits. Serena Williams’s audience will be planetary. The sports nation is a network, not a fence.

Richmond crossed that network before there was much of a network to cross.

He also complicates the idea that the earliest Black sports history is only a story of people excluded from white competition. Richmond fought white opponents. Molineaux challenged the leading white English champion. The question was not always whether interracial competition could physically occur. Often it was what the culture would permit the result to mean.

That distinction becomes critical in American boxing.

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, boxing’s “color line” would be enforced more deliberately around heavyweight title opportunities. Jack Johnson would spend years being denied a shot at the championship despite being a leading contender. When he finally won the title in 1908, white America’s search for a “Great White Hope” would become a national obsession. The barrier was not created because Black fighters were unknown. It was created because their ability was known too well.

Richmond and Molineaux belong to the prehistory of that fear.

Their existence shows that Black excellence in elite fighting did not suddenly emerge with Johnson. It had been visible for a century.

There is another reason prizefighters matter so early in this book. Boxing is unusually efficient at producing individual celebrity. Team sports distribute causality. Horse racing pairs human and animal. A prizefight simplifies the poster: two names, two bodies, one result. That simplicity makes it ideal for storytelling and gambling. It also makes the boxer an unusually exposed symbol.

If a baseball team loses, blame can travel across nine players and a manager. If a boxer loses, the defeat sits on one person’s face.

If a boxer wins when a society has invested racial meaning in his defeat, the victory is equally difficult to diffuse.

Molineaux discovered that long before motion pictures made boxing a mass visual medium.

The surviving accounts of the Cribb fights are messy enough to remind us that spectatorship itself changes history. People saw different things. Partisans interpreted the same round differently. Later writers shaped the contest into cleaner narratives. A fight that lasts dozens of rounds invites memory to become selective.

That is not a reason to abandon the story. It is a reason to resist false precision.

We know the stakes were large. We know Molineaux was a credible challenger. We know the fights drew enormous interest. We know racial language saturated the way he was discussed. We know Richmond was part of his preparation and that both men occupied significant places in British prizefighting.

We do not need to invent a perfect blow-by-blow to understand what changed.

After Richmond and Molineaux, an American sports history that begins Black athletes only with twentieth-century integration is impossible to defend.

The story was already transatlantic.

It was already commercial.

It was already racialized.

It was already fascinated by the transformation of one body into a public argument.

And it was already producing a second career that modern athletes would recognize: the former competitor who becomes coach, adviser, host, connector, and keeper of the sport’s practical memory.

Richmond’s life is especially useful because it refuses the narrow definition of victory. His greatest contribution may not be a single bout. It may be the durable position he built inside a sporting ecosystem that had no obvious place waiting for him.

Molineaux’s contribution is the opposite. He is remembered because the title fight was almost too large to contain him. His chance to beat Cribb turned him into a vessel for anxieties that had little to do with how a punch was thrown.

One built a place.

The other shook one.

Together they establish the pattern for the century ahead. American athletes will not merely compete. They will travel into institutions, markets, and identities that are larger than the contest. Their importance will often be visible in the reaction they provoke.

The score will matter.

The story around the score will matter more.

## The challenger test: infrastructure versus the event

The nineteenth century had no ESPN to tell us which athlete mattered most on December 31. Richmond and Molineaux therefore force a more useful question than “who had the better résumé?”

What kind of influence are we trying to measure?

Richmond’s strongest case is not a championship. The National Portrait Gallery in London describes him as one of the most accomplished and respected boxers of the early nineteenth century, but the biography becomes more interesting after the fighting line. He becomes a trainer. He builds a boxing academy. His celebrity carries him into elite public life. He becomes a teacher of the sport rather than merely a user of it.

That makes Richmond an unusually early example of athletic knowledge becoming institutional capital.

If the player’s only value disappears when the legs slow, the sports economy has consumed the body and moved on. If technique, judgment, relationships, reputation, and teaching survive the competitive peak, the athlete can become part of the machinery that produces the next generation.

Richmond does that.

His case for the 1800s therefore defeats a hypothetical rival with more individual victories but less downstream effect. A better fighter can be less important if the fighter leaves no new route, no durable school of knowledge, no network, and no public model that changes what comes next.

Molineaux wins the 1810s for almost the opposite reason.

His two championship challenges become too large to remain private athletic events. The spectacle creates the importance. Contemporary racial and national anxiety attaches itself to the possibility that an American-born Black challenger could become champion of England. The fight is valuable precisely because the public believes the outcome might say something about a hierarchy the sport cannot logically settle.

That mismatch—between what a contest can prove and what spectators ask it to prove—will recur for the next two centuries.

Jesse Owens can win four gold medals without defeating racism.

Joe Louis can knock out Max Schmeling without defeating fascism.

Billie Jean King can beat Bobby Riggs without proving every proposition about sex and athletic performance.

The result is narrower than the story.

The story still changes culture.

Molineaux is our first clear transatlantic example of that mechanism.

The location matters economically too. The best competitive market available to an American-born Black fighter was not necessarily in the United States. That is a reminder that talent markets are shaped by institutions, not national romance. Athletes move when opportunity is unevenly distributed.

Modern fans recognize the pattern in a different form. Baseball players move across the Pacific and the Caribbean. Basketball players leave home for American colleges or professional leagues abroad. Soccer players cross continents before adulthood. Boxers train in cities far from where they were born. Tennis players build transnational coaching and residency arrangements. The nation still claims the champion after the labor market has already become global.

Richmond and Molineaux were doing a primitive version before modern sports migration had a name.

Their careers also expose the role of patronage before salaries become standardized. A league contract creates a visible exchange: labor for compensation under rules. Prizefighting depended on a messier ecology of backers, bettors, taverns, aristocrats, promoters, trainers, and side wagers. That made relationships themselves a form of capital.

Richmond learned the ecology.

Molineaux entered it with Richmond’s guidance.

The trainer therefore did not simply teach a left hand or a defensive move. He translated the institution.

Where do you fight?

Who can make the match?

Which patrons matter?

What customs govern the ring?

How does a foreign-born Black challenger become legible to an English audience?

Those are professional questions, not merely athletic ones.

The chapter’s two-decade structure lets us watch the network produce an event. Richmond builds a place inside the sport. Molineaux uses that place to reach the champion. The first athlete’s importance is accumulated; the second athlete’s is concentrated.

This is why a single ranking formula would fail.

One kind of greatness compounds quietly.

Another detonates in public.

Both belong in a history of importance.

The archive again demands restraint. The first Cribb-Molineaux fight has accumulated disputed details about rounds, interruptions, long counts, and crowd conduct. The exact sequence is less important than later boxing mythology often suggests. The secure facts already do the work: Molineaux was a credible American-born Black challenger, Richmond trained him, the fights drew enormous attention, and their racial and national meaning was understood by contemporaries.

The story does not improve when uncertain details become certain through repetition.

That is another kind of sporting discipline.

Do not chase the anecdote when the mechanism is already visible.

Richmond and Molineaux show that American sports history had become international before it became nationally organized.

The athlete crossed the ocean before the league crossed the continent.

And long before the modern sports industry learned to call a coach, promoter, manager, and media strategist different professions, these two men were already living inside the network those professions would later formalize.
