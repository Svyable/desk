# Chapter 7 — The First Derby

The first Kentucky Derby was won by a Black jockey.

This is the kind of fact that sounds as though it has been selected for surprise. It has not. It is simply the beginning.

On May 17, 1875, Oliver Lewis rode Aristides to victory at the new Louisville Jockey Club course in front of roughly ten thousand spectators. Lewis was nineteen years old. Aristides was a chestnut colt trained by Ansel Williamson, a Black trainer who had been born into slavery. The race would grow into one of the most durable spectacles in American sport. Its first winning rider and trainer were Black.

The 1870s belong to Oliver Lewis because the first Derby is not merely a famous race. It is a hinge between two sporting Americas.

Behind it is the older racing world in which Black horsemen had long supplied elite expertise under slavery. Ahead is a commercial national sporting culture that will build institutions, traditions, statistics, recurring championships, and branded annual events. The Derby becomes one of the clearest examples of a sports property whose continuity itself creates value.

Lewis was there at creation.

The race in 1875 was not yet “the Derby” in the modern sense, a two-minute national ritual wrapped in television, fashion, sponsorship, breeding economics, celebrity, and accumulated mythology. It was a new event modeled partly on European classics and organized by a Louisville institution trying to establish prestige.

That makes Lewis’s victory more historically interesting than winning an already-established crown.

He helped establish the crown.

Every recurring sporting event has a first edition when nobody knows whether there will be a hundredth. The tradition is only an experiment with good marketing. Somebody has to make the first memory.

Lewis and Aristides made it.

The story also interrupts the easiest Reconstruction narrative.

The Civil War had ended a decade earlier. Slavery was abolished. Constitutional amendments had formally transformed citizenship. Black political participation expanded in parts of the South and then met violent resistance. The country was attempting, unevenly and temporarily, to construct a post-slavery order.

Racing offers a peculiar window into that transformation because Black expertise did not need to be invented after emancipation. It was already there.

Ansel Williamson did not become knowledgeable because the Thirteenth Amendment told him he could. Black riders did not suddenly learn horses when slavery ended. Emancipation changed the legal ownership of people, not the distribution of expertise accumulated over generations.

The postwar racing economy therefore depended on people whose knowledge had been developed under the old system.

Oliver Lewis belonged to the first generation able to enter elite American racing without being legally owned.

That is a distinction worth holding beside Abe Hawkins. Hawkins had been famous while enslaved and continued riding after freedom. Lewis entered national fame after slavery. The sport looks continuous across the political rupture because human knowledge is continuous.

Institutions change faster than skill traditions.

The first Derby also demonstrates how quickly a country recovering from civil war resumed building spectacles.

Sport is often described as an escape from politics, but “escape” is too passive. Recurring events help normalize time. They create calendars. They tell people there will be another spring, another season, another race. The annual championship becomes a civic metronome.

In 1875 the United States was still arguing violently about Reconstruction. Yet Louisville could stage a horse race intended to recur, and spectators could invest attention in an institution designed for a future.

That future orientation is part of sports’ social power.

A war is historical rupture. A league schedule is promised repetition.

The Derby would eventually become repetition with almost absurd durability.

But the origin contains an irony. Black horsemen were central to the event’s first decades, and later popular imagery of the Derby would largely forget them.

Black jockeys won fifteen of the first twenty-eight runnings. That is not token participation. It is competitive dominance over a meaningful span of the event’s early history.

Then Black riders were pushed out of the top level of American racing.

By the early twentieth century, segregation, violence, exclusion by white jockeys and owners, and the hardening of Jim Crow had transformed opportunity. Jimmy Winkfield’s consecutive Derby victories in 1901 and 1902 would come near the end of an era. Black jockeys who had helped define elite American racing became exceptions in a sport their predecessors had helped build.

Oliver Lewis therefore represents more than a first.

He represents a fork the sport did not have to take.

One possible future was visible at Churchill Downs before the course carried that name in the public imagination: Black riders and trainers operating at the highest level of a national sporting institution.

The later exclusion was not inevitable. It was a choice produced by power.

That is an important correction to the comforting language of “progress.” American sports history is not a staircase in which each decade becomes more inclusive than the last. Openings can close. Rights can be narrowed. Institutions can become more exclusionary after periods of participation.

The Derby’s first decades make that visible.

Lewis’s own career is difficult to reconstruct with the richness available for later athletes. He does not have a preserved archive of interviews, endorsements, autobiographies, and game film. His fame is concentrated in the race that survived him.

That unevenness presents a methodological challenge.

Is the most important athlete of a decade the person with the most historically consequential moment, or should the title require a long documented career?

For the 1870s, the event wins.

Lewis’s case is like a photograph with a long exposure. One victory captures several transformations at once: the creation of the Kentucky Derby, the post-emancipation continuity of Black horsemanship, the emergence of recurring national sports institutions, and the later erasure that makes the original lineup surprising.

A longer career would not necessarily reveal more.

It is also important not to make Lewis carry more symbolism than the evidence allows. We do not need to imagine him thinking about Reconstruction as he approached the starting line. We do not know whether he considered himself part of a racial breakthrough. We do not know how he would have described the future significance of the event.

He was a jockey trying to win a race.

The symbolic meanings belong to the institutions and history around him.

That separation protects athletes from being flattened into lessons.

Jackie Robinson understood the integration project surrounding him because Branch Rickey explicitly recruited him for it and the press made it unavoidable. Billie Jean King understood the gender politics of her career and organized around them. Lewis left us no comparable body of public argument.

His importance does not require one.

The result itself entered an institutional history that outgrew everybody involved.

This is another way sports create immortality. The annual event keeps replaying its origin. Every new Derby winner is placed into a list that begins with Aristides and Lewis. The continuity gives the first result renewed relevance each year.

Records are memory machines.

Baseball will become obsessive about them. The Olympics will build entire identities around them. Television graphics will turn statistical lineage into instant story. A new champion is never merely new; the broadcast tells us who last did the thing, who first did it, how long the drought lasted, what record was broken.

Recurring sports institutions make history commercially useful.

The Derby’s first winner therefore never entirely disappears, even when the human story around him fades.

That distinction between record survival and person survival is one of the book’s recurring concerns.

Oliver Lewis survives as a line: 1875, Aristides, Oliver Lewis.

A line is enough to prove presence. It is not enough to explain a life.

The historian’s job is to resist treating the lack of personal detail as lack of importance.

We know the competitive context. Aristides was trained by Williamson. The field contained fifteen horses. Lewis brought the colt home first. The crowd was substantial. The event became permanent.

We also know that the early Derby was saturated with Black skill. That wider pattern prevents Lewis from being dismissed as a one-off accident.

He was one representative of a strong professional community.

The community itself developed under conditions that should make admiration complicated. Much of the knowledge originated in plantation and stable labor performed by enslaved people. Emancipation opened legal freedom but did not erase economic dependency or racism. Black horsemen could be experts in a sport whose wealthy ownership remained largely white.

Again the central question is who controls the value created by athletic labor.

Lewis had more legal agency than Austin Curtis had possessed in the 1770s. He still competed inside an industry where ownership of capital, horses, tracks, and press determined most of the surrounding economics.

The structure is recognizable today in a different form. Athletes can become extremely wealthy while leagues, team owners, media companies, sponsors, and platforms own much of the infrastructure around attention.

Power is relative, not binary.

The twentieth century will produce athletes with vastly greater individual earnings and bargaining power. Michael Jordan will transform endorsement economics. Tiger Woods will become one of the most valuable commercial figures in sport. Serena Williams will invest, build businesses, and speak directly to audiences. Yet even the richest star operates within institutions that schedule, sanction, distribute, and monetize competition.

Lewis stands near the beginning of the long migration from athlete as labor input toward athlete as partial owner of the attention economy.

The Derby also matters because racing was beginning to formalize itself in ways that prefigure modern leagues. Courses, clubs, stakes races, breeding registries, published results, and recurring schedules created a network of recognized competition.

Institutionalization changes what greatness means.

In Austin Curtis’s era, reputation could be powerful but records were fragmentary. By Lewis’s era, the sport was better able to preserve winners. The event had a name, a date, a field, a purse, and an intended future.

The athlete could now be entered into an archive designed to continue.

This is a major technological change even though no machine is involved.

Standardization is a technology of memory.

A league table, a race record, a bracket, a stopwatch, a box score, and a ranking system let performances separated by time become comparable. They create the possibility of statistics, arguments, anniversaries, and records.

The Derby gave Lewis an institutional afterlife that many earlier athletes lacked.

It also gave Louisville a civic identity that grew around sport. Cities increasingly learned that recurring sports could function as economic and cultural anchors. A famous event brings visitors, gambling, hospitality, media attention, and status. It produces an annual moment when the place itself becomes part of the broadcast.

Louisville and the Derby would become nearly inseparable in the national imagination.

That relationship begins with a Black jockey crossing the line first.

There is another possible 1870s selection: one of the stars of the first openly professional baseball clubs, or a great rower, boxer, or other jockey. Baseball’s National Association had begun professional league play. The Cincinnati Red Stockings had made professionalism visible in the previous decade. The sport was building the institutional structure that would eventually eclipse racing in national memory.

Choosing Lewis is therefore also a judgment about historical timing.

Baseball’s future dominance should not be projected backward too early.

In 1875 horse racing remained a major national sport with deep history, enormous betting interest, and elite social significance. The first Derby was an important institutional creation. The fact that modern readers are more likely to know the names of nineteenth-century baseball players says as much about baseball’s later archival power as it does about contemporary importance.

Sports history can become winner-take-all after the fact. The sport that wins the twentieth century gets to rewrite the nineteenth as its prelude.

Lewis resists that.

He keeps us on the track long enough to see what America was actually watching.

His victory also produces one of the book’s sharpest contrasts with the next decades.

The 1880s will give us John L. Sullivan, perhaps the first American sports celebrity whose fame feels fully national in a modern sense. The 1890s will give us Willie Simms, another Black jockey at the absolute top of racing. Then Jack Johnson will bring race and heavyweight boxing into explosive confrontation.

The sporting nation is becoming more connected, more commercial, and more capable of turning athletes into symbols.

Lewis arrives at the moment recurring institutions become part of that machine.

He does not need to have foreseen it.

The first person through a door rarely knows what the building will become.

The Kentucky Derby would become an American ritual.

Oliver Lewis won it before it was one.
