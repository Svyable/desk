# Chapter 5 — The Black Prince

The most important American athlete of the 1850s was famous enough to have nicknames and powerless enough to be owned.

Abe Hawkins was called the Black Prince, the Dark Sage, and, in one wonderfully specific newspaper phrase preserved by racing historians, the Slayer of Lexington.

He was a jockey.

He was also enslaved.

The contradiction is no longer surprising by this point in the book, which is itself a measure of how badly the conventional chronology has trained us. Austin Curtis had been a celebrated enslaved jockey before the Revolution. Cato rode one of the most remembered races of the 1830s. Black horsemen were not side characters wandering into a white sport. They were part of the labor, intelligence, and athletic excellence that made American racing possible.

Hawkins makes the case impossible to miss.

The National Museum of Racing and Hall of Fame describes him as one of the most talented and accomplished American athletes of the years before and immediately after the Civil War. That wording matters because it does not say “Black athletes” or “jockeys.” It places him in the larger athletic culture of the country.

He deserves to be there.

Hawkins rode elite horses for powerful owners in Louisiana and beyond. His reputation spread across the major racing circuits. He became known for composure, judgment, and a forward riding style that later horsemen would associate with the development of the “American seat.” He competed in a world where the value of a successful horse could be enormous and the value assigned to the human being riding it could be legally monstrous.

That last fact should not become decorative tragedy.

It changed the economics of his career.

A modern athlete sells labor. Even under a restrictive contract, the athlete is not legally the property of the owner of the team. Hawkins’s labor, body, movement, and earning capacity existed within slavery. His victories could increase the prestige and wealth of people who held legal power over him. His fame did not imply control of the money his performances generated.

This is one reason “professional athlete” is an awkward category for the period. Hawkins performed at an elite level in a commercial sport. Money surrounded him. Wagers depended on him. Owners relied on him. Crowds knew him. Yet the ordinary modern relationship between professional status and contractual agency did not apply.

He was economically central without being economically free.

That makes Hawkins a foundational figure in another American sports story: the struggle over who owns the value created by athletic labor.

The legal terms will change radically. Slavery will end. Reserve clauses will bind baseball players to clubs. College athletes will generate enormous revenues while being classified as amateurs. Boxers will depend on promoters. Olympic athletes will face restrictions on compensation. Women will fight for prize-money equality. Name, image, and likeness rights will eventually become an explicit battleground.

Those later disputes are not equivalents of slavery. Treating them as such would cheapen the difference between property in a person and an unequal labor contract. But they belong to the same broad question about sports economics: who is allowed to capture the value produced by the athlete?

Hawkins makes the origin of that question unusually stark.

His decade is also the final full decade before the Civil War. Racing culture in the South was deeply entangled with plantation wealth and slavery. Horses, land, capital, and enslaved expertise formed part of the same economy. The sport could appear glamorous because the coercion sustaining it was normalized by the people with power.

That is useful to remember whenever sports history isolates competition from its infrastructure.

A race begins at the starting line only for the spectator. For everyone else, it begins much earlier: breeding, feeding, grooming, training, transport, financing, labor, course maintenance, and the organization of the event. If we look only at the moment of visible competition, we can miss the system that made the moment possible.

Hawkins was unusually visible inside the system because he was the rider.

He carried the owner’s colors, but the intelligence of the ride was his own.

Jockeying rewards a kind of judgment that does not always produce theatrical gestures. Pace must be felt. The horse’s energy must be managed. Position has to be gained without wasting the animal. A rider needs to know when a rival is in trouble and when apparent weakness is a trap. The margin between patience and hesitation can be a race.

Hawkins’s fame suggests that knowledgeable observers recognized those skills.

Later racing accounts describe him as unusually cool under pressure. That adjective appears often in sports writing because composure is difficult to measure and easy to recognize after a good result. We should not turn it into mystical temperament. The more grounded interpretation is that Hawkins repeatedly made decisions people around the sport trusted.

Trust is one of the highest forms of sporting reputation.

An owner can buy a horse. He cannot buy the outcome. He has to put the horse under somebody else’s judgment.

In Hawkins’s case that trust coexisted with ownership of the rider himself.

The moral incoherence is severe enough that modern readers may search for a hidden explanation. Perhaps white owners did not really respect Black jockeys. Perhaps the jockey was merely a lightweight passenger. Perhaps fame has been exaggerated by later historians trying to recover forgotten figures.

The records do not support those evasions.

Black jockeys were elite competitors. They were trusted with exceptional horses. They won major races. Their knowledge was valued. Their later exclusion from the highest levels of American racing was not caused by sudden discovery that white riders were inherently better. It was the result of institutional and racial change.

This matters because exclusion often rewrites the history that came before it.

Once a field becomes overwhelmingly white, later audiences can assume it always was. Once segregation is established, integration can look like the first arrival of the excluded group. The visible present becomes a false explanation of the past.

Horse racing is one of the clearest examples in American sport.

The first Kentucky Derby in 1875 was won by Oliver Lewis, a Black jockey. Black riders won fifteen of the first twenty-eight Derbies. Isaac Murphy became a towering late-nineteenth-century champion. Willie Simms won the Kentucky Derby twice, the Belmont twice, and the Preakness. Jimmy Winkfield won consecutive Derbies in 1901 and 1902.

Then the top of the sport closed.

Violence, discrimination, licensing practices, exclusion by owners and riders, and the broader tightening of Jim Crow pushed Black jockeys away from the major tracks. By the time modern sports memory began to harden around film, radio, and national statistical record keeping, one of racing’s foundational populations had largely disappeared from the most prestigious events.

Hawkins therefore has two kinds of importance.

In his own time, he was an elite athlete with national notoriety.

In ours, he is evidence against historical amnesia.

Those are not the same thing, and the second should not overshadow the first. He does not belong here merely because forgetting him would be unjust. He belongs because he was consequential while he was alive.

The Hall of Fame’s characterization of Hawkins as perhaps the first Black athlete to achieve truly national notoriety in the United States is especially striking. Any “first” claim from this period deserves caution because archives are incomplete and fame is hard to measure. Austin Curtis was well known across states. Molineaux and Richmond achieved transatlantic fame from Britain. Cato’s race was nationally reported.

Still, Hawkins’s reach appears unusually broad.

That breadth came from the racing circuit itself. He rode in important regions, for leading stables, against top competition, and on horses whose performances attracted national attention. His career connected local racing cultures into a larger sporting map.

The map would be shattered by war.

The Civil War did not simply interrupt a season. It transformed the legal and economic order of the country. For Hawkins, emancipation changed the most basic condition of athletic life. He continued to ride after slavery. That continuity allows us to compare the same person across two systems.

Freedom did not eliminate racism or guarantee wealth. It did mean that Hawkins could no longer legally be owned as a human asset by the people whose horses he rode.

The change is so fundamental that it can make every smaller sporting detail look trivial.

But the sporting details are where continuity becomes visible.

Hawkins remained good.

That sounds obvious. It is also devastating to the logic that had justified the old order. His expertise was not produced by his owner’s legal possession of him. Emancipation did not remove the knowledge from his hands. The athlete survived the institution that claimed ownership of the athlete.

Sport sometimes gives history these unusually clean natural experiments. A rule changes. A barrier falls. The player remains.

Jackie Robinson will later make a similar point under completely different conditions. Integration did not create his ability. It created the opportunity for Major League Baseball to stop pretending that ability did not exist. Billie Jean King’s victories did not begin when prize money became more equal. Serena Williams’s power did not come from the tennis establishment’s comfort with her presence.

Institutions often confuse permission with creation.

Hawkins’s career exposes the mistake.

The 1850s also sit at a turning point in the history of the American athlete as public figure. The country was becoming more connected through railroads, telegraphy, and expanding print circulation. Sporting news could move farther and faster. A major performance could be known outside the local audience with increasing speed.

That communications network magnified reputations like Hawkins’s while preserving the ownership structure around them.

Technology, by itself, does not democratize sport.

It can simply make an unequal system more efficient at distributing spectacle.

The same caution applies later to radio, television, cable, and social media. Every new medium is initially described as if wider access will automatically flatten power. Sometimes it does. Often the people who own the distribution channel capture much of the gain.

Hawkins’s fame traveled. His autonomy did not travel at the same rate.

This is why the phrase “athlete empowerment” needs a long historical horizon. Empowerment is not fame. It is not salary alone. It is not the right to speak if speaking destroys access to competition. It includes bargaining power, mobility, ownership, legal standing, control of one’s image, and the ability to leave.

By those measures, Hawkins’s early career sits near one extreme.

He was famous without being free.

By the 2020s, Simone Biles can withdraw from an Olympic event because continuing is unsafe, explain the decision publicly in her own words, return three years later, win again, and remain the central commercial and competitive figure in her sport. That does not mean modern athletes possess complete power. It means the historical distance is enormous.

The line between Hawkins and Biles is one of the hidden spines of this book.

At one end, the athlete’s body is legally owned.

At the other, the athlete asserts ownership of the decision to use the body at all.

The intervening 170 years contain reserve clauses, amateurism rules, racial exclusions, gender restrictions, promoter contracts, endorsement systems, unions, free agency, collective bargaining, Title IX, prize-money fights, athlete activism, and direct-to-audience media.

No single story explains that transformation.

Hawkins gives it a starting point that is impossible to soften.

His career also shows the limits of judging historical athletes by modern statistics. Racing records from the period are incomplete. We cannot produce a database query that settles his rank among every American jockey of the 1850s. The responsible method is to triangulate contemporary reputation, quality of mounts and competition, major victories, geographic reach, and the testimony preserved by racing institutions.

That method produces uncertainty, but not ignorance.

Hawkins was a major athlete. His peers knew it. Owners knew it. Crowds knew it. Later racing historians knew it.

The gap is in popular memory.

Why did that memory fade?

Partly because horse racing lost its place as the uncontested center of American sports culture. Partly because team sports, especially baseball and football, came to dominate national narratives. Partly because the earliest athletic archive is harder to access. And partly because the racial transformation of racing made Black jockeys look anomalous to later generations even though they had once been foundational.

That is how a national story gets smaller than the nation that lived it.

Abe Hawkins expands it again.

He also offers a useful counterweight to the heroic integration story. American sports history often celebrates the moment an institution opens: the first Black player in a white league, the first woman admitted, the first barrier broken. Those moments matter.

Hawkins asks an earlier question.

What if the excluded people helped build the sport before the institution later decided they did not belong?

Then integration is not entrance.

It is return.

That distinction changes the moral ownership of the game.

Black jockeys did not ask to join somebody else’s ancient tradition at the end of the nineteenth century. They were part of the tradition. The exclusion came later.

Hawkins’s nickname, the Black Prince, now sounds almost too perfect for a book title. It captures fame and racial marking in two words. He could be elevated and separated at the same time.

The public could admire him while constantly naming him as different.

That pattern will not disappear.

Serena Williams will be praised as one of the greatest athletes alive and still encounter commentary about her body, anger, clothing, motherhood, and behavior that cannot be separated from race and gender. Muhammad Ali will become beloved nationally only after spending years as one of the country’s most polarizing athletes. Jack Johnson will be celebrated retrospectively after white America treated his championship as a racial emergency.

The Black Prince belonged to an older version of the same American habit.

Admiration did not require equality.

His career made the contradiction visible every time he won.

## The challenger test: the athlete as value before the athlete as worker

The 1850s are the easiest decade in the book to misunderstand through modern economic language.

We know what a professional athlete is supposed to be. The player signs a contract. The owner or promoter purchases labor for a defined period. The athlete may have weak bargaining power, bad representation, or an unfair share of the revenue, but the legal relationship begins with the premise that the athlete is a person selling services.

Hawkins competed before that premise applied to him.

That is why his selection defeats a challenger whose case rests only on more documented wins. The chapter is not asking which 1850s competitor produced the cleanest ledger. It is asking which athlete most clearly reveals the economic structure beneath American sport.

Hawkins does because the value chain is almost painfully visible.

Horse racing depended on expensive animals, breeding programs, land, transport, gambling, and human expertise. The owner controlled capital. The jockey controlled a critical part of the performance. But under slavery, the person providing that judgment could himself be part of the owner’s property system.

Modern sports business separates human capital from owned capital conceptually, even when executives speak crudely about “assets.” Hawkins’s world did not have to pretend.

The athlete could be an asset in law.

That legal fact makes every later argument about athlete power easier to calibrate. A reserve clause restricting a baseball player’s movement is coercive in a labor-market sense. It is not slavery. An amateurism rule restricting compensation can exploit value created by an athlete. It is not slavery. A sponsor threatening to terminate an endorsement can constrain speech. It is not slavery.

The book can connect these arrangements because they answer the same structural questions—who controls mobility, revenue, image, and bodily labor—while refusing to collapse the moral and legal distance between them.

Hawkins gives us the baseline.

The choice also survives a baseball challenge. The 1850s are the decade in which baseball clubs, rules, and urban competition become increasingly important. A future-oriented history might therefore choose an early baseball organizer or player and call the decade the birth of the national pastime.

That would import the twentieth century backward.

Horse racing still possessed mature money, elite competition, public prestige, and a national circuit that baseball had not yet matched. Hawkins was not a historical leftover standing in the way of baseball’s inevitable rise. He was competing at the center of an existing national sports economy.

The eventual winner of the sports-attention market should not erase the market it displaced.

This principle is essential to the book. History is full of technologies and institutions that look inevitable after they win. Baseball’s later cultural dominance makes nineteenth-century racing seem like an eccentric prelude. Television makes radio look transitional. Social media makes newspapers look slow. The present turns its predecessors into waiting rooms.

Hawkins refuses the waiting-room treatment.

His sport was already real enough to create national fame and enormous economic value.

The athlete’s post-emancipation career strengthens the selection further because it creates continuity across the greatest legal rupture in the country’s labor system. Hawkins’s racing intelligence does not change because the Thirteenth Amendment changes his legal status. He remains an athlete other people trust with valuable horses.

This is the cleanest rebuke to any theory that equates domination with the production of excellence.

The institution owned the person.

It did not own the source of his judgment.

Freedom reveals the distinction.

The same insight will recur under less extreme conditions. Major League Baseball did not create the ability of Negro League stars when integration allowed some of them into the majors. Olympic officials did not create Jim Thorpe’s 1912 performances when they later restored the medals. A women’s tour does not create athletic talent by offering equal prize money. Institutions can enable, certify, monetize, suppress, or exclude excellence.

They should not be confused with excellence itself.

That is why Hawkins is more than an early Black sports pioneer.

The word “pioneer” can become too polite. It suggests a person who bravely arrives first at a frontier.

Hawkins did not arrive at a frontier.

He was already inside a sophisticated sporting industry whose profits, prestige, and public fascination depended on expertise like his.

The injustice was not that the industry failed to discover him.

It discovered him perfectly well.

It valued the performance while denying the performer autonomy.

That pattern is harder to comfort ourselves about because merit cannot solve it. Hawkins did not need to become better to deserve freedom. He did not need one more victory. He did not need to prove economic usefulness.

The legal order needed to stop treating usefulness as ownership.

That is the chapter’s decisive distinction and the reason Hawkins owns the decade.

Other athletes can show how American sport grew.

Hawkins shows what American sport was willing to grow on top of.
