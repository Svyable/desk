# Chapter 1 — The First American Star

The first problem with writing a history of American sports is that the country did not begin with baseball.

It did not begin with football, basketball, the Olympics, the World Series, the Super Bowl, Madison Square Garden, ESPN, Nike, or the idea that an athlete should have a shoe line. In 1776, several sports that Americans would later treat as national inheritances either did not yet exist in recognizable form or had not become national institutions. The archive is thinner, the rules are less familiar, and the people whose bodies supplied the spectacle are not always the people whose names survived.

So the first name in this book is Austin Curtis.

He appears here three times: the most important American sports player of the 1770s, the 1780s, and the 1790s. That repetition is deliberate. A project that promises one athlete for every decade can create a bad incentive to invent novelty where history offers continuity. Curtis’s career crosses the founding era so cleanly, and reveals so much about the country that was being founded, that replacing him merely to keep the table visually tidy would make the book less true.

Curtis was an enslaved Black jockey in North Carolina and Virginia before he became a free horseman. He rode for Willie Jones, a wealthy North Carolina planter and political figure. He became known as a jockey, trainer, groom, and horseman in a sporting world in which elite racing was both serious competition and public theater. Mount Vernon’s history of eighteenth-century racing describes Curtis as a particularly famous enslaved jockey who gained renown in Virginia and North Carolina during the 1770s. Later North Carolina records preserve something even more consequential: the legislature emancipated him in 1791 after a petition described his service and loyalty, including his protection of Jones’s horses during the Revolutionary War.

There is a temptation to make that trajectory sound cleaner than it was. Enslaved athlete becomes celebrated athlete, proves his value, earns freedom, opens a new life. It has the satisfying shape of an American parable.

The problem is that the satisfaction belongs to the reader, not necessarily to the history.

Curtis’s fame did not cancel his enslavement. His expertise could make him valuable without making him autonomous. Racing could place a Black rider at the center of elite attention while leaving the legal order that owned him intact. The same society could wager heavily on his judgment and athletic ability, entrust him with expensive horses, carry stories about his victories, and still treat his freedom as something that required the permission of white lawmakers.

That contradiction makes Curtis more important, not less.

American sports did not enter the national story as a separate, innocent realm that would later become entangled with race, money, status, politics, and citizenship. Those things were present at the beginning. The track was already a social map.

Horse racing was well suited to the eighteenth-century world because it joined several economies at once. A racehorse was an animal, an investment, a status object, a breeding asset, and a vehicle for gambling. A race meeting brought together owners, trainers, riders, bettors, spectators, local prestige, and long-distance reputation. The sport could be informal or highly organized. It could be a quick contest over a short distance or a multi-heat endurance event. It could happen on a local course and still matter far beyond the county if the horses or patrons carried important names.

The people who rode were central to that machine, even when the documentary record centered the owners.

That is one of the recurring distortions in sports history. Money produces paper. Ownership produces paper. Institutions produce paper. The athlete produces a performance, which disappears unless somebody writes it down. The farther back we go, the more the surviving record tends to follow the person who bought the horse, posted the purse, owned the land, printed the newspaper, or held the legal right to sign a contract. The rider might be the decisive intelligence in the race and still enter the archive as a first name, a racial description, or a piece of property attached to someone else.

Curtis survives that filter unusually well.

He was not merely present around fast horses. Contemporary and later accounts treat him as an expert. The distinction matters. Riding at speed on eighteenth-century tracks demanded strength, balance, judgment, timing, and an understanding of an animal whose physical condition could determine a fortune. Training and grooming required another body of knowledge. A rider who could also judge horses, prepare them, travel with them, and manage them was not an interchangeable component. He was part athlete, part technician, part strategist.

Modern sports vocabulary can obscure this. We are used to separating the player from the coach, trainer, general manager, equipment staff, and owner. Early racing could collapse several of those roles into one horseman. Curtis’s importance comes partly from that older sporting world, in which bodily skill and practical knowledge were difficult to separate.

The 1770s version of Curtis belongs to a colonial America already obsessed with comparison. Whose horse was fastest? Whose bloodline was superior? Which rider understood pace? Which plantation or stable could produce the better animal? Competition was a way for elites to convert wealth into public standing. But when the race began, wealth still had to pass through a jockey’s hands.

Curtis’s hands were enslaved.

That sentence should sit uneasily. It should also change how we imagine the founding generation. The same decade that Americans turned political arguments about liberty into revolution contained Black athletes whose exceptional skill was visible in public but whose liberty was not presumed. The contradiction was not hidden at the margins. It could be galloping down the course in front of a crowd.

Then the war came close enough to the stable to rearrange the story.

During Lord Cornwallis’s 1781 campaign in North Carolina, Curtis is credited in later petitions and historical accounts with protecting Jones’s horses from British forces. The details vary across retellings, and a careful history should resist turning sparse evidence into cinematic scene. We do not know every movement, every conversation, or every fear. We do know that the service was remembered strongly enough to become part of the formal case for his emancipation a decade later.

That makes the 1780s selection different from the 1770s selection even though the name stays the same.

In the 1770s, Curtis shows us an athlete inside the machinery of colonial status. In the 1780s, the horseman enters the Revolutionary story itself. Sport does not stop when politics begins. The expertise developed around racing—the ability to handle valuable animals, move them, protect them, understand their worth—becomes useful under wartime conditions. A sporting asset becomes a military and economic asset. An athlete’s knowledge becomes civic service, even though the civic order has not yet recognized him as fully free.

There is an important difference between saying that sport caused Curtis’s eventual emancipation and saying that his sporting expertise helped make his value legible to powerful men. The first claim is too neat. The second is harder to ignore.

Willie Jones and others petitioned for Curtis’s freedom. North Carolina’s 1791 legislation names him in the legal act. The person who entered the 1770s as somebody another man could own entered the 1790s as a free horseman.

Freedom did not make the sporting world equal. It did change Curtis’s position inside it.

Accounts of his later life describe him as a respected breeder and trainer. That transition is easy to understate because it lacks the obvious drama of a championship bout or a record-breaking sprint. Yet it may be the most revealing part of his career. The athlete did not simply leave the track. His knowledge became a form of economic agency. What had been cultivated under coercion could now be exercised, however imperfectly, on different legal terms.

The 1790s pick therefore belongs to Curtis not because he was still winning the same races in the same way, but because his career reveals a transformation in what an athlete could become after competition. Modern sports culture is full of the phrase “life after the game.” Curtis had one of the earliest American versions, though the stakes were incomparably larger. His life after enslavement was built partly on expertise acquired before freedom.

This creates an uncomfortable question for any national sports history: when we celebrate skill developed under an unjust system, what exactly are we celebrating?

The answer cannot be the system. The answer has to be the person who made something from the room available to him, while refusing to let admiration for the performance sanitize the conditions around it.

Curtis also forces us to reconsider what counts as a “sports player.” The phrase is intentionally broad in this book. It includes jockeys, boxers, runners, gymnasts, golfers, basketball players, and athletes whose competitive role does not fit modern team-sport language. A jockey is not less of an athlete because the horse supplies much of the raw speed. The partnership is the event. Human judgment matters precisely because the animal is powerful, temperamental, and not a machine.

Horse racing would remain central to American sport long after Curtis. Black jockeys would dominate many of the nation’s most important races in the nineteenth century. The first Kentucky Derby would be won by Oliver Lewis in 1875. Isaac Murphy would become one of the great riders of any era. Willie Simms would lead North America in wins in 1893 and 1894 and capture races that later became the Triple Crown. Then Black jockeys would be pushed out of the top level as segregation hardened and white riders, owners, and institutions closed ranks.

That later erasure can make the early history look surprising today. It should not have been surprising to people who lived through it. Black riding expertise was part of American racing from the beginning.

One reason Curtis belongs at the front of the book is that a familiar national story tends to run in the other direction. We start with baseball. We move to football. We treat Black athletic participation as a later integration narrative. We place Jackie Robinson near the center of the moral arc, which he deserves, but the structure can imply that Black athletes arrived after white sports institutions had already been built.

Curtis corrects the sequence.

Black athletes were not late arrivals to American sport. They were there before the United States had finished becoming the United States.

Another reason is methodological. Selecting the “most important” athlete of a decade is not the same as selecting the best. The best can sometimes be argued with statistics. Importance is more troublesome. It asks what an athlete reveals, changes, accelerates, symbolizes, or makes possible. It asks whether the sport became more visible because of the athlete, whether institutions reacted, whether culture absorbed the person into a larger argument, whether later athletes inherited a different landscape.

For the eighteenth century, statistical comparison is particularly fragile. We do not possess a complete database of mounts, wins, purses, competitors, or audiences. It would be foolish to manufacture quantitative certainty. Curtis wins these first three decades on a different ground: he is one of the clearest surviving human links among elite colonial sport, slavery, the Revolution, emancipation, and a post-emancipation life in the horse economy.

If a better archive appears, the selection can be challenged. That is part of the design. This book is a sequence of argued judgments, not tablets carried down from a mountain.

A challenger might nominate another jockey, a wrestler, a rower, a cricketer, a local champion whose fame was enormous but poorly preserved. That would be healthy. The early decades are exactly where archival survival most distorts our field of view. We should be especially suspicious of confidence when the paper trail itself was distributed unequally.

But Curtis has something beyond documentary luck. His story compresses the central American sports question into one life: how can a society admire the body while denying the person?

That question will return with Jack Johnson. It will return with Jim Thorpe. It will return with Jesse Owens and Joe Louis and Jackie Robinson and Muhammad Ali and Serena Williams. The legal regimes will change. The media will change. The money will become almost unrecognizable. The athlete will gain bargaining power, fame, and eventually direct access to millions of people without an editor in the middle. Yet the country will keep using athletes to test ideas about belonging.

Curtis stands before all of them.

There is a second theme here too: American sports stars have always been technologies of attention. In the eighteenth century the technology was not television. It was reputation carried by letters, newspapers, race notices, tavern talk, betting markets, owners, breeders, and spectators moving from course to course. Fame traveled slower, but it still traveled. A successful rider could become known beyond the place where the race was run because money and prestige gave people reasons to remember.

The machine of celebrity was smaller. Its logic was already recognizable.

People wanted to see excellence under pressure. They wanted uncertainty. They wanted rivalry. They wanted to wager. They wanted the result to mean something beyond the result. They wanted a horse, a rider, an owner, a region, or a social faction to carry their hopes into competition.

That is sport.

The nation’s first athletic stars were therefore not merely playing games before modern America arrived. They were helping create habits modern America would scale: the conversion of performance into story, story into reputation, reputation into economic value, and economic value back into competition.

Curtis’s place inside that cycle was constrained by slavery, but it was not invisible.

His freedom in 1791 also resists another modern habit: treating an athlete’s social significance as something that begins only when the athlete makes an explicit political statement. Some athletes will do that later in this book. Billie Jean King will organize. Muhammad Ali will refuse induction. Jackie Robinson will write, speak, campaign, and challenge institutions. Simone Biles will alter a global conversation by deciding not to continue an Olympic competition when she could not safely locate herself in the air.

Curtis’s significance worked differently. His body, expertise, service, legal status, and eventual emancipation were political even without a preserved manifesto. The society around him made them political.

There is no need to invent his interior monologue. We do not have to pretend to know what he thought at the starting line or what freedom felt like on the day the law changed. History becomes weaker when we decorate silence with imagined certainty. What survives is enough.

A celebrated enslaved jockey became a wartime protector of valuable horses, then the subject of an emancipation petition, then a free horseman whose knowledge retained value in the new republic.

The United States was fifteen years old when North Carolina freed him.

By then American sport had already produced one of the recurring dramas of the next two centuries: a performer whom the culture needed more than its categories could comfortably contain.

That is why Austin Curtis gets three decades.

The repetition is not a failure to find three names.

It is the point.

## The challenger test: why three decades can belong to one man

A list of one athlete per decade creates a visual expectation before it creates a historical one. The eye wants twenty-six different faces. Repetition looks like a failure of imagination.

That is precisely why Curtis has to remain.

If I replaced him in the 1780s or 1790s with a thinner candidate merely to satisfy the design, the book would commit the same error it is trying to diagnose: confusing the neatness of the archive with the messiness of the past. The founding sporting world did not organize itself into ten-year award cycles. Careers crossed wars, legal regimes, migrations, and changes in status. The decade is our measuring device, not theirs.

The strongest challenger to Curtis is therefore not one specific athlete. It is the argument that no person should be allowed to occupy three consecutive slots because the method should reward variety.

That argument loses because variety is not one of the criteria.

Competitive authority matters. Institutional meaning matters. Cultural reach matters. Downstream inheritance matters. And, in the earliest period, the ability of the surviving archive to connect those things matters because we cannot responsibly fill gaps with confidence we do not possess.

Curtis gives us an unusually rare continuous record. North Carolina’s own historical interpretation now preserves the 1791 petition and emancipation act, including the claim that his wartime protection of Willie Jones’s horses was remembered as evidence of fidelity and service. The same state historical material describes the free Curtis as gaining wealth and national renown as a horse breeder and trainer. That does not prove he was the fastest rider in every race between 1770 and 1799. It proves something more relevant to this book: his sporting expertise remained socially legible across three different legal identities—enslaved athlete, Revolutionary-era horseman, free professional.

The change in status lets us see what slavery had hidden.

Before emancipation, economic value generated through Curtis’s knowledge could be appropriated within a property relationship. After emancipation, the knowledge did not vanish. The social system around the same expertise changed. That makes Curtis a kind of historical control case without pretending history is a laboratory.

The horseman is continuous.

The law is not.

That distinction matters for the book’s long argument about ownership because it prevents a common intellectual shortcut. Later chapters will discuss reserve clauses, amateurism rules, promoters, league restrictions, salary caps, sponsor expectations, and other forms of control over athletes. None of them should be casually equated with slavery. The reason to begin with literal ownership is to make the differences sharper, not blur them.

Slavery meant property in the person.

A restrictive contract means something else.

A sponsor clause means something else again.

The continuity is the question—who captures value, who controls mobility, who decides how the body may be used—not the legal severity of the answer.

Curtis establishes the most extreme starting point.

He also changes how we should read later integration stories. If Black athletic expertise is present before national independence, then Black participation cannot be narrated primarily as an arrival into sports somebody else built. The nineteenth-century chapters will show Black jockeys shaping racing at its highest level. The twentieth-century chapters will show formal barriers in baseball, boxing, golf, and other sports. Those barriers are not proof that Black athletes were historically absent. Often they are proof that institutions became better at excluding people whose skill was already known.

That reversal matters.

It means some of American sports history is not a story of entry.

It is a story of erasure followed by return.

Curtis also gives us the first example of expertise surviving the sporting event. His value was not exhausted at the finish line. Training, breeding, animal judgment, stable management, and reputation could produce an afterlife for athletic knowledge. Modern sports industries treat this as ordinary. Retired players become coaches, executives, commentators, investors, academy founders, and owners. Knowledge migrates from body to institution.

Curtis’s later horse career suggests an early version of that migration under radically different conditions.

The athlete becomes more than the performance because the performance teaches something transferable.

That is another reason his story deserves the space of three decades. If we chose one race and moved on, we would miss the transformation from exploited expertise to partially self-directed expertise. The legal change matters because the sporting knowledge remains.

The archive still requires humility. We should not invent a complete win-loss record. We should not transform every later compliment into proof of nationwide celebrity at modern scale. We should not assume the emancipation petition captures Curtis’s own understanding of his life; it records how powerful men represented his case to lawmakers. The words of a petition are evidence and advocacy at once.

That distinction will matter again throughout the book. Newspaper praise can be reporting and promotion. Hall of Fame language can preserve fact and celebrate legacy. Official records can certify results while omitting the social world around them.

Every source has a job.

The 1791 act can tell us Curtis was legally emancipated.

It cannot tell us what freedom felt like.

The racing record can tell us he was respected as a horseman.

It cannot give us every race he rode.

The later reputation can tell us his expertise endured.

It cannot repair everything the archive failed to preserve while he was enslaved.

That is enough.

The first chapter therefore sets not only the book’s subject but its evidentiary rule. When the record is strong, say the strong thing. When the record is partial, preserve the partiality. When a legend improves the story but weakens the evidence, choose the evidence.

America was already using sport to turn bodies into public meaning before the republic had a constitution.

Austin Curtis was already showing the country that an athlete could be central to the spectacle and denied ownership of himself.

Three decades later, his freedom made the contradiction easier to see.

The book will spend the next two centuries watching athletes negotiate the distance between those two conditions.
