# Chapter 21 — Serena and the Right to Be Uncomfortable

Serena Williams spent enough time at the top of tennis that the culture had to keep finding new ways to explain why her dominance was somehow unusual.

She was too powerful.

Too emotional.

Too muscular.

Too fashionable.

Too angry.

Too ambitious.

Too absent.

Too old.

Then she kept winning.

That is why Serena Williams takes the 2010s.

The decade did not contain the beginning of her career. She had won her first major singles title at the 1999 U.S. Open and reached world No. 1 in 2002. By 2010 she was already established as one of the great players of her generation.

The 2010s turned longevity into a second form of revolution.

She won major titles across the decade, held all four majors at once for a second time from the 2014 U.S. Open through Wimbledon in 2015, won Olympic singles gold in 2012, dominated 2013 with a 78–4 record, and captured the 2017 Australian Open while pregnant with her first child. She finished her initial full-time career phase with twenty-three major singles titles, the Open Era record for a woman.

The numbers matter because they prevent the cultural argument from floating free of performance.

Serena was not important merely because people talked about race, gender, fashion, anger, motherhood, or body image around her.

They talked because she kept placing herself at the center of the most important matches in women’s tennis.

Excellence created the platform.

The reaction to excellence revealed the culture.

Her power game is the obvious starting point. Serena’s serve became one of the most formidable shots in tennis history: fast, accurate, difficult to read, and capable of turning pressure points into short problems. Her groundstrokes combined pace with the ability to change direction early. Movement and competitive resilience made the power usable rather than decorative.

The game did not simply become “bigger hitting” because of Serena and Venus Williams, but their success accelerated a version of women’s tennis in which athleticism, serve quality, return aggression, and first-strike offense became impossible to treat as exceptional traits.

That influence resembles Tiger Woods’s effect on golf.

A dominant athlete can change what the next generation believes a professional body must be prepared to do.

Then imitation changes the field.

Serena had to keep adapting to competitors who had grown up watching Serena.

That is one of longevity’s hidden difficulties.

The young star solves the old sport.

The veteran has to solve the sport after the sport has copied her.

A decade of dominance therefore proves something different from a brilliant peak. It demonstrates repeated adaptation under increasingly specific opposition.

Opponents had years of video, data, coaching knowledge, and experience. They knew the serve patterns, the return positions, the emotional rhythms, the favorite combinations. The fact that Williams could remain a major-title threat deep into her thirties shows competitive intelligence beyond raw physical superiority.

Yet “power” remained the easiest public description.

That word did cultural work.

Black athletes have long been described through physicality in ways that can minimize technique, preparation, and strategy. Black women face an additional history of being treated as physically excessive relative to narrow standards of femininity. Serena’s body therefore became a subject of commentary that often had little to do with whether she could hit a forehand inside the line.

The body was read socially before it was analyzed athletically.

This is where sports and fashion became inseparable in her career.

Tennis has always had clothing rules, traditions, sponsor relationships, and aesthetic expectations. Women’s tennis in particular has been a site where athletic performance and femininity are publicly negotiated through apparel.

Serena understood clothing as performance, comfort, identity, and spectacle.

Her outfits could generate headlines independent of the result. The attention sometimes celebrated creativity and sometimes treated her body as a problem to be managed.

The 2018 French Open catsuit became the clearest example. Williams said the compression garment had a health function after complications associated with childbirth, including concerns about blood clots. The French Tennis Federation’s leadership later announced stricter expectations around attire.

The episode was larger than a garment because it asked who gets to define professionalism on a woman athlete’s body.

Rules are never merely rules when they emerge from traditions built around a narrow image of who belongs.

That does not mean every dress code is discriminatory by definition. Sports institutions legitimately regulate equipment and presentation. The historical question is whose comfort and whose aesthetic are treated as neutral.

Serena repeatedly made neutrality look less neutral.

Her career also changed the public conversation around anger.

Tennis has a long history of male players shouting, smashing rackets, insulting officials, and turning volatility into part of their competitive legend. Women have never been granted exactly the same interpretive range. A forceful woman can move from “competitive” to “hysterical,” “difficult,” or “out of control” more quickly, and race can sharpen the stereotype further.

Williams had real confrontations with officials and moments in which her conduct deserved ordinary sporting criticism. Historical fairness does not require pretending every dispute was justified.

The question is whether the scale and language of the reaction were ordinary.

Often they were not.

The 2018 U.S. Open final against Naomi Osaka became the most famous case. A sequence of code violations and arguments with chair umpire Carlos Ramos escalated in a match Osaka ultimately won. The episode generated global debate about officiating, sexism, sportsmanship, coaching rules, and Serena’s behavior.

The most important fact is not that one side of the debate can be declared completely correct years later.

It is that the argument became so much larger than a tennis rules dispute because Williams’s body and voice already carried decades of cultural meaning.

A less important athlete can have an argument with an umpire.

Serena had a referendum.

That is the cost of symbolic centrality.

Every incident becomes evidence for somebody’s prior theory.

Her 2017 Australian Open victory and subsequent motherhood opened another territory.

Williams later disclosed that she had been pregnant during the tournament. After childbirth, she experienced serious medical complications and wrote publicly about the experience, including the importance of listening to women—especially Black women—when they report health concerns.

The athlete’s platform expanded from performance to maternal health.

Then she tried to return to elite tennis.

The comeback forced sports culture to confront an assumption it had often hidden: the male athlete can become a parent without pregnancy changing his body or competitive calendar.

For women, parenthood can be a physiological event, medical event, scheduling event, ranking event, sponsorship event, and identity event all at once.

Sports systems built around male career patterns can treat that reality as an individual inconvenience rather than structural design problem.

Williams’s visibility made the problem difficult to ignore.

She reached four major singles finals after returning, though she did not win a twenty-fourth major.

That near-miss became an obsession in coverage.

The number twenty-four carried statistical significance because it would have tied Margaret Court’s all-time major singles total, a figure accumulated across amateur and Open eras. The chase was understandable.

It also threatened to reduce an enormous career to one number she did not reach.

This is one of sports history’s most persistent traps.

A round number becomes a moral boundary.

Twenty-three is framed as incomplete because twenty-four exists.

The athlete’s actual influence has no reason to respect the arithmetic.

Serena’s importance does not depend on winning one more tournament.

Her twenty-three major singles titles across an eighteen-season span, 319 weeks at No. 1, four Olympic gold medals including doubles, and longevity across several generations establish the competitive case.

The cultural case is larger.

She and Venus came from public courts in Compton rather than the elite academy pipeline associated with much of tennis. Their father, Richard Williams, developed an unconventional plan and resisted parts of the junior-tennis machinery. The sisters’ arrival challenged assumptions about where champions came from and what a tennis family should look like.

By the 2010s, the effect had matured.

Young Black players did not have to imagine elite tennis without Serena in the picture.

Representation had become continuous enough to alter expectation.

Naomi Osaka, Coco Gauff, Sloane Stephens, Madison Keys, and others developed distinct games and identities; none should be reduced to “next Serena.” The important point is that the visual field of possibility had expanded.

A Black girl watching tennis could see the most important player in the world and not have to treat Black presence as historical exception.

That is different from a first.

It is normalization through dominance.

Serena also pushed the athlete-brand model beyond endorsements into ownership and investment. Fashion businesses, philanthropy, and Serena Ventures demonstrated a broader pattern among twenty-first-century athletes: convert attention and capital into equity rather than only advertising fees.

This is the long economic line from Jordan.

The athlete as platform becomes athlete as investor.

Ownership changes the relationship to time. An endorsement pays while the contract runs. Equity can grow after the playing career.

The star begins building an economic identity that does not depend on the body remaining young.

That is one of the most important transformations in modern sports labor.

For most of American sports history, the athlete’s earning window was narrow. The body depreciated. Owners and institutions endured.

Modern superstars increasingly try to make capital outlive performance.

Serena belongs at the center of that shift.

She also changed the language around departure. In 2022, when she stepped away from regular tour competition, she publicly resisted the conventional retirement framing and described the move as an evolution toward other parts of life and business.

That language became even more interesting in 2026. At forty-four, nearly four years after her 2022 farewell, she returned to WTA competition in doubles at Queen’s Club, accepted a Wimbledon singles wild card, played mixed doubles at the U.S. Open, and reunited with Venus for U.S. Open women’s doubles.

The return does not change the 2010s selection.

It strengthens the underlying point.

The athlete is claiming authorship over the timeline.

Sports institutions and media prefer clear categories: active, retired, comeback.

A person may prefer something less tidy.

Serena’s relationship to discomfort is therefore bigger than the discomfort she caused opponents.

She made institutions uncomfortable with her power.

Commentators uncomfortable with her anger.

Traditionalists uncomfortable with her fashion.

Markets uncomfortable with the breadth of her identity.

And eventually she became comfortable enough with her own authority to decide that the sport did not get to define the only meaningful shape of her life.

That makes her a direct predecessor to Simone Biles in the 2020s.

The two athletes are different in almost every competitive way. Tennis rewards prolonged head-to-head problem solving. Gymnastics compresses enormous technical risk into seconds. Serena’s controversies often centered on how institutions interpreted a powerful Black woman who kept speaking. Biles’s defining 2020s moment began when she stopped performing because she could no longer safely locate herself in the air.

The common thread is athlete sovereignty.

Billie Jean King fought for women to control the economic structure of their sport.

Serena expanded control over identity, business, motherhood, and career timeline.

Biles will make the decision itself—the decision to continue or stop—the central act.

By the 2010s, the athlete had traveled an extraordinary distance from Abe Hawkins.

The body could generate wealth, own businesses, reach audiences directly, challenge officials publicly, return after childbirth, and reshape the terms of celebrity.

The struggle was no longer for permission to exist in the sport.

It was for the right to exist without becoming comfortable for everybody watching.

Serena Williams won that argument simply by refusing to make comfort the standard.

The strongest challenger for the 2010s is LeBron James, and the comparison clarifies why Serena wins. James was a championship center of gravity, changed the modern economics of player mobility, demonstrated how a superstar could exert leverage over roster construction and free agency, built media and business interests beyond endorsements, and used his platform politically. His 2010 decision to leave Cleveland for Miami became a spectacle about whether fans believed the athlete owed a franchise something beyond the contract.

That is a major ownership story.

Serena’s case is broader because the decade required her to renegotiate not one employment relationship but the cultural terms attached to a woman athlete’s body across age, race, fashion, pregnancy, health, anger, and motherhood while remaining the sport’s competitive standard. James changed what an NBA superstar could control within a league. Serena changed what audiences had to accept a woman superstar could be.

Another challenger is Simone Biles herself. Her dominance began in the 2010s, and by pure gymnastics achievement she already had a decade-defining claim before Tokyo. She takes the 2020s because that later decade adds something historically rarer than medals: a public rejection of the idea that an institution is entitled to performance when the athlete judges the performance unsafe.

The placement lets the chapters form a handoff rather than force two extraordinary Black women into a single symbolic slot.

Serena’s technical influence also deserves more than the word power. Power that cannot be placed is error. A serve that is merely fast becomes predictable. Williams’s greatness came from converting physical capacity into decision advantage: disguise, placement, first-ball aggression, and the ability to raise serve quality under pressure. Her return game punished second serves aggressively enough to change how opponents experienced their own service games.

This is an important correction to the physical stereotype. The body produced options. The player chose among them.

As tennis analytics matured, the strategic richness became easier to describe. Point construction could be decomposed into serve direction, return depth, rally length, court position, and shot tolerance. The older language of overwhelming power was not false; it was incomplete. Serena’s longevity makes the incompleteness obvious because pure explosiveness tends to decline with age. She kept winning by adapting patterns, scheduling, movement, and shot selection as well as by remaining physically formidable.

Longevity is intelligence under changing constraints.

The motherhood chapter makes that intelligence institutional. Pregnancy is not an injury, yet sports systems often treated the absence as though the athlete had simply stopped supplying labor. Rankings, sponsorship protections, seeding, medical care, and scheduling all had to reckon with a career interruption that belonged to ordinary human life and yet could disproportionately affect women’s economic position.

Serena’s fame ensured that every weakness in the system was visible at unusual scale. That visibility benefited debates larger than her own career, but it also placed a burden on her to represent mothers, Black women, athletes with health complications, and women seeking career continuity. No individual should be required to carry all of those constituencies.

Importance often means being assigned them anyway.

Her 2017 Australian Open title while pregnant became culturally irresistible because it seemed to collapse categories the public had treated separately: pregnant body and championship body. The historical lesson should not be that pregnant athletes ought to compete through any condition. That would turn one person’s circumstance into another coercive standard. The lesson is narrower: pregnancy does not erase athletic identity, and institutions should not assume the person’s competitive future has ended.

Her 2026 return makes that point vivid in a different way. The standard career narrative had already closed the book in 2022. She had “evolved away.” Business and family life expanded. Then she chose to compete again. The comeback was not required to justify her legacy and did not need to produce a twenty-fourth major to be meaningful.

The act exposed how much retirement language belongs to observers rather than athletes. Fans want ceremony because ceremony gives memory a clean edge. Broadcasters want “last match.” Brands want farewell campaigns. Hall-of-fame timelines prefer finality.

A person can choose ambiguity instead.

That choice links Serena to the book’s deepest theme. The early athlete could be physically owned. The nineteenth-century professional fought for the right to earn. The twentieth-century star fought for access, speech, organization, and commercial leverage. By Serena’s era, control includes narrative time: when a career begins, what interrupts it, what counts as leaving, and whether leaving must be permanent.

Ownership has become temporal.

Who owns the calendar of the athlete’s body?

The tour has a calendar. Sponsors have campaign calendars. media have seasons. fans have expectations. biology has its own timetable.

Serena’s career refused to let any one of them become sovereign.

That refusal may be the most modern form of her importance.

Jordan taught the industry that the athlete could become a brand.

Serena demonstrated that the brand did not have to consume the person.
