# Back Matter — Who Owns the Athlete?

A book built around twenty-six decade selections ends with a temptation.

Count the winners.

Which sport appears most often? How many boxers? How many baseball players? How many women? How many Black athletes? Which decades feel obvious? Which selection will make the most people throw the book across a room?

Those are entertaining questions.

They are not the final question.

The final question is what the sequence reveals that the individual biographies do not.

Across 250 years, American sports history looks like a long transfer of power toward the athlete.

Not a clean transfer.

Not an irreversible one.

Not a transfer in which every athlete receives the same share.

But a transfer large enough that the endpoints almost look like different concepts of a human being.

Austin Curtis begins this book as an athlete whose expertise can be nationally meaningful while his body is legally owned by another person.

Simone Biles ends the current sequence as an athlete who can stand inside the most valuable competition in her sport and decide that the next performance will not happen because she cannot perform it safely.

Between those points lie most of the institutions we now mistake for the natural order of sport.

Professional leagues.

National championships.

The Olympics.

Mass newspapers.

Radio.

Film.

Television.

Cable.

Endorsements.

Players’ associations.

Collective bargaining.

Free agency.

Title IX.

Global shoe culture.

Direct-to-audience media.

Name, image, and likeness rights.

Athlete-owned companies.

Every layer changes the bargaining relationship around performance.

## The scoreboard never stayed in its lane

The first lesson of the twenty-six selections is that sport was never politically pure.

The eighteenth-century racetrack was entangled with slavery, property, class, gambling, and regional status.

The nineteenth-century prize ring carried immigration, masculinity, race, legality, and urban power.

Horse racing encoded plantation wealth and Black expertise. Baseball’s professionalization raised questions about labor and amateur purity before the word “sports business” sounded natural. The Olympics turned bodies into national representatives. Television made gender arguments primetime entertainment. Shoes turned athletic identity into consumer culture.

The idea that politics entered sport sometime around Muhammad Ali or Billie Jean King survives because modern politics is easier to recognize than old politics.

Ownership was political.

Exclusion was political.

Amateurism was political.

Segregation was political.

Military conscription was political.

Prize money was political.

What changes over time is not whether sport touches power.

What changes is which form of power feels normal enough to disappear.

## Performance can contradict a hierarchy without abolishing it

Jack Johnson is the clearest case, but not the first.

Tom Molineaux could make racial hierarchy physically unstable in an English ring in 1810. Abe Hawkins could become an elite national athlete while enslaved. Willie Simms could dominate North American racing while the country was hardening Jim Crow. Jesse Owens could win four gold medals in Berlin while the United States remained segregated. Joe Louis could become a democratic national symbol while serving in a segregated military.

Sport creates public facts.

It does not control the interpretation of those facts.

That may be the most important difference between a scoreboard and a social system.

A race has a finish line.

A hierarchy can move its justification.

When the supposedly inferior athlete wins, prejudice can invent an exception, attack the institution, narrow access, create a new category, or simply refuse to care.

The result still matters because it removes one excuse.

But it does not automatically remove the structure.

This is why Jackie Robinson’s importance is larger than one first game. The change becomes durable when the institution reorganizes around the new reality. Once Black players are producing championships and becoming central stars, resegregation is not merely immoral; it becomes competitively absurd.

Institutional dependence can do what one symbolic victory cannot.

## The most important player is often the one who changes everyone else’s job

Another pattern appears across sports.

Jim Creighton changes the pitcher from facilitator into attacker.

Magic Johnson expands the point guard beyond a conventional body type.

Tiger Woods changes what elite golfers believe they must do away from the course in order to compete on it.

Serena Williams changes the level of serve, return, power, movement, and durability opponents must solve across generations.

The great innovator creates adaptation debt.

Everyone else has to pay it.

That may be a more durable measure of importance than the record book.

A record is a number waiting to be exceeded.

A changed job description becomes the new baseline.

## Attention became an asset

John L. Sullivan shows the first mature national version of a problem that becomes enormous later: fame is not merely applause.

It is economic infrastructure.

Newspapers and railroads let Sullivan carry attention around the country.

Babe Ruth meets a denser system—daily sports pages, photography, radio, newsreels, advertising—and becomes a national market.

Magic Johnson makes basketball feel native to television.

Michael Jordan makes the athlete portable through product. The shoe can travel where the game broadcast cannot. The logo can outlive the jump.

Tiger Woods concentrates an individual sport’s media economics so heavily that every tournament changes value depending on whether he is present and competitive.

Serena Williams belongs to the next stage, where the athlete tries to turn attention into ownership rather than only endorsement income.

The economic migration is enormous.

Athlete as property.

Athlete as labor.

Athlete as star.

Athlete as intellectual property.

Athlete as platform.

Athlete as investor.

That sequence is not complete liberation. Platforms can own distribution. Sponsors can police behavior. Leagues can constrain salaries. Fans can demand access. Algorithms can reward constant self-exposure.

The form of control changes as the athlete’s leverage grows.

## Organizing beats exceptionalism

Billie Jean King provides one of the book’s most important corrections to hero history.

A star can be treated as an exception.

An organized field is harder to contain.

King’s great 1973 cultural image is the Battle of the Sexes. Her more durable achievement is helping players build an institution and bargaining power around women’s professional tennis.

The lesson extends far beyond tennis.

An exceptional athlete may prove a barrier irrational.

Collective structure determines whether the opening survives for ordinary professionals.

This is why unions, associations, tours, leagues, ranking systems, maternity policies, development pipelines, and prize structures belong inside sports history rather than in an appendix about business.

They decide who can remain an athlete long enough to become great.

## The athlete gained a voice, then discovered the cost of using it

Muhammad Ali is the sharp turn.

He understands that attention can be used against the institutions that created the platform.

He names himself.

He refuses induction.

He loses the heavyweight title and years of his prime.

The cost matters because it distinguishes historical courage from retrospective branding.

By the time Ali lights the Olympic flame in 1996, the country can admire the old dissenter safely.

The harder question is always how the current dissenter is treated before the history books declare the issue settled.

Modern athletes have more direct voice than Ali ever had. They can reach millions without a sportswriter deciding which sentence gets printed.

They also live inside a much larger real-time judgment machine.

Direct voice reduces one gatekeeper and creates thousands of new ones.

Power does not remove scrutiny.

It scales it.

## The body became a jurisdiction

The deepest arc is not commercial.

It is bodily.

Slavery makes the early endpoint brutally simple: another person can possess legal authority over the athlete’s body.

Professional contracts narrow the claim but preserve control over labor.

Reserve systems limit movement. Amateur rules control earning. Segregated institutions control access. Governments make claims through military service. Sponsors make claims through public identity. Fans make claims through moral expectation.

By the 2020s, the athlete’s most consequential assertion can be refusal itself.

Simone Biles does not create athlete mental-health advocacy alone. Michael Phelps, Naomi Osaka, Kevin Love, DeMar DeRozan, and many others are part of a larger change in public language around mental health and performance.

Her Tokyo decision becomes uniquely powerful because of the physical problem involved.

Gymnastics is not a sport in which a confused performer can safely jog through a few minutes at reduced intensity.

If spatial awareness fails during twisting flight, the danger is structural to the event.

Stopping is therefore not withdrawal from the competitive problem.

It is a solution to the safety problem.

Then Biles returns.

That matters because it denies both cheap stories available to the public.

The first cheap story says stopping proves weakness.

The second says stopping must permanently end the old form of excellence.

Paris 2024 destroys both.

The athlete can protect the body and remain great.

## The unfinished decade

The 2020s selection is deliberately provisional.

That label should survive every edition published before January 1, 2030.

Shohei Ohtani is changing the imaginable structure of baseball stardom by sustaining elite two-way value in the modern major leagues.

Patrick Mahomes has already altered the championship history and tactical imagination of the NFL.

Caitlin Clark has become a measurable accelerator for women’s basketball audiences, attendance, sponsorship, and media attention.

Katie Ledecky continues to extend one of the greatest endurance-swimming careers ever assembled.

Other athletes will create events we cannot name yet.

A book about historical importance should not pretend an unfinished decade has finished merely because publication requires a final page.

So Biles leads.

She does not own the decade permanently.

That is how the method is supposed to work.

## Twenty-six answers, one argument

The book began by asking for a player every decade.

The better question emerged while following them.

What does America ask an athlete to be?

Property.

Entertainment.

Evidence.

Representative.

Worker.

Patriot.

Role model.

Brand.

Activist.

Investment vehicle.

Symbol.

Sometimes all of them at once.

What does the athlete ask in return?

At first, history gives some athletes almost no ability to ask.

Over time the demands become clearer.

Pay me.

Let me compete.

Call me by my name.

Let me organize.

Let me speak.

Let me own something.

Let me become a parent without treating my career as an error.

Let me decide when my body is safe to use.

America’s sports history is often sold as a collection of winning moments.

The deeper history is a negotiation over the person who produced the moment.

The scoreboard tells us who crossed the line first.

The stories tell us who was allowed to own the crossing.
