# Chapter 17 — The Match That Became an Argument

Billie Jean King’s most important victory of 1973 was not the match against Bobby Riggs.

That match is the one the culture remembers because television knows how to preserve theater.

The more durable victory happened through organizing.

In June 1973, King helped lead more than sixty women players into a meeting at the Gloucester Hotel in London, where they formed the Women’s Tennis Association. Later that summer, after sustained pressure and sponsorship work, the U.S. Open became the first Grand Slam tournament to award equal prize money to women and men.

Then, in September, King walked into the Houston Astrodome and beat Riggs in straight sets before a global television audience estimated at roughly ninety million.

The three events belong together.

King is the most important American sports player of the 1970s because she understood that athletic excellence without institutional power leaves the athlete dependent on somebody else’s generosity.

She did not only win inside the system.

She helped build a different system.

That is a rare form of sporting importance.

Most stars influence institutions indirectly. They attract audiences, increase revenue, force rule changes, or become symbols around which pressure gathers. King acted more like an organizer and entrepreneur. She saw the economic structure of women’s tennis as something players could change collectively.

The problem was visible in prize money.

Women could perform on the same tournament grounds, sell tickets, create drama, and still receive dramatically smaller checks. The inequality was defended through familiar claims about market value: men attracted more interest, men’s sport was the real product, women should be grateful for the opportunity.

King attacked the premise by helping build demand and bargaining power at the same time.

The Original 9 had already broken away in 1970 to create a women’s professional circuit, risking sanctions and career damage. The WTA in 1973 unified women players more formally. Collective organization changed the negotiation.

One player asking for more money can be replaced.

A field of the best players acting together is harder to ignore.

This is labor power expressed through sports.

The athlete is often described as an individual entrepreneur, especially in tennis, golf, boxing, and other sports without a conventional team contract. But tournaments require a field. A star needs opponents. The product is collective even when the trophy is individual.

King understood that hidden interdependence.

The equal-prize-money fight at the U.S. Open demonstrates how change actually happens: pressure, coalition, media attention, sponsor money, tournament leadership, and the credible possibility that important players might not participate.

No single ingredient was sufficient.

The victory can be romanticized into one brave woman demanding equality. The reality is stronger because it was institutional.

The tournament needed the women.

The women had organized enough to make that need visible.

This is what bargaining power looks like when it stops being theoretical.

Then Bobby Riggs provided television with a plot.

Riggs, a former champion more than two decades older than King, had turned male chauvinism into promotion. Earlier in 1973 he defeated Margaret Court, then the leading women’s player, in a highly publicized match. The result gave the spectacle credibility and raised the stakes of the next challenge.

King agreed to play.

The event was designed as theater because theater was the point. Costumes, entrances, commentary, a giant stadium, and primetime television turned a tennis exhibition into cultural referendum.

This structure creates an obvious sporting objection.

A twenty-nine-year-old elite woman beating a fifty-five-year-old retired man does not prove women and men are physically equal across tennis. It does not establish how the top women’s player would fare against the top male professional of the same age. The match was never a scientifically useful experiment about sex differences in elite athletic performance.

Its importance did not come from scientific validity.

It came from social stakes.

Riggs had framed the match as evidence about women’s competence and place. He turned a sports exhibition into a claim that audiences understood far beyond tennis. King recognized that losing would be used against a professional women’s tour still fighting for legitimacy and money.

So she treated the exhibition as consequential competition.

She won 6–4, 6–3, 6–3.

The score was simple enough to fit into a culture-war headline.

That simplicity is one reason the match endured.

Politics often seeks sporting evidence because sport ends with numbers. A complex dispute about women’s work, equal pay, reproductive rights, education, and social roles cannot be settled by three tennis sets. But a televised contest can create an image of female competence more visceral than an editorial.

Again, the scoreboard cannot force society to accept what a performance means.

It can give one side a remarkably portable fact.

King used the fact.

Her importance also comes from understanding television as a political institution.

By 1973, television had become central to American domestic life. The same medium that carried war footage, political conventions, advertisements, sitcoms, and news could bring a gender argument into the living room as entertainment.

The audience scale was unprecedented for tennis.

People who did not know the rankings knew the match.

That is how a sports event escapes the sports page.

King’s performance therefore belonged to the history of women’s rights as much as the history of tennis, but it should not be inflated into sole causation. The women’s movement was already transforming law, work, education, politics, and culture. Title IX had been enacted in 1972. Women were organizing across sectors. King did not create that movement.

She made elite sports one of its most visible arenas.

The distinction respects collective history while preserving her specific role.

Title IX is especially important because its eventual effect on American athletics would be enormous. The law prohibits sex discrimination in federally funded education. Its sports consequences grew over time through regulation, enforcement, litigation, institutional adaptation, and investment.

King’s professional tennis campaign was separate from school and college sports, yet the changes reinforced each other culturally.

Girls could increasingly imagine athletic opportunity as normal.

Professional women could increasingly demand to be paid as serious performers.

Television could increasingly treat women’s competition as a commercial product rather than a novelty.

Progress in one layer increased expectations in another.

This is how sports ecosystems change.

King also understood that equality required institutions beyond the tour itself. She co-founded World TeamTennis and created the Women’s Sports Foundation. She treated sports participation as a pipeline problem: if girls lack access, coaching, facilities, and role models, the professional stage cannot repair the missing years later.

The star thought upstream.

That distinguishes her from athletes who use influence primarily for personal brand or post-career philanthropy. King’s organizing directly targeted the structure that produced athletic opportunity and compensation.

Her career therefore expands the definition of “player” in this book.

The important sports player may change the game by changing the labor market around the game.

That is still athletic history.

One reason sports institutions resist this idea is that they prefer to separate “the product” from labor politics. Fans are encouraged to see negotiations over pay as distractions from the pure contest.

But the contest only exists because athletes agree to perform under particular conditions.

Prize money determines who can afford to keep playing.

Travel support determines who enters.

Maternity policies affect careers.

Scheduling affects health.

Facilities affect development.

Media revenue affects what leagues can pay.

The economics are not outside the sport.

They determine who gets to become an athlete long enough for us to watch.

King made that infrastructure visible.

Her own sexuality later became another part of the public story. In 1981, after the end of the decade covered here, she was publicly outed in connection with a former relationship and lost significant endorsement support. The episode demonstrated that women athletes could be economically punished not only for performance or politics but for private identity.

That later event matters to the 1970s story because it reveals the fragility of commercial empowerment.

The same celebrity system that gives an athlete a platform can withdraw money when the athlete no longer fits a marketable norm.

Sponsorship is power on loan.

King’s institutional work created forms of power less dependent on one advertiser’s comfort.

That lesson remains current.

Athletes who build companies, unions, leagues, foundations, media channels, and investment vehicles have more durable leverage than athletes who rely entirely on endorsement approval.

The 1970s are where that logic becomes unusually visible through a woman athlete.

The decade has other strong candidates. Muhammad Ali’s fights against Joe Frazier and George Foreman are among the most important events in sports history. Kareem Abdul-Jabbar combined basketball greatness with cultural and political importance. Hank Aaron broke Babe Ruth’s career home run record in 1974 while enduring racist hatred. Pelé’s arrival with the New York Cosmos helped transform American attention to soccer, though his primary career was Brazilian and global.

King wins the decade because she altered the economic and institutional possibility of women’s professional sport.

A record can be broken.

A title changes hands.

An organization can persist.

The WTA outlived King’s playing career. Equal prize money at the U.S. Open became a permanent expectation rather than a one-year promotional stunt. Women’s tennis developed into one of the strongest commercial women’s sports in the world.

That durability is the evidence.

The Battle of the Sexes remains culturally useful because it offers a vivid scene, but the real breakthrough was not that a woman beat an older man.

It was that women players were learning to stop asking men to determine the terms of women’s tennis.

The match told the story in one night.

The organization changed the next fifty years.

King’s most consequential skill may therefore have been the ability to understand that individual excellence and collective leverage are complements.

Without excellence, she lacked credibility.

Without collective action, her credibility could be consumed as one exceptional woman.

Institutions often celebrate exceptions because exceptions do not require structural change.

The exceptional woman can be praised as proof that the system works.

King refused the role.

She wanted the system to produce more women with real careers.

That is a fundamentally different ambition.

It also changes how we should interpret “equal prize money.” Critics sometimes reduce the debate to whether men and women produce identical ticket sales, television ratings, match lengths, or physical performance. The commercial history is more dynamic.

Markets are not discovered fully formed.

They are built.

Promotion changes audiences. Distribution changes audiences. Investment changes quality. Scheduling changes visibility. Narrative changes demand. Repeated exposure creates familiarity, and familiarity creates fans.

King’s generation was not merely asking for a larger slice of an existing women’s sports market.

It was constructing the market.

That is why the language of “deserve” is insufficient. Equal prize money at a flagship tournament was also an investment signal: women’s competition is part of the main product.

The signal affected sponsors, broadcasters, young athletes, and other tournaments.

Sports institutions create value partly by deciding what they will treat as valuable.

King forced one of the biggest institutions in tennis to make the decision publicly.

Her 1973 season therefore contains almost every element of modern athlete power: competition, collective organization, threat of withholding labor, sponsor negotiation, television spectacle, gender politics, direct public voice, and institution-building.

It is difficult to imagine a more consequential year for the business of women’s sports.

And yet the most famous image remains the theatrical match.

That is fitting.

Sport needs a scene.

History needs the structure behind it.

Billie Jean King gave us both.
