# Chapter 9 — The Champion the Story Almost Lost

Willie Simms led North America in victories in 1893 and 1894.

He won the Belmont Stakes in both years. He won the Kentucky Derby in 1896 and again in 1898. He won the Preakness in 1898. The National Museum of Racing and Hall of Fame credits him with 1,173 victories from 4,701 mounts and notes that he briefly rode in England, where his short-stirrup style helped introduce a riding position that would soon become associated with American jockeys.

If athletic importance were allocated according to what people knew at the time, Simms would be easy to recognize.

The strange part is that he needs recovery at all.

The 1890s belong to Willie Simms because his career sits at the peak of one American sporting tradition and just before its deliberate narrowing. He was a Black athlete dominating a national sport during the decade when the United States was hardening a racial order that would soon make careers like his far less possible.

The contradiction is almost perfectly timed.

In 1896, the Supreme Court decided *Plessy v. Ferguson*, blessing the legal architecture of “separate but equal.” Across the South, Black voting rights were being attacked through law and violence. Lynching remained a terror system. Segregation was not a fading residue of slavery. It was being institutionalized with new energy.

In the same period, Willie Simms was winning the Kentucky Derby.

Sport did not stand outside that racial order, but it did not always reproduce it on the same schedule.

Horse racing had inherited a deep tradition of Black expertise. Owners still hired Black jockeys because they could win. The betting public still cared about results. Elite performance could keep a door open after other doors were closing.

Then power caught up.

The top American tracks became increasingly hostile to Black riders. White jockeys used intimidation and violence. Owners shifted opportunities. Licensing and club structures allowed exclusion to harden. By the early twentieth century, the Black jockey who had once been a standard figure at the highest level of American racing became rare.

Simms therefore represents a kind of historical high-water mark.

That does not make him a tragic figure only. He was a champion.

He won too much for the chapter to become merely a preface to exclusion.

In 1893 he won 182 races. In 1894 he won 228 from 688 mounts, a remarkable 33.1 percent. He captured prestigious stakes for leading owners. His victories were not confined to one track or one lucky horse. He was a professional at the center of the sport’s competitive economy.

The term “professional” now fits more comfortably than it did for Abe Hawkins. Simms was not enslaved. His career belonged to a postwar commercial racing system in which riders could earn money, cultivate relationships with powerful stables, and build reputations that crossed regions.

That freedom was real and incomplete.

A jockey’s economic power still depended on access to mounts. Owners and trainers controlled the horses. Track officials controlled licenses. Other riders could affect safety. A brilliant jockey with no mount has no race.

This is one reason gatekeeping matters so much in sports labor. Talent can be measurable and still require institutional permission to become visible.

The same structure appears elsewhere in different form. A baseball player needs a roster spot. A quarterback needs a coach willing to let him play quarterback. A woman tennis player needs tournaments willing to offer meaningful competition and prize money. A boxer needs promoters and opponents. An Olympic athlete needs federation eligibility.

Sports meritocracy is always mediated.

Simms had enough access to become dominant. Later Black jockeys had less.

His trip to England adds another layer to his importance.

Racing historians credit Simms with demonstrating the American short-stirrup riding style there before riders such as Tod Sloan popularized it more widely. The change looks technical: raise the stirrups, shift the rider’s position, reduce the amount of body moving against the horse, and change how the jockey travels with the animal at speed.

Technique becomes culture when it crosses borders.

American athletes are often treated as exporters only in the twentieth century, when basketball, jazz-age celebrity, Hollywood, television, sneakers, and the Olympics create recognizable global brands. Simms was exporting technique in the nineteenth.

That matters because sporting influence is not only who wins an international event. It is whose method other athletes copy.

The “American seat” became associated with a more forward position and shorter stirrups. Racing technique was evolving through bodies before biomechanics could explain every advantage in laboratory language.

An athlete feels an efficiency first.

The sport explains it later.

This pattern is common. Players discover usable truths through practice that analysts eventually formalize. Pitchers manipulate spin before high-speed cameras quantify it. Basketball players stretch shooting range before models calculate expected value. High jumpers change body position before every coach agrees on the physics.

Simms was part of that empirical tradition.

He also had the difficult job of being visible in a sport where the horse absorbs most of the mythology.

The names people remember from racing are often animals: Secretariat, Man o’ War, Seabiscuit. The jockey’s job is paradoxical. Perfect execution can make the ride disappear. The horse looks inevitable.

That visual hierarchy makes it easier for human sporting history to lose riders.

Simms’s record resists disappearance because the same jockey keeps appearing beside winning horses.

That is how judgment becomes visible statistically. One mount can be luck. A pattern of major victories across years and horses points toward skill.

The numbers are especially valuable because the late nineteenth-century archive is becoming denser. Newspapers cover racing intensely. Results are better preserved. Institutions are maturing. Simms can be compared more securely than Austin Curtis could be a century earlier.

Even here, precision needs care. Complete racing records from the era are not identical to modern databases. Hall of Fame figures are built from available evidence. Percentages should be treated as the best reconstructed record, not as surveillance from an age of digital timing chips.

The broad conclusion is not fragile.

Simms was one of the leading jockeys in America.

The more difficult question is why he is not one of the obvious names in standard American sports history.

Part of the answer is that horse racing’s cultural share declined relative to baseball and later football and basketball. A dominant nineteenth-century jockey can disappear from general memory even while remaining famous inside racing history.

Part is the sport’s later racial image. Once Black jockeys were largely forced out, later generations did not see people like Simms regularly enough for the past to feel continuous.

And part is the way national memory selects pioneers.

We like a first because a first is easy to label. Oliver Lewis: first Kentucky Derby winner. Jackie Robinson: first Black player in modern Major League Baseball. Althea Gibson: barrier breaker in elite tennis. A dominant athlete who is not the first can be harder to package.

Simms was not a symbolic exception.

He was what normal excellence looked like before exclusion changed the norm.

That may be the more radical fact.

The story of Black participation in American sport is often told as a sequence of doors opening. Simms belongs to a door closing.

That reversal changes how we understand progress.

If inclusion had existed and was later destroyed, then later integration cannot be described as simply the natural maturation of a previously immature sport. The institution first lost capacity.

It became less meritocratic.

Racing deprived itself of skilled competitors because racial power mattered more than competitive optimization.

This is worth emphasizing because sports organizations like to describe themselves as ruthless markets for talent. Winning supposedly washes away prejudice. If you are good enough, somebody will find you.

History says otherwise.

Institutions can prefer discrimination to performance for a long time.

Major League Baseball excluded Black players for decades despite the existence of elite Black talent. The NFL restricted opportunities for Black quarterbacks long after ability was obvious. Golf and tennis protected club structures that limited access. Coaching and executive ranks across sports have repeatedly lagged behind player integration.

Competition creates an incentive toward meritocracy. It does not guarantee meritocracy.

Simms is a case study in the difference.

Owners who wanted to win could see his value. The wider racial order could still decide that careers like his should become harder to sustain.

This tension between local incentives and institutional norms is one reason change can be uneven. One team integrates because winning matters. Another resists because social cost matters more. One promoter hires the best fighter. Another refuses an interracial title bout. One tournament increases prize money. Another defends tradition.

Sports do not change as a single organism.

They change through conflicts among people who value different things.

Simms’s presence at the highest level shows one set of incentives temporarily winning.

His successors’ exclusion shows another set later prevailing.

The 1890s also belong to him because the decade is frequently remembered through baseball’s rapid professionalization and through boxing’s transition from John L. Sullivan to James J. Corbett. Those are important stories. Yet choosing another white male star from those sports would reproduce the very memory bias this book is trying to inspect.

The test is not diversity for its own sake.

The test is importance relative to the actual sporting landscape.

Horse racing remained one of America’s major sports. Simms was its leading rider in consecutive seasons and won the biggest races. His career connected domestic dominance, international technical influence, and the racial transformation of a foundational American sport.

That is an unusually strong case.

It also creates the right transition into the twentieth century.

The next selection, Jack Johnson, will confront a color line that boxing had hardened around the heavyweight championship. Simms shows us the quieter version first: exclusion not through one dramatic denial but through the shrinking of opportunity until a once-common Black elite presence becomes historically surprising.

Johnson’s battle will be visible because the heavyweight title is singular.

Racing’s exclusion is distributed across mounts, licenses, owners, tracks, rider behavior, and opportunity.

Distributed discrimination can be harder to narrate because there is no one door with a sign on it.

The result can be just as complete.

By the time twentieth-century mass media begins to define national sports memory, Black jockeys are already being removed from the center of the picture. The camera arrives after the room has been rearranged.

That sequence matters.

It explains why visual archives can mislead us about demographic history. Film makes what it records feel more real than what preceded it. The twentieth century leaves moving images; the nineteenth leaves photographs, illustrations, print, and ledgers. We unconsciously give the filmed world more weight.

Simms’s career demands that we correct for the camera.

He was not obscure when he was winning.

The obscurity came later.

There is something fitting about a jockey being the figure who exposes how history can move the finish line. Simms accomplished the things sports usually promise will guarantee remembrance: he won repeatedly, won prestigious events, led his peers, and influenced technique.

Yet remembrance depends on more than performance.

It depends on whether institutions keep telling the story.

Baseball built a vast archive of itself. Boxing built lineages of heavyweight champions. College football created annual rituals and record books. Horse racing preserved its own history, but general American sports culture shifted its attention elsewhere.

When attention moves, memory follows.

This book is partly an argument that importance should not.

The most important athlete of a decade should be recoverable even when the modern sports menu has changed.

Willie Simms earned that recovery on the track.

The 1890s did not need to invent a Black sports superstar.

They already had one.
