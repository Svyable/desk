# Chapter 11 — The Country Could Not Decide What Jim Thorpe Was

Jim Thorpe’s 1912 Olympic victories were so overwhelming that the argument afterward was not whether he had been the best athlete in Stockholm.

The argument was whether he was allowed to count as the best athlete.

He won the pentathlon. He won the decathlon. He did it across a range of events that required speed, strength, jumping, throwing, endurance, and the ability to remain competent while the competition kept changing shape. King Gustav V of Sweden is remembered for telling him he was the greatest athlete in the world.

Then the Amateur Athletic Union and the International Olympic Committee removed his victories from the record after learning he had been paid modest amounts to play minor-league baseball.

Thorpe takes the 1910s because his career turns a sporting triumph into a question about who gets to define legitimacy.

He was a member of the Sac and Fox Nation. He had attended the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, an institution within a federal system designed to assimilate Native children by separating them from languages, communities, families, and cultural practices. Carlisle’s football team became nationally famous, and Thorpe became its extraordinary center of gravity.

The country celebrated an Indigenous athlete produced inside an institution whose stated purpose was to transform Indigenous people into something white America found more acceptable.

No metaphor is required.

The contradiction is already complete.

Thorpe’s importance begins before Stockholm because Carlisle football showed how sport could become a stage for institutional rebellion even when the institution itself was coercive. Native students from a school built around assimilation could defeat elite colleges whose students represented the most privileged parts of American society.

The results were irresistible to journalists.

A football field turned the hierarchy upside down for an afternoon.

Again, that did not reverse the hierarchy outside the field. Native nations did not gain sovereignty because Carlisle won a game. Federal policy did not become just because Thorpe could run through Harvard or Army. But the public image mattered because it made Indigenous excellence visible inside a national culture that frequently treated Native peoples as remnants of the past.

Thorpe was violently contemporary.

He could do almost everything.

Track and field is the obvious evidence. Football made him famous. He also played professional baseball and later professional football. The multiplicity has become part of his myth, but it is grounded in a real range that modern specialization makes difficult to imagine.

What matters historically is not whether he could beat every specialist at every sport. He could not.

What mattered is that one athlete could become credible at the elite level across radically different games.

That versatility made Thorpe legible as “natural” talent, a description that contained admiration and racial stereotype at the same time.

Sports writing has long used the word “natural” unevenly. White athletes are often praised for intelligence, preparation, leadership, or craft; athletes from marginalized groups have frequently been described as physically gifted in ways that erase labor and learning. Indigenous athletes were especially vulnerable to romantic language that connected physical ability to imagined racial essence.

Thorpe did not arrive in Stockholm from the woods with no training.

He trained. He competed. He learned events. He had coaches. He operated within one of the country’s most innovative football programs. Talent was real. So was work.

The “natural athlete” story made his dominance easier for white America to admire because it placed excellence in the body rather than in a modern Indigenous mind navigating complex institutions.

Then amateurism gave those institutions a way to punish him.

Thorpe had played minor-league baseball for money in North Carolina. The sums were modest. The violation was also common enough in an era when athletes sometimes used aliases to preserve amateur eligibility. Thorpe had played under his own name.

After the payments became public in 1913, his Olympic titles were removed.

The decision has to be understood in the context of amateurism as a class system as much as a sporting philosophy.

Amateur rules were presented as protection of pure competition from commercial contamination. In practice they often favored athletes who could afford to train and compete without being paid. A wealthy person could remain “amateur” because family resources supported the life. A working-class athlete who took money for labor could become professionally tainted.

The rule claimed neutrality.

The economic burden was not neutral.

Thorpe’s treatment became one of the most famous examples because the punishment erased extraordinary performances everybody had already witnessed.

The sport tried to make reality administratively invalid.

That is a powerful institutional act.

Sports organizations do not merely schedule contests. They define which contests count, which athletes are eligible, which records are official, and which identities enter the archive.

The medal is not the performance.

The medal is the institution’s certificate that the performance belongs to history.

Thorpe lost the certificate.

He did not lose what his body had done.

This distinction became the basis for a century-long campaign to restore his Olympic status. In 1983, the IOC gave his family replacement medals and reinstated him in a qualified way, listing him as co-champion. In 2022, after further advocacy and agreement involving the families of athletes elevated by the original decision, the IOC recognized Thorpe as the sole gold medalist in the pentathlon and decathlon.

The repair took 110 years.

That fact is part of his 1910s importance even though it belongs to the twenty-first century.

A sports institution can reverse an official decision after everyone directly involved is dead because records remain politically alive.

History is not only what happened.

It is what institutions currently certify happened.

Thorpe’s case shows why archival power matters. A person can be present in photographs, newspaper accounts, memories, and public mythology while the official table says something else. The dispute becomes a contest over which record has authority.

Modern sports audiences encounter smaller versions constantly. A title is vacated. A medal is stripped for doping. A record is disqualified because of wind. A race result changes after appeal. A championship is marked by scandal. The performance exists, but the institution changes how it is counted.

Thorpe’s case is not equivalent to a modern doping sanction. The point is structural: official sport is a system of recognition layered on top of physical action.

That layer can carry class, racial, political, and institutional assumptions.

His professional career after the Olympics adds another dimension.

Major League Baseball turned the amateur scandal into market value. The New York Giants signed Thorpe. His baseball career was less dominant than his football and track careers, which is a useful reminder that “greatest athlete” rhetoric can flatten sport-specific skill. Being extraordinary at many things did not make him the best hitter in the National League.

Football fit him better.

Thorpe played professionally and became the first president of the American Professional Football Association, the organization that would become the National Football League.

That administrative role is often buried beneath the medals, but it strengthens his importance enormously.

He was not merely a star used to draw attention to early professional football. His name helped legitimate an institution whose future value nobody could have fully imagined.

The NFL of the 1920s was not the NFL of the Super Bowl era. Professional football still competed with college football for prestige. Teams could be unstable. Finances were uncertain. The sport needed recognizable figures.

Thorpe’s celebrity was institutional capital.

This is another recurring American sports pattern: emerging leagues borrow credibility from athletes before athletes borrow credibility from leagues.

The mature NFL makes a rookie famous because the shield is already famous.

The early professional game needed Jim Thorpe to persuade people that the competition mattered.

The athlete can precede the institution.

Magic Johnson and Larry Bird will later help rescue and transform the NBA’s television appeal. Tiger Woods will radically increase attention around golf tournaments that already existed. Caitlin Clark in the 2020s will demonstrate how one player can alter television audiences, attendance, and economic expectations around women’s basketball.

Thorpe’s version happened when professional football was still trying to become a stable idea.

His Indigenous identity made that role even more complicated.

American popular culture often treated Native people as historical symbols rather than contemporary citizens. Sports teams themselves would later adopt Native names and imagery while real Native athletes and communities challenged the caricatures.

Thorpe’s celebrity offered a different kind of visibility: an Indigenous person whose modern accomplishment could not be contained inside nostalgia.

Yet publicity around him often used stereotypes anyway.

That is one of assimilation’s paradoxes. Success can grant access while the dominant culture interprets success through categories designed to reduce the person.

Thorpe could become “the greatest athlete in the world” and still be discussed as evidence of racial type.

The country could admire him without understanding him.

His Carlisle experience makes the problem more severe. The school gave him athletic opportunity, coaching, competition, and national visibility. It also belonged to a system built on coercive assimilation.

Both are true.

A mature history should be able to hold them without using athletic success to redeem the institution or institutional harm to deny the agency of the athlete.

People make real things inside bad systems.

Austin Curtis developed real horsemanship under slavery. Abe Hawkins became a genuine champion under slavery. Thorpe became a genuine athlete at Carlisle. The injustice of the system does not make the expertise unreal. The expertise does not make the system just.

That double truth is one of the book’s basic methods.

The 1910s also contain a strong alternative candidate: Jack Johnson remains heavyweight champion into the decade and defeats Jim Jeffries in 1910, arguably the single most racially explosive sports event of the period. If decades were rigid boxes that erased carryover, Johnson might deserve both the 1900s and 1910s just as Austin Curtis takes three early decades.

Thorpe narrowly wins because his significance touches more distinct institutional transformations: Indigenous representation, the federal boarding-school system, amateurism, the Olympics, multisport professionalism, and the founding era of professional football.

He also introduces a new national frame.

The Olympics allow American athletes to represent the country explicitly.

A baseball star represents a club. A boxer may be treated as national symbol, but the title is individual. An Olympian wears national identity as part of the event’s structure.

This changes the relationship between athlete and citizenship.

The country can claim an athlete’s victory as its own.

Thorpe’s gold medals were counted for the United States even though Native citizenship was legally and politically complex and many Indigenous people did not receive U.S. citizenship until the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924.

The Olympic team could use a Native athlete as national representative before the nation had resolved Native citizenship on equal terms.

That is not a footnote.

It is the same symbolic citizenship problem seen earlier with Molineaux and later with Owens and Louis, now built into an international event.

Sport can say “our champion” before law says “full citizen.”

The contradiction becomes even sharper because Thorpe’s amateur status was policed so aggressively. The nation was willing to claim his performance and then willing to erase it over compensation rules designed by institutions that did not carry the same economic pressures he did.

Belonging was conditional in both directions.

Thorpe’s life after peak competition was not a smooth celebrity retirement. He worked in various roles, appeared in films, struggled financially at times, and saw others profit from the growth of sports he had helped legitimate.

The most versatile athlete in the country was not insulated from precarity.

That should complicate any tendency to project modern superstar wealth backward.

Fame is not a pension.

The sports economy had learned how to create stars before it learned—or chose—to provide durable security for them.

In Thorpe’s case, the mismatch is especially stark because his name retained value for decades. He could be invoked as athletic legend while the person himself navigated an unstable post-playing life.

The athlete as cultural asset outlived the athlete’s market power.

Again, modern parallels are easy to find. Leagues celebrate retired players whose bodies paid costs not reflected in their compensation. Universities build traditions around athletes who left without degrees or wealth. Brands revive dead stars. Archives monetize memory.

The economics of legacy are rarely controlled by the person whose life created the legacy.

Thorpe could not control how his records were listed for most of the century after his death.

The 2022 restoration therefore feels like more than statistical housekeeping. It is a reminder that official memory is revisable and that revision can matter even when it arrives impossibly late for the athlete.

It also exposes how strange the original punishment had become.

A man who had won the events was not recognized as sole winner because he had once accepted small payments for baseball.

The line between pure amateur and contaminated professional had looked important enough in 1913 to erase two Olympic championships.

A century later it looked indefensible.

Sports rules age.

That is another form of importance: an athlete becomes the case through which later generations see the assumptions hidden inside an old rule.

Jim Thorpe was too large for the categories around him.

Amateur and professional.

Native and American.

Football player, baseball player, track athlete.

Schoolboy and international champion.

Symbol and person.

The institutions kept trying to put him in one box.

His career kept breaking the box.
