# Falsification and uncertainty notes — America, Played

The book’s central device is provocative by design: choose one most important American sports player for every decade.

That device becomes intellectually weak if every selection is protected from revision after the fact.

This file records what evidence would materially weaken or overturn the main judgments.

## General falsification rule

A decade selection should change if a rival athlete is shown, with better evidence, to have had a materially larger effect on at least two of the following without losing decisively on the others:

- competitive authority in the decade;
- institutional change;
- cultural reach relative to the communications environment of the time;
- downstream effect on later athletes or the sport;
- explanatory power for the distinctive American social/economic story of that decade.

A new statistic alone is not enough if the book’s claim is institutional importance. A newly recovered archive can be enough if it changes what we know about who was actually central to an early sport.

## Early decades — highest uncertainty

### Austin Curtis, 1770s–1790s

Would weaken the triple selection:

- credible archival evidence that Curtis’s racing reputation has been substantially overstated;
- a better-documented athlete whose interstate fame and sporting consequence were clearly greater in one of the three decades;
- evidence that the 1791 emancipation record has been misidentified or materially mischaracterized.

Would **not** by itself weaken the selection:

- discovering another excellent Black jockey from the period;
- proving that Curtis was not the single statistically best rider, because no complete statistical field exists and the selection is not based on that claim.

### Bill Richmond, 1800s

Would weaken the selection:

- evidence that the period assigned to his major professional emergence is materially wrong;
- a rival American athlete with clearly greater transregional or institutional effect in the 1800s.

### Tom Molineaux, 1810s

Would weaken the selection:

- serious evidence that the Cribb fights did not have the international scale or racial/national significance attributed to them;
- proof that the accepted biographical link to Virginia/enslavement is substantially mistaken.

The exact first-fight round count is **not** load-bearing and should not decide the chapter.

### Samuel Purdy, 1820s

Would weaken the selection:

- credible evidence that the Union Course crowd estimate or sectional framing is a much later invention unsupported by contemporary sources;
- a rival whose decade-level national effect clearly exceeds the 1823 match.

The 60,000 figure is already treated as an estimate, not a census.

### Cato, 1830s

This is one of the book’s most vulnerable selections.

Would overturn or materially revise:

- a stronger archive establishing a different athlete as clearly more important to national sport in the 1830s;
- primary evidence resolving Cato’s status/freedom chronology in a way that contradicts the current chapter;
- evidence that Cato’s role in the Wagner–Grey Eagle races has been substantially misattributed.

The chapter can survive the emancipation legend being false because the myth-making process is part of its argument. It cannot survive the athlete’s documented role in the races collapsing.

### Tom Hyer, 1840s

Would weaken:

- evidence that the Sullivan fight’s national/ethnic framing is mainly later projection rather than contemporary meaning;
- a rival athlete with substantially greater public reach during the decade.

### Abe Hawkins, 1850s

Would weaken:

- a major revision of the racing archive reducing Hawkins’s standing among elite American jockeys;
- evidence that the decade’s national sports attention was dominated by a different athlete with greater institutional effect.

Because the Hall of Fame now describes Hawkins as one of the leading American athletes around the Civil War, the baseline competitive claim is comparatively strong.

## Institutional turn

### Jim Creighton, 1860s

Would weaken:

- evidence that the pitching innovations credited to Creighton were already common and did not materially alter competitive expectations;
- proof that his supposed professional compensation is entirely legendary if the manuscript presents it too strongly.

The exact cause of death is deliberately non-load-bearing.

### Oliver Lewis, 1870s

Would weaken:

- evidence that the first Kentucky Derby’s later importance is being projected backward so aggressively that another athlete more clearly defined 1870s American sport;
- a reassessment showing Lewis’s role was historically ordinary rather than representative of a larger Black jockey tradition.

The latter is unlikely because the broader early-Derby record independently documents substantial Black jockey success.

### John L. Sullivan, 1880s

Would weaken:

- a demonstrated contemporary reach for King Kelly or another athlete exceeding Sullivan across national print, touring, and commercial celebrity;
- evidence that Sullivan’s national-fame “first” claims are later boxing promotion. The manuscript should therefore prefer “first great national sports hero of the modern United States” as interpretation, not unqualified database fact.

### Willie Simms, 1890s

Would weaken:

- revised racing records materially changing his 1893–94 leading-rider status;
- stronger evidence that baseball or boxing produced an athlete with substantially greater cross-sport national importance in the decade.

## Twentieth century — lower factual uncertainty, harder selection disputes

### Jack Johnson, 1900s

Would weaken the selection only with a very strong rival case. The core facts—the 1908 title, racial backlash, later Jeffries spectacle, media regulation context—are too central to be erased by modest factual corrections.

A correction to a Johnson anecdote should trigger prose repair, not necessarily a selection change.

### Jim Thorpe, 1910s

Would weaken:

- evidence materially changing the nature of his 1912 dominance or the amateurism dispute;
- a compelling argument that Jack Johnson’s 1910 Jeffries fight and championship significance should control the entire decade rather than the 1900s.

The 2022 restoration strengthens rather than creates Thorpe’s case.

### Babe Ruth, 1920s

Would weaken only if the book overstated causality. Ruth did not single-handedly create mass media, the live-ball era, or Yankees success. The selection survives as long as the narrower claim holds: performer and mass-media system amplified each other at unprecedented American scale.

### Jesse Owens, 1930s

Primary challenger: Joe Louis.

Would switch to Louis if a revised comparative analysis concludes the 1938 Schmeling rematch and Louis’s broader decade-long heavyweight status had a larger domestic cultural effect than Owens’s Berlin performance and its international political afterlife.

Do not let the selection depend on the simplified Hitler-handshake story.

### Joe Louis, 1940s

Primary challenger: Jackie Robinson.

Would switch to Robinson if the method weights direct institutional desegregation over the war/patriotism/segregated-military argument enough to make 1947 decisive for the decade.

The current architecture deliberately assigns Robinson to the 1950s to distinguish barrier breach from irreversible institutional normalization.

### Jackie Robinson, 1950s

Primary challenger: Willie Mays.

Would switch to Mays only if the book changes its definition of “importance” toward pure athletic greatness. Under the present institutional definition, Robinson’s continued presence and post-breakthrough civic role remain decisive.

## Athlete voice and organization

### Muhammad Ali, 1960s

Primary challenger: Bill Russell.

Russell’s championships, player activism, Boston racial context, and leadership make this a serious alternative. Ali remains the selection because the name/religion/war/refusal/license-loss sequence changed the public category of athlete citizenship nationally and internationally.

Would switch if a more rigorous comparative chapter demonstrates Russell’s direct civil-rights action plus basketball dominance had the greater decade-level institutional effect.

### Billie Jean King, 1970s

Primary challengers: Muhammad Ali, Hank Aaron, Kareem Abdul-Jabbar.

Would weaken if the WTA/equal-prize-money causal story is overstated as King acting alone. The correct response is to restore the roles of the Original 9, Gladys Heldman, fellow players, sponsors, and tournament leadership—not necessarily to change the selection.

Would change if another athlete’s 1970s effect is shown to be broader and more durable than the creation of collective economic power in women’s professional tennis.

### Magic Johnson, 1980s

This is one of the strongest modern selection disputes.

Primary challengers: Wayne Gretzky, Larry Bird, Martina Navratilova.

Would switch to Gretzky if the weighting moves toward unmatched sport-specific dominance and North American cultural effect. Would switch to Bird only if the rivalry is judged inseparable and Bird’s Boston/NBA effect exceeds Magic’s Showtime/position/entertainment impact. The manuscript explicitly avoids saying Magic alone saved the NBA.

### Michael Jordan, 1990s

Primary challengers: Tiger Woods, Mia Hamm.

Would weaken if the book treats Jordan as sole cause of NBA globalization or sneaker culture. Those are multi-causal systems. The selection survives on acceleration and concentration of effects around one player.

### Tiger Woods, 2000s

Primary challengers: Michael Phelps, Tom Brady.

Would switch to Phelps if Olympic performance and medal-scale cultural reach are weighted more heavily than Woods’s sport-wide economic/training/course effects. Would switch to Brady only if the decade analysis is expanded to include the long downstream NFL dynasty more heavily than contemporaneous 2000s effects.

### Serena Williams, 2010s

Primary challenger: LeBron James.

This requires an explicit countercase before final release.

Would switch to LeBron if the analysis concludes that player agency, free-agency power, athlete-owned media, basketball globalization, political voice, and championship influence collectively exceed Serena’s multi-axis effect on tennis, race, gender, motherhood, body politics, longevity, and athlete ownership.

Serena’s 2026 comeback is downstream evidence of career authorship; it must not be back-projected as if it happened in the 2010s.

## The unfinished 2020s

### Simone Biles — provisional

Primary challengers as of September 6, 2026:

- Shohei Ohtani
- Patrick Mahomes
- Caitlin Clark
- Katie Ledecky

The selection **must change** if a rival’s full-decade effect becomes clearly larger by the end of 2029.

The label **provisional** must remain in every edition released before January 1, 2030.

Biles’s case would materially weaken if:

- the manuscript inaccurately describes the physical/safety character of the twisties;
- Paris 2024 results are misstated;
- the book treats her as the sole origin of athlete mental-health advocacy;
- later 2020s events create a substantially stronger decade-defining case elsewhere.

## Thesis-level falsification

The larger book thesis is:

> Across 250 years, American sports history can be read as a long, uneven movement from athlete as property toward athlete sovereignty.

Evidence against that thesis would include:

- a better historical model showing that athlete control did not generally increase across legal status, mobility, compensation, voice, image rights, collective organization, and business ownership;
- evidence that the selected examples are cherry-picked exceptions while the median elite athlete’s control moved in the opposite direction;
- a stronger explanation in which institutional control merely changes form without producing meaningful increases in athlete agency.

The manuscript already concedes the last point partially: new power creates new constraints. The thesis is therefore not “athletes became free.” It is that the **bargaining position and recognized jurisdiction of the athlete expanded dramatically**, even as institutions found new ways to monetize and regulate that power.

If future evidence makes that narrower claim untenable, the book’s conclusion should change rather than protect the slogan.
