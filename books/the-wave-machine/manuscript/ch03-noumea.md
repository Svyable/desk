# Noumea

Before Auckland there was New Caledonia. Preliminary experiments near Noumea in 1944 appear in the historical accounts as the moment when an odd observation acquired bureaucratic momentum. The tests were encouraging enough that Admiral William Halsey's command supported further investigation by New Zealand.

Encouraging is one of the most dangerous words in experimental weapons work. It can mean that a predicted effect was observed. It can mean that an instrument moved in the expected direction. It can mean that the sponsor saw enough possibility to purchase the next experiment. None of those meanings establishes operational feasibility.

The Noumea phase belongs firmly in the documented history of Seal, but its role should be kept narrow. It justified more work. It did not settle scaling.

That distinction also protects the story from retrospective ridicule. Investigating wave generation in 1944 was not irrational. Amphibious warfare made coastlines strategic objects. Underwater explosives were mature technology. Hydrodynamic scaling was experimentally accessible. The military value of inundation was obvious. The question was whether these facts could be assembled into a practical device.

A program can be rational to begin and rational to abandon. Exotic-technology history becomes distorted when those two propositions are treated as opposites.
