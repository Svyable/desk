# Gibson's Observation

The origin story is almost offensively modest. An American naval officer, E. A. Gibson, reportedly noticed that blasting used around Pacific reefs could produce unexpectedly substantial waves. The observation did not require a new force of nature. It required curiosity about an ordinary one.

This is how military technology often starts: somebody sees an effect in the wrong context and asks whether the nuisance can be turned into a capability. Radar grew around inconvenient reflections. Penicillin grew around contamination. Here the object was a blast wave entering water and the possibility that geometry might make the resulting surface motion useful.

The leap from observation to weapon is where the book earns its keep. A coral-clearing explosion can make a conspicuous local wave. A tsunami is a different hydrodynamic object, with wavelengths often vastly larger than the water depth and with energy distributed through a long-wave mode that can cross ocean basins efficiently. Calling both phenomena waves does not make them interchangeable.

Gibson's observation was nevertheless legitimate enough to investigate. That matters. The sensible response to an odd physical effect is not embarrassment. It is measurement. The mistake comes later, when the existence of measurement is remembered as confirmation of the largest imagined application.

Project Seal's history repeatedly crosses that seam.
