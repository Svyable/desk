# Three Thousand Seven Hundred Explosions

The number survives because it is cinematic: roughly 3,700 experimental detonations are reported in later accounts of Seal. Repetition at that scale tells us something important, but not the thing the legend wants.

A campaign of thousands of small experiments can map a response surface. Charge mass, depth, spacing, delay, distance, and basin geometry can be changed while gauges record what follows. Such work can reveal optima and expose assumptions. Accounts of Seal say, for example, that the investigators learned shallow placement could be more useful for surface-wave production than a simplistic deep-charge model suggested.

But repeated small tests do not automatically validate a gigantic extrapolation. Scaling laws must be demonstrated, not merely hoped. Surface gravity, water depth, compressibility, cavitation, bubble dynamics, nonlinear breaking, and bottom interaction do not all preserve similarity under convenient laboratory ratios.

This is the central methodological lesson. The more spectacular the extrapolation, the more aggressively similarity itself must be audited.

Three thousand seven hundred explosions can be a sign of seriousness. They can also be a monument to how much remained unresolved.
