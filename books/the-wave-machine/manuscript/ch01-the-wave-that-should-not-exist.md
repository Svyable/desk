# The Wave That Should Not Exist

A secret weapon story usually arrives backward. First comes the enormous claim, then the missing documents, then a person who once knew a person, then a machine whose operating principle is permitted to remain foggy because classification is said to explain the fog. Project Seal is troublesome because it arrives in the opposite order. There are records. There were experiments. There was a mechanism simple enough to draw on a blackboard: put explosive energy into water and try to arrange the displacement so that a coherent wave reaches a coast.

That simplicity is deceptive. An explosion can move a great deal of water without making the particular long wave a military planner wants. Energy is not the same thing as coupling. A spectacular plume is not the same thing as a tsunami. A laboratory or harbor wave is not automatically a scaled-down strategic weapon. The missing step is the transfer function between source and shore.

Project Seal therefore gives us something rarer than a secret program. It gives us a secret program whose central claim can be forced back into public physics.

The first evidentiary class in this book is documentary: the wartime project existed, New Zealand and American authorities supported experimental work, and Thomas Leech's team conducted a large series of underwater-explosion tests. Contemporary and later archival accounts place the work around New Caledonia and the Whangaparaoa Peninsula. These facts do not depend on accepting later weapon folklore.

The second class is credible but incomplete reconstruction. Much of what the public knows comes through released files, later historical reporting, recollection, and summaries of the technical work. Numbers repeated in journalism—roughly 3,700 experimental explosions, and a proposed large array totaling around two million kilograms of explosive—matter, but they require the original reports before they should be treated as precision engineering specifications.

The third class is allegation. Later writers have connected Project Seal to broad ideas of tectonic warfare, weather control, artificial earthquakes, and deliberate modern disasters. Those connections are not established by the existence of Seal. They require their own mechanisms and evidence.

The fourth class is synthesis. Mine is that Project Seal is best understood as a natural experiment in the sociology of exotic weapons. A real classified program can be simultaneously more real and less capable than its legend. The archive can prove that somebody tried without proving that the thing worked at strategic scale.

This distinction sounds obvious until secrecy enters the room. Then the grammar changes. Failure becomes evidence that the successful version went deeper black. Missing records become evidence of importance. Abandonment becomes a cover story. Every negative observation is converted into a positive one. An argument constructed that way cannot lose.

Water is less accommodating. A proposed wave weapon has to specify source geometry, depth, bathymetry, spectrum, propagation, breaking, run-up, and the fraction of explosive energy that survives as the destructive mode of interest. Classification can hide measurements. It cannot repeal those variables.

That is where we begin: not with whether governments are capable of bizarre ideas. They plainly are. We begin with the harder question. What does the ocean permit?
