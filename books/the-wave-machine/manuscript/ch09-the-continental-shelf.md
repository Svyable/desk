# The Continental Shelf

A weapon aimed at a coastline must survive the coastline.

This sounds ridiculous until one notices how often theoretical wave weapons are evaluated at the source and not at the target. Between them sits the continental shelf, where depth changes, waves slow, wavelengths shorten, energy refracts, and breaking can consume the very structure the weapon needs.

Later U.S.-sponsored research on explosion-generated waves has been summarized as finding poor wave-making efficiency and substantial loss before useful coastal impact. The primary Office of Naval Research material should be recovered and annotated before publication; for now it belongs in the research queue, not in a confident numerical claim.

The larger point is robust. A target coastline is not a passive screen. It is part of the transfer function.

This makes a generalized tsunami weapon awkward. The planner cannot buy merely an explosive package. The planner buys reconnaissance, bathymetry, placement, timing, environmental prediction, and a target-specific propagation problem. The ocean becomes both medium and adversary.
