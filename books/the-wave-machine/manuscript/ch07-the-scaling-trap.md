# The Scaling Trap

Suppose a kilogram of explosive makes a measurable wave. Ten kilograms make a larger one. Somewhere in that sequence a planner asks the fatal question: how much explosive makes a city-sized wave?

The arithmetic answer is easy. The physical answer is not.

Explosions are efficient at producing shock, heat, bubble motion, fragmentation, and local displacement. A destructive far-field tsunami requires energy in long gravity waves that propagate toward shore rather than spending themselves near the source. The relevant efficiency can be miserably small. Worse, a coastline can destroy the neatness of a calculation through shoaling, refraction, breaking, shelf geometry, and harbor resonance.

This is why an energy comparison between an explosive and an earthquake can mislead. Earthquakes capable of major tsunamis move enormous areas of seabed coherently. The source dimensions are already suited to long waves. A compact explosion has a different spatial spectrum.

The weapon designer therefore faces a geometry tax. If a single compact source couples poorly, distribute the source. That is the logic behind the reported Seal concept of multiple large charges arranged offshore. But distribution introduces deployment, synchronization, depth control, detection, and logistical burdens.

Physics sends an invoice. Operations adds interest.
