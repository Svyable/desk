# Research Notes, Evidence Ledger, and Source Trail

## Evidence classes

**Class 1 — verified/declassified/documented.** Project Seal existed as a wartime U.S.–New Zealand research effort. Experimental underwater detonations were conducted in 1944–45. Thomas Leech led major New Zealand technical work. Released archival material and historical reporting document military sponsorship and postwar analysis.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete.** Public historical accounts report approximately 3,700 experiments and summarize a proposed multi-charge array totaling about two million kilograms of explosive as capable, under modeled conditions, of producing a large coastal wave. These figures should be checked directly against the complete original Seal reports before publication-grade treatment.

**Class 3 — disputed/unconfirmed.** Claims that Project Seal matured into a secretly operational strategic system, that it proves later tectonic or weather-control weapons, or that particular modern earthquakes or tsunamis were deliberate attacks are not established by the Seal record.

**Class 4 — synthesis/open conjecture.** The book proposes using Seal as a calibration case for exotic weapons: declassification can establish institutional interest without establishing operational success; claims should be decomposed into source, coupling, propagation, signature, and attribution burdens.

## Contradictions and incentive audit

The project had incentives for optimism: wartime urgency, amphibious-war relevance, sponsor interest, and the natural tendency of successful small experiments to encourage extrapolation. Later storytellers have a different incentive: the phrase `tsunami bomb` is more memorable than a complicated account of coupling efficiency and bathymetry. Skeptics can make the opposite error by assuming that because a practical strategic system looks implausible, the documented experiments must themselves have been frivolous. Neither shortcut is acceptable.

The strongest counterevidence to a practical weapon is physical and operational: compact explosions couple imperfectly into long gravity waves; target bathymetry matters; large distributed charge arrays impose extraordinary deployment burdens; and spectacular local water motion does not establish useful far-field inundation.

## Chronology

- 1944 — preliminary work near Noumea follows E. A. Gibson's observation about blast-generated waves.
- May–June 1944 — New Zealand authorities organize expanded work; experiments begin around Whangaparaoa.
- 1944–45 — thousands of underwater-explosion trials are reported.
- January 1945 — the principal wartime experimental phase winds down as strategic priorities change.
- 1946–50 — postwar correspondence and technical analysis continue; the final wave-generation work is compiled.
- 1999–2000 — released files bring Project Seal to broad public attention.
- 2012–13 — renewed archival reporting popularizes the `tsunami bomb` story internationally.

## Defeat conditions

The book's synthesis should be revised if original Seal documents show that commonly reported scale, geometry, or conclusions are materially wrong; if later controlled work demonstrates efficient, operationally realistic explosive coupling into destructive long waves at strategic range; or if independently authenticated evidence establishes an operational geophysical weapon with a causal chain absent from the public literature.

The point is not to protect the thesis. The point is to make it possible for evidence to beat it.
