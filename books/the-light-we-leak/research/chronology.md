# Chronology

- **1923** — Alexander Gurwitsch reports the onion-root experiments that become the foundation of “mitogenetic radiation,” proposing weak ultraviolet emission associated with proliferating tissue and capable of affecting mitosis in a receiver.
- **Late 1920s–early 1930s** — Mitogenetic-radiation research expands internationally across onion roots, yeast, bacteria, chemical reactions, blood, tumors, and nerve preparations. Biological detectors are commonly used because direct physical detection is difficult.
- **1933** — Gurwitsch publishes a reply in *Nature* defending his laboratory’s work on mitogenetic radiation of nerve against A. V. Hill’s criticism.
- **1937** — *Nature* summarizes the Hollaender–Claus National Research Council investigation as an extensive negative study using both biological and physical detection and notes that roughly six hundred papers had already appeared.
- **1938** — Glasser and Barth publish contrasting experience from the Cleveland Clinic, illustrating live disagreement over reproducibility and detection methods.
- **1939** — Hollaender’s *Radiology* review frames the field’s key unresolved issue as reliable independent demonstration. Gurwitsch and L. Gurwitsch publish a *Nature* letter explicitly connecting the phenomenon to ultraviolet chemiluminescence.
- **Mid-twentieth century** — Mitogenetic radiation loses mainstream momentum. Weak biological luminescence continues to be studied through other experimental lineages as detector technology improves.
- **1970s–1980s** — Fritz-Albert Popp and collaborators popularize the term “biophoton” and advance stronger claims involving coherence, delayed luminescence, DNA, and biological regulation.
- **1984** — Popp et al. publish “Biophoton emission. New evidence for coherence and DNA as source” in *Cell Biophysics*.
- **1988** — *Experientia* publishes a cluster of papers/reviews on biophoton emission, including historical discussion and Popp’s “Physical aspects of biophotons.”
- **1999** — Kobayashi et al. publish in-vivo imaging of spontaneous UPE from rat brain correlated with cerebral energy metabolism and oxidative stress.
- **2009** — Kobayashi, Kikuchi, and Okamura image UPE from the human body and report a diurnal rhythm in five volunteers.
- **2011** — Rastogi and Pospíšil publish human-skin work tying UPE to oxidative metabolic processes, oxygen, and antioxidant defenses.
- **2013** — Prasad and Pospíšil publish two-dimensional UPE imaging across microbial, plant, and animal cells. Kučera and Cifra publish a quantitative critique of cell-to-cell optical signaling, emphasizing weak flux and ambient-noise constraints.
- **2014** — Cifra and Pospíšil publish a major review of UPE mechanisms, properties, detection, and applications, consolidating oxidative chemiluminescence as the modern physical framework.
- **2015** — Volodyaev and Beloussov publish a modern reassessment of the historical mitogenetic effect and argue that parts of the question deserve renewed testing.
- **2020** — Tsuchida et al. report characteristic spectral patterns in UVA-induced UPE from skin-relevant biomolecules and tissue, supporting a chemically plural source picture.
- **2023** — Mould et al. report non-chemical effects between physically separated mitochondrial preparations, including dependence on ambient light, and propose light as a candidate carrier while leaving the mechanism unresolved.
- **2024** — Babcock publishes an open-quantum-systems theoretical treatment revisiting Gurwitsch’s onion experiment as a quantum-biology prototype. A separate 2024 review surveys biophoton emission in neuronal communication and neurodegenerative disease.
- **2026** — Reviews continue to assess UPE both as a candidate cellular-communication phenomenon and as an emerging neural-metabolism biomarker. Neither review status nor historical continuity establishes endogenous optical signaling without direct causal experiments.

## Interpretation guard

This chronology records publication and research-history events. It does not convert the continued existence of a research topic into evidence that its strongest claims are true. In particular, the persistence of coherence, communication, or mitogenetic hypotheses after the 1930s is not itself evidence for those mechanisms.
