# Chemistry That Glows

It is difficult to preserve mystery around a photon once you can draw the reaction pathway that produced it.

That is not the same as saying the pathway is simple.

Oxidative metabolism is full of molecular accidents with consequences. Oxygen is indispensable to most complex life and chemically unruly enough that cells devote substantial machinery to handling its reactive derivatives. Superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, lipid peroxyl radicals: the vocabulary sounds like a list of villains because uncontrolled oxidation damages membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids. Yet reactive oxygen species are also normal products and, at controlled levels, participants in signaling.

Ultraweak photon emission sits in this borderland between metabolism and damage.

One important route begins with lipid peroxidation. Polyunsaturated fatty acids in membranes are vulnerable to radical attack. Once initiated, lipid peroxidation can propagate through chain reactions and produce unstable intermediates. Decomposition of lipid hydroperoxides can form triplet-excited carbonyl species. Those excited molecules may return to their ground state by emitting photons, commonly in the near-ultraviolet or visible range. Energy transfer can also produce singlet oxygen, which has characteristic dimol emission bands. Proteins and nucleic acids participate in related oxidative chemistry and can contribute to the spectral mixture.

The resulting light is weak because photon emission is a minor branch among many relaxation pathways. Most chemical energy does not leave as a detectable photon. Enough does.

This matters for the signaling argument because the photons are not generated by a neatly isolated transmitter. Their production is entangled with redox state, membrane composition, oxygen tension, antioxidant defenses, mitochondrial activity, inflammation, and injury. If a receiver responds to UPE, it may be responding indirectly to the physiological state of the source. That makes the emission potentially informative even if the source never evolved to “send” anything.

There is a semantic trap here. Biological signaling is often described in intentional language because it is convenient. Cells “send” cytokines. Hormones “tell” tissues what to do. A receptor “reads” a ligand. No consciousness is implied. But the metaphor hides an evolutionary distinction between a molecule released as part of a regulated communication system and a by-product that another system happens to exploit.

Carbon dioxide is a useful analogy. It is a metabolic product. It is also sensed exquisitely by physiology. Changes in carbon dioxide alter pH, ventilation, vascular tone, and behavior. The molecule does not have to be secreted as a message in order for its concentration to carry information that organisms evolved to use.

A photon could occupy the same logical category.

That possibility is much less demanding than the grander claim that organisms maintain coherent photonic communication networks. It requires only three things: a sufficiently predictable relation between source state and emitted photons, a receiver sensitive to some feature of that emission, and a fitness-relevant response. No optical nervous system is necessary.

But even this weaker hypothesis faces serious quantitative constraints.

A bulk emission rate reported at the tissue surface cannot be treated as the photon flux at a target molecule. Photons are emitted in different directions. Tissue absorbs and scatters light strongly and nonuniformly by wavelength. Water, hemoglobin, melanin, flavins, porphyrins, cytochromes, and other chromophores shape the optical environment. A signal generated one micrometer away is a different proposition from one expected to cross centimeters of tissue.

The local geometry may therefore be everything.

Consider mitochondria. They are major sites of oxidative metabolism and reactive oxygen production. They also sit in dense intracellular environments containing molecules capable of absorbing light. If mitochondrial oxidative events generate photons, nearby chromophores may see a different flux from an external detector placed beyond the cell. The external measurement is a lower-dimensional leakage sample of an internal radiative field filtered by tissue.

That observation is sometimes used to leap directly to intracellular optical communication. It should instead motivate measurement.

The correct question is not whether external UPE counts look too small. It is whether physically realistic intracellular generation rates, spectra, path lengths, absorption cross-sections, and competing noise sources permit a receptor-level effect. That calculation is possible in principle. It is also surprisingly absent from many ambitious discussions of biophotons.

Numbers are rude to metaphors.

Suppose a tissue emits one hundred photons per second per square centimeter at its surface. That value alone tells us almost nothing about a receptor buried in the tissue. We need the source density and depth. We need the fraction of internally generated photons that escape. We need the receptor’s effective absorption cross-section. We need the probability that absorption triggers a downstream event. We need the baseline rate of the same downstream event from thermal, chemical, or electrical causes. We need to know whether the signal is integrated across seconds, minutes, or hours.

A biological system can compensate for weak inputs with gain. Phototransduction is the obvious demonstration. A single absorbed photon in a rod cell can trigger a cascade affecting many downstream molecules. But gain brings false positives unless the system also suppresses noise. The retina pays heavily for its sensitivity through specialized anatomy and biochemistry. If generic cells possess comparable optical sensitivity, there should be molecular evidence of the machinery or at least reproducible action spectra and pharmacological perturbations.

This is where oxidative chemistry gives us an experimental advantage.

Because UPE is linked to redox processes, the source can be manipulated. Increase oxidative stress and emission often rises. Add antioxidants and emission can fall. Change oxygen. Inhibit segments of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Irradiate tissue. Alter temperature within controlled bounds. If a putative receiver changes in parallel, one can then ask whether the response follows the photons themselves or the other consequences of the perturbation.

The distinction is brutal because almost every manipulation of UPE also manipulates biology.

Antioxidants alter cellular signaling independent of light. Mitochondrial inhibitors change ATP production, membrane potential, metabolites, heat, and reactive species. Light exposure can create photochemistry. Oxygen affects respiration. A correlation between photon count and biological outcome therefore does not establish photons as the causal intermediate.

The ideal experiment needs orthogonal control: change photon transmission without changing the source chemistry, then restore photons artificially without restoring the other source products.

This is the logic Gurwitsch’s quartz barrier was reaching toward. Modern instrumentation makes it possible to do much better.

First characterize the source physically. Then insert a spectrally defined optical path while controlling temperature, volatiles, electromagnetic coupling, and vibration. If blocking the relevant photons abolishes the receiver effect, replace the biological source with a calibrated light source matched in spectrum, intensity, and temporal pattern. If the artificial source restores the effect, the case becomes much stronger. If ten times the natural photon flux is required, the natural mechanism becomes less plausible. If a million times is required, the story changes completely.

Chemistry gives the field its photons. Quantitation decides whether biology can afford to care about them.

The irony is that the more ordinary the source becomes, the more demanding the functional claim should become. A mysterious radiation can be blamed for anything. Oxidative chemiluminescence comes with units.
