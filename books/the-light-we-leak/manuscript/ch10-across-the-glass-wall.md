# Across the Glass Wall

The experiments that matter most are the ones that try to make two biological systems interact without touching.

Put one culture near another. Separate them with a transparent barrier. Perturb the first. Watch the second. If the receiver changes, replace the transparent barrier with an opaque one. If the effect disappears, an electromagnetic explanation becomes tempting.

Variations of this design appear throughout the distant-cell-interaction literature. Some studies report altered growth, metabolism or stress responses in optically coupled cultures. Reviews have collected experiments involving quartz windows, glass walls, cell containers placed side by side, and configurations intended to eliminate chemical exchange.

The design is clever because it appears to isolate light.

It usually does not.

Two nearby cultures share more than photons. They share temperature fluctuations, vibration, electromagnetic noise from incubators, gas composition if the chambers are not truly isolated, handling schedules, illumination history and the experimenter's timing. Transparent and opaque materials differ in thermal conductivity and surface properties. A foil wrap can change temperature. A black barrier can alter radiative heat transfer. Quartz and glass transmit different ultraviolet bands but also differ in other physical properties.

At weak effect sizes, these details are not annoyances. They are rival mechanisms.

The 2013 analysis by Kučera and Cifra is valuable because it asks what optical communication would require physically. How many photons are emitted? How many reach the receiver? What is the ambient photon background? Are the wavelengths biologically effective? The authors were not arguing that electromagnetic interaction is impossible. They were arguing that claims of light-mediated signaling had rarely been forced through a quantitative channel model.

That criticism still bites.

A decisive experiment would separate source and receiver more aggressively. Place each in independently controlled chambers. Route only the optical channel through a fiber or a window with known transmission. Record the source emission simultaneously. Use a shutter controlled by software unknown to the person handling samples. Include transparent sham paths that terminate at a beam dump. Swap in calibrated synthetic light matched to the measured source flux. Randomize conditions. Analyze before unblinding.

If the receiver responds only when the measured optical stream arrives, then the result is no longer “cells placed near each other changed.” It is an identified channel.

The intensity must also match biology. Many photobiomodulation experiments use light doses enormously above spontaneous ultraweak emission. Showing that a wavelength affects cells under a lamp proves photosensitivity, not endogenous communication. The relevant replay must deliver the same order of photons as the biological source.

This constraint is severe enough that it may kill many proposals. It may also reveal local geometries where the usual macroscopic measurements are misleading. Two membranes separated by micrometers experience a different solid angle than two flasks separated by centimeters. A source embedded in a structured tissue may couple differently from a dish emitting into free space.

The glass-wall experiments are therefore not worthless because they are imperfect. They are prototypes. They expose the experimental object that needs to be isolated.

The historical irony is satisfying. Gurwitsch began with a window because he wanted to discriminate radiation from contact. A century later, the window remains the problem. It lets one thing through while changing several others. The entire field has been trying to turn that crude optical boundary into a channel with known properties.

Until it does, distant interaction remains evidence class two at best: credible enough to justify better experiments, incomplete enough that testimony from the culture dish should not be turned into proof of a photonic language.
