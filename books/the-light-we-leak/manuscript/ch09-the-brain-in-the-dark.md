# The Brain in the Dark

The brain is an obvious place to go looking for weak light and a dangerous place to interpret what you find.

It consumes a disproportionate share of the body’s energy. Neurons maintain ion gradients, recycle neurotransmitters, move vesicles, remodel synapses, and demand continuous mitochondrial work. High oxygen use and intense metabolism create abundant opportunities for reactive oxygen chemistry. If UPE is partly an optical trace of oxidative metabolism, neural tissue should produce it.

Experiments indicate that it does.

In 1999 Masaki Kobayashi and colleagues reported two-dimensional imaging of spontaneous ultraweak photon emission from a rat brain. Emission intensity varied with conditions related to cerebral energy metabolism and oxidative stress and correlated with electrophysiological activity measured at the cortical surface. Later studies and reviews have extended the idea that neural UPE changes with metabolic state, glutamatergic activity, oxidative stress, ischemia, and disease models.

The conservative interpretation is powerful: photons may offer a noninvasive or minimally invasive readout of neural redox metabolism.

The ambitious interpretation is irresistible: perhaps neurons use photons to communicate.

The distance between those statements is the entire chapter.

Brains invite explanatory excess because they are the organ in which correlation with activity feels like information. If a liver emits more photons when metabolism rises, nobody immediately proposes that hepatic thought is optical. If cortical emission covaries with EEG, the vocabulary of signaling arrives almost automatically.

But any metabolic variable that tracks neural activity may correlate with EEG. Blood flow does. Oxygen use does. glucose utilization does. heat does. None of those correlations alone establishes that the variable is part of the information-processing channel.

The same caution applies to spectral changes. Oxidative chemistry can shift with mitochondrial state, neurotransmitter activity, or tissue injury, producing changes in photon emission. A receiver outside the brain might use such shifts diagnostically even if no neuron inside the brain cares about them.

Recent reviews have nevertheless explored a more adventurous possibility: endogenous photons could participate in neuronal communication, perhaps through axons, myelin, microtubules, mitochondria, or photosensitive molecules. Some theoretical papers model neural tissue as though optical propagation could occur in waveguide-like structures. Other work asks whether mitochondrial UPE can influence neighboring organelles or cells.

These hypotheses are not all equally evidentially mature.

A useful filter is to ask what each proposal has actually demonstrated. Showing that a structure can guide externally introduced light under laboratory conditions does not show that endogenous photons enter, remain in, and meaningfully modulate that structure in vivo. Showing that neurons emit photons does not identify a receiver. Showing that a receptor molecule can absorb a wavelength does not establish sufficient natural photon flux. Showing a mathematical possibility is not a physiological measurement.

The brain is full of things that could happen.

Biology narrows that set to what happens often enough, reliably enough, and with enough consequence to matter.

There is also an anatomical problem. Neural communication already possesses exceptionally fast and precise channels. Action potentials propagate along axons. Neurotransmitters cross synapses. Gap junctions directly couple cells. Neuromodulators operate over broader spatial and temporal scales. Glia coordinate chemical and electrical environments. Any endogenous optical channel must add something those systems do not already provide or interact with them in a measurable way.

This does not make optical signaling impossible. Evolution often layers channels rather than replacing them. Nitric oxide diffuses where synapses do not. Endocannabinoids travel backward across synapses. Volume transmission complements point-to-point release. A photon could in principle serve a niche function with a different speed, geometry, or chemical coupling.

But “light is fast” is not enough. A single photon moving quickly through tissue does not create a high-bandwidth communication system if photons are rarely emitted, mostly absorbed, or impossible to distinguish from background chemistry. Information rate depends on signal generation, channel loss, coding, receiver sensitivity, and error—not merely propagation velocity.

This is where quantitative information theory could be useful, provided it is used as a calculator rather than a metaphysics. Given measured photon fluxes, tissue transmission, receptor efficiency, and noise, what is the maximum plausible information rate? The answer might be vanishingly small. That would not rule out a trigger mechanism in which rare events matter. It would rule out some grander descriptions of an optical neural network.

The receiver remains the decisive missing object.

In ordinary vision, we know the molecules. Opsins bind retinal. Photon absorption changes molecular conformation. A G-protein cascade amplifies the event. Ion channels respond. The anatomy concentrates and preprocesses light. Mutations alter function. The system has an action spectrum that makes physical sense.

For endogenous neural UPE, no comparably established chain has been demonstrated.

Candidate chromophores abound because cells contain many light-sensitive molecules. Flavins absorb blue and ultraviolet light. Cytochromes absorb visible and near-infrared wavelengths. Cryptochromes participate in light sensing in many organisms and have become central to magnetoreception research in birds. Porphyrins absorb strongly. But a candidate absorber is not a receptor unless natural photons reach it at functional rates and its state changes a relevant pathway.

One can turn that requirement into an experiment.

Measure UPE from a defined neural preparation while independently measuring activity. Identify a candidate receptor with an absorption spectrum overlapping the emission. Manipulate the receptor genetically or pharmacologically without changing source metabolism. Place an optical barrier or absorber in a geometry that interrupts photons while preserving other coupling. Then deliver a calibrated artificial source matched to the measured endogenous spectrum and flux. If the biological effect disappears when the receptor or optical path is removed and returns when matched photons are restored, the hypothesis begins to earn the word signaling.

The experiment is difficult because manipulating one variable in neural tissue tends to disturb ten others. But difficulty is not an excuse for replacing the missing causal chain with suggestive vocabulary.

A 2026 review in *Neurology* framed UPE more modestly as an emerging noninvasive biomarker of neural metabolism. That direction may prove clinically important regardless of whether neurons communicate optically. The distinction is worth protecting because biomarker research can succeed while signaling theories fail.

This is one of the recurring lessons of the subject: a weaker interpretation can be valuable enough that we do not need to inflate it.

If UPE reveals oxidative stress in stroke or neurodegeneration, it may become a useful optical measure. If changes in emission report mitochondrial dysfunction earlier or differently than existing methods, the clinical consequence could be substantial. None of this is diminished by calling the photons reporters rather than messengers.

And if some neural system eventually turns out to exploit them causally, the discovery will be stronger because we did not award the conclusion in advance.

The brain in the dark is emitting.

The unresolved question is whether anything in the brain is listening.
