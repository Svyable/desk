# Signal Is a Much Bigger Word

A photon is not a message because it came from something alive.

That sentence sounds severe only because the language of biophotons is loose. Papers and popular accounts routinely move among *emission*, *signal*, *communication*, and *information* as though the words differ mostly in tone. They do not.

Emission is a physical event. A system releases photons.

A cue is a feature another system can exploit, whether or not the emitter evolved to produce it for that purpose.

A signal, in the biological sense, usually implies that the emitted feature has been shaped or maintained because it affects a receiver, while the receiver has likewise evolved or adapted to respond.

Communication requires an interaction in which the state of one system changes the state or behavior of another through some channel.

Information is the slipperiest word of all because it can be defined mathematically without implying meaning, function, or intention. A fluctuating photon count can contain statistical information about oxidative state even if no cell ever senses it.

Once those categories are separated, much of the biophoton controversy becomes easier to see.

Modern UPE research strongly supports emission. Biomarker studies support informational content in the statistical sense: photon intensity and spectrum can covary with oxidative processes, stress, circadian state, or neural metabolism. Some experiments report effects between separated biological preparations and therefore raise the possibility of communication. The evolutionary claim—that organisms possess an endogenous photonic signaling system—remains much stronger and less established.

This is not word policing. Each category requires a different experiment.

To show emission, measure photons above background.

To show information about the source, demonstrate a reproducible relationship between photon features and source state, preferably out of sample.

To show a receiver effect, manipulate the optical channel while controlling alternatives.

To show biological signaling, identify the receiving mechanism, establish natural operating ranges, and explain why the interaction is not an incidental photochemical side effect.

To show coding, demonstrate that different source states produce distinguishable photon patterns that receivers respond to differentially in a way not reducible to total energy.

The farther down that list one travels, the less evidence is available.

This matters because weak light invites a particular kind of theoretical inflation. Since the photons are too sparse for ordinary human perception, the phenomenon already feels hidden. Hiddenness makes it easy to imagine hidden structure. Perhaps the photons are coherent. Perhaps timing carries codes. Perhaps cells synchronize through them. Perhaps DNA acts as an antenna. Perhaps microtubules guide them. Each “perhaps” can be physically discussable while the stack as a whole becomes evidentially weightless.

A field can drown in plausible mechanisms.

The cure is to ask what observation would differ if the mechanism were true.

Suppose cells encode physiological state in UPE pulse timing. Then the temporal pattern should contain predictive information beyond average intensity. The pattern should recur under the same state, change under different states, survive replication, and affect a receiver differently when replayed at matched total photon number. Shuffle the timing while preserving the spectrum and energy. If the receiver no longer responds, timing matters. If it responds identically, the code claim weakens.

Suppose wavelength carries the relevant information. Use filters or tunable artificial sources to map an action spectrum. The receiver’s sensitivity should align with a molecular absorber or photochemical pathway. If every broad-spectrum perturbation works and narrow-band tests do not, “optical signaling” may be covering for some other variable.

Suppose coherence matters. Then coherence must be defined and measured. First-order temporal coherence, spatial coherence, photon statistics, phase relationships—these are not interchangeable. Biological light at very low intensity can have statistical properties worth studying, but the word *coherent* has been used so freely in parts of the biophoton literature that it sometimes functions as praise rather than a measurement.

The burden is especially high because ordinary chemiluminescence can generate structured output without communication. A burst of oxidative stress may produce a burst of photons. Circadian metabolism may produce periodic photon counts. Tissue geometry may shape spectra. Correlation and pattern are expected from a structured emitter.

The presence of structure in the output does not imply a code any more than the rhythm of an exhaust pipe implies that an engine is sending messages to the car behind it.

Yet exhaust can become a cue. A driver can infer engine condition from the sound. Another organism can exploit a by-product. This is the narrow opening that keeps the biological hypothesis interesting.

Evolution does not require a sender to intend a cue. If a neighboring cell reliably emits more photons under oxidative stress and another cell has a photochemical pathway sensitive to those photons, selection could favor a response. Over time the source might even evolve emission characteristics that improve the interaction, turning a cue into a signal.

This gives us an evolutionary ladder rather than an all-or-nothing choice.

Stage one: unavoidable metabolic photon leakage.

Stage two: receiver exploitation of leakage as a cue.

Stage three: selection on the receiver for greater sensitivity or specificity.

Stage four: selection on the emitter because emission changes receiver behavior in a beneficial way.

Stage five: dedicated regulation or coding.

There is solid evidence for the first stage. The later stages are hypotheses of increasing strength.

This ladder also clarifies why low intensity is not automatically fatal to function. If the receiver evolves around the natural signal from the beginning, amplification can be built into the system. What matters is not brightness to us but signal-to-noise at the receptor and the cost of false responses. A bacterium can respond to a handful of molecules in the right context. A rod cell can amplify one photon. A sperm cell can follow shallow chemical gradients. Biology routinely detects small differences.

But examples of exquisite sensitivity elsewhere cannot substitute for evidence here.

The temptation is especially strong to invoke quantum mechanics. Every photon is quantum mechanical, and every photochemical absorption event ultimately requires quantum theory. That fact does not make a biological communication system uniquely quantum in the sense usually implied. If a proposed mechanism depends on nonclassical coherence, entanglement, or other specifically quantum resources, those resources must be identified experimentally rather than inferred from the presence of photons.

The discipline required is almost linguistic humility. Use the weakest word the data have earned.

If we measure photons, say emission.

If they correlate with metabolism, say biomarker or optical readout.

If isolated receivers change when only the optical path is open, say a photon-mediated effect is supported.

If a receptor and causal pathway are identified, then we can talk about signaling.

If distinct patterns are generated and differentially decoded, we can start talking about a code.

This may sound less exciting than “cells communicate with light.” It is actually more exciting because it turns a grand claim into a sequence of experiments capable of surprising us.

Signal is a much bigger word than photon.

The field will become more interesting when it stops spending the bigger word cheaply.
