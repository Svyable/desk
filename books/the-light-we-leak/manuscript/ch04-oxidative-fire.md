# Oxidative Fire

The phrase “living light” invites a special mechanism. The chemistry is less theatrical.

Cells move electrons. Aerobic metabolism ultimately hands many of them to oxygen. Most of the time the choreography works well enough that oxygen becomes water through controlled steps. Some fraction escapes into reactive oxygen species. Superoxide appears. Hydrogen peroxide appears. Radical chemistry propagates through lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. Membranes can enter chain reactions in which one oxidized lipid helps create the next.

Chemistry at this edge can create electronically excited products. Two families matter repeatedly in the ultraweak photon literature: triplet excited carbonyls and singlet oxygen. When excited states relax, energy can leave as a photon. The exact pathways depend on the substrate and reaction, and biological tissue presents an untidy mixture rather than a single clean emitter.

This mechanism changes the philosophical status of the glow. Photon emission is not necessarily an added biological feature. It can be an accounting residue of redox chemistry.

That interpretation is supported by experiments in which oxidative stress changes emission. Ultraviolet irradiation of skin increases ultraweak photon output. Antioxidants can suppress it. Work by Katsuhiko Tsuchida and Masaki Kobayashi has measured characteristic spectral patterns from oxidizing biomolecules found in skin and argued that tissue emission is a composite produced by multiple molecular contributors. Other studies link long-lasting emission after UV exposure to lipid peroxidation and later skin erythema.

Nothing in those results requires a photon-based communication system. A smoke detector can infer a fire without smoke being a message from the building.

The distinction between cause and information is easy to mishandle because a by-product can carry exquisite information about its source. An engine's sound tells a mechanic about combustion timing. Blood lactate says something about metabolism. Exhaled volatile compounds can reveal disease states. None of these observations imply that the source evolved to broadcast a diagnostic stream.

Ultraweak photon emission may be a particularly rich reporter because excited states sit downstream of chemistry that changes with stress, oxygen availability, membrane damage, immune activation and metabolic rate. The emitted spectrum and timing can therefore contain structure even when the organism does not use that structure.

This point matters for the word signal. Engineers use signal to mean something measurable against noise. Biologists often reserve signaling for a functional process in which production and reception affect regulation. The same photon stream can be a signal to our detector and not a signal to the cell next door.

The chemistry also puts pressure on claims of coherence. If photons emerge from many spatially distributed oxidative reactions with varied lifetimes and emitters, a default expectation is a weak, noisy, broadband field. Coherent optical behavior is not forbidden, but it requires evidence beyond the fact that emission decays in an interesting way. A biological sample contains enough coupled processes to make simple statistical inferences dangerous.

There is a further twist. Reactive oxygen species themselves are biological signals. Hydrogen peroxide, for example, can participate in regulated redox signaling. Plants use oxidative bursts in defense. Immune cells deliberately generate reactive oxygen species. If photon emission accompanies those processes, then light can correlate tightly with real signaling while remaining epiphenomenal to the signal pathway.

That is a perfect trap for interpretation. You perturb a cell. Reactive oxygen species rise. Photon counts rise. Neighboring biology changes. It is tempting to draw a line through the photons. But ROS chemistry may have changed diffusible molecules, membrane state, calcium signaling, volatile compounds or other mediators at the same time.

The clean experiment has to separate the light from the chemistry that produced it.

This is difficult because the light is so weak. Blocking photons with an opaque barrier may also alter gas exchange or thermal coupling. Filtering wavelengths may introduce different materials. Increasing photon flux artificially may push the system into a regime that never exists endogenously. An antioxidant suppresses light but also alters redox biology. The intervention that seems to isolate the optical channel often cuts several wires at once.

Oxidative fire therefore explains why the phenomenon is real and why its function is hard to prove. The photons are born inside a dense causal web. They inherit information from that web. Whether biology reads any of it remains a separate question.
