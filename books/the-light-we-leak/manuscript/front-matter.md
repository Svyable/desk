# The Light We Leak

*Biophotons, Oxidative Fire, and the Question of Whether Cells Talk in Light*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a version of this subject that begins with a revelation: you glow.

That version is true and mostly useless.

Human skin emits photons. Leaves emit photons. Yeast, bacteria, neural tissue, germinating seeds, injured plants, inflamed tissue and oxidizing biomolecules can all produce light so faint that ordinary vision never notices it. With a cooled camera, a photomultiplier tube, darkness, patience, and enough care about thermal noise, cosmic rays, leakage, afterglow and the recent lighting history of the sample, the signal can be measured.

The interesting question begins after the revelation has worn off.

Why is there light at all? Modern redox chemistry gives a good answer. Reactive oxygen chemistry can produce electronically excited species. Triplet carbonyls and singlet oxygen can relax by emitting photons. A living system doing chemistry far from equilibrium is not a black box. Some of its energy leaves as heat. Some leaves in molecules. A vanishingly small fraction can leave as visible or near-visible light.

Then comes the dangerous step. If cells emit photons, do other cells use them?

That question is almost exactly one hundred years old. Alexander Gurwitsch, working with onion roots in the 1920s, claimed that dividing tissue could stimulate division in nearby tissue through a radiation that passed through quartz but not ordinary glass. He called it mitogenetic radiation. The claim generated excitement, replications, failures, increasingly elaborate apparatus, and eventually the scientific fate reserved for effects that are fascinating but unreliable: it became embarrassing.

The photons did not disappear. Better detectors arrived decades later and found ultraweak emission all over biology. What did not arrive with them was a clean vindication of Gurwitsch's biological claim.

That gap is the book.

I am going to treat four things as different even when the literature does not. First, there is photon emission itself. Second, there is evidence about the chemistry that generates it. Third, there are correlations between emission and biological state. Fourth, there is the far stronger proposition that an organism has evolved to encode, transmit, receive, and act on information in those photons.

The first three can be investigated without believing the fourth. The fourth cannot borrow certainty from the first three.

That sounds like a narrow methodological point. It is not. Biology is full of outputs that became inputs. Carbon dioxide is waste until another organism uses it. Electrical fields are consequences of ion movement until a fish evolves an organ that reads them. Heat is metabolism until a pit viper makes infrared part of its perceptual world. Evolution is a relentless scavenger. An unavoidable by-product can become a cue; a cue can become a signal; a signal can become a system.

So the skeptical position is not that optical communication inside living systems is impossible. The skeptical position is that possibility is cheap. A signaling claim has to survive photon budgets, geometry, absorption, background light, receptor sensitivity, competing chemical pathways, and adversarial controls designed to make the light disappear while leaving everything else intact.

The field has not yet cleared that bar. It has, however, become interesting enough that dismissal is no longer the same thing as rigor.
