# The Receiver Problem

Every theory of biological photon communication eventually arrives at the same empty chair.

Who receives the photon?

The question can be answered casually because cells are full of chromophores. Flavins absorb light. Porphyrins absorb light. Cytochromes absorb light. Aromatic amino acids absorb in the ultraviolet. Opsins are not confined to the eye; extraocular opsins participate in diverse physiological processes. Cryptochromes are photoreceptive proteins with roles in circadian biology and, in some animals, magnetoreception hypotheses. Nitric-oxide-related pathways can be light-sensitive under some conditions. Photobiomodulation research explores effects of red and near-infrared light on mitochondrial and cellular physiology.

The cell is not optically inert.

But a molecule that can absorb a photon is not automatically the receiver for endogenous ultraweak photon emission.

This is the equivalent of finding microphones in a building and concluding that a whisper in the basement runs the fire alarm. The hardware category is suggestive. The operating chain still has to be shown.

A credible endogenous receiver needs at least five properties.

Its absorption spectrum must overlap the photons actually available at the relevant location.

Its sensitivity must be high enough at natural fluxes.

Absorption must produce a downstream change rather than harmless relaxation.

The receptor must be positioned where the photons can reach it before tissue absorption and scattering erase the opportunity.

And perturbing the receptor must perturb the claimed biological effect.

The last requirement is the most important because it converts optical plausibility into physiology.

Consider cytochrome c oxidase, often discussed in photobiomodulation. Red and near-infrared light at externally applied intensities can alter biological processes, with mitochondrial chromophores among the proposed targets. Even if those mechanisms are fully accepted for therapeutic illumination, the relevant irradiances can be orders of magnitude above spontaneous UPE. The existence of a photochemical response under a lamp cannot establish a response to the body’s own weak emissions.

Dose is not an annoying detail. Dose is the hypothesis.

The same caution applies to cryptochrome. It is tempting to recruit cryptochrome because it is already a photoreceptor and, in radical-pair models of magnetoreception, a candidate for extraordinary sensitivity to weak physical effects. Yet cryptochrome photochemistry typically depends on specific wavelengths and states. To make it an endogenous UPE receptor, one would need to show that natural photon flux reaches cryptochrome in the proper spectral band and that the resulting photochemical events occur often enough to change biology.

There is a more subtle route. Perhaps the receiver is not a dedicated photoreceptor at all. A photon may be absorbed by an ordinary metabolic chromophore and slightly alter redox chemistry. The effect could then be amplified by biochemical networks. In that case the “receiver” is distributed and opportunistic rather than specialized.

This is physically easier to imagine and experimentally harder to prove.

A dedicated receptor offers clean interventions: knock it out, block it, mutate the chromophore-binding site. A distributed mechanism may involve many overlapping absorbers. Blocking one does little. Artificial illumination perturbs broad chemistry. The phenomenon becomes real but difficult to assign.

That is why action spectra matter.

An action spectrum plots biological effect as a function of wavelength. If a receiver is molecular, its effect spectrum should bear some relationship to its absorption properties. If a claimed UPE-mediated response peaks sharply where a candidate chromophore absorbs, that is useful evidence. If the response is flat across unrelated wavelengths or appears only under broad-band conditions, the mechanism needs another explanation.

Natural source spectra matter equally. The most elegant receptor is irrelevant if the emitter does not produce photons in its operating band.

The historical mitogenetic literature often used filters and biological effects to infer wavelength ranges. Modern experiments can reverse the logic: physically measure source emission first, then test receiver sensitivity only within the bands that exist at natural flux.

There is also the problem of photon statistics.

At ultraweak intensities, photons may arrive sparsely. A receptor cannot integrate indefinitely if its state resets quickly. A cell may integrate downstream consequences over seconds or minutes, but then temporal precision disappears. If a signaling claim requires fast coding, sparse arrival becomes a constraint. If the proposed role is slow stress coordination, sparse photons may be less problematic.

Function determines the required bandwidth.

This is one reason sweeping claims about “cellular communication” are too vague. A mechanism that changes gene expression over hours can operate with a very different photon budget from one claimed to coordinate millisecond neural firing. The same measured UPE intensity may be plausible for one and absurd for the other.

The receiver problem should therefore be posed together with the task.

What does the system need to know? How quickly? Over what distance? At what false-positive cost?

A neighboring cell detecting sustained oxidative distress is a modest task. A neuron transmitting high-dimensional information through spontaneous photons is a demanding one. An embryo using photons as one of several slow positional cues sits somewhere else. These hypotheses should not borrow credibility from one another simply because they use the same noun.

Evolutionary evidence could help. If a specific photoreceptor is proposed to mediate endogenous UPE effects, species or tissues lacking that receptor should show reduced or absent effects under comparable conditions. Conversely, organisms living in darkness may retain internal photoreceptive machinery for reasons unrelated to external light; such cases could provide natural experiments.

Subcellular localization matters too. If a receptor sits nanometers from a major UPE source, local coupling becomes more plausible. If it sits behind strongly absorbing tissue with no clear path, the hypothesis weakens. Imaging and structural biology can therefore contribute to what initially sounds like an abstract question about light.

The most convincing future study may not begin with a photon detector at all. It may begin with genetics.

Imagine a receiver cell line that responds reproducibly to an optically isolated stressed source. A candidate chromophore pathway is identified. Knockout eliminates the distant response but leaves baseline physiology intact. Re-expression restores it. The receptor’s action spectrum matches the measured source spectrum. A calibrated artificial source at natural flux reproduces the effect. An opaque barrier abolishes it. Temperature, volatiles, electrical coupling, and vibration are independently monitored.

At that point, “biophoton signaling” would stop being a phrase and become a mechanism.

Until then, the empty chair should stay visible.

The source has become measurable. The path can be engineered. The receiver remains where the most interesting science is missing.
