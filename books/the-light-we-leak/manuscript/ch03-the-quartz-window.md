# The Quartz Window

Quartz occupies an odd place in this story. It is both evidence and accusation.

The appeal of Gurwitsch’s barrier experiments was straightforward. If a supposed influence crossed a quartz plate but failed to cross ordinary glass, then the barrier was behaving like a crude spectral filter. Quartz can transmit ultraviolet wavelengths that common window glass absorbs. The biology on the far side appeared to know which material had been inserted between source and receiver.

That is much more interesting than two uncovered roots affecting one another. It seems to turn a vague “distant influence” into optics.

But a filter is only as informative as the rest of the apparatus.

Put two biological preparations close together and many things can cross the gap besides the signal you care about. Volatile organic compounds travel through air. Water vapor changes local humidity. Carbon dioxide changes. Heat moves by conduction, convection, and radiation. Mechanical vibration reaches the bench. Static electric fields change with geometry. A barrier alters airflow and thermal exchange simply by being there. Different materials have different surface properties and thicknesses. They can carry contamination. They can change the investigator’s handling. They can modify not just one wavelength band but an entire microenvironment.

The phrase “quartz-versus-glass control” therefore sounds cleaner than the physical situation actually is.

Modern optical experiments deal with this problem by measuring the transmission spectrum of the actual barrier, keeping thickness and geometry controlled, monitoring temperature, blocking alternative pathways, and using physical detectors where possible. The historical experiments often had fewer tools. This does not make them worthless. It makes their evidentiary role narrower.

What quartz could establish, at best, was a clue about mechanism if the biological endpoint was reliable and every meaningful difference except optical transmission had been controlled. It could not rescue a noisy endpoint by itself.

The importance of this becomes clearer when we distinguish a *filter test* from a *causal identification*. Imagine that a neighboring culture changes its growth when separated from a source by quartz but not glass. There are at least four possibilities. Ultraviolet photons are the cause. Some other physical difference between the barriers is the cause. The result is a chance fluctuation selected from repeated trials. Or an experimenter expectation entered through preparation, scoring, or exclusion. The quartz result raises the optical hypothesis. It does not abolish the others.

The early field nevertheless used material selectivity as one of its strongest arguments. Spectral claims were reported, often based on biological receivers and filters rather than direct spectrometers. Mitogenetic radiation was commonly placed in the ultraviolet. Gurwitsch and L. Gurwitsch later connected the phenomenon with ultraviolet chemiluminescence, and a 1939 *Nature* letter explicitly argued that ultraviolet chemiluminescence discussed by physical chemists appeared identical with mitogenetic radiation.

This is one of the most important historical pivots in the book.

The proposed source was drifting away from an unspecified biological emanation toward ordinary excited-state chemistry.

Chemiluminescence is not exotic. Chemical reactions can leave products electronically excited. When those products return to a lower-energy state, energy can be released as photons. The emitted light may be fantastically weak and still be physically real. A living cell contains enormous numbers of reactions, including oxidation reactions capable of generating reactive intermediates. If some fraction end in excited species, photon emission follows without requiring a dedicated luminous organ.

Once that mechanism is admitted, the word *radiation* changes character. It stops sounding like a separate force and starts sounding like an optical trace of chemistry.

That does not solve the receiver problem. In fact, it makes it sharper. A chemical reaction may emit photons without those photons doing anything useful. Hot metal emits infrared radiation. A stressed cell can emit photons for the same broad reason a reaction vessel can: energy has to relax. The existence of a physical carrier no longer implies biological purpose.

The quartz window therefore belongs to two histories. In the first, it is part of the attempt to prove a novel long-range biological communication system. In the second, it is an early, awkward probe into a real class of ultraweak chemiluminescent emissions that later instrumentation could measure directly.

Those histories overlap without being identical.

This is a recurring problem in the interpretation of abandoned science. A field can be wrong at the level of function while having touched a real phenomenon at the level of mechanism. Mesmerism did not need “animal magnetism” to reveal that expectation and social context could alter symptoms. N-rays were not a new radiation, but their story exposed observer effects in threshold perception. Early continental-drift arguments included bad mechanisms while the movement of continents was real. The lesson is not that every rejected theory is secretly right. It is that theories are bundles, and evidence may support one component while destroying another.

Mitogenetic radiation was a particularly tangled bundle because its name fused mechanism and effect. “Radiation” named the carrier. “Mitogenetic” named the function. Once the physical emission could be independently measured, those two words needed to be separated.

Modern ultraweak photon emission research largely performs that separation. Investigators measure photons from tissue without assuming those photons stimulate division. The emission is studied as a consequence of oxidative metabolism, stress, injury, photosensitization, lipid peroxidation, and other biochemical processes. In such work, a photon is an output variable. It can be informative without being instructive.

The distinction between informative and instructive is easy to underestimate.

Smoke is informative about fire. Smoke does not tell the fire how to burn.

Blood lactate is informative about metabolic conditions. Lactate can also have signaling roles, but those require separate evidence. Carbon dioxide is a metabolic waste product and a regulated physiological signal. Nitric oxide can arise chemically and serve as a messenger. Biology is full of molecules that cross the line from by-product to cue or signal. That history keeps the photonic question open without answering it.

If endogenous photons are to be more than optical exhaust, some biological structure must receive them or some photochemical reaction must be sensitive enough to respond. The relevant wavelengths must reach that structure through real tissue. The photon flux must exceed or exploit the noise floor. The timing must matter. A causal perturbation of the optical path should alter the downstream biology while controls leave competing channels unchanged.

This is where the quartz window still has something to teach.

The original barrier idea was conceptually sound. Change the path and see whether the effect changes. The problem was that the path was underspecified and the receiver too complicated.

A century later we can build a better window.

We can use filters with measured transmission spectra. We can use optical fibers. We can use matched materials that differ only in targeted bands. We can monitor volatile compounds, temperature, electromagnetic fields, vibration, and pressure. We can replace a living source with a calibrated artificial source whose photon flux matches the natural emitter. We can blind the analyst. We can randomize condition order. We can preregister the endpoint. We can measure the photons on both sides of the barrier rather than infer their passage from biology alone.

The historical experiment does not deserve to be repeated ceremonially. It deserves to be decomposed and rebuilt until every elegant inference has to survive an ugly control.

Quartz was once asked to distinguish glass from mystery.

Now it can distinguish a photon from everything we accidentally call one.
