# The Receiver Problem

A communication theory can survive a surprisingly long time without a receiver.

The sender is easy to imagine because we can measure emission. Oxidative chemistry creates excited species; photons leave. The detector confirms it. Once that fact is secure, the mind supplies the rest: some nearby cell must be able to notice.

Biology does contain molecules built to notice light. Opsins alter conformation after absorbing photons. Flavins participate in photochemistry. Cryptochromes absorb blue light and mediate plant development, circadian regulation and, in animals, possibly magnetic sensitivity through radical-pair chemistry. Cytochrome c oxidase and other chromophores are invoked in photobiomodulation research. Photosensitivity is not rare.

The receiver problem is therefore not whether molecules can react to light. It is whether they can react to the endogenous photon flux available in the right place, at the right wavelengths, against the right background, in a way that changes physiology.

This is a threshold problem and thresholds are where vague theories go to hide.

If a receptor requires thousands of photons concentrated on a molecule over a short interval and the endogenous field delivers one relevant photon every hour, the mechanism is dead no matter how elegant the story. If, by contrast, a receptor sits nanometers from a recurring excited-state source and one absorption event can trigger a high-gain cascade, a macroscopic flux measurement may underestimate local plausibility.

Distance matters brutally. Emission from an excited molecule is not automatically directed. In open tissue, photons spread over solid angle, scatter and are absorbed. A detector centimeter away integrates over many sources; a receptor molecule only cares about what reaches its absorption cross-section.

The field needs more measurements at that scale.

It also needs to distinguish a receptor from a victim. Light can cause chemistry without the receiving system having evolved to use it. Ultraviolet photons can damage DNA. Blue light can generate reactive oxygen species in chromophores. A photon-induced effect is not yet communication. For signaling, the response should improve or regulate something in a way linked to the source state, and there should be evidence that the production-reception relationship is biologically organized rather than accidental.

A useful analogy is odor. Many volatile molecules leak from metabolism. Another organism may detect them as cues. If the emitter later evolves to alter production because detection benefits it, the cue can become a signal. The receiver may evolve before the sender does.

Biophotons could follow the same path. A cell might initially eavesdrop on oxidative light from neighbors. If that predicts stress, injury or density, sensitivity could be useful. Only later would selection favor regulated emission, if regulation were possible and advantageous.

This possibility is more modest than a dedicated optical language. It also fits the chemistry better. The raw photons already exist. The evolutionary innovation could be on the receiving side.

How would we know? Comparative biology would help. If a receptor hypothesis is real, species or tissues exposed to different optical backgrounds should show predictable differences. A subterranean root, a transparent embryo, a deep tissue and a sunlit epidermis face very different signal-to-noise environments. A universal mechanism should either explain those differences or occupy protected microenvironments where external light is irrelevant.

Genetics would help more. Identify a candidate receptor, remove it, and preserve the source emission. If the distant biological effect disappears, the case strengthens. Rescue the receptor and restore the effect. Shift its absorption spectrum and show that the effective wavelength shifts with it. Now the argument is becoming specific enough to fail.

This is what a mature field should want.

The receiver problem is not an objection designed to stop research. It is the map of the research. Emission gives us a phenomenon. A receptor would give us a mechanism. Until then, “cells communicate with light” remains a description searching for anatomy.
