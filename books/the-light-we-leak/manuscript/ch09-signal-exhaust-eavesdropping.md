# Signal, Exhaust, and Eavesdropping

The word “signal” is doing too much work.

A detector engineer calls any recoverable variation a signal. A physiologist may call a hormone a signal because it participates in regulation. An evolutionary biologist distinguishes a signal shaped for communication from a cue that merely provides information. Those usages overlap enough to create confusion and differ enough to matter.

Ultraweak photon emission is unquestionably a signal in the first sense. It can be detected. Its intensity changes with injury, oxidation, metabolic state and time of day. Under controlled conditions, the counts carry information about the sample.

That tells us nothing yet about whether the sample uses the information.

Consider smoke. Smoke contains information about a fire. Humans evolved no smoke specifically for the benefit of smoke detectors. We built detectors that exploit a by-product. Birds may use smoke as a cue. A plant exposed to smoke may change germination behavior because specific molecules evolved ecological relevance. The same output can therefore be exhaust, cue and eventually part of a signaling relationship depending on who is reading it and why.

This vocabulary is useful because it creates more than two options. The debate is often staged as “biophotons are meaningless metabolic waste” versus “biophotons are a hidden communication system.” Biology rarely respects such clean choices.

There could be no native receiver at all. There could be opportunistic sensing of oxidative state. There could be regulated local signaling in one tissue and meaningless exhaust in another. Plants may differ from animals. Microbes in dense colonies may differ from vertebrate skin. A photon pathway could matter only under darkness, hypoxia, stress or development.

The question then becomes empirical rather than tribal.

A cue does not need a sophisticated code. If photon rate rises reliably when a neighboring cell enters oxidative stress, a receiver could benefit from a threshold response. That is enough for information transfer in the ecological sense. The source need not modulate a carrier or encode symbols. It merely has to correlate with a state the receiver cares about.

This makes the communication hypothesis easier to satisfy than some critics assume and harder than some enthusiasts claim. It is easier because a useful biological cue can be crude. It is harder because correlation alone is still insufficient. The receiver must actually use the photons rather than a chemical change traveling alongside them.

The strongest experiments would therefore manipulate the information channel itself. Preserve the source physiology while scrambling the optical output. Preserve total photon number while scrambling temporal structure. Preserve spectrum while changing intensity. Use an optical fiber or waveguide to route the measured emission to a remote receiver with no shared atmosphere, substrate or electrical connection. Then replay a matched synthetic photon stream.

If the receiver tracks the routed or replayed photons, the argument changes.

This is the advantage of thinking like an eavesdropper. An eavesdropper does not care whether the sender meant to communicate. The first question is whether the observable can predict a hidden state. The second is whether another biological system has access to that observable. Only later do we ask whether selection shaped production for the purpose.

The field already has good evidence for the first question. A wounded plant emits differently from an unwounded one. Oxidatively stressed skin emits differently from protected skin. Neural tissue under stimulation can emit differently from baseline.

The second question remains unsettled.

The third is mostly speculative.

Separating them relieves the subject of a burden it does not need. Ultraweak photons do not have to be a language to be biologically consequential. A primitive eavesdropping mechanism would be interesting enough. A diagnostic readout useful only to us is interesting enough. Even complete functional irrelevance would teach us something about how excited-state chemistry leaks out of living systems.

The light does not owe us a secret message.
