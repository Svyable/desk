# Noise That Biology Could Steal

The most plausible version of biophoton signaling may be less flattering than the grand theories.

Perhaps the light began as waste.

Biology is full of thefts from physics. Organisms use gravity without having invented it. Bacteria exploit naturally occurring chemical gradients. Cells turn membrane voltage into computation although voltage follows from ordinary electrochemistry. Animals use polarization patterns in skylight. Migratory species may exploit the geomagnetic field through molecular reactions whose chemistry existed before navigation did. Evolution does not require a clean engineering brief. It notices regularities after the fact.

Ultraweak photon emission supplies a regularity.

Oxidative stress changes it. Metabolic activity changes it. Mitochondrial state changes it. Injury can change it. Time of day can change it. Different molecules contribute different spectral features. In principle, any nearby biological system with suitable photochemistry is exposed to a weak, noisy summary of the emitter’s state.

The word *summary* matters.

No codebook is necessary. A receiver does not need to distinguish thousands of messages. It may need only to know that the neighborhood has become unusually oxidative, that a population is growing rapidly, that tissue has been damaged, or that metabolism has crossed a threshold. Low-bandwidth cues can be biologically useful.

This reframes the problem from “can cells communicate through light?” to “is there any ecological or physiological situation in which naturally generated photons change receiver behavior enough for selection to care?”

That is a smaller question and a better one.

The geometry could be microscopic. Imagine adjacent organelles, packed cells, a biofilm, a developing tissue, or a densely organized microbial colony. The optical path is short. Ambient light may be absent or attenuated. A receiver molecule is close to the source. If oxidative events produce photons and a fraction are absorbed by sensitive chromophores, the interaction need not resemble a wireless communication system. It may look more like a local perturbation field.

The distinction matters because distance is expensive. Every extra path length loses photons to geometric spread, absorption, and scattering. The most plausible endogenous uses should therefore be sought first where source and receiver are close, the environment is dark, and amplification already exists.

This is almost the opposite of the imagery that has surrounded biophotons. The popular version imagines light radiating from whole organisms, perhaps coordinating at a distance. The physically conservative version begins inside a crowded cellular neighborhood where a photon may travel micrometers rather than meters.

The smaller scale is not less interesting. It is where biology usually hides its machinery.

There is a second possibility: the photons need not be reliable one by one. They may matter statistically. A receptor could integrate exposure over time, the way molecular systems integrate ligand concentration. If oxidative stress increases local photon flux several-fold for minutes, a slow photosensitive pathway could respond even when individual events are unpredictable.

This turns “noise” into a distribution shift.

Signal detection theory is useful here. A receiver does not need perfect separation between normal and stressed states. It needs enough separation that the benefit of responding exceeds the cost of false alarms. Evolution can tune thresholds. The relevant comparison is not whether a human-built detector sees a clean pulse but whether a biological circuit can extract a useful change from its own local background.

Again, the analogy cannot substitute for measurement. We need photon-count distributions at the scale of the putative receiver. We need receptor kinetics. We need false-event rates from thermal and chemical noise. We need to know whether the change produced by the source is even in the right order of magnitude.

The hypothesis makes predictions.

If UPE is exploited as a local stress cue, responses should be strongest at short distances and in geometries that preserve optical coupling. They should weaken with spectral filters that remove the relevant bands. They may be easier to detect in dark internal tissues or shielded microbial systems than at exposed surfaces dominated by ambient light. They should often operate slowly, integrating state rather than carrying high-speed symbolic messages.

If dedicated evolution has occurred, receptor sensitivity and source emission may be matched. If the effect is merely incidental photochemistry, matching may be sloppy and species-specific.

The cue hypothesis also predicts that many organisms will not use UPE at all.

This is important because the field sometimes assumes universality. Living cells emit photons, therefore photon communication should be a basic property of life. That conclusion does not follow. Carbon dioxide is produced by enormous numbers of organisms, but not every cell uses local carbon dioxide as a signal. Heat is universal, yet thermal signaling is specialized. Mechanical vibrations are everywhere, but only some organisms build sensory systems around them.

A physical by-product can be universal while its biological exploitation is rare.

This makes negative results scientifically valuable. If dozens of well-controlled systems show no receiver effect at natural photon flux, the search should narrow rather than invent new conditions. Perhaps the phenomenon is restricted to particular tissues, developmental stages, or organisms. Perhaps it is absent. A field that cannot metabolize negative evidence will repeat the history of mitogenetic radiation.

There is also a philosophical gain in the by-product-first model. It removes the need for purpose at the source.

One of the recurrent mistakes in biology is to read function backward into existence. A feature has consequences, therefore it must exist for those consequences. Evolutionary reasoning is more complicated. A feature may begin as a constraint or waste product, then acquire secondary uses. Feathers preceded flight. Enzymes moonlight. Metabolic intermediates become signals. A photon generated by lipid oxidation need not have been “sent” to become useful to a receiver later.

This is how the old and new literatures could be reconciled without resurrecting the old theory wholesale.

Gurwitsch may have been right that metabolically active tissue emits weak radiation and wrong that the observed mitotic effect established an organizing radiation system. Modern photobiology may be right that UPE is largely oxidative chemiluminescence and still leave open the possibility that some organisms exploit that leakage.

The synthesis is not a compromise between believers and skeptics. It is a different causal history.

First came chemistry.

Then came light.

Only perhaps, and only somewhere, did biology learn to listen.

This possibility has one virtue that more extravagant theories lack: it can fail cleanly. If natural fluxes are quantitatively incapable of driving known photochemistry under relevant geometry, the hypothesis shrinks. If calibrated natural-flux replay produces no receiver effect across well-chosen systems, it shrinks further. If purported distant effects persist when photons are blocked but volatiles are not, the carrier changes.

A good speculative synthesis should tell us how to kill it.

Noise that biology could steal is interesting because it asks for no special kind of photon, no universal field, and no hidden intelligence in metabolism. It asks only whether evolution has ever turned a faint physical leak into a useful local cue.

That question deserves an experiment before it deserves a mythology.
