# The Noise Floor

The Earth is an inconvenient place to invent a weak optical communication system.

At the surface, sunlight can deliver roughly 10^20 visible photons per square meter per second depending on wavelength and conditions. Indoor lighting is lower and still enormous compared with typical ultraweak biological emission. A cell trying to broadcast externally with a handful of endogenous photons is whispering inside a stadium.

This observation is sometimes treated as a complete refutation. It is not. It is a demand for geometry.

Biology does not live only on exposed surfaces. Tissues contain dark compartments. Roots occupy soil. Embryos develop inside eggs and bodies. Cells touch. Membranes face one another across nanometer-scale clefts. Organelles sit micrometers apart. An external photon background may be irrelevant to an internal optical path if the tissue strongly attenuates ambient light.

The opposite problem then appears: the tissue also attenuates the endogenous photons.

A useful theory has to win both arguments at once. It cannot invoke tissue opacity to escape environmental noise and then assume transparent transport for the signal.

Wavelength matters. Near-infrared light penetrates tissue farther than ultraviolet, but the oxidative emitters and candidate photoreceptors have their own spectra. Deep ultraviolet is strongly absorbed by biomolecules and water over relevant paths. Visible red and near-infrared photons travel farther but may couple less efficiently to proposed receptors. There is no generic “biophoton” propagation length.

Timing matters too. A low average photon rate can still carry information if events are clustered in short bursts and a receiver integrates appropriately. Conversely, a smooth increase over minutes may be useful as a stress cue but useless for fast neural computation. Claims about cognition require different bandwidth than claims about growth or development.

This is where appeals to stochastic resonance sometimes enter. A weak signal can influence a nonlinear threshold system in the presence of noise. The concept is real. It is also easy to use as an escape hatch whenever a photon budget looks inadequate. Stochastic resonance does not mean arbitrarily weak signals become biologically meaningful. It requires a system with appropriate thresholds, noise statistics and coupling. Those parameters can be measured.

The noise floor therefore belongs in the experiment, not in the footnotes.

For each proposed pathway, we should be able to write a crude budget: source photon rate, emission spectrum, geometry, losses, receiver cross-section, background rate and integration time. The purpose is not to pretend biology is a fiber-optic network. It is to discover whether the hypothesis lives in a physically plausible region before designing elaborate mechanistic stories.

A surprisingly small number of papers do this end to end.

That omission helps explain why the literature splits into camps. Experimentalists can point to reproducible photon counts and biological correlations. Skeptics can point to absurdly low fluxes in free space. Both can be right because they are answering different questions.

The decisive scale may be local. If an excited molecule transfers energy radiatively to a nearby chromophore, calling the process “cell-to-cell biophoton communication” may be grandiose. If the same process coordinates tissue behavior over millimeters, the label becomes more reasonable. The distance at which the mechanism remains effective is therefore not a detail. It is part of its identity.

The noise floor also disciplines human claims. Any idea that people exchange meaningful ultraweak photon signals across a room has to compete with environmental light, clothing, thermal and chemical cues, and the enormous attenuation implied by inverse-square geometry. The burden is much heavier than for intracellular or tissue-local signaling.

Weak phenomena are not automatically irrelevant. But every weak phenomenon lives in a budget.

The budget is where poetry ends and biology begins.
