# The Experiment That Went Dark

In 1934, Eugene Lorenz published a paper with a title almost aggressively free of romance: “Search for Mitogenetic Radiation by Means of the Photoelectric Method.” It is one of the documents this subject needs because it records what happened when a disputed biological effect met a detector built to count light rather than cell divisions.

Lorenz did not begin by assuming Gurwitsch's claimed intensity was beyond reach. He estimated it from published reports, compared biological and physical sensitivity, built a photoelectric counter using cadmium, described spurious effects, and tested materials that were supposed to be strong emitters. His apparatus, he reported, should have detected on the order of ten to fifteen quanta per square centimeter per second at the mercury line near 254 nanometers.

He found no mitogenetic radiation.

Negative results rarely become the founding myth of anything. They are especially inconvenient in a field that later discovers a related real phenomenon. Once ultraweak photon emission became measurable with photomultiplier tubes, it became tempting to write the 1930s failures as a simple technology problem: the instruments were not yet sensitive enough. Sometimes that is true. It is not enough here.

Lorenz's paper matters because he explicitly treated detector artifacts as part of the experiment. At extremely low count rates, the apparatus begins to look alive. Tubes produce dark counts. Surfaces charge. Temperature changes matter. Radioactivity exists. Cosmic particles exist. Mechanical and electrical instabilities can mimic a signal whose defining feature is that it barely rises above the background. The less light you are trying to measure, the more the detector itself becomes part of the organism under study.

There is another problem. Modern ultraweak photon emission is strongest in spectral regions and physiological conditions that do not map neatly onto the original claim. Oxidative chemistry can generate visible and near-ultraviolet photons, but the specific deep-UV band historically assigned to mitogenetic radiation is difficult. A 2013 physical critique by Ondřej Kučera and Michal Cifra noted how little modern evidence existed for endogenous emission in the old 190–250 nanometer range and emphasized that proposed communication models have to confront both photon flux and ambient optical noise.

This is where the history becomes useful instead of merely quaint. A failed detector experiment can mean at least four different things. The phenomenon does not exist. The detector is not sensitive in the right band. The phenomenon is intermittent or state dependent. Or the original biological response was real but caused by something else.

Those possibilities should not be merged after the fact.

The strongest modern version of the field accepts Lorenz rather than arguing around him. It says: the original mitogenetic literature contained effects that were not robustly established by physical detection; later technology independently established ultraweak photon emission from biological oxidation; some modern experiments again report distant biological interactions compatible with optical coupling; but no straight evidentiary line turns the onion experiment into a proven signaling pathway.

That version is less satisfying than vindication. It is also more interesting.

Science often advances by salvaging a mechanism from the wreckage of an explanation. Meteorites were real even when particular stories about stones falling from the sky were wrong. Continental drift was real even though early mechanisms were inadequate. Peptic ulcers could involve bacteria without validating every fringe claim about infection. A rejected package can contain a durable component.

Ultraweak photon emission may be one of those components. But this history also warns against a familiar move in anomalous research: once any nearby fact is confirmed, the old disputed claim is treated as retroactively confirmed too. It is not.

Lorenz's null result belongs near the front of the book because it imposes discipline on everything that follows. We are not searching for a way to make Gurwitsch right. We are trying to find out what living light is doing, if anything, beyond announcing that oxidation happened.

That requires better detectors. It also requires better questions.
