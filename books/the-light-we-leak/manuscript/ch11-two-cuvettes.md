# Two Cuvettes

Two cuvettes sit next to each other.

That is the experiment, stripped to its nerve.

Inside each is a preparation of isolated mitochondria. They are chemically separate. Nothing is pipetted between them. One population is perturbed; the other is monitored. If the second changes, something has crossed the space between the containers or some variable has reached both.

In 2023, David Mould and colleagues reported experiments of this kind using mitochondria isolated from cancerous MCF7 cells and non-cancerous MCF10A cells. One preparation was stressed with antimycin, an inhibitor of mitochondrial electron transport. The investigators measured oxygen consumption in the neighboring, chemically isolated preparation and reported significant changes. They also found that the effect depended on the source of the mitochondria and on the presence of ambient light. Their interpretation was cautious but provocative: the data supported some form of non-chemical signaling, with light as a candidate mechanism.

This is exactly the kind of experiment the field needs.

It is also exactly the kind of experiment that can be overinterpreted.

The strongest result is not “mitochondria talk with biophotons.” The strongest result is that under the reported conditions, perturbing one isolated mitochondrial population was associated with a reproducible change in respiration in another preparation that was not chemically mixed with it. The geometry and light dependence make optical coupling plausible. They do not uniquely identify it.

That distinction is not timidity. It is what makes the result worth pursuing.

When chemical contact is excluded, the menu of alternatives narrows but does not disappear. Heat can cross. Vibration can cross. Electromagnetic fields can cross. Volatile compounds can sometimes travel where liquid cannot, depending on vessel sealing and headspace. Light can cross. Instrument channels can share noise. The perturbation can change local temperature or electronics. An operator can introduce timing differences. A common environmental fluctuation can affect both preparations.

A transparent cuvette is a physical invitation to photons, but transparency is not proof that photons accepted it.

The ambient-light dependence is especially interesting because it can cut in more than one direction. If the effect changes when the experiment is performed under different illumination, perhaps the relevant biological machinery is photosensitive. Perhaps external photons overwhelm or prime a weak endogenous optical channel. Perhaps the source’s oxidative chemistry changes. Perhaps the detector or temperature changes. Perhaps the dark adaptation of the preparation alters metabolism independently.

Light is both a candidate carrier and a confound.

This is one reason the old quartz experiments still echo. A barrier that changes an effect can implicate optics, but only if the barrier’s other consequences are understood. The modern version can go much further. Place an opaque optical absorber between cuvettes while maintaining identical thermal conduction and airflow. Place a transparent barrier with matched thermal properties. Route only the optical channel through a fiber. Use narrow-band filters. Measure the emitted photons simultaneously. Monitor temperature at the relevant scale. Repeat with dummy cuvettes. Randomize which side receives antimycin. Automate the perturbation and analysis so nobody handling the experiment knows which condition is active.

Most important, replay the light.

If stressed mitochondria emit a measurable spectrum and temporal pattern, reproduce that output with a calibrated artificial source at the receiver. Match the flux. Match the wavelength. Match the timing as well as the detector allows. If the receiver’s respiration changes without any stressed source present, the photonic hypothesis becomes substantially stronger.

Then turn the intensity down.

A common weakness in mechanistic demonstrations is to show that an artificial stimulus can produce an effect at intensities far above the natural signal. This establishes biological photosensitivity, not endogenous signaling. The decisive question is whether the measured natural photon flux is sufficient.

The cuvette experiment is valuable because it can, in principle, close that loop.

There is also an underappreciated conceptual possibility between artifact and communication. Suppose the receiver genuinely responds to photons emitted by stressed mitochondria. That still does not mean mitochondria evolved to communicate optically. The effect could be incidental photochemistry. An endogenous chromophore may absorb a photon and alter redox state without any adaptive signaling function. In an isolated preparation, the effect may be measurable but biologically irrelevant in intact tissue.

Function has to be demonstrated in the organism, not granted to the petri dish.

This distinction protects the experiment from having to prove too much. A real low-flux photochemical interaction between organelles would be interesting even if evolution never uses it. Physics creates couplings before biology decides whether to exploit them.

The cancer-versus-non-cancer difference reported in the mitochondrial work adds another layer. Cancer cells reorganize metabolism and redox handling. Their mitochondria can differ in respiration, membrane potential, reactive oxygen species, substrate use, and stress response. A different response to a non-contact perturbation could therefore reflect altered source emission, altered receiver sensitivity, or both. That is scientifically rich territory, but it multiplies the variables.

One should resist the seductive story in which cancer cells possess a hidden light language that healthy cells do not. The data do not establish that. They establish a system in which cell origin mattered under specific experimental conditions.

The next useful experiment would cross the sources and receivers systematically. Stress MCF7-derived mitochondria and expose MCF10A receivers. Reverse the pairing. Measure photon spectra from each source under the same perturbation. Normalize receiver response to incident photon flux. Ask whether the response follows source identity, receiver identity, wavelength, intensity, or some interaction among them.

A communication model makes predictions about asymmetry.

A generic heating artifact should scale with heat. An optical mechanism should track transmitted photon flux and relevant wavelengths. A shared electrical artifact should appear in dummy preparations or instrument channels. A volatile mechanism should respond to gas barriers differently from optical barriers. The point is not to dream up alternatives forever. It is to design one apparatus in which the alternatives separate.

This is what makes the two-cuvette geometry so appealing. Unlike a whole organism, it is simple enough that the channels can be enumerated.

Simple is relative. Mitochondria are not passive particles. They consume oxygen, maintain membrane potentials, generate reactive species, swell, depolarize, respond to calcium, and decay after isolation. Their behavior changes over minutes. The receiver remains biological and nonlinear.

But the experiment has one enormous advantage over Gurwitsch’s onion roots: we can measure the proposed carrier directly while the biological response occurs.

That is the bridge the old field lacked.

If the photon count rises in the source, a spectrally matched flux crosses the gap, the receiver changes, an optical block abolishes the change, an artificial replay restores it, and non-optical controls remain flat, we are no longer arguing from analogy.

We are watching causality move between two cuvettes.

The reported 2023 work does not yet deliver that entire chain. It gives the chain somewhere concrete to begin.
