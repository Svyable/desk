# Counting Almost Nothing

A photomultiplier tube turns a photon into an electrical event large enough to count. That sentence makes the instrument sound cleaner than it is.

The tube has a photocathode. A photon arrives with enough energy and, with some probability, releases an electron. That electron is accelerated toward a dynode, where it knocks loose more electrons. Those electrons strike another dynode. The cascade continues until one original photoelectron becomes a measurable pulse. The device can turn almost nothing into something.

It can also turn something else into something that looks like a photon.

Thermally generated electrons produce dark counts. Radioactive decay in materials can produce pulses. Cosmic rays do not ask permission before crossing the apparatus. Electrical noise enters cables. Light can leak through seams that looked perfectly black to the person who assembled the box. A sample moved from a bright room into darkness can exhibit delayed luminescence that is not the same thing as spontaneous emission. A camera sensor can retain history. The detector's quantum efficiency changes with wavelength. Its sensitivity drifts. Cooling helps, but cooling changes the engineering problem rather than abolishing it.

This is why ultraweak photon experiments are unusually honest when the methods section feels tedious.

A biological signal may sit only modestly above background in raw counts. To claim a spatial pattern, investigators may need long integration times. To claim a spectral distribution, they may sacrifice already scarce photons through filters. To claim a temporal response, they have to choose a bin width that can reveal dynamics without manufacturing them from stochastic counts. Every improvement in one dimension spends signal somewhere else.

The word “image” can mislead. In 2009, Masaki Kobayashi, Daisuke Kikuchi and Hitoshi Okamura used an improved cryogenic CCD system to image spontaneous photon emission from the human body. The resulting figures are memorable because they resemble a dim thermal portrait, except the mechanism is not heat. The body appears to glow. Yet each image required long exposure in darkness, and the reported intensity was roughly a thousand times below the sensitivity of naked vision.

That gap in scale matters. A person looking at another person is drowning in reflected environmental photons compared with the photons generated endogenously. Any proposal for external biophotonic communication has to survive that fact. A signal that exists in a black box may be physically irrelevant at noon.

Internal communication is different. Tissue is dark in the ordinary sense, but not optically simple. Photons scatter, absorb, and may be re-emitted. Hemoglobin, melanin, water, lipids and proteins impose wavelength-dependent losses. If a proposed source is inside tissue, the correct comparison is not sunlight at the skin. It is the local optical background and the probability that an emitted photon reaches a biologically responsive molecule before being absorbed by something else.

This is where measurement and theory meet. You cannot infer a signaling channel from a detector count outside the tissue without asking how many photons were generated inside, how many escaped, in what wavelengths, over what solid angle, and with what temporal structure. A photomultiplier records survivors.

The survivor bias is literal.

The field became scientifically sturdier when investigators stopped treating “biophoton” as a special kind of photon. There is no biophoton particle. There are photons emitted by biological chemical reactions. That language removes some unnecessary mystery. It also raises the standard. Once the photon is ordinary, every extraordinary claim about its function must compete with ordinary photochemistry and optics.

The detector therefore does two jobs. It makes a faint phenomenon visible, and it destroys excuses. If an antioxidant suppresses emission, that is evidence about chemistry. If a wound produces a wave of counts, that is a physiological correlate. If two cell cultures allegedly communicate across a barrier, the detector can ask whether enough photons cross the barrier at the relevant wavelengths to plausibly matter.

Counting almost nothing sounds like a technical prelude. It is actually the ethical center of the subject. When effects are tiny, care is not a style preference. It is the difference between studying a weak signal and studying the apparatus's imagination.
