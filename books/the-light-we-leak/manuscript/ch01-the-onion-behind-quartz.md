# The Onion Behind Quartz

The old experiment sounds like something invented after the fact to make a science story feel inevitable. Two onion roots are arranged so that one growing tip faces another. Between them sits a window. When the window is quartz, the receiving root is reported to show more cell division. When the window is ordinary glass, the effect disappears. Quartz transmits deeper ultraviolet wavelengths that common glass blocks. Therefore, the reasoning went, the first root must be emitting an ultraviolet signal that tells the second root to divide.

Alexander Gurwitsch called it mitogenetic radiation. The word has aged badly. The experiment has aged better, not because it was proven correct, but because it contains almost every problem that still makes ultraweak photon biology difficult.

Gurwitsch was not working in an intellectual vacuum. Developmental biology in the early twentieth century had a shape problem. An organism somehow coordinated growth across space. Chemical gradients were not yet the default explanatory language they would become. Fields, radiation, induction and organized states were live possibilities. Gurwitsch was interested in the organization of living matter before he was interested in photons. The onion apparatus was an attempt to force an invisible influence to reveal a physical property.

The quartz-versus-glass contrast mattered because it was a crude spectrometer. If an effect crossed one material and not the other, the boundary might tell you something about wavelength. That is good experimental instinct. It is also where the trouble begins, because a barrier changes many things besides optical transmission: surface chemistry, temperature, geometry, airflow, static charge, contamination risk, and the mechanical arrangement of the sample. A clever discriminator is not automatically a specific one.

The Gurwitsches and other laboratories reported effects through the 1920s. The proposed wavelengths moved into the ultraviolet. Biological detectors were often used because physical detectors of the period were not sensitive enough for the claimed flux. That decision created a loop: the phenomenon was inferred from a biological response, and the biological response was then used as the detector for the phenomenon.

This is not disqualifying. Biology has often been a detector before electronics caught up. The human eye can register individual photons under favorable conditions. Frog muscle and nerve preparations historically revealed electrical effects that early instruments could barely follow. But a biological detector comes with an enormous tax. It has its own rhythms, thresholds, adaptation, batch effects and hidden variables. If the readout is mitosis, one is no longer measuring only radiation. One is measuring the entire state of a growing tissue and hoping the unknowns cancel.

By the early 1930s the debate had become sharp enough for investigators to try physical photon detection. That shift is important. A field matures when it becomes less dependent on the phenomenon it is trying to explain. If a root stimulates another root, the clean next question is not whether a third root also responds. It is whether a detector with known response characteristics registers radiation at the claimed wavelength and intensity.

The answer, in some careful hands, was no.

The story of biophotons is often told as if Gurwitsch discovered a truth that conventional science ridiculed until modern instruments finally vindicated him. This is too clean. Modern instruments did validate a broad fact that resembles part of his picture: living systems emit extremely weak photons. They did not thereby validate the original mitogenetic effect, its ultraviolet spectrum, or its proposed role in controlling cell division.

That distinction is not pedantry. Suppose I claim that a factory's workers coordinate shifts by tapping on steam pipes. Decades later, sensitive microphones prove that the pipes make faint sounds. The existence of the sound rescues one premise. It does not rescue the communication system.

Still, Gurwitsch deserves more than a cautionary footnote. He asked a question that remains open in a more disciplined form. Can spatially separated biological systems influence one another through emitted electromagnetic radiation at intensities generated endogenously? The modern answer is not a confident yes. It is also not the same no that a physicist in 1934 might have given, because the emission itself is now measurable.

The onion behind quartz survives as a useful irritant. It forces us to separate three claims that people enjoy bundling together: living cells emit light; the light can change another cell; and the cell has evolved to use that change as information. A century of research has made the first claim ordinary. The second remains fragile. The third is where the real book starts.
