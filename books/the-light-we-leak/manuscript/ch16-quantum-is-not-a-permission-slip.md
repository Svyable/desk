# Quantum Is Not a Permission Slip

Every photon is quantum mechanical.

That fact has ruined a surprising amount of writing.

The problem is not that quantum mechanics is irrelevant to biology. It is fundamental to chemistry. Electron transfer, molecular orbitals, photon absorption, fluorescence, photosynthesis, enzymatic reactions, magnetic radical-pair chemistry—all depend on quantum physics at some level. The problem begins when “quantum” changes from a theory with equations and observables into a permission slip for whatever mechanism the evidence has not yet supplied.

Biophotons are unusually vulnerable because the experimental object is already a quantum: a photon. The linguistic bridge is effortless. Cells emit photons. Photons are quantum. Therefore cellular communication may be quantum. Perhaps coherence explains organismal order. Perhaps entanglement links distant cells. Perhaps consciousness emerges from the field.

Nothing in the first two sentences establishes the rest.

A useful distinction is between *quantum origin* and *nonclassical resource*.

The emission of a photon from an excited molecule requires quantum electrodynamics for a fundamental description. Yet an ensemble of emitted photons can often be modeled perfectly well as ordinary incoherent light for biological purposes. A fluorescent lamp relies on quantum transitions without producing the sort of nonclassical optical state studied in quantum-information experiments.

If a biological theory requires specifically nonclassical properties—long-lived coherence, squeezing, entanglement, sub-Poissonian statistics, nonlocal correlations—those properties must be measured under the relevant physiological conditions.

The burden cannot be paid with the word *photon*.

This is why modern quantum optics could be so clarifying for the field. The physics has operational definitions. Photon-counting statistics can be measured. Correlation functions can be estimated. Coherence times can be tested. Coincidence measurements can compare observed statistics with classical predictions. If the light emitted by living systems exhibits unusual nonclassical properties, a properly designed experiment should be able to show that without appealing to biological complexity as a shield.

The difficulty, again, is low count rate.

When photons are sparse, detector noise and dead time can distort statistics. Long integration may mix changing biological states. A preparation can drift metabolically while enough counts are collected. Background photons can dominate. Any claim of unusual photon statistics therefore needs unusually careful controls with nonliving chemiluminescent systems matched in intensity and spectrum.

The comparison is crucial.

Suppose stressed tissue shows bursty emission. Is that evidence of coherent biological organization? Or does lipid peroxidation itself generate bursty chemistry? Suppose delayed luminescence follows a hyperbolic decay after illumination. Does that require a coherent optical field? Or can a heterogeneous set of metastable states and relaxation times produce similar kinetics?

Theoretical uniqueness matters. If multiple ordinary chemical models produce the same observed curve, the curve cannot decide among them.

A 2024 paper revisiting Gurwitsch’s onion experiment used open-quantum-systems theory to explore ultraweak ultraviolet photon emissions and quantum-biological interpretations. Such work is useful when it generates quantitative predictions. Theory can tell experimentalists what to measure and what parameter range would distinguish mechanisms. It becomes less useful when the mathematical language makes a historical claim feel rehabilitated without a new causal experiment.

The original onion-root question remains biological. Does the source alter mitosis in the receiver through photons at natural intensity? No amount of formalism can answer that without data from the source, path, and receiver.

Quantum biology offers a good comparison because some of its strongest cases are strong precisely because they are narrow.

Photosynthesis involves coherent quantum dynamics on ultrafast timescales, though the functional significance and persistence of such coherence have been debated and refined. Enzyme tunneling is studied through isotope effects and kinetic models. Avian magnetoreception research proposes radical-pair mechanisms in cryptochrome and tests them with behavioral, biochemical, magnetic-field, and radiofrequency perturbations. In each case, the word *quantum* points to a specific mechanism with experimental consequences.

The lesson is not that biophoton hypotheses should avoid quantum ideas. It is that they should become equally specific.

If coherence increases the effective detectability of UPE, calculate how. If a receptor depends on entangled photon pairs, identify the source process and measure pair correlations. If quantum statistics distinguish living emission from ordinary oxidative chemiluminescence, run the matched comparison. If a theory predicts a wavelength, coherence time, or temperature dependence, commit to it before the data are collected.

A theory earns exotic language by risking a boring failure.

There is another seductive argument worth disarming. Life maintains order far from thermodynamic equilibrium. Coherent fields are ordered. Therefore coherent photonic fields may organize life.

This is analogy, not derivation.

Living systems maintain nonequilibrium order through continuous energy and matter flows, regulatory networks, compartmentalization, feedback, and selection. Many ordered phenomena in biology do not require macroscopic optical coherence. The existence of one kind of order does not imply another.

Entropy is often recruited the same way. Organisms maintain local structure, therefore information or coherence must somehow counter entropy. In reality, organisms export entropy while consuming free energy. The thermodynamics is strange only if the boundary is drawn around the organism and the environment is forgotten.

Biophoton theories do not need cosmic exceptions. If endogenous photons matter, they will matter through ordinary physical coupling in a nonequilibrium biological system.

That is enough.

The most exciting outcome would not be proof that life violates our expectations of physics. It would be proof that evolution has exploited a part of ordinary physics we failed to notice because the signals were too faint for our old instruments.

That possibility is almost more subversive.

The universe would not need a new law. Biology would have found another use for an old one.

Quantum mechanics is welcome in this story. It is unavoidable. But it enters through measured photon statistics, molecular transitions, receptor photochemistry, and falsifiable models.

It does not enter as mood lighting.
