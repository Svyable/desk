# Back Matter

## Evidence note

The book uses four evidence classes throughout its research apparatus: verified/documented facts; credible but incomplete or interpretation-dependent reporting; disputed claims without independent confirmation; and speculative synthesis. A government memorandum establishes what its author wrote and who received it, not automatically that every factual claim inside it was true or institution-wide policy.

## Source method

Primary and authoritative sources are preferred: the 1946 naval pipe-covering paper; surviving wartime correspondence and reports; British factory-inspector material; contemporary medical literature; later NIOSH/CDC records; peer-reviewed cohort studies; court opinions where they expose what evidence was actually argued; and later historical scholarship used adversarially rather than as a substitute for the record.

Litigation-generated historical compilations are treated with particular care. They can contain authentic documents and useful citations while still selecting and interpreting those materials for a party. Whenever practical, the next research pass should move upstream to the underlying memorandum, journal article, specification, hearing, or agency record.

## Open questions

The final edition must reconstruct the exact distribution chain for the 1945 Bath warning and the later multi-yard report; determine what occupational-health controls the Bureau of Ships and Bureau of Medicine and Surgery required at specific dates; distinguish Navy yards from private contract yards and Maritime Commission practice; map the wartime workforce tenure distribution quantitatively; and test the 1946 authors’ reasoning against the strongest contemporary—not modern—counterevidence.

The manuscript should also deepen workers’ experience without inventing scenes. Oral histories, union records, compensation testimony, and shipyard-specific archives are priorities for the expansion pass.
