# Evidence Matrix

This book separates four evidence classes.

## 1 — Verified / documented

- Pulmonary fibrosis from asbestos exposure was established before World War II; Merewether and Price (1930) is a central anchor.
- Wartime investigators found substantial asbestos dust and suspected disease at Bath Iron Works.
- The inquiry expanded to multiple Navy and contract yards.
- Fleischer, Viles, Gade and Drinker published the 1946 naval pipe-covering survey and gave a reassuring occupational conclusion.
- Later insulation-worker and shipyard studies documented excess asbestos-related disease, including mesothelioma.
- Later cohort work established that asbestos-related disease can have a latency measured in decades.

## 2 — Credible but incomplete / interpretation-dependent

- How widely each wartime memorandum circulated inside Navy and Maritime organizations.
- How much wartime production urgency affected the threshold for changing materials or work practices.
- How effective contemporary dust-control rules were in actual shipboard conditions.
- How strongly the 1946 paper influenced later Navy practice, as distinct from reflecting it.

## 3 — Disputed / not established

- A single institutional moment at which every relevant asbestos risk was understood.
- Claims that wartime investigators possessed the later epidemiological picture of mesothelioma.
- Claims of deliberate falsification without direct supporting records.
- Claims that every shipyard worker or sailor experienced comparable exposure.

## 4 — Speculative synthesis / open conjecture

- Latency as an institutional blind spot that can favor premature reassurance.
- Publication as a mechanism that allowed the 1946 conclusion to outlive the narrower study design that produced it.
- Asbestos as a case where the observation window of an institution was shorter than the biological window of harm.

## Defeat conditions

The central argument must be narrowed if primary records show that the 1946 workforce had substantially longer prior exposure than current sources indicate, that common quotations materially misstate the published conclusion, that the study had little practical influence, or that wartime controls were stronger and more consistently enforced than the first-pass manuscript currently suggests.
