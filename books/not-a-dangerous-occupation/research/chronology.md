# Chronology

- 1920s — Clinical reports connect asbestos exposure with pulmonary fibrosis.
- 1930 — Merewether and Price publish a major British government report on asbestos dust, lung disease, and dust suppression.
- 1930s–early 1940s — Medical literature begins to raise concern about lung cancer in asbestos-exposed workers, without the later epidemiological certainty.
- 1940s — U.S. wartime ship construction sharply expands the population exposed to asbestos-containing insulation.
- 1945 — Philip Drinker correspondence describes Bath Iron Works evidence as indicating a serious dust risk and supports broader investigation.
- 1945 — Multi-yard Navy/Maritime survey work examines pipe coverers, work processes, dust, and chest radiographs.
- January 1946 — Fleischer, Viles, Gade and Drinker publish “A Health Survey of Pipe Covering Operations in Constructing Naval Vessels.”
- 1964 — Selikoff, Churg and Hammond publish strong cancer evidence among insulation workers.
- 1965 — Additional insulation-worker asbestosis findings are published in the *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*.
- 1970s — Occupational standards and asbestos-control efforts intensify while large exposed cohorts enter the disease-latency window.
- 1975 — *Karjala v. Johns-Manville* documents legal use of the 1946 naval survey in foreseeability arguments.
- 1979–1980 — Large insulation-worker mortality analyses quantify the long latency of asbestos disease; shipyard mesothelioma studies appear in naval regions.
- 1980 — Coastal Virginia study links a regional mesothelioma excess strongly to shipbuilding employment.
- 1980s onward — Shipyard, sailor, household and occupational cohorts continue to refine risk by exposure pathway and trade.
- 2003 — Devonport study reports a decline in mesothelioma incidence following earlier control and substitution, illustrating the same long latency on the prevention side.
- 2026 — Richard J. Kelly publishes a critical historical reappraisal of the 1946 Navy-linked survey, renewing debate over study design, retraction, and institutional consequences.
