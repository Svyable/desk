# After the Last Ship

The ship can be scrapped before the disease arrives.

That fact gives asbestos history its peculiar shape. A wartime destroyer might be built, fight, enter reserve, return to service, and disappear into a breaker’s yard while a worker who insulated its steam lines remained clinically well. Years later, after the ship itself no longer existed, the exposure could become medically visible.

Occupational history usually likes objects. Factories can be photographed. Machines can be preserved. Specifications can be archived. Asbestos disease often outlived the objects that created it. What remained were employment records, chest films, death certificates, pathology slides, compensation files, epidemiological cohorts, and the memories of families.

That delayed archive eventually made the scale of the problem difficult to deny. Naval dockyards, commercial shipyards, insulation trades, and other heavily exposed industries produced patterns that the 1946 survey could not see. Controls improved. Asbestos use declined and was restricted in many settings. Mesothelioma incidence in some dockyard populations eventually fell, decades after exposure reduction.

The fall is as important as the rise. It links intervention to consequence across the same long time scale. Better controls do not produce an immediate disappearance of a disease whose cases were initiated years earlier. Prevention can therefore look ineffective for a long time before the cohort turns.

The 1946 paper should survive in the historical record. Retraction debates are understandable, especially when an old paper continues to be used outside the context of its limitations, but erasing the paper would remove one of the clearest artifacts of the problem. Its value now lies precisely in the mismatch between what its authors could observe and what they concluded.

The sentence “not a dangerous occupation” belongs beside the later cohort studies, not instead of them. Bath belongs beside the multi-yard survey. Merewether belongs beside Selikoff. Navy memoranda belong beside legal briefs that later quoted them. None of these documents is the whole story.

The lesson is narrower and more durable than a claim about one service or one material. Long-latency hazards defeat institutions that demand visible harm before revising a useful practice. The evidence can be collected honestly, the measurements can be real, the reassuring result can be publishable, and the conclusion can still arrive decades too early.

By the time the future answers, the ship is gone.
