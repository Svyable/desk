# Cancer Enters the Record

By the 1960s, the argument had changed. The question was no longer whether heavy asbestos dust could scar lungs. It was whether workers with what had often been treated as tolerable, intermittent, or controlled exposure were dying of cancer at rates that demanded a different model of risk.

Irving Selikoff, Jacob Churg, and E. Cuyler Hammond provided one of the pivotal answers. Their 1964 study of 632 insulation workers found far more lung and pleural cancer deaths than expected and identified mesotheliomas in a workforce where that tumor should have been rare. The trade was revealing what the 1946 snapshot could not.

The contrast is striking because insulation workers were not a laboratory population. They worked across jobsites, handled varied products, and accumulated exposure over years. That complexity made exact dose reconstruction difficult, but it also made the disease pattern harder to dismiss as an artifact of one factory.

The science did not become simple overnight. Smoking complicated lung-cancer analysis. Different asbestos minerals had different industrial histories. Exposure estimates were imperfect. Diagnostic criteria evolved. But the epidemiological denominator had changed: there were now enough workers far enough from first exposure for the delayed diseases to become visible.

This is what latency had been hiding. A hazard can move from plausible to undeniable without the exposure itself changing. The difference is that time has supplied cases.

The new cancer evidence also changed the meaning of old documents. A 1945 dust warning now looked less like concern about a manageable pneumoconiosis and more like the beginning of a larger exposure history. The 1946 reassurance looked less like closure and more like premature confidence.

That retrospective transformation is dangerous if abused. A later discovery cannot be smuggled backward as proof that earlier actors knew it. But later evidence can reveal whether earlier uncertainty was resolved in the optimistic direction too aggressively.

By the mid-1960s, asbestos risk was no longer confined to a theory about fibrosis in exceptionally dusty factories. The insulation trade had become a long-term experiment, and its workers were supplying the endpoint data.
