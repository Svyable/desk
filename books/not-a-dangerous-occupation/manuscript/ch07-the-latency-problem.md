# The Latency Problem

Latency is not a footnote to asbestos disease. It is the mechanism by which early reassurance becomes possible.

Later studies of insulation workers made the timing visible. In a cohort of 17,800 workers followed through the 1970s, little increase in cancer deaths or asbestosis appeared during roughly the first fifteen to nineteen years from onset of exposure. Lung cancer rose later. Mesothelioma appeared later still and could continue increasing decades after first exposure. A study that stops at year five is therefore not merely incomplete. It can point in the wrong direction.

This creates an uncomfortable distinction between absence of evidence and evidence collected before its biological due date. A chest film taken from a young wartime insulator may be accurately normal. The error occurs when that normal film is made to answer a question about lifetime risk.

The 1946 investigators were not ignorant of latency in asbestosis. Their own field understood that chronic dust disease followed prolonged exposure. But the practical force of that knowledge was weakened by the wartime workforce they had available to examine. If most men had only a few years in the trade, the sample could not contain enough mature disease to provide a reliable denominator for long-term safety.

Cancer made the mismatch larger. Mesothelioma is especially unforgiving to short studies because its latency can stretch across most of an adult career. The worker can retire before the tumor appears. The supervisor can change jobs. The ship can be scrapped. The company can reorganize. The medical officer can die. Causal responsibility becomes administratively separated from the exposure that produced it.

Latency therefore redistributes evidence. The institution that benefits from an exposure sees the benefit immediately. The medical system that diagnoses the harm sees it decades later. The worker carries the missing data in between.

That asymmetry is the book’s central piece of physics, even though it is biological rather than mechanical. Time itself biases the archive. Early documents contain procurement decisions, dust counts, work rules, and optimistic medical surveys. Later documents contain cancer registries, death certificates, compensation claims, epidemiology, and litigation. Reading only one period produces a different moral universe.

A fair history has to connect them without pretending that the future was visible in full. The people in 1945 did not possess the 1980 latency curves. They did possess enough information to know that chronic asbestos disease could take years to appear. The difficult question is whether that should have made them more cautious about a survey whose subjects had not yet accumulated those years.
