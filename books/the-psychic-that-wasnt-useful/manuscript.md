# The Psychic That Wasn't Useful

## 1. The File Marked Psychic

There is a bureaucratic sentence more interesting than almost every story ever told about psychic spies: *the information was not useful enough*.

That is where this book begins. Not with Uri Geller bending a spoon. Not with a man lying in a room in California describing a Soviet installation. Not with a blacked-out memo that somebody on the internet has mistaken for a sacrament. With utility.

The United States government spent years examining what came to be called remote viewing: an attempt to describe a person, place, object, or event not available through ordinary sensory channels. The documentary fact is not controversial. Records released by the CIA include operational session reports, protocols, evaluations, contracts and retrospective assessments. One 1980 GRILL FLAME report states plainly that a session was conducted in response to a Pentagon request concerning the hostages in Tehran. It also contains a warning that deserves more attention than the paranormal content: the viewer's impressions were “raw intelligence data,” not yet analyzed, evaluated or collated.

That phrase is the hinge. Raw intelligence is cheap. Reliable discrimination is expensive.

A satellite image can be blurry and still be useful because the blur has geometry, provenance, calibration and known error. A human source can be wrong and still be useful because the case officer can estimate access, motive and history. An intercepted message can be ambiguous and still enter a decision system because analysts know where it came from. Remote viewing had a stranger problem. Even if some descriptions exceeded chance expectation, what was the likelihood that *this* description, today, about *this* target, was the good one?

The believer wants to ask whether psychic perception is real. The skeptic wants to ask why anyone intelligent ever entertained it. An intelligence service has to ask a crueller question: what can I do with the next report?

That question will follow us through every chapter.

## 2. The Russians Made Us Do It

Governments routinely investigate things they do not believe because another government might believe them.

This is one of the least appreciated engines of Cold War science. A report that an adversary is pursuing an exotic capability creates at least three possibilities. The adversary has discovered something. The adversary is wasting money. The adversary wants you to think it has discovered something. All three justify some amount of attention.

American interest in Soviet parapsychology therefore cannot be read as a secret government certification that psi worked. Threat assessment is not endorsement. Intelligence institutions are paid to examine low-probability, high-consequence possibilities precisely because ordinary scientific priors do not exhaust strategic risk.

The same logic produced serious investigations of improbable weapons, strange propulsion concepts, biological effects and deception programs. The threshold for *looking* is much lower than the threshold for *fielding*.

This distinction matters because declassification creates a visual illusion. A stamped page saying SECRET feels like stronger evidence than an unstamped journal article. Often it is evidence only that somebody with a clearance cared enough to write the page. Classification tells us about custody and sensitivity, not truth.

The Soviet-interest story is therefore evidence of a feedback loop: one state's curiosity can become another state's requirement. A small research literature can acquire strategic mass without acquiring scientific certainty.

## 3. Stanford, Lasers, and a Strange Side Project

The early American story runs through Stanford Research Institute, an institution associated with engineering, lasers, computing and defense research rather than séance rooms. That setting matters. Frontier claims travel differently when they enter laboratories populated by people who already build improbable things for serious customers.

The central personalities became famous enough to distort the record. Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff are now characters in competing mythologies. In one, they proved psychic functioning and frightened the intelligence establishment. In another, credulous researchers fooled themselves in a permissive methodological era. Both stories are too clean.

What actually matters is experimental architecture: who knew the target, how it was selected, what the viewer was told, how transcripts were judged, whether feedback could leak, how many possible targets existed, whether misses disappeared from the narrative, and whether scoring rules were fixed before the session.

Parapsychology's history is full of dramatic anecdotes because anecdotes are the natural unit of human attention. Statistics require the denominator. Intelligence requires something harder still: prospective reliability.

A single astonishing description can justify another experiment. It cannot justify a collection platform.

## 4. What Counts as a Hit?

Suppose I ask someone to describe a hidden target and receive: water, metal, vertical structure, movement, cold, open space.

Have I received information?

The answer depends less on the romance of the words than on the scoring rule. If the target is a suspension bridge, “metal” and “vertical structure” look good. If it is a submarine, water, metal and movement look good. If it is a hydroelectric dam, nearly everything looks good. If it is a cathedral in winter, perhaps vertical, cold and open space survive. Human language is generous after the fact.

This is why judging procedures are not housekeeping. They are the experiment.

A forced-choice protocol can narrow interpretive freedom but may poorly resemble intelligence work. Free-response descriptions resemble intelligence work but create enormous scoring latitude. Independent blind judges help. Target pools help. Predefined ranking rules help. None completely removes the problem that the thing being measured is semantic resemblance.

The scientific dispute around remote viewing is therefore partly a dispute over how to turn prose into numbers without letting the number inherit the judge's expectations.

That is not unique to psi. Medicine has endpoint selection. Psychology has researcher degrees of freedom. Intelligence analysis has confirmation bias. Machine learning has benchmark leakage. Remote viewing merely makes the problem embarrassingly visible.

## 5. The Coordinate Without a Map

One clever protocol used arbitrary coordinates as target cues. The viewer was not supposed to know what the numbers referred to. The coordinates functioned as an address without an ordinary map.

There is something almost beautiful about this design because it tries to amputate meaning from the cue. If the cue carries no descriptive content, successful description cannot obviously be attributed to ordinary inference from the tasking language.

But cue isolation is only one leakage path. The experimenter can know. The target pool can be patterned. Feedback can alter later behavior. Judges can make flexible comparisons. Sessions can be selected for presentation. Operational customers can remember the useful fragments and forget the sludge.

Every paranormal protocol eventually becomes an information-security problem.

That observation will matter later, because the strongest modern experiment would look less like a Victorian test of telepathy and more like a cryptographic protocol: compartmented roles, auditable randomization, immutable timestamps, preregistered scoring, blinded analysis, complete data release and adversarial red-team inspection.

If psi exists, it should not need methodological darkness.

## 6. Tehran

The hostage crisis generated real remote-viewing tasking. Declassified session reports make that much plain. A January 1980 report describes a request related to the U.S. Embassy compound in Tehran and records the viewer's impressions as raw intelligence. Other sessions followed.

This is where storytelling becomes dangerous. Operational use sounds like validation. It is not.

Intelligence organizations sometimes use weak sources because strong sources do not exist. During a hostage crisis, a low-cost additional channel may be worth trying even if its expected reliability is poor. The decision to task a viewer therefore demonstrates institutional willingness, not demonstrated efficacy.

The correct reconstruction requires every session, the exact cue, the time it was recorded, what conventional intelligence was already known, the evaluator's contemporaneous judgment, and whether the information changed a decision. Famous fragments cannot carry the burden.

The Tehran files are valuable precisely because they drag the phenomenon out of a laboratory hit rate and into the world of consequence. A description can be statistically interesting and operationally inert. It can also be wrong in a way that is impossible to recognize until after the decision.

The hostage crisis did not need wonder. It needed location, identity, timing and confidence.

## 7. The Problem of the Famous Success

Every anomalous field has a greatest hit.

The trouble begins when the greatest hit becomes the dataset.

Remote-viewing lore contains spectacular stories involving hidden facilities, equipment, hostages and future events. Some have documentary support for at least part of the sequence. Others accreted details in retelling. The epistemic mistake is not necessarily believing that an impressive session occurred. It is treating the memorable session as representative.

A collection discipline must survive random Tuesday.

If ten sessions produce one uncanny match, two weak matches and seven unusable reports, the uncanny one is scientifically interesting. But an analyst receiving session eleven does not know which category it belongs to. Reliability must be estimated prospectively, not assigned retrospectively by astonishment.

This is the central cruelty of weak effects. They can be real enough to generate stories and weak enough to defeat use.

## 8. Noise Has a Career

Intelligence organizations are professional consumers of noise. Radar clutter, intercepted fragments, rumors, defectors, satellite shadows, procurement anomalies, diplomatic language: almost everything arrives mixed with uncertainty.

So why would remote viewing be uniquely intolerable?

Because useful noisy systems usually have calibratable error. A radar has detection probabilities. A source develops a reporting history. A forecast can be scored. A sensor can be tested against known targets. The user learns when to trust it.

Remote viewing struggled at this second-order task. Even if aggregate experiments hinted at above-chance performance, the user lacked a dependable confidence marker attached to an individual product. A viewer saying “I feel very certain” did not necessarily solve it. Neither did stylistic vividness.

The problem was not simply signal-to-noise. It was signal-identification.

## 9. The Ganzfeld Detour

Remote viewing was not the only laboratory architecture built to test anomalous information transfer. Ganzfeld experiments reduced ordinary sensory stimulation and asked a receiver to identify target material, often from a small set of possibilities.

The literature became a long-running argument over small deviations from chance, methodological quality, publication bias and replication. That argument matters to our intelligence story because it provides a partially independent experimental tradition. If different protocols repeatedly produce small anomalies, the case for “something worth explaining” strengthens. If effects depend on particular laboratories, analytic choices or eras, it weakens.

The crucial word is small.

A small statistical effect can be scientifically profound and practically useless. The Higgs boson did not need to tell anyone where a hostage was being held. Intelligence does.

## 10. Hyman and Honorton

Ray Hyman and Charles Honorton became opposing poles of the ganzfeld debate, but their methodological exchange is more interesting than the team sport built around it. They disagreed about what the accumulated experiments established while converging on the need for stronger procedures.

That convergence produced a rare thing in controversial science: criticism that altered experimental design.

The right question is not who “won.” It is what happened to the effect after the rules tightened.

A mature anomalous science should want its critics to design the next experiment. If the effect survives, the claim improves. If it dies, knowledge improves.

## 11. The Replication That Didn't Settle It

In 1999 Julie Milton and Richard Wiseman published a meta-analysis of thirty ganzfeld studies from seven independent laboratories that followed stringent methodological guidelines. Their result did not confirm the principal above-chance effect: Stouffer z = 0.70, one-tailed p = .24. Their conclusion was direct: the ganzfeld technique did not then offer a replicable laboratory method for producing ESP.

That is serious counterevidence.

It is not the end of the literature. Later meta-analyses have reported positive aggregate effects, and proponents dispute whether study selection, participant characteristics and protocol variants explain differences. The existence of continued statistical dispute decades later is itself evidence about maturity. A phenomenon ready for intelligence deployment should not depend on which meta-analysis the reader finds persuasive.

Science can tolerate an unresolved small effect. Operations cannot outsource a hostage rescue to a forest plot.

## 12. Jessica Utts Says There Is an Effect

The 1995 government-commissioned evaluation is often compressed into a single verdict, which is unfortunate because its internal disagreement is the point.

Statistician Jessica Utts argued that the experimental evidence had reached the point where anomalous cognition should be regarded as established and that further proof-oriented experiments were less useful than work on understanding and application. That is a strong claim and deserves to be stated without sneering.

It also does not establish operational utility.

Aggregate statistical departure from chance answers one question. Whether a field report should alter an intelligence judgment answers another. A phenomenon can clear the first bar and fail the second by a mile.

## 13. Ray Hyman Says That Isn't Enough

Hyman's side of the 1995 evaluation emphasized methodological and replication concerns and resisted the inference that the experimental database established psychic functioning. His skepticism was not merely “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.” It concerned what kinds of experimental independence and methodological closure were necessary before the anomaly could be treated as a new human capability.

The disagreement between Utts and Hyman is often presented as proof that experts can see whatever they want. A better reading is that they were applying different thresholds to a weak and unusual evidence base.

One saw cumulative statistical structure demanding explanation. The other saw a literature still unable to exclude enough ordinary explanations.

Both positions are more intellectually respectable than the internet versions of them.

## 14. The User's Test

The American Institutes for Research evaluation did not recommend restoring the program as an intelligence capability. CIA's own later public summary says the assessment found enough accurate experiences to defy randomness while judging the phenomenon too unreliable, inconsistent and sporadic for intelligence purposes.

That sentence should be famous.

It describes a category that modern culture handles badly: possibly real, not useful.

We prefer discoveries to sort themselves into true and false. Engineering adds another axis. A superconducting effect that exists only under extreme laboratory conditions is real before it is commercially useful. A drug can alter a biomarker without improving outcomes. An AI system can exceed a benchmark while failing unpredictably in deployment. A remote-viewing effect, if genuine, could be statistically detectable and still fail every operational requirement that matters.

The user's test is not anti-scientific. It is science under consequence.

## 15. Why Intelligence Kept Paying

Why continue funding something unreliable?

Because expected value is not the same as probability. Because Soviet activity mattered. Because the cost was small relative to major collection systems. Because occasional apparent successes were psychologically powerful. Because bureaucratic sponsors existed. Because termination also carries risk when nobody wants to be the official who killed the one strange program that might have mattered.

And perhaps because the data remained irritating.

Institutions do not need certainty to sustain a small program. They need unresolved possibility plus a constituency plus tolerable cost.

This is how fringe subjects can survive inside serious bureaucracies without a conspiracy and without vindication.

## 16. How Secrecy Distorts Science

Classification protects sources and methods. It also damages scientific memory.

A classified experiment cannot easily recruit broad methodological criticism. Operational failures may remain invisible while successes circulate orally. Later declassification is uneven. Documents lose context. Code names change. Participants write memoirs. The surviving archive becomes a biased sample of a biased process.

Then the public makes the opposite mistake: because a document was once secret, its contents acquire an aura of suppressed truth.

Secrecy can therefore inflate and erase at the same time.

The remote-viewing archive should be read as an intelligence archive, not a holy text. A session report proves a session occurred. A program name proves a program existed. A sponsor proves somebody paid. None of those facts independently proves the claimed human faculty.

## 17. The Experiment We Should Run Now

The next useful experiment should be designed so believers expect it to work and skeptics agree in advance that a positive result would be difficult to explain away.

Multiple independent laboratories. Participant-selection rules frozen beforehand. Targets drawn by a publicly auditable random process but concealed cryptographically until all responses are committed. No experimenter with participant contact knows the target. Free-response material timestamped and preserved in full. Several scoring systems preregistered, including machine-assisted semantic scoring whose model and version are frozen before collection. Every trial reported. Sequential analysis rules specified. A replication phase triggered automatically. A red team paid to find leakage.

Then add the intelligence test.

Give ordinary analysts and remote viewers the same blinded set of questions. Require probability judgments, not mystical prose. Score calibration and decision value prospectively. Ask whether adding remote-viewing output improves forecasts over the analyst baseline. Do not let anyone select the impressive cases after the answers are known.

If there is an effect, this design should help it become boring.

Boring is what real capabilities eventually become.

## 18. The Psychic That Wasn't Useful

The temptation at the end of a book like this is to reveal which side was secretly right.

I cannot.

The documentary record establishes that the U.S. government investigated and operationally tasked remote viewers. It establishes that serious researchers produced experiments they believed showed anomalous information transfer. It establishes serious methodological criticism and failed or disputed replications. It establishes that the final intelligence judgment was not “nothing ever happened.” It was that whatever happened was not dependable enough to use.

That is a much stranger ending.

The psychic who is always wrong is easy. The psychic who is always right is easy too, at least epistemically. The difficult creature is the one who is right slightly too often, or appears to be, but cannot tell you when.

That creature lives everywhere now.

It lives in medical screening. It lives in financial models. It lives in machine-learning systems that are brilliant until distribution shifts. It lives in intelligence tips that are individually unverifiable but collectively patterned. It lives anywhere a weak signal meets a consequential decision.

Remote viewing may ultimately belong in the history of human self-deception. It may belong in the history of a small anomalous effect that science has not adequately explained. It may contain pieces of both stories. The evidence does not license me to choose the exciting answer simply because it makes a better final chapter.

What it does license is a rule.

Never ask only whether an effect is real.

Ask whether you can know, before the outcome, when to trust it.

That was the problem in the room with the coordinate and the blank paper. It remains the problem now.
