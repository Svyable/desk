# Science’s Missing TTL

A scientific paper does not expire.

That is mostly a virtue.

A paper published in 1905 can remain important in 2026. An experiment can be reanalyzed. A dataset can acquire new uses. A wrong theory can remain historically valuable. A result superseded by better work can still show how knowledge developed. Science depends on durable records because claims need to be inspected, challenged, reproduced, and placed in sequence.

The archive should be stubborn.

Operational authority should be less so.

This distinction becomes obvious in fields where published evidence is converted into recommendations. A physician does not want the newest paper merely because it is newest. Nor does she want a guideline to remain unchanged for ten years merely because the committee that wrote it disbanded. The practical question is whether accumulated evidence has changed enough to alter what should be done now.

Living systematic reviews and living guidelines were developed to make that question part of the process rather than an occasional project.

A traditional systematic review takes a defined body of literature, searches it according to a protocol, appraises studies, synthesizes results, and publishes. The publication is a snapshot. New studies begin appearing immediately after the search closes.

A living review continues surveillance and updates the synthesis when new evidence is sufficiently important. The World Health Organization has used living-guideline approaches in areas where evidence changes rapidly, including maternal and perinatal health and COVID-19. The exact processes vary, but the principle is consistent: a recommendation can have continuing maintenance rather than a ceremonial rebirth every several years.

This is a scientific version of time to live.

The underlying studies remain in the archive. The synthesis is periodically refreshed. The recommendation’s authority depends on a process that knows new evidence can arrive.

Most scientific claims do not have such an explicit mechanism.

A paper is published. It receives citations. It enters review articles. A number appears in textbooks. A finding becomes part of a grant proposal, press article, corporate presentation, policy memo, or model training corpus. Years later, the original evidence may have been challenged, replicated, refined, contradicted, or limited to a narrower context.

The citation remains perfectly valid as a pointer to what the paper said.

Its rhetorical use can become stale.

This is how facts acquire immortality through repetition.

A study reports an effect under a particular sample, method, period, and measurement. Later writers compress it into a sentence. The sentence loses the sample. another writer cites the second writer. the result becomes a general proposition. Eventually the claim circulates independently of the conditions that made it true.

Scientific institutions try to resist this through replication, peer review, retraction, correction, meta-analysis, systematic review, and methodological criticism. None is a complete temporal-governance system.

Peer review happens before publication and cannot see the future.

Retraction addresses serious problems but is too blunt for results that remain legitimate yet less general than originally believed.

Citation counts often reward persistence rather than current confidence.

A meta-analysis can become stale too.

A textbook has an edition cycle.

A regulatory guideline may have a review date.

The missing piece is often a visible signal of when a claim’s operational authority was last evaluated against the current evidence base.

That sounds like a rating system for truth, which would be dangerous if centralized or oversimplified. Science is not a database in which one authority flips findings from valid to expired. Different communities can reasonably disagree. Evidence strength depends on the question. New studies can introduce conflict rather than resolution.

The expiration layer should not pretend to solve epistemology.

It can solve a narrower problem: make age and review status harder to ignore.

A clinical recommendation can state when its evidence search was last updated. A policy synthesis can state the date through which literature was considered. A machine-readable research corpus can distinguish a retracted paper from a current one and link corrections. A review article can expose when a field has changed materially since publication. A model retrieving scientific sources can prefer current syntheses for current-practice questions while still retrieving older primary studies when historical context matters.

This is provenance with time attached.

The design matters because generative AI changes how scientific claims travel.

A language model can compress hundreds of papers into an answer. That can be extraordinary. It can also flatten chronology. A result from 2004, a review from 2012, and a guideline from 2026 can appear in adjacent sentences with the same grammatical confidence.

If the system does not know which source is current for the question, abundance becomes a new form of staleness.

The problem will grow as automated agents conduct literature reviews, design experiments, draft regulatory documents, and recommend research directions. An agent can retrieve more science than a person could read, but retrieval volume does not answer which claims deserve present authority.

An old but foundational paper may be essential. A recent preprint may be exciting and unreliable. A living review may be current but broad. A retracted study may need to be included because it explains why a field changed. Temporal metadata must work with evidence hierarchy, not replace it.

That complexity is an argument for explicitness.

A paper can remain permanent while carrying evolving relationships: replicated by, contradicted by, corrected by, superseded for this use, included in this review, excluded from that review, data updated, code unavailable, concern raised, retracted.

Science already has pieces of this graph.

The question is whether current decision systems use them.

A hospital guideline committee may. A casual web search may not. A language model trained once almost certainly cannot know every post-publication development unless connected to current sources and instructed to reason about them.

Expiration gives the retrieval system a reason to ask again.

Suppose an AI assistant has a cached answer to a biomedical question derived from a high-quality review published three years ago. The answer may still be correct. But if the domain changes quickly, the system should not reuse that synthesis indefinitely without checking whether new guidance or major evidence has appeared.

The TTL belongs on the synthesis, not the individual paper.

This is similar to DNS. The cached answer is valuable because asking the whole internet every time is wasteful. The authoritative source can remain the same when the clock expires. The point is to revisit, not to assume change.

Scientific synthesis is more contested than DNS authority, but the computational pattern survives. A system can define freshness requirements appropriate to the question.

For a historical question about the discovery of penicillin, the relevant sources barely age.

For current treatment guidance in a rapidly developing disease, months may matter.

For a safety signal involving a newly introduced medical product, days can matter.

For a theorem, age may be irrelevant unless an error is discovered.

For a social-science estimate tied to a particular labor market, context can change quickly.

Knowledge does not have one half-life.

That makes a universal “recent sources only” rule as foolish as no temporal rule at all.

Recency bias can be destructive in science. New papers can be underpowered, unreplicated, methodologically fashionable, or simply wrong. Older work can be more thoroughly tested. A living review can overreact if update thresholds are poorly designed. Constant updating can amplify publication noise.

The expiration layer should therefore reward revalidation, not novelty.

A fifty-year-old claim that survives repeated serious tests may deserve more confidence than a finding published yesterday. The relevant temporal question is not “How old is the paper?” but “How long since this claim was seriously checked against what we now know?”

That is a much harder metadata problem.

It is also closer to scientific practice at its best.

Fields maintain confidence through ongoing use, replication, theory, measurement, criticism, and application. Much of this is tacit. Researchers know which old paper is still foundational and which famous result has become a cautionary tale. Outsiders often do not.

AI can help externalize that tacit temporal map if it has access to the right evidence.

Imagine a research assistant that, when presenting a major empirical claim, can distinguish among the original study, the latest systematic review, known replication attempts, retractions or corrections, and current authoritative guidance. It does not need to assign a single truth score. It needs to tell the reader whether the claim’s authority has been renewed.

This is particularly important for numbers.

Statistics detach from time easily. A prevalence estimate, productivity effect, treatment response, public opinion measure, cost estimate, or technology adoption rate can remain quoted long after the underlying population changes. The number is accurate to the study and misleading as a description of now.

Scientific writing often protects against this with dates and sample descriptions. Secondary writing strips them away.

Every frequently reused statistic should carry a vintage.

That sounds obvious, yet public debate is filled with numbers whose year is omitted because the number itself carries more rhetorical force without it.

Expiration is an antidote to timeless statistics.

A policy model can require that key inputs be refreshed on a schedule. A dashboard can flag measures older than their expected update cycle. A report generator can refuse to call a number “current” after a defined period. An AI assistant can search again when a user asks “today” or “currently” rather than relying on stored knowledge.

This is not a small feature for AI. It is part of epistemic honesty.

A model with an internal knowledge cutoff has a crude global expiration boundary. The world does not. Different facts age at radically different speeds. A static cutoff therefore treats a 2024 theorem and a 2024 exchange rate as equally old, which is absurd.

Connected systems can do better by assigning freshness to sources and claim types.

The deeper institutional lesson is that science works because it never grants publication infinite authority, even when the paper itself survives forever.

A result remains open to challenge.

What science often lacks is a visible schedule for that challenge when the result has crossed into policy, medicine, software, or public belief.

Living reviews provide one answer. Mandatory guideline reviews provide another. Post-market surveillance in medicine provides another. Replication efforts provide another. Retractions and expressions of concern handle severe failures. Each mechanism asks the present to reconsider the past under different triggers.

They form an informal expiration layer around durable knowledge.

Making that layer more explicit could improve both science and the systems built on it.

The obvious danger is bureaucratizing inquiry. A central authority declaring findings “expired” would be intolerable. Researchers must remain free to use old evidence, reinterpret it, challenge consensus, and recover forgotten ideas. Scientific progress often comes from refusing current fashion.

The right target is operational certainty, not intellectual permission.

Do not delete the old paper.

Do not ban the old idea.

Do not make novelty a requirement.

Do mark when a recommendation has not been reviewed in ten years.

Do show that a statistic describes 2014 rather than 2026.

Do distinguish a retracted result from an uncontested one.

Do tell a decision system when its evidence search ended.

Do require current-practice claims to consult current sources.

This keeps the archive radically open while making present authority more demanding.

There is a final benefit. Expiration can make uncertainty less embarrassing.

Institutions like definitive documents. A guideline issued with an explicit review horizon admits that its authority is provisional. A model approval with a revalidation date admits that performance can change. A policy forecast carrying a vintage admits that assumptions age.

That admission is not weakness.

It is what serious knowledge looks like in a moving world.

Science has never needed its discoveries to disappear.

It needs our confidence to know when it is borrowing from the past.
