# The Right to Be a Stranger Again

For most of human history, familiarity had geography.

The baker knew what you bought because you returned to the same bakery. The bartender knew your drink. The physician knew your history. The neighbor knew your family. The shopkeeper might extend credit because your reputation lived in the town.

Then you went somewhere else.

A stranger was not a person without a past. A stranger was a person whose past was not automatically present in this room.

Digital systems are eliminating that condition.

A person visits a website once and can be recognized later. A retailer remembers purchases. An advertising network connects behavior across contexts. A platform remembers searches, follows, pauses, clicks, locations, and relationships. A recommendation system develops a model of taste. A personal assistant may eventually know more durable context than many friends.

Recognition is useful.

It is also a form of power.

The person entering a new context may no longer get to decide how much history arrives with them.

This chapter is about an expiration problem that does not fit neatly into retention schedules. It concerns the lifetime of familiarity itself.

How long does an interaction entitle a system to stop treating you as a stranger?

The commercial internet has usually answered: until you clear the cookie, delete the account, invoke a legal right, change devices, defeat the identity graph, or the company chooses otherwise.

That is a technical answer disguised as a social norm.

Human familiarity does not normally work this way. A cashier who served you once does not acquire a lifelong claim to recognize you. A hotel where you stayed ten years ago may keep legally required records, but you would be surprised if every employee greeted you with intimate details from that trip. A store can remember a warranty purchase without assuming every future visit should be personalized from your entire history.

Context has a natural half-life.

The law has approached pieces of this through privacy, purpose limitation, data minimization, storage limitation, deletion rights, opt-outs, and rules around tracking. Those frameworks are important. The social question remains broader.

Even lawful memory can be overfamiliar.

A system can have permission to store a fact and still be wrong to surface it here.

Imagine walking into a pharmacy and the clerk loudly saying, “Welcome back. Are you still taking the medication you bought for that condition three years ago?” The data may be accurate. The customer relationship may be real. The failure is contextual.

Digital interfaces commit softer versions constantly.

A person shops for a funeral and receives reminders months later. Searches for a health concern and sees the topic follow them. Buys a gift and has the recipient’s interest incorporated into their own recommendation profile. Watches one video during a moment of curiosity and spends weeks teaching the algorithm that curiosity was not identity.

The system interprets observation as durable preference.

This is memory without social expiry.

Personalization systems usually optimize for prediction. If an old signal still improves the probability of a click, purchase, or watch, there is an incentive to keep it. The question of whether the person wants that old self continuously reintroduced is external to the objective.

The model can be statistically right and socially intrusive.

A right to be a stranger again would not require companies to erase every transaction. It would create a presumption that familiarity itself has scope.

A purchase can remain in accounting records while leaving ordinary recommendation.

A past location can remain in a private timeline while no longer driving advertising.

A conversation can remain in a user-controlled archive while dropping out of active personalization.

A completed project can remain searchable without shaping unrelated future work.

A relationship can move from active familiarity to historical memory.

This is the same archive-versus-authority distinction applied to identity.

The idea becomes more important with AI assistants because their value increases with memory.

A good assistant should remember that you prefer concise answers, that a particular name refers to your colleague, that you are working on a long-running project, that you dislike a recurring travel option. Requiring the assistant to forget everything every week would make it tedious and stupid.

But useful memory can become identity lock-in.

Suppose a user spends a difficult year discussing a divorce. The assistant learns the names, conflicts, anxieties, legal questions, and routines surrounding the event. Five years later, the user asks for a restaurant recommendation and the system subtly filters choices based on preferences inferred during that period.

Nothing is technically wrong.

Something is humanly wrong.

The person should be able to leave an era.

Today deletion is usually object-based: delete this conversation, clear this history, remove this memory. Human change is often period-based or context-based.

Forget the job search.

Archive the divorce period.

Stop using my old city as evidence about where I belong.

Keep my writing project but forget the medical scare around it.

Treat my teenage activity as history, not personalization.

Do not infer my current politics from what I read during an election six years ago.

These are temporal identity commands.

They will require memory systems more sophisticated than a list of facts with delete buttons.

A personal agent may need eras.

An era is not a hard partition. Important facts can cross it. A child’s birthday remains relevant. A durable allergy remains relevant. A professional qualification remains. But many observations should lose default retrieval priority when the life context that produced them ends.

The user should be able to declare that transition explicitly, and the system can infer candidates for review without making sensitive assumptions on its own.

This would create something digital systems rarely offer: a fresh start without total amnesia.

The distinction matters because starting over has historically been a source of freedom.

People move to new cities. change careers. enter new schools. leave communities. adopt new styles. join new social circles. recover from public embarrassment. escape abusive relationships. become sober. change names. reinvent professional identities.

Not all reinvention is virtuous. A fraudster can exploit anonymity. An abuser can seek a clean slate to find new victims. Creditors, courts, employers, platforms, and communities can have legitimate reasons to preserve certain histories.

The right to be a stranger again cannot be absolute.

It is not a right to become unaccountable.

It is a right to contextual distance.

That difference is essential.

A platform can preserve a fraud-prevention record while stopping an old shopping preference from following the user. A professional licensing board can retain discipline relevant to public safety while an advertising system forgets embarrassing searches. A court can preserve evidence while a recommendation engine stops treating a legal crisis as a consumer identity.

Different systems deserve different memories of the same person.

The contemporary data economy often moves in the opposite direction. Identity resolution seeks to connect fragments across devices, services, channels, and contexts so the organization sees one customer. From the company’s perspective, fragmentation is a defect.

From the person’s perspective, fragmentation can be privacy.

The self is not always improved by perfect joins.

A person may want the bookstore to know one version of them and the employer another. The medical system needs information the entertainment platform does not. A dating service should not inherit an old professional profile merely because the email address matches. A child’s school record should not become advertising context.

Contextual separation is not dishonesty.

It is ordinary social life.

AI threatens to collapse it because the most capable assistant may sit above many contexts. One agent could manage email, calendar, money, health, travel, work, shopping, and relationships. The convenience would be extraordinary.

So would the concentration of familiarity.

The system could know that a meeting with a certain person raises the user’s stress, that a purchase followed an argument, that medical news changed travel plans, that a salary negotiation affects housing decisions. Cross-context reasoning is precisely what makes an agent powerful.

The expiration layer has to decide when cross-context knowledge is still invited.

A user may want an assistant to connect health and calendar for medical appointments but not health and employer communications. The user may want financial context used for budgeting but not gift recommendations. A temporary crisis may justify broader integration that should narrow afterward.

Consent therefore needs a temporal topology: which contexts can see which other contexts, for how long, and for what purpose.

That sounds complicated because human boundaries are complicated.

The alternative is simpler only for the system.

Everything remembers everything forever.

The person pays the social cost.

There is a commercial opportunity in the opposite design. Trustworthy AI could compete on its ability to forget appropriately. Not “we delete all your data,” which sacrifices continuity, but “we know the difference between your history and your present.”

That is a much more ambitious promise.

It requires provenance, memory types, user control, contextual retrieval, expiry, and transparent correction. It requires the system to expose why an old fact appeared. It may require models to operate on temporary context without absorbing every interaction into durable personalization.

The reward is a form of intimacy that does not become surveillance.

Human relationships have this quality at their best. Someone can know you deeply and still allow you to change. They remember your history without using every old fact against every new version of you.

A machine will not acquire that grace accidentally.

Its default advantage is perfect retrieval.

We will have to design the mercy.

The right to be forgotten was an early legal response to a world in which retrieval made the past newly powerful.

The next question is subtler.

Can a system remember what happened and still meet you as if something new might happen next?

That is the right to be a stranger again.
