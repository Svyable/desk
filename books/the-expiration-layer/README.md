Why the Future Needs an Expiration Date.

The central argument is that expiration is one of civilization’s most useful but undergeneralized design primitives. Computer networks already know that cached answers, borrowed addresses, certificates, sessions, and credentials become dangerous when they persist without revalidation. Medicine asks how long a drug is known to remain stable. Finance puts clocks on options. Privacy law limits how long some personal data should remain identifiable. Legislatures sometimes attach sunset clauses to extraordinary powers.

Yet much of modern life still defaults to permanence. Permissions survive job changes. Rules outlive emergencies. subscriptions renew from silence. models keep making decisions after their operating environment has moved. records trail people long after their predictive value has collapsed. committees, policies, data stores, forecasts, and institutional assumptions acquire a peculiar advantage simply by already existing.

This book asks what changes if we treat expiry not as deletion but as a renewal test. The claim is not that everything should disappear. Rights, archives, obligations, evidence, and memory can deserve extraordinary durability. The claim is narrower and more demanding: when persistence itself creates risk, continued authority should periodically have to prove that it is still justified.

The manuscript moves from the internet’s time-to-live mechanisms through cybersecurity, public-key certificates, law, bureaucracy, AI model drift, machine memory, reputation, subscriptions, markets, medicine, science, and organizations. It ends with the limits of the idea and a practical architecture for deciding what should expire, what should merely be reviewed, and what should remain durable.
