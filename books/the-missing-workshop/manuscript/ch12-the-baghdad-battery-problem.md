# Chapter 12 — The Baghdad Battery Problem

The so-called Baghdad Battery is useful because it sits almost perfectly at the boundary between possibility and evidence.

The objects commonly given that name consist of ceramic vessels associated with copper cylinders, iron rods and bitumen. Wilhelm König proposed in the twentieth century that the arrangement might have functioned as a galvanic cell. Modern reconstructions can indeed produce voltage when an electrolyte is added.

That last sentence has hypnotized generations.

A replica works, therefore the ancient object was a battery.

No.

A reconstruction proves that a chosen arrangement of materials *can* generate electrochemical potential under chosen conditions. Historical function requires more: secure original assembly, residue evidence, wear, context, associated wiring or electrodes, downstream electroplated objects if plating is claimed, textual evidence, repeated standardized examples, or another independent trace of electrical use.

The Baghdad objects are therefore a perfect test of experimental archaeology’s limits.

Recent popular treatment still reflects the dispute. A 2026 History.com review quotes archaeologists emphasizing that König did not observe an intact battery-like assembly in undisturbed context and that later replicas inherit assumptions from his reconstruction (https://www.history.com/articles/baghdad-battery). The popular label has become more certain than the archaeology.

This is exactly what must not happen to Antikythera.

The difference between the two cases is enormous.

Antikythera has directly observed gears, inscriptions, astronomical scales, meshing relationships and a shipwreck context. Its astronomical-computing interpretation grew out of physical structure and deciphered text. Competing reconstructions disagree about missing architecture, but not about whether the object was, say, a funerary container accidentally capable of turning.

The Baghdad case has functional ambiguity at the core.

This gives us a second workshop test: **downstream necessity**.

If an artifact is interpreted as a technology, what other things should that technology require or produce?

Electroplating requires more than voltage. It requires an electrolytic process, electrical connections, controlled deposition, repeated practice and objects whose surfaces show the result. A ritual container, by contrast, can plausibly exist without any electrical ecosystem. The more elaborate interpretation carries a larger burden.

Antikythera’s astronomical interpretation has strong downstream fit. The ratios correspond to known cycles. The inscriptions concern astronomical and calendrical functions. The dials are appropriate outputs. The mechanism sits inside an intellectual culture with relevant astronomy.

No such dense convergence rescues the battery claim.

This chapter matters because books about surprising ancient technology routinely suffer evidentiary contagion. Once one impossible-looking object is proved real, weaker anomalies gather around it as if authenticity were transferable.

“People said Antikythera was impossible too.”

This sentence is rhetorically powerful and logically useless.

People have been wrong before does not tell us which current claim is true. Science would collapse if every rejected hypothesis could claim support from the historical fact that some rejected hypothesis was eventually vindicated.

The existence of black swans proves black swans exist. It does not prove every dark bird is a swan.

Antikythera should therefore make us more open to anomalous artifacts and more demanding about them.

A genuine anomaly has a wonderful property: scrutiny creates structure. Imaging reveals more gears. inscriptions resolve. material analysis narrows chronology. excavation refines context. Independent methods converge.

A weak anomaly often behaves differently. Scrutiny dissolves provenance, reveals reconstruction assumptions, or shows that the spectacular interpretation explains less than an ordinary one.

That difference is not universal, but it is a useful heuristic.

There is also an ethical dimension. Ancient peoples do not need imaginary electricity to be sophisticated. Calling an ambiguous jar a battery can actually flatten the real cultural context by forcing the object into a modern technological narrative.

We recognize ourselves and call that respect.

It can be the opposite.

The missing-workshop thesis avoids this by demanding local ecology. A real technology should connect to the materials, practices, problems and knowledge of the culture that made it. The connection may be surprising. It may revise our estimate of that culture dramatically. But it should not require the artifact to escape history altogether.

Antikythera becomes more Hellenistic the more we understand it, not less.

Its eclipse cycle points toward Babylonian mathematical astronomy. Its lunar modeling points toward Greek astronomical theory. Its bronze work belongs to Mediterranean metallurgy. Its inscriptions use Greek language. Its calendar and Games dial belong to ancient civic and calendrical life.

The machine is extraordinary because these ordinary streams were combined brilliantly.

The Baghdad Battery problem is what happens when we reverse the procedure.

We start with the extraordinary interpretation and ask history to catch up.
