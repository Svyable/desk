# Chapter 17 — A Scale for Impossible Objects

We need a better unit than astonishment.

An object looks impossible. Fine. How impossible?

Not emotionally. Institutionally.

I propose seven questions for any alleged anomalous technology.

**1. Is the object itself secure?**

Provenance, dating, excavation context, chain of custody and material integrity come first. Antikythera scores unusually well because the mechanism is a physical assemblage from a documented ancient wreck, studied in a national collection with modern imaging. An antiquities-market object with a dramatic story starts far lower.

**2. Is the function read from the object or projected onto it?**

Antikythera’s astronomical function is supported by gears, dials, ratios and inscriptions. The Baghdad Battery’s electrical interpretation requires a more speculative functional reconstruction. Resemblance alone is weak.

**3. What supporting knowledge is independently attested?**

Antikythera sits beside Hellenistic astronomy, Babylonian cycles, Greek mechanics and advanced bronze work. A claimed ancient laser would require optical, material and energy knowledge for which the independent support is radically poorer.

**4. What production ecology is required?**

A few bespoke calculators need a small specialist workshop. A fleet of machines needs material supply, standardization, repair and training. A civilization-scale technology needs infrastructure. The larger the implied ecology, the harder it is for preservation bias to erase.

**5. What durable externalities should exist?**

Mining, slag, pollution, foundations, roads, standardized waste, repeated artifacts, inscriptions, trade patterns. Technologies modify their environments. The modification may be subtle at small scale and unavoidable at large scale.

**6. Does independent evidence converge or merely repeat?**

Multiple papers analyzing the same ambiguous photograph are not independent. A physical artifact, a secure inscription, a distinct excavation and a textual tradition can be.

**7. Does scrutiny constrain the claim?**

Good anomalies become narrower under investigation. Tooth counts replace vague complexity. dates tighten. possible functions are eliminated. Bad anomalies often become more protean: every failed prediction produces a new hidden capability.

These questions are not a scoring system in the numerical sense, but they produce an evidentiary profile.

Antikythera performs strongly because it does not require the most spectacular story to remain astonishing.

That is a hallmark worth noticing.

The machine does not need Atlantis. It does not need electricity. It does not need extraterrestrial tutors. It does not need a suppressed global civilization. Remove every speculative layer and thirty bronze gears still sit inside a Hellenistic wreck implementing serious astronomy.

Weak anomalies often have the opposite structure. Remove the disputed provenance or modern resemblance and nothing interesting remains.

The scale also helps with allegations that are partly right.

Suppose an object is genuinely ancient but its claimed function is wrong. The authenticity score can be high while the technological interpretation is low. Suppose an ancient text accurately describes a device that has not survived. The historical-concept score can be high while archaeological confirmation remains incomplete. Suppose a modern replica works. Physical possibility rises without historical deployment rising equally.

Keeping dimensions separate prevents evidentiary laundering.

This is why the four evidence classes in this book are not a hierarchy of respectability but a bookkeeping system.

Class I: documented/direct evidence.

Class II: credible inference or incomplete evidence.

Class III: disputed claims lacking independent confirmation.

Class IV: speculative synthesis.

A strong book can contain all four. The sin is not speculation. The sin is promotion without notice.

The missing workshop itself remains partly Class IV.

We know a workshop or equivalent production setting necessarily existed in the trivial sense that the mechanism was made somewhere by someone. The more specific model—a lineage of specialist astronomical instrument makers, perhaps producing multiple bespoke geared devices—is an inference with varying components in Class II and IV. We do not possess the sign over the door.

That humility does not weaken the thesis.

It tells us exactly what discovery would strengthen it.

Find another geared Hellenistic astronomical fragment in secure context. Find workshop debris. Find a technical inscription specifying gear production. Find a simpler predecessor. Identify repeated manufacturing conventions across independent objects.

Conversely, exhaustive failure to find any relatives while our survey sensitivity improves should move us toward a rarer, perhaps one-off production model.

This is how mystery becomes science.

Not by eliminating wonder.

By giving wonder error bars.
