# Chapter 2 — What Rot Removes

Imagine the ancient machine not as bronze but as a list of materials.

Bronze gears. Bronze plates. Bronze axles. Wooden case. Wooden bench. Iron or steel cutting tools. Abrasives. Oil. Charcoal. Leather bellows. Cord. Wax. Ink. Papyrus. Templates. Possibly gauges. Hands.

Now give the list two thousand years.

The bronze may corrode, but if it reaches an anoxic wreck or protected deposit some of it can remain. The iron usually fares worse. The wood disappears unless waterlogging, charring, aridity, or mineral replacement intervenes. The papyrus requires exceptional conditions. The oil is gone. The leather is gone. The bench is gone. The measuring scratches on the bench are gone. The apprentice is very gone.

Archaeology is not a representative sample of the past. It is a filter.

This sounds obvious until you notice how often historical narratives ignore it. A technological tradition is judged by the objects available for display. The durable part of a system becomes the system. Monumental stone architecture is therefore overrepresented relative to temporary structures; ceramics over food; coins over contracts; weapons over maintenance; inscriptions over spoken instruction; finished goods over jigs.

The missing-workshop problem starts here.

At Barbegal in southern France, a second-century CE complex used sixteen waterwheels arranged in two parallel cascades. The wooden machinery disappeared. For a long time, the ruins were a skeleton with the most informative anatomy missing. Then calcium carbonate deposits that had accumulated on the wet wooden components became molds of vanished engineering. Researchers used the deposits, basin geometry, hydraulics, and stable isotopes to reconstruct unusual elbow-shaped flumes and operating histories. The 2020 *Scientific Reports* paper is worth reading precisely because the evidence is so strange: rock deposited on wood became a ghost of the wood after the wood vanished (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-74900-5). A 2024 *Geoarchaeology* study went further, extracting an eight-year operational history from the carbonate stratigraphy, including wheel replacement and changes to flumes (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/gea.22016).

Barbegal is not Antikythera. It is more useful than that. It demonstrates the general point independently.

If the carbonate had not formed, much of the machinery would be absent. If the masonry also disappeared, the industrial complex might be inferred weakly or not at all. The technological fact would still have been true in the second century. Our knowledge of the fact would merely be poorer.

This distinction between past reality and present recoverability should sit in the front of every argument about anomalous technology.

An artifact can be rare in the archaeological record for at least four different reasons. It may have been rare in antiquity. It may have been made from materials unlikely to survive. It may have been systematically recycled. Or archaeologists may not yet have recognized the relevant traces.

Those mechanisms have different signatures.

A genuinely rare prestige instrument may survive as one object amid texts indicating a specialized craft. A perishable technology may survive through impressions, mineral accretions, postholes, wear, or written descriptions. Recycled metal may survive statistically through alloy histories, scrap cargoes, casting debris, or textual economic evidence. Misrecognized technology often changes category when imaging or experimental work reveals function.

The lazy version of preservation bias says, “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.” That sentence is too blunt to be useful. Sometimes absence is excellent evidence. If a civilization allegedly ran continent-scale railways, the missing rails would be only one problem; cuts, embankments, mines, slag, settlements, standardized parts, transport corridors, and environmental signatures would remain. If somebody alleges an industrial electrical civilization in the Bronze Age, the failure to find generators is less important than the failure to find the enormous supporting ecology.

The right question is not whether evidence is absent. It is whether the alleged system should have produced durable independent traces.

That is the first workshop test.

Antikythera passes a modest version of it. We have the machine itself. We have ancient textual traditions describing sophisticated mechanics. We have surviving and excavated hydraulic machinery. We have evidence for lathes and bronze-working. We have mathematical astronomy capable of supplying the cycles. We have later authors such as Hero and Pappus describing geared and automated devices. The surrounding ecology is incomplete, but it is not zero.

Atlantis does not get to borrow that evidence.

Neither does every “out-of-place artifact.” The fact that one extraordinary Hellenistic machine is genuine tells us the ancient world could surprise us. It does not make every surprising claim equally likely.

Preservation bias should make us more discriminating, not less.

I think of it as a forensic budget. Every lost component consumes some probability. Losing a wooden case is cheap. Losing a workshop bench is cheap. Losing all papyrus technical manuals in a damp region is plausible. Losing an entire mining industry is expensive. Losing a continent-scale power grid is absurdly expensive. Losing a specialized court workshop that produced a handful of bronze astronomical instruments may be quite affordable.

The argument becomes quantitative in spirit even when exact numbers are impossible.

What fraction of a proposed system is made from durable material? How geographically distributed was it? How many people interacted with it? What waste did it produce? What infrastructure did it require? What texts would it likely generate? How economically valuable were its materials for recycling? Would its traces be recognizable to present archaeology?

This is more interesting than asking whether the ancients were “advanced.” Advanced is a comparison word without a denominator.

Barbegal was advanced at moving water through a constrained cascade of mills. Antikythera was advanced at packing astronomical cycles into bronze gearing. Neither implies steam locomotives. Both imply workshops with more competence than a museum case can contain.

The missing workshop is not an excuse to fantasize.

It is an instruction to reconstruct the parts that rot.
