# Chapter 7 — Barbegal, or the Factory That Became Stone

If Antikythera is a machine that survived without its workshop, Barbegal is almost the opposite: a workshop-scale machine system whose working parts disappeared.

South of Arles, Roman engineers built a cascade of sixteen watermills in two parallel rows of eight. It was not a mill with extra wheels. It was a coordinated hydraulic installation that concentrated mechanical power at a scale large enough for modern researchers to call it one of antiquity’s first industrial complexes.

Then the wood vanished.

This is the sort of sentence that should permanently change how we interpret ancient technological absence.

The walls and basins remained sufficiently to identify a mill complex, but the moving machinery—the wheels, flumes and wooden fittings that actually converted falling water into useful work—did not survive as wood. Their ghost survived in calcium carbonate. Mineral-rich water deposited layers on the wet machinery during operation. After the timbers decayed, portions of those carbonate crusts remained, preserving shapes, surface relationships and time series.

The 2018 *Science Advances* work used stable isotope patterns in these deposits to infer operational interruptions and seasonal use (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aar3620). The 2020 *Scientific Reports* study reconstructed unusual elbow-shaped flumes and the hydraulics of the lower mills (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-74900-5). A 2024 *Geoarchaeology* analysis extracted evidence of changes over the final years of operation, including replacement of wheels and adjustment of flumes (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/gea.22016).

The wood disappeared. Maintenance did not.

That is extraordinary.

It also reveals why archaeology improves when it stops demanding the original object as the only admissible witness. The machine left a secondary record through interaction with its environment. Mineral deposits became time-resolved evidence of engineering decisions.

Antikythera’s missing workshop may have left analogous indirect records that we have not learned to recognize.

Not carbonate, necessarily. Wear surfaces. Metal chemistry. tool marks. repeated hole patterns. failed castings. nonfunctional gear blanks. inscriptions. repair pieces. residues on abrasive stones. workshop spatial organization. The point is not to predict the exact proxy. The point is that vanished systems can imprint other materials.

Barbegal also exposes a common mistake in technological history: assuming that high sophistication must look like our sophistication.

The mill complex was adapted to a particular slope, water supply and sequence of basins. Its elbow-shaped flumes, as reconstructed by Passchier and colleagues, do not appear to have become a universal canonical design. They were a local solution. The engineering achievement is therefore easy to underestimate if we search only for inventions with long descendant trees.

A technology can be brilliant and local.

That is exactly the possibility Antikythera forces us to entertain.

Modern people are trained by industrial standardization to expect successful machines to multiply. If a design works, factories copy it. Patents spread it. Competitors imitate it. Textbooks formalize it. Supply chains create standardized components. This expectation gets projected backward into societies with different capital structures, labor economics, patronage, transport, literacy and markets.

But replication is not automatic.

A device can be too expensive, too specialized, too tied to one patron, too difficult to maintain, too dependent on one skilled lineage, too politically vulnerable, or simply not useful enough to produce many copies. A mill that pays for itself in flour behaves differently from an astronomical calculator that may serve education, prestige, calendrical display, astrology, or elite curiosity.

Barbegal also reminds us that scale leaves traces even when machinery does not. Sixteen wheels require water management, masonry, channels and a landscape intervention. The architecture survives because the system was large.

This gives us a way to discipline the missing-workshop model.

If Antikythera mechanisms had been manufactured at industrial scale, we should expect industrial traces. If they were made in a handful of specialist shops, the expected footprint shrinks dramatically. The artifact’s sophistication does not tell us the number produced.

That parameter has to be inferred separately.

The comparison also exposes why “lost technology” is usually the wrong phrase. Barbegal’s water-power principles were not metaphysically lost. Watermills existed elsewhere and later. What disappeared was a particular installation, configuration, operating history and local engineering solution. Likewise, the knowledge embodied in Antikythera may have fragmented rather than vanished all at once. Some gearing concepts persisted in texts. Some astronomical cycles persisted. Some craft practices persisted. What may have disappeared was the exact social arrangement that fused them into this object.

Technologies die by decomposition.

The mathematician survives but the instrument maker does not. The astronomical text survives but the workshop template does not. Bronze work survives but the market for expensive calculators disappears. A later culture inherits parts without inheriting the assembled competence.

This is less romantic than a lost civilization and more realistic.

It is also, to me, stranger.

Civilizations can forget how to combine things they still know separately.

Barbegal became stone and carbonate.

Antikythera became bronze and argument.

Both are records of systems larger than what survived.
