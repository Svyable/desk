# The Last Customer

The first customer proves a product can exist.

The last customer proves whether the company ever understood the cost of that existence.

Growth has a familiar rhythm. A product launches. Customers arrive slowly, then quickly if the product works. Support expands. Features accumulate. The company celebrates adoption because every new user spreads fixed costs across a larger base.

Then the curve bends.

A successor appears.

Demand moves.

The product stops attracting new customers.

Existing customers leave at different speeds.

The economics reverse.

Fixed costs remain while the customer base shrinks.

The first thousand users can make a product look efficient because many of the same systems serve them all.

The final thousand can make the same product look absurdly expensive because almost every shared cost is now carried by a smaller group.

This is the tail problem.

The tail is where expiration becomes a distribution question.

The company wants to leave because the product no longer earns its operating burden.

The remaining customers want the company to stay because the product still earns theirs.

Both positions can be rational.

This is why retirement becomes more difficult as the customer base shrinks.

The users who remain are rarely a random sample.

They are often the users with the highest switching costs.

The ones whose workflows are deeply customized.

The ones running older hardware.

The ones in regulated environments.

The ones with fewer technical resources.

The ones who built businesses around old behavior.

The ones for whom the new product removed a feature everyone else considered minor.

Easy customers leave first.

Hard customers become the tail.

The company sees declining revenue.

The tail sees dependence.

This dynamic appears across technology because standardization creates leverage while customization creates stickiness. A large customer can spend years adapting a platform to local processes. A hospital can configure systems around clinical workflows. A manufacturer can connect old software to equipment expected to last decades. A small business can build its entire accounting process around one version of a product.

The vendor may see legacy behavior.

The customer sees infrastructure.

That difference is not solved by declaring one side correct.

It has to be priced and transitioned.

The easiest commercial response is extended support.

Charge the tail more.

This can be fair. If supporting a shrinking customer base requires specialized engineers, old environments, separate security maintenance, and continued compliance work, the cost should not necessarily be subsidized by customers using the new product.

A higher support price turns hidden burden into a visible choice.

Some customers migrate.

Some pay.

The vendor learns which demand is real.

The model works best when customers have agency and the vendor is not using the threat of abandonment to extract monopoly rents from a captive base.

Those two conditions can be difficult to distinguish from outside.

A vendor can plausibly say the old system costs more to maintain.

A customer can plausibly say the vendor deliberately made migration painful to create pricing power.

Both can be true.

This is why exit quality needs to be judged earlier than shutdown.

By the time a product reaches the tail, the structure of dependence was created years ago.

Were data portable?

Were APIs stable and documented?

Could third parties support the product?

Were customers encouraged to build customizations in ways that remained transferable?

Did the vendor warn that certain components had shorter lives?

Was the product sold as durable while depending on services the company never committed to sustain?

The last customer is the bill for the original architecture.

Public services reveal the ethical limit of simple tail pricing.

A commercial streaming service can discontinue an old application if supporting it becomes irrational.

A government service cannot always abandon the last rural community because usage is low.

A hospital cannot decide a rare disease is not worth treating simply because the patient population is small.

A telecommunications provider may operate under obligations that recognize service availability as more than a private revenue calculation.

The last user can have rights.

That matters because efficiency metrics naturally punish tails.

Cost per user rises.

Utilization falls.

The system looks wasteful.

Sometimes the apparent inefficiency is the price of universality.

Public systems often exist precisely so the last person still has access.

This is why the expiration economy cannot be reduced to optimization.

The objective matters.

A company serving optional consumer entertainment has different duties from a city operating drinking water. A social platform has different duties from an emergency communications system. A hobby software package has different lifecycle expectations from a medical device.

The end state must fit the institution's purpose.

The tail in public infrastructure can last decades.

A road remains important to a small population because there is no substitute. A postal route serves few addresses. A public records system must preserve access to old filings even after most activity moves elsewhere. An old benefit program may retain recipients whose rights were established under earlier rules.

The modernizer's frustration is understandable.

Why must the new system carry every old edge case?

Because sometimes the edge case is a person who was promised continuity.

That promise belongs in the lifecycle cost.

The economic mistake is pretending universal service can be purchased at mass-market unit economics forever.

Tails need explicit funding.

If society wants the final user served, the cost should be recognized as a public choice rather than hidden inside a system expected to look efficient by private-market measures.

This distinction can improve policy.

Instead of forcing an entire old platform to remain alive for a small population, governments can fund migration assistance, specialized support, adapters, alternative delivery channels, or targeted services for the remaining users.

Preserve the obligation without preserving the whole architecture.

That is one of the book's recurring moves.

Keep the right.

Retire the implementation.

The same idea matters in enterprise technology.

A company can spend millions keeping an old system alive for one internal department because nobody separated the department's underlying need from the specific legacy interface.

Ask what function must survive.

Maybe the department needs access to historical records.

It does not need the original application server.

Maybe a customer needs one regulatory report.

It does not need the entire billing system.

Maybe a factory needs communication with one machine.

It does not need every deprecated protocol exposed across the corporate network.

The last customer problem becomes easier when the required function can be isolated.

Contain the tail.

This is what compatibility gateways, archival viewers, data exports, emulation, extended-support environments, and specialized migration tools do.

They reduce the surface area of the old world while preserving a narrow bridge for the people who still need it.

Containment is not free. It creates another system that must eventually be retired.

But the new system can be intentionally smaller.

A legacy environment serving ten special cases is different from keeping the entire primary platform constrained by those cases.

The tail becomes visible and bounded.

That can make its economics honest.

Artificial intelligence may make tail support dramatically cheaper.

An AI coding agent can help maintain old code.

A support agent can answer questions about obsolete products from archived documentation.

Translation systems can bridge old data formats.

Models can reconstruct dependencies and generate migration scripts.

This is good news for customers who previously faced forced abandonment because support was too expensive.

It can also make the tail longer.

If AI lowers support costs enough, vendors may keep old products alive indefinitely.

Customers have less incentive to migrate.

The organization carries more versions.

The tail becomes economically tolerable and architecturally permanent.

This is another rebound effect.

Cheaper maintenance can delay expiration.

There is no contradiction. Lower support cost is valuable. The question is whether the lower cost is used to make transitions more humane or simply to postpone them forever.

The answer may depend on risk more than money.

Old software can contain security weaknesses that maintenance cannot cheaply erase without breaking compatibility. Old hardware can rely on unavailable components. Old standards can constrain new safety requirements. An old model can behave in ways the provider no longer considers acceptable.

At some point support cost is not the main issue.

The system no longer belongs in the active environment.

This is where vendors need the courage to end.

The customer relationship makes that courage uncomfortable because product culture is trained to please users. Retiring a beloved feature guarantees complaints. Shutting an old product can produce outrage disproportionate to its current revenue because the remaining users are the most invested.

The intensity of opposition can be evidence that the product mattered.

It is not necessarily evidence that it should remain forever.

A company can honor users by treating the ending seriously rather than pretending no one will care.

Notice should be long enough for the stakes.

Migration tools should work.

Data should move where feasible.

Critical use cases should be identified.

Paid extensions may be appropriate.

Independent support can be enabled where possible.

The timeline should be credible.

The company should explain not only what ends but what obligation it believes it has fulfilled before ending it.

That last point matters.

A product retirement is a moral claim disguised as an operational decision.

The company is saying: we have done enough for this relationship to conclude.

Customers can disagree.

Sometimes they will be right.

That is why product promises need clearer lifetimes at sale.

If a device requires cloud support, what support horizon is reasonable to expect?

If a model API becomes core infrastructure, what transition commitment accompanies deprecation?

If an enterprise platform is sold on a ten-year transformation story, how much portability should exist if the relationship ends in year three?

Contracts handle some of this.

Market norms handle more.

The expiration economy will make lifecycle promises a competitive dimension.

Companies will differentiate not only on uptime but on shutdown quality.

Not only on onboarding but on offboarding.

Not only on data ingestion but on export.

Not only on compatibility but on deprecation.

Not only on customer success but on customer departure.

The concept sounds anti-growth until you notice what credible exit does to adoption.

A buyer is more willing to enter a relationship that does not threaten captivity.

This is one reason money-back guarantees, trial periods, portability, and cancellation rights can increase conversion. Reducing exit fear makes entry easier.

The same can be true for infrastructure.

A company may move more workloads to a platform if it trusts that future migration remains possible. A user may let an AI assistant remember more if the user knows memory can be inspected and moved. A government may adopt advanced systems faster if contracts preserve operational continuity after a vendor exit.

The last customer is not merely a cost center.

The last customer is a signal to the first customer.

People watch how institutions treat those who are hard to serve.

A company that abandons the tail carelessly teaches new customers what their own future may look like.

A company that can end responsibly demonstrates confidence in the relationship rather than dependence on lock-in.

This does not require infinite generosity.

Every product eventually reaches a point where continuation is irrational or unsafe.

The test is whether the customer can see the path coming.

The best endings begin before the tail exists.

They begin in the architecture, the contract, the data model, the support promise, and the expectation that one day the product will have only one customer left.

The company should already know what it owes them.

Telecommunications offers a useful version of the problem because networks do not retire one customer at a time. A carrier can want to move from an old technology to a newer one while a small number of customers still depend on devices, alarm systems, accessibility equipment, business lines, or local infrastructure built around the older service. The new network can be cheaper, faster, and easier to maintain. None of those facts guarantees that the last dependent user is ready.

Regulators have treated some network changes as more than private product decisions because communications systems can carry public obligations. Notice, continuity, replacement services, emergency access, and consumer protection become part of the transition. The exact regulatory requirements vary by service and jurisdiction, but the structural lesson is portable.

A private modernization can create a public tail.

This is especially true when the product became infrastructure slowly. Nobody signed a ceremony declaring that a particular line, interface, or device would become essential. People simply built around it. Alarm companies chose it. elevators used it. Rural businesses relied on it. Municipal systems connected to it. The dependency emerged through ordinary adoption.

Then retirement reveals the infrastructure status that growth concealed.

AI services can reach the same condition much faster.

A model API begins as a developer product. Within a few years it can sit behind customer support, coding workflows, document review, scheduling, analytics, procurement, accessibility tools, and small businesses whose owners never think of themselves as AI infrastructure operators.

The provider sees an old model generation.

The users see payroll, customers, and Monday morning.

That mismatch creates a future version of the last-mile problem.

Model providers will need more than a replacement endpoint. They will need migration evidence, behavioral comparison, export paths for persistent state, and a way to identify uses where abrupt retirement would create disproportionate harm.

The harder question is what happens when the provider itself disappears.

A product end-of-life program assumes there is still a company to run it.

Bankruptcy can remove that assumption.

Startups fail. Acquirers shut products. A vendor can lose financing before it has money for a graceful sunset. The last customers then discover whether lifecycle promises were backed by anything more durable than management intention.

This is where reserves, escrow arrangements, source availability, data portability, contractual transition rights, and independent service ecosystems can matter. Not every product justifies elaborate protection. A weekend photo filter does not need a decommissioning trust. A system embedded in hospitals, governments, industrial equipment, or critical business records may deserve stronger continuity planning.

Risk should determine the tail institution.

This is common sense in physical industries. Some operations are required to plan or financially assure closure because the public should not inherit the entire cleanup cost if the operator fails. Digital products rarely think in those terms because their tails used to look lighter.

The tails are getting heavier.

A cloud platform can hold years of customer records. An AI provider can mediate institutional knowledge. A home-automation company can determine whether installed devices keep working. A specialized SaaS company can become the only practical interface to a regulated archive.

If the company fails, the product's ending can become a bankruptcy problem, a records problem, a security problem, and a consumer-protection problem at once.

The last customer exposes whether the business model reserved anything for its own funeral.

There is a temptation to solve this through mandatory permanence. Require vendors to support products for long minimum periods. Require every service to escrow code. Require every device to function offline. Require universal portability.

Some rules will be appropriate in some sectors.

Universal rules can create their own costs.

Mandatory support can make niche products uneconomic. Source escrow may not recreate a functioning service whose value depends on people, infrastructure, data, and third-party licenses. Offline operation can conflict with legitimate security or service requirements. Portability can be technically possible while still failing to reproduce the surrounding ecosystem.

The aim is not to legislate immortality.

It is to align tail protection with consequence.

A medical implant and a novelty lamp should not inherit the same retirement ceremony merely because both contain software.

A public-benefits platform and a casual game should not have the same continuity obligations merely because both use cloud services.

The more severe the dependency, the stronger the case that the ending was part of the product from the beginning.

Customers also have responsibilities in the tail.

Large enterprises can create their own captivity by refusing upgrades for years, preserving customizations no longer worth their cost, or treating every historical behavior as contractual. A vendor can provide reasonable migration paths and still face a customer that will not move because internal politics make change harder than paying another year of support.

The last customer is not always a victim.

Sometimes the tail survives because the remaining user has externalized the cost of indecision onto the vendor and the rest of the customer base.

A fair retirement process has to distinguish hardship from preference.

That is difficult, but markets already contain tools for it. Extended support can carry a real price. Migration assistance can be subsidized for users with genuine barriers. New dependence on the old system can be prohibited while existing dependence winds down. Security exceptions can be isolated. Contract renewals can shorten as the end approaches.

The tail can become progressively narrower rather than simply flipping from supported to abandoned.

This is a better mental model than a cliff.

Endings can taper.

Sales stop.

New integrations stop.

Feature work stops.

Standard support stops.

Specialized support continues.

Archival access remains.

The live environment shrinks.

Eventually the last operational dependency leaves.

The product can then become history without requiring history to vanish.

The cost curve matters because a well-designed taper can move cost with dependence. The primary engineering organization stops carrying the whole tail. A smaller team, partner, community, archive, emulator, or replacement interface serves what remains.

The function survives in a smaller container.

That is often the difference between responsible persistence and architectural captivity.

There is one final reason the last customer belongs near the end of the book.

Every argument for expiration becomes easy when nobody cares.

Delete the unused file.

Turn off the empty server.

Dissolve the committee no one attends.

Retire the product with zero users.

Those are housekeeping.

The real test arrives when one person still cares enough to object.

Then the institution has to explain why the ending is justified, what obligation remains, who pays for transition, and which value is being preserved when the old implementation disappears.

That is where ending becomes governance rather than cleanup.

The last customer makes the invisible stakeholder visible.

If the book's thesis cannot survive that customer, it does not deserve to govern the first one.
