# The Expiration Economy

*Why the Future Belongs to Things That Know How to End*

Sven Hardy Benson

© 2026 Sven Hardy Benson. All Rights Reserved.

There is a small category of work that becomes visible only after everyone has lost interest.

The launch has happened. The ribbon has been cut. The account has been opened. The model has shipped. The regulation has been announced. The building has generated its last useful day. The software has been replaced. The committee has fulfilled the purpose written into its charter. The customer has moved on. The people who were rewarded for bringing the thing into existence are already discussing what comes next.

Someone still has to turn it off.

This book is about that person, that moment, and the economic system we have built around avoiding both.

Modern civilization has astonishing machinery for beginnings. Capital markets finance them. Politics celebrates them. Careers are made by them. News organizations cover them. Product teams organize around them. Software tools make them cheaper. Generative artificial intelligence accelerates them further by reducing the cost of producing a plausible first version of almost anything made from symbols: a document, a program, a presentation, a contract, a design, a plan, a simulation, an advertisement, an agent, a workflow, a policy memo, a research hypothesis, or another automated process that will itself begin producing more things.

The machinery for endings is less glamorous. It has names such as migration, decommissioning, repeal, disposition, offboarding, deprecation, archival, discharge, remediation, demolition, withdrawal, revocation, retirement, deletion, and forgetting. These words rarely appear in a founder's keynote. They appear in operating manuals, regulatory filings, maintenance budgets, court orders, records schedules, security advisories, and the meeting nobody volunteered to attend.

That asymmetry matters even when creation is expensive. It matters much more when creation becomes cheap.

The central argument of this book is that abundance creates a disposal problem long before most people recognize one. Every new artifact carries a possible future obligation. A file may have to be secured. A service may need patching. A law may remain enforceable after its political purpose has faded. A database may contain personal information somebody later has a legal right to erase. A bridge can no longer simply be ignored when it becomes unsafe. A machine-learning model can drift, become vulnerable, acquire dependencies, or remain embedded in downstream systems after the team that built it has reorganized. An account can keep privileges nobody remembers granting. An agent can retain credentials, scheduled jobs, memory, unfinished commitments, and access to other agents.

The cost is not always large. Most things die quietly. A draft is deleted. A subscription is cancelled. A process stops because nobody uses it. A plant decays. A market contract settles. An old protocol loses enough support that the remaining users migrate.

But systems do not reliably die just because their usefulness does.

That gap is the subject here.

This is not an argument for disposability. Cheaply made things can produce enormous waste, and a society that treats people, places, tools, or institutions as instantly replaceable can become cruel as well as inefficient. Durable design still matters. Maintenance still matters. Memory still matters. Archives matter. Continuity matters. Some institutions deserve to outlive their founders, some buildings deserve restoration rather than demolition, some records should remain accessible for generations, and some promises should be difficult to escape.

The question is not whether things should last.

The question is whether duration is chosen.

A civilization that cannot preserve what matters is forgetful. A civilization that cannot end what no longer matters is congested.

Biology solved part of this problem before economics had a name for it. Multicellular organisms depend on regulated cell death as well as cell division. Development and tissue homeostasis require not only the production of cells but their timely elimination. Too much cell death can be catastrophic; too little can be catastrophic too. Forests are shaped by cycles of growth, disturbance, decay, and renewal. Human memory is not a perfect recording device, and research increasingly treats forms of forgetting as adaptive rather than merely defective. Bankruptcy law gives some debtors a legally structured path out of obligations that would otherwise impair future economic activity. Financial contracts often contain maturity dates. Legislatures sometimes write sunset provisions because a rule intended for one condition should not always survive by default into another.

These are not identical mechanisms. The book does not pretend they are.

They rhyme around a design problem: healthy systems need an exit path.

Technology has made that problem easier to ignore because digital objects often look weightless. A file copied to a server does not pile up in the hallway. An abandoned cloud service does not rust visibly beside the road. A deprecated API can continue answering requests for years. A forgotten user account can sit untouched until the day it is useful to an attacker. A model checkpoint can be duplicated across machines without anybody feeling its mass. A policy can survive inside a compliance manual after the people who wrote it have left.

The physical world eventually announces neglect. Roofs leak. Steel corrodes. Batteries fail. Vegetation grows through pavement. The digital world is better at impersonating continuity.

Artificial intelligence intensifies the illusion. If software can be generated quickly, then a team can create ten internal tools where it once created one. If agents can operate continuously, then processes no longer need a person to remember to start them each morning. If every department can produce custom automations, reports, datasets, models, prompts, assistants, and integrations, then the stock of active machinery grows faster than the organization chart responsible for it.

The natural response is to celebrate productivity. Much of that celebration will be justified.

Then comes the second ledger.

Who knows what exists?

Who knows what depends on it?

Who can turn it off?

Who is allowed to delete the data?

Who must preserve the data instead?

Who tells the users?

Who pays to migrate the last customer?

What happens to the credentials?

What happens to the promises the system made while it was alive?

What remains for an auditor, a historian, a court, or a successor to inspect after the running thing is gone?

And what happens when nobody owns those questions?

The chapters ahead move through several worlds because the same imbalance appears in surprising places. Software shows what happens when compatibility turns yesterday's decision into today's obligation. Privacy law shows that accumulation and erasure can become competing duties. Nuclear decommissioning shows the difference between shutting something down and making its end safe. Bankruptcy shows that societies sometimes protect the future by refusing to make every past obligation permanent. Neuroscience complicates the cultural fantasy that perfect memory is obviously superior. Internet standards show that deprecation can reduce attack surface and maintenance burden. Artificial intelligence brings all of these questions together because it can create systems that are cheap to start, difficult to inventory, dependent on one another, persistent in operation, and socially entangled with users.

The book ends with a practical claim, but not a checklist pretending to solve every domain.

We need to design expiration as deliberately as we design creation.

That can mean a literal date. More often it means an owner, a review trigger, a dependency map, a migration path, a records rule, a revocation mechanism, a budget for cleanup, a preserved trace, and a clear statement of what would justify keeping the thing alive.

The burden of proof in modern organizations usually falls on ending. A project continues unless somebody makes the case to stop it. A record remains unless somebody is authorized to delete it. A service survives unless a team accepts the migration risk. A rule persists unless lawmakers spend scarce time repealing it. An account stays open unless a process catches it. A model remains in production unless somebody is willing to own the consequences of replacement.

In an age of expensive creation, that bias often felt conservative.

In an age of abundant creation, it can become the main source of disorder.

The future will still belong to people who know how to build.

It will increasingly belong to people who know what deserves to remain after the building becomes easy.
