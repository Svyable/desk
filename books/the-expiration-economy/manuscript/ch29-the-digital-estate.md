# The Digital Estate

Death used to leave drawers.

Keys. Letters. Bank statements. Photographs. A deed. A notebook with phone numbers. Maybe a safe-deposit box. Maybe a desk whose order made sense to one person and nobody else.

The modern equivalent is less visible.

It is a phone that locks itself.

A password manager.

A cloud account.

A domain registration.

A subscription renewing against a card that will eventually fail.

A photo archive spread across devices and services.

A business that exists partly in email, partly in a payment processor, partly in a marketplace account, partly in a private repository, and partly in the head of the person who built it.

It is a model that knows years of personal history.

An assistant that can draft in someone’s style.

An automation that keeps paying, posting, scheduling, answering, ordering, renewing, backing up, reminding, routing, and filing after the person who created it can no longer supervise it.

The estate used to be mostly property.

It is becoming behavior.

That difference makes death one of the hardest edge cases in the expiration economy.

Property can usually be described as something that is owned, transferred, sold, divided, donated, destroyed, or inherited. Digital life does not fit neatly into that grammar.

An account can contain property, identity, private communication, licensed content, contractual rights, memories belonging partly to other people, business obligations, and access to systems that can still act.

The same login may open five different legal and moral categories at once.

That is why “give the executor the password” is not a serious digital-estate strategy.

Access is not authority.

Possession of a credential does not answer whether the person using it is entitled to read the messages, impersonate the deceased, continue the business, delete the archive, transfer the asset, cancel the subscription, or speak to customers.

The distinction is uncomfortable because families often meet it during grief, when the appetite for governance is lowest.

Somebody dies and the survivors want simple answers.

Where are the photographs?

What bills are still running?

Can we get into the laptop?

Who owns the website?

What happens to the small online business?

Can the social account be memorialized?

Should the messages be preserved?

What did this person want us to delete?

Did they owe anyone work?

Are there recurring donations?

Is an automated investment or purchasing process still active?

Does the AI assistant contain conversations they would never have wanted their family to read?

There is rarely one document that answers all of this.

The digital estate is therefore less like a box of assets and more like a map of continuing relationships.

This is the important shift.

The dead can remain operationally present.

Not because consciousness persists inside a machine, but because instructions persist.

A scheduled email can arrive after death.

A subscription can renew.

A storefront can accept an order.

A domain can continue resolving.

An advertising campaign can keep spending.

A newsletter can send automatically.

A server can keep paying for itself from an account with enough balance.

A model can answer a family member using years of retained context.

A social account can continue receiving messages from people who do not know the person is gone.

This is a new kind of posthumous tail.

Human beings have always left obligations behind. Estates settle debts. Businesses continue. Families inherit property. Intellectual work survives its author. Institutions maintain archives.

What is different is that the tail can now act without waiting for a successor.

The old estate was mostly static until someone touched it.

The digital estate can keep moving.

That makes inactivity a decision.

If nobody knows an automated system exists, the absence of intervention does not preserve the status quo. It permits the system to continue.

The same principle applies to incapacity.

A person does not need to die for digital authority to become ambiguous.

A stroke, injury, cognitive decline, disappearance, detention, or temporary loss of consciousness can create the same practical question: who is allowed to stop, continue, or redirect the systems this person set in motion?

The financial world has long dealt with versions of this through powers of attorney, trustees, joint ownership, beneficiary designations, and institutional procedures.

Digital systems are often much less mature.

They may know only the account holder and the credential.

If the credential works, access works.

If the credential does not work, a legally authorized successor may be locked out.

Both outcomes can be wrong.

Too much continuity creates unauthorized action.

Too little continuity destroys legitimate value.

That tension is the heart of the digital estate.

The goal is not maximum preservation.

The goal is selective continuity.

Some things should transfer.

Some should pause.

Some should become read-only.

Some should be preserved for a limited period.

Some should be destroyed.

Some should require the consent of other people.

Some should continue only until a successor can make a decision.

Some should never survive the person at all.

Digital life needs modes of ending, not one button.

Consider personal correspondence.

A person may value privacy while alive and still care deeply about preserving letters, journals, photographs, research, or creative work after death. Those desires are not contradictory. Privacy is not a single setting called “keep” or “delete.” It depends on audience, purpose, time, and context.

A private conversation with a friend may also contain the friend’s private life.

A family archive may preserve one person’s memory by exposing another person’s secrets.

A complete digital record can become less respectful precisely because it is complete.

This is where the culture of total storage collides with mortality.

For most of human history, forgetting was partly automatic. Paper decayed. Possessions were sorted. Houses changed hands. The volume of personal records imposed physical selection. Families kept some things and lost others.

Cheap storage weakens that filter.

A person can leave behind more recorded language than earlier generations could have produced in a lifetime.

Messages, documents, search histories, location histories, photographs, recordings, drafts, model conversations, notes, screenshots, backups, calendars, health data, purchase records, and generated content can remain available with no natural moment of review.

The dead person becomes a data lake.

That may sound crude, but it captures the governance problem.

More memory does not automatically produce better remembrance.

The archive needs boundaries.

This is especially true of personal AI.

A sufficiently used assistant may contain a strange mixture of diary, coworker, search history, confidant, editor, project manager, and externalized memory. It may know what the person was planning, fearing, hiding, buying, writing, researching, regretting, or considering.

Families will want access to some of this.

They may have good reasons.

A surviving spouse may need to reconstruct financial obligations. A collaborator may need project files. A business partner may need information necessary to keep customers whole. A child may want family photographs. An executor may need a list of accounts.

But “the model knows it” is not the same as “the heirs should know it.”

Personal AI will force a sharper distinction between informational utility and informational inheritance.

A person’s assistant may know how to continue a project while also containing confidences that should die with the user.

The system must be able to transfer one without transferring the other.

That is technically and institutionally difficult because AI memory tends to blur categories.

A conventional filing cabinet separates folders. A model can compress information into context, summaries, embeddings, personalization layers, inferred preferences, and generated representations. The survivor may not know which hidden memory produced an answer.

Deletion becomes harder to reason about when the thing to be deleted is not a single file.

This does not mean safe digital estates are impossible.

It means personal AI should be designed with succession in mind before it becomes essential.

The system should know which information is operational and which is intimate.

Which projects can be handed off.

Which accounts are important.

Which memories are temporary.

Which information can be disclosed to a named successor.

Which content requires a higher threshold.

Which automations must stop immediately upon incapacity or death.

Which ones can continue briefly to prevent harm.

Which models or representations must not be used to impersonate the user after death.

These distinctions should not be made entirely by grieving survivors after the fact.

The person should be able to participate while alive.

This is the digital equivalent of writing a will, but the will metaphor is incomplete.

A will disposes of an estate.

A digital continuity plan disposes of authority.

Who may act?

Who may read?

Who may preserve?

Who may destroy?

Who may speak in your name?

Who may train or configure a model to imitate you?

Who may continue a business process you created?

Who may terminate one?

Who may decide when two of these duties conflict?

These are not all property questions.

Some are identity questions.

That becomes obvious with posthumous simulation.

A family may treasure the ability to hear a deceased person’s voice. A writer’s estate may want a digital archive that explains the author’s work. A museum may create an interactive historical exhibit. A company may preserve the founder’s reasoning as an internal archive.

There can be legitimate uses.

But a simulation becomes ethically different when it shifts from representation to agency.

A recording says, this person once said this.

A model says, this is what this person would say now.

The second claim is far stronger.

It creates synthetic continuity where no living continuity exists.

The risk is not only deception.

It is governance by a person who can no longer revise the representation.

A dead person cannot object to a bad answer generated in their style.

Cannot update an outdated political belief.

Cannot correct a false memory.

Cannot tell a grieving relative that the simulation has become unhealthy.

Cannot revoke consent after the use changes.

A posthumous model is therefore unusually dependent on limits set in advance.

The person is not available for the normal social mechanism of correction.

This is another reason expiration must be part of design.

A system that can preserve someone indefinitely should also know when it no longer has authority to present itself as that person.

The dead should be allowed to become historical.

That phrase may sound cold.

It is actually protective.

History preserves without pretending the subject is still participating.

An archive can be rich, intimate, useful, and alive to interpretation while remaining clear about the fact that the person is gone.

The temptation of AI is to erase that boundary because continuity feels comforting and commercially valuable.

A product can promise that the voice remains.

The style remains.

The advice remains.

The companion remains.

But an expiration-aware culture would ask a harder question: should the relationship remain in the same form?

Grief has always involved objects that hold traces of the dead. Photographs. Clothing. Letters. Rooms. Recipes. Recordings. The difference is that these objects do not usually initiate new speech.

Generative systems do.

That places the vendor in a powerful position.

The vendor may control access to a deceased person’s synthetic presence. It may charge for storage. Change the model. Alter policy. Shut down the service. Introduce advertising. Lose data. Be acquired. Fail.

A family that believes it has preserved a loved one may discover that it has actually rented an interface.

This is the last-customer problem inside grief.

Digital estates therefore require portability as well as preservation.

Important records should not depend entirely on the continued operation of one company.

Neither should the instructions governing them.

A digital estate plan that exists only inside the platform it governs is vulnerable to the platform’s own expiration.

The same is true for small businesses.

A growing number of businesses are close to one-person institutions. A consultant, creator, seller, developer, landlord, researcher, investor, or operator can run a surprisingly complex economic life through connected software.

The person may be the brand, salesperson, accountant, customer-service lead, product owner, and administrator all at once, with automation filling the gaps.

This is efficient while the person is alive.

It is fragile at death.

Customers may have prepaid.

Vendors may have invoices outstanding.

Subscriptions may need cancellation.

Domains may need renewal long enough to preserve the business.

Data may need export.

Employees or contractors may need instructions.

Tax records must survive.

Orders may need fulfillment or refund.

Automated messages may need to stop.

A buyer may exist, but only if the assets are actually transferable.

The estate inherits not only value but unfinished service.

This is where the cultural image of inheritance becomes misleading.

We imagine heirs receiving assets.

Increasingly they may receive systems in motion.

An online business can be worth something and still become a burden within days if nobody can operate it.

A popular domain can expire.

A marketplace can freeze funds.

A cloud bill can consume cash.

A customer database can create privacy obligations.

A subscription business can continue charging customers for a service nobody is capable of delivering.

The value of the estate depends on how quickly authority can be transferred without giving away more access than the successor needs.

That is exactly the selective inheritance problem from earlier chapters, now compressed into the most personal transition of all.

The digital estate also reveals the limits of automation as a substitute for preparation.

It is tempting to imagine an AI that simply figures everything out after death.

Find the accounts. Categorize the files. Answer the heirs. Continue the business. Shut down what is unnecessary.

But the central problem is not classification.

It is legitimacy.

The AI cannot infer who should be allowed to read a private journal merely because it can summarize it.

It cannot decide whether a model should keep speaking in the deceased person’s voice merely because the family wants it.

It cannot determine whether destroying a record protects privacy or destroys evidence needed by someone else.

It cannot choose the rightful successor to a disputed business merely from operational convenience.

Automation can reduce the work of transition.

It cannot manufacture authority that was never defined.

This is one of the recurring boundaries of the expiration economy.

The hardest endings are not technically difficult because deletion is impossible.

They are institutionally difficult because different claims survive the person.

Privacy.

Property.

Debt.

Contract.

Memory.

Family.

Business continuity.

Historical value.

Other people’s rights.

The dead do not leave one obligation behind.

They leave a conflict among obligations.

A mature digital estate therefore does not seek one rule.

It prepares states.

If I am temporarily unavailable, preserve and pause these systems.

If I am incapacitated, give this person limited operational authority.

If I die, stop these automations immediately.

Keep these records for the executor.

Transfer these business assets.

Archive these creative works.

Delete these private materials after a stated period.

Do not permit a synthetic version of me to make new decisions.

Allow these photographs to be shared with family.

Keep this correspondence private unless a specified condition is met.

The exact instructions will differ because people differ.

The important part is the existence of transitions.

Digital systems should not treat death as an authentication failure.

Nor should they treat it as permanent continuation.

The person is gone.

Some of the person’s obligations remain.

Some of the person’s work remains.

Some of the person’s memories remain.

Some of the person’s secrets should remain secret.

Some of the person’s systems need to keep working just long enough to stop correctly.

That last sentence may be the simplest definition of a good digital estate.

Not immortality.

Not deletion.

A controlled handoff from a living operator to a world that must continue without them.

Law already recognizes that the categories inside a digital estate are not interchangeable. The Uniform Law Commission’s Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act extends traditional fiduciary management into digital property, but it deliberately treats private electronic communications differently. A fiduciary may be able to manage things such as files, domains, or virtual currency while access to email, texts, or social-media communications can require the original user’s consent.

That distinction is more important than the specific model law. It rejects the idea that becoming an executor automatically turns every digital trace into ordinary inheritable property. The estate may own an asset while lacking a blanket right to every conversation around it.

Major platforms have begun building the same idea into product design.

Apple’s Legacy Contact system allows a user to designate someone who can seek access to certain Apple Account data after death. The process requires an access key and a death certificate. Even then, access is bounded: purchased media and subscriptions are treated differently, and Keychain data such as passwords and passkeys is excluded. The successor does not simply become the deceased user with the same credential and the same authority.

Google’s Inactive Account Manager takes another route. A user can define an inactivity period, nominate contacts, and choose which categories of data those contacts can receive. Different people can receive different data. The trigger is inactivity rather than a universal legal conclusion about death, and the plan can be changed while the user is alive.

These systems are imperfect and platform-specific, but they reveal a useful design pattern. The transition is expressed before the crisis. The user participates in deciding who receives what. Access is scoped. The successor is not assumed to inherit the entire account as an undifferentiated object.

The platforms also reveal the weakness of platform-based succession. A legacy plan is only as durable as the service implementing it. Product rules can change. Data categories can change. Accounts can be deleted under inactivity policies. The company itself can eventually alter or discontinue the mechanism. A person who believes they have solved digital inheritance by checking one box may have created a plan that depends on another institution’s continued existence.

This is why the digital estate needs at least two layers of memory.

The platform can hold implementation details: access keys, selected data categories, nominated contacts, inactivity triggers.

The person’s broader intent should survive outside the platform: what matters, what must stop, what can be shared, what should remain private, what business responsibilities need continuity, and what synthetic uses are forbidden.

The distinction becomes especially important for personal AI because future assistants may collapse several platforms into one relationship. An assistant could know the location of the will, the domain registrar, the customer list, the family archive, the unfinished manuscript, the recurring bills, and the user’s private fears. That makes it powerful during transition. It also makes indiscriminate inheritance dangerous.

A robust design would not ask one question—“Who gets my AI?”—because the AI is not one thing.

It would ask which capabilities survive.

Can the successor retrieve business records but not private reflections?

Can an executor receive an inventory of obligations without receiving the content of intimate conversations?

Can a family preserve photographs while disabling automations that speak publicly in the deceased person’s name?

Can an archive retain the person’s work while refusing to generate new claims as if the author were still alive?

Those distinctions are technically awkward. They are also the point.

If the system cannot separate memory, property, communication, and authority while the user is alive, death will not magically make the categories clearer.

The counterargument is that most people will never configure all of this. Elaborate digital-estate planning can become another form of administrative burden imposed on ordinary life. A system that asks users to pre-classify every future asset, conversation, and automation may simply be ignored.

So the best design cannot depend entirely on perfect foresight. Defaults matter. High-consequence authority should generally contract at death or verified incapacity unless continuation has been deliberately arranged. Records needed to settle obligations should remain available through lawful processes. Private communications should not be treated as automatically inheritable merely because they are technically retrievable. Synthetic impersonation should require a higher bar than archival preservation.

The precise rules will vary by jurisdiction, product, family, and use.

The deeper principle is stable: death changes the authority structure even when the data remains exactly where it was.

A digital estate is mature when the transition of authority is at least as deliberate as the preservation of memory.
