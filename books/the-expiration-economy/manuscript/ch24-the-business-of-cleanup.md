# The Business of Cleanup

Every boom creates a second industry.

The first industry builds.

The second industry deals with what building leaves behind.

Mining creates reclamation.

Oil production creates plugging and remediation.

Electronics create recycling and reverse logistics.

Construction creates demolition, salvage, waste handling, asbestos removal, and brownfield redevelopment.

Finance creates restructuring and bankruptcy administration.

Software creates migration, deprecation, archival, identity cleanup, dependency management, data retention, and security work.

The second industry is less glamorous because it arrives after the story everyone wanted to tell.

It can be just as important.

Artificial intelligence is about to produce an unusually large cleanup economy because it accelerates creation across many categories at once.

More code.

More models.

More agents.

More documents.

More synthetic data.

More internal applications.

More automated decisions.

More personalized workflows.

More temporary companies.

More experiments.

More machine-made infrastructure that somebody eventually has to reconcile with the rest of the world.

The obvious commercial opportunity is maintenance.

The more interesting one is subtraction.

Maintenance keeps systems alive.

Cleanup decides which systems still deserve maintenance.

That decision is economically valuable because modern organizations often pay more to preserve uncertainty than to preserve value.

They keep the old database because nobody knows what depends on it.

They keep the account because nobody knows who owns it.

They keep the vendor because nobody knows whether migration will work.

They keep the data because nobody knows whether deletion is allowed.

They keep the model because nobody has revalidated the downstream workflow.

Unknown becomes immortal.

A cleanup business sells knowledge strong enough to make an ending possible.

This can take the form of inventory.

Find every cloud resource.

Find every service account.

Find every model in production.

Find every stale domain.

Find every database holding a category of personal information.

Find every application still calling a deprecated interface.

Find every agent that can spend money.

Discovery is valuable because organizations cannot retire what they cannot see.

But discovery is not the market's endpoint.

A dashboard full of old things merely turns hidden clutter into visible guilt.

The customer needs confidence to act.

Which things are actually unused?

Which are duplicated?

Which can be consolidated?

Which have legal obligations attached?

Which can be archived instead of operated?

Which should be destroyed?

Which must remain?

What breaks if one disappears?

The winning cleanup products will combine inventory with evidence.

Evidence converts fear into a decision.

Consider cloud cost optimization. The simplest tool identifies resources that appear idle and estimates potential savings. A mature version observes traffic, ownership, dependencies, schedules, business context, and historical usage. It distinguishes the abandoned development server from the disaster-recovery system that should remain quiet most of the time.

Silence is not enough.

The same principle applies to agents.

An agent has not run in sixty days.

That could mean it is obsolete.

It could mean it exists for a quarterly process.

It could be an emergency workflow designed to remain dormant until a rare event.

It could be broken.

A cleanup system needs context before deletion.

This creates an opportunity for AI itself because AI is good at joining weak signals.

Logs say one thing.

Code references say another.

Documentation says another.

Tickets, contracts, calendars, procurement records, and organizational changes add more context.

A model can assemble the case that a system has probably outlived its purpose.

The human or accountable policy can decide whether the case is strong enough.

Cleanup becomes assisted judgment.

This is one reason the expiration economy may produce some of the most valuable enterprise AI applications.

Creation demos beautifully.

Cleanup saves money quietly.

A generated application can impress an executive in a minute.

A system that safely removes two hundred obsolete applications can produce a larger financial effect over years without a dramatic interface.

Enterprise software has always rewarded boring value eventually.

The second market is migration.

Migration is the bridge between an old thing and its ending.

Data have to move.

Users have to move.

Interfaces have to move.

Workflows have to move.

Contracts and permissions sometimes have to move.

Testing has to prove that the new environment preserves what matters.

Historically, migration has been expensive because every system contains undocumented local history.

AI can reduce that cost by reading schemas, code, logs, documentation, support tickets, and usage patterns. It can generate transformation scripts. It can compare outputs. It can identify edge cases. It can help users reconstruct why strange behavior existed.

If migration gets dramatically cheaper, the economic power of lock-in changes.

That would be one of the most important second-order effects of AI.

Most discussion assumes AI strengthens dominant platforms because large companies have data, distribution, and capital.

That may be true.

But AI can also attack switching costs.

If an agent can understand a proprietary workflow well enough to reproduce it elsewhere, the customer gains bargaining power.

If it can translate old data into an open format, migration becomes less frightening.

If it can maintain compatibility during transition, the customer can move incrementally.

The expiration economy can become a competition economy.

This will produce conflict.

Incumbent vendors may make migration technically or contractually difficult. Data exports can remain incomplete. APIs can restrict access. Custom logic can be hidden. Licensing can complicate automated translation. Security restrictions can make broad agent access inappropriate.

The existence of capable migration tools will not abolish lock-in.

It will change the price of enforcing it.

The third market is proof.

Customers, regulators, auditors, insurers, and buyers increasingly need to know that something was actually removed.

The model is no longer in production.

The employee's access is gone.

The customer's data were deleted where required.

The deprecated protocol is no longer reachable.

The old agent cannot transact.

The cloud resource no longer exists.

Negative facts are hard to prove.

Presence is easy to demonstrate. Show the server.

Absence requires confidence that no relevant copy remains.

This is why architecture matters. Centralized identity makes revocation easier to verify. Short-lived credentials make lingering access less likely. Declarative infrastructure makes deployed resources easier to compare with intended resources. Encryption can make key destruction meaningful. Data lineage can make deletion propagation more complete.

The cleanup business will increasingly sell verifiable absence.

That phrase sounds abstract until an acquisition closes.

A buyer does not merely want to know the target owns useful systems.

The buyer may also want to know what dangerous systems remain.

Unsupported software.

Unlicensed data.

Stale credentials.

Unknown models.

Shadow AI agents.

Legacy customer promises.

Unfunded decommissioning obligations.

The liabilities of continuation can alter the value of the deal.

Lifecycle diligence becomes financial diligence.

The fourth market is specialized long-tail support.

The last customer chapter showed why tails can be difficult to eliminate. AI can create a viable middle ground between universal support and abandonment.

A vendor can move legacy customers into a contained support environment maintained with heavy automation. An independent provider can take over an old product after the original company exits. A public agency can fund adapters for a small population without preserving an entire obsolete architecture.

This can extend useful life without forcing the future to carry the old system everywhere.

The support becomes explicit.

The tail gets a price and an owner.

This is healthier than pretending the legacy burden is free.

The fifth market is digital estates.

Individuals will accumulate more automated infrastructure around themselves.

Domains.

Subscriptions.

Cloud storage.

Financial accounts.

Persistent assistants.

Agent memories.

Smart-home systems.

Creator platforms.

Digital assets.

Automated businesses.

Long-running tasks.

A person's death or incapacity can leave these systems active in ways traditional estate planning never had to consider.

The same is true for tiny companies operated substantially by software.

A founder can die while the business's agents continue answering customers and accepting payments.

Who has authority to stop them?

Who inherits credentials?

What records must survive?

Which subscriptions should end?

What happens to customer data?

The legal estate and the operational estate will need to meet.

That creates work for lawyers, identity providers, banks, cloud companies, and new lifecycle services.

The sixth market is public decommissioning.

Governments will adopt AI into benefits administration, permitting, fraud detection, procurement, public safety, education, health, and internal operations. Some systems will fail. Some vendors will fail. Some systems will work and later become obsolete.

Public institutions need exit capability because vendor dependence can become sovereignty dependence.

A government that cannot replace a critical system without losing records or operational continuity has handed part of its future to the vendor.

Procurement should therefore buy an ending along with the service.

Data export.

Documentation.

Transition support.

Model and version records.

Defined decommissioning duties.

Continuity plans.

Portability where feasible.

Public records separation.

The provisions may look boring in the contract.

They determine whether the state can still govern after the vendor leaves.

This market will grow slowly because public procurement changes slowly. Then a few failures will make lifecycle terms standard.

That is how many boring requirements become obvious.

The expiration economy will also create bad businesses.

Companies will manufacture churn and call it lifecycle management.

Vendors will force unnecessary upgrades.

Consultants will recommend deleting systems they do not understand.

Security products will revoke useful access to produce impressive metrics.

AI systems will confidently classify important archives as clutter.

Cleanup will become another optimization fashion.

The antidote is counterevidence.

Every proposed ending should have a credible case for continuation considered too.

What value survives?

What resilience disappears?

Which user is stranded?

Which historical record becomes inaccessible?

Which future option is lost?

The cleanup economy should not be paid simply for reducing counts.

It should be paid for reducing burden without destroying justified capability.

That is harder to measure.

Hard measurements are often where defensible businesses emerge.

A company that can prove a retired system reduced cloud cost, attack surface, support burden, compliance scope, and future migration complexity while preserving the necessary function has created real value.

The output is not deletion.

The output is a smaller obligation set.

That phrase may be the cleanest economic definition of the industry.

The creation economy expands what the organization can do.

The cleanup economy reduces what the organization is forced to keep doing.

Both increase freedom.

One creates options.

The other releases them.

Artificial intelligence will be remembered for making many kinds of production cheaper.

Its quieter effect may be making cleanup finally worth automating at the same scale.

A machine that can read the entire organization can help the organization decide which parts of its own past still deserve to run.

That is not the flashy use case.

It may be one of the biggest.

The market will probably not emerge as one category because customers do not buy theories.

A chief information officer buys application rationalization.

A security team buys identity governance and attack-surface reduction.

A privacy office buys deletion workflows and retention controls.

A procurement team buys portability terms.

A regulator buys auditability.

A finance team buys cost reduction.

A family buys digital-estate help.

An industrial operator buys decommissioning.

The suppliers may never share a conference banner.

Their revenues can still be driven by the same structural pressure: the installed base of things whose continuation requires judgment.

That makes the expiration economy less like a new sector and more like a horizontal cost layer.

Cybersecurity developed similarly. Security is not one industry in the narrow sense. It appears in identity, networks, endpoints, cloud infrastructure, insurance, consulting, software development, compliance, and operations because digital systems create security obligations everywhere.

Expiration can become another cross-cutting property.

Every serious system will eventually need an owner, a review condition, an export path, a retirement state, and evidence of what happened to its remains.

The suppliers that make those properties cheap can sit inside many existing markets.

This matters for investors because the biggest opportunities may not advertise themselves as cleanup.

A company that reduces cloud spend by identifying dead infrastructure is selling ending capacity.

A tool that maps service dependencies before migration is selling ending capacity.

A records platform that can prove disposition is selling ending capacity.

An agent-identity product with automatic revocation and succession is selling ending capacity.

A compatibility layer that lets a bank retire a mainframe subsystem without breaking customers is selling ending capacity.

The label is secondary.

The unit economics are not.

Cleanup businesses have an interesting problem: they can eliminate their own recurring revenue if they succeed too well.

A consultant paid to retire twenty systems can finish the job.

A migration vendor can move the data once.

A decommissioning contractor can close the site.

This is unlike a subscription whose ideal customer stays forever.

The best business models will therefore differ by function.

Some will be project businesses because endings are discrete. Others will be continuous because accumulation never stops. Identity lifecycle, retention enforcement, agent governance, dependency mapping, and asset discovery can be recurring services precisely because new things keep entering the system.

The cleanup company does not need the old mess to persist.

It needs the organization to keep creating enough new things that lifecycle discipline remains necessary.

That is a healthier alignment.

A bad cleanup vendor benefits when migration stays hard.

A good one benefits when the customer can keep moving.

This is another reason standards matter.

If data, identity, model metadata, agent roles, and application dependencies become more portable, some one-time migration services will commoditize. The value will move toward orchestration, verification, high-consequence judgment, and domain-specific transition work.

AI itself will commoditize parts of cleanup fastest where the evidence is already structured.

Unused cloud resources are easier than undocumented public obligations.

Duplicate code is easier than determining whether two welfare programs serve genuinely different populations.

Stale permissions are easier than deciding whether a historical archive should remain searchable.

The labor that remains valuable will sit closer to consequence.

This does not mean humans manually inspect every ending. It means the scarce human attention moves toward cases where values conflict.

Automation can close the obvious account.

People decide whether the rare account should survive.

Automation can identify a dormant model.

People decide whether it must be preserved for evidence.

Automation can propose a migration.

People decide whether the last customer's hardship justifies an exception.

The cleanup economy can therefore become a labor-compression economy rather than a labor-elimination economy. Machines clear routine endings so experts can spend time on disputed ones.

That would be a good outcome because the current system often does the reverse. Experts spend time hunting for inventory while the difficult judgment receives whatever time remains.

There is also a financing opportunity hidden in the tail.

Physical industries sometimes reserve money or provide financial assurance for future closure because the party earning money during operation may not exist when cleanup arrives. Digital systems can develop lighter versions of the same idea.

A vendor serving a critical function could fund transition rights through contract structure.

An enterprise could treat migration reserves as part of the cost of strategic dependence.

An insurer could price weak exit architecture as operational risk.

A lender or buyer could discount a company whose systems have large undocumented retirement liabilities.

A board could ask not only how much technical debt exists but how much funded capacity exists to remove it.

The accounting will not be clean. Most ending obligations are contingent, distributed, and hard to value.

That has never stopped finance from pricing uncertainty badly and then improving.

The important conceptual move is to stop treating future exit cost as zero merely because nobody can calculate it precisely today.

A rough liability estimate can be more honest than a blank cell.

Insurance may be one of the forces that makes this practical. Insurers care about attack surface, unsupported software, stale identities, business continuity, vendor dependence, and recovery capability because those conditions affect losses. If lifecycle discipline reduces claims, premiums and underwriting requirements can make expiration economically visible before a disaster.

The same can happen through acquisitions.

A buyer performing diligence can discover that the target's apparent software estate contains hundreds of unowned applications, proprietary data with unclear rights, persistent agents without accountable sponsors, and customer contracts requiring years of tail support.

The purchase price now contains an ending discount.

That is when cleanup stops being housekeeping and enters valuation.

The strongest counterargument is that AI may make this entire industry less important than the book predicts.

If models become excellent at maintenance, migration, dependency reconstruction, records classification, and automated support, then the cost of keeping old systems could collapse. A company may rationally preserve thousands of versions because agents manage them cheaply. Portability may become so easy that lock-in weakens without dedicated cleanup firms. Storage may remain cheap. Security may become more automated. The tail may cease to be scarce.

That future is possible.

It would falsify the strongest version of the expiration-economy thesis.

The weaker version would still ask what happens to total inventory when carrying cost falls.

If the number of systems grows faster than the cost per system falls, cleanup remains valuable. If AI reduces both creation and retirement friction enough that inventories remain bounded, ending becomes an ordinary platform feature rather than a major scarcity.

That would be success.

The book should be happy to lose its market thesis to better engineering.

A functioning sewage system is economically important even if citizens rarely think about the waste industry as the defining business of the age.

The ideal expiration economy may disappear into infrastructure.

Things end correctly by default.

Credentials lapse.

Data reach disposition.

Agents hand off work.

Products export state.

Models retire with evidence.

Public systems preserve rights without preserving every implementation.

Migration becomes routine.

Decommissioning gets funded before the tail arrives.

If that happens, cleanup becomes less visible precisely because it became normal.

That is a strange ambition for a new market.

Build the industry well enough that ending stops feeling like an industry at all.
