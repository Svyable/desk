# Notes on Endings

This book began with a nuisance that is easy to mistake for a collection of unrelated nuisances.

Old accounts remain open. Old code remains callable. Old files remain searchable. Old laws remain enforceable. Old devices remain in drawers, warehouses, closets, hospitals, factories, cars, substations, and landfills. Old organizations acquire new missions. Old interfaces survive because one customer still needs them. Old credentials retain authority because nobody is certain what will break if they are revoked. The things are different. The administrative sensation is strangely similar.

The temptation, once the pattern becomes visible, is to make it too clean. One can start to see expiration everywhere and then become the kind of person who wants to put a timer on civilization.

That is not the argument of this book.

Some things should outlive us. A bridge that remains safe and useful is not a failure of imagination. A constitutional protection should not disappear because a project manager forgot to renew it. A medical record can matter years after it was created. An archive may become more valuable as the people who made it vanish. A protocol can remain boring and dependable for decades. A language, a forest, a public institution, a family photograph, a scientific dataset, a cathedral, a treaty, a piece of software that does its job: longevity is not a defect.

The distinction that matters is between durability that has been chosen and persistence that has merely happened.

That distinction is harder to maintain than it sounds because endings create their own obligations. Deleting a record can destroy evidence. Retiring a product can strand a customer. Repealing a rule can remove a protection as easily as a burden. Closing an institution can scatter expertise that took years to build. Decommissioning physical infrastructure may require decades of work after the revenue stops. Retiring an AI system may increase rather than reduce the need to preserve evaluations, logs, model versions, incident evidence, or information about dependencies. A clean ending in one ledger can become an unpaid cost in somebody else's.

That is why the examples in this book should not be read as interchangeable proofs. Programmed cell death is a fact about biology, not a charter for corporate liquidation. Prescribed fire is an ecological tool used under particular conditions, not a universal defense of destruction. Bankruptcy discharge is a legal procedure with exceptions, not a theory that obligations should evaporate whenever they become inconvenient. A technical standard that deprecates an old protocol is not a model for every political institution. Each domain has its own rights, failure modes, timescales, and reasons to preserve what came before.

The common question is narrower and, I think, more useful: when a system is created, who is responsible for the work that begins when its first purpose ends?

The answer may be an archivist rather than a demolition crew. It may be a migration path rather than deletion. It may be a successor rather than a shutdown. It may be a funded decommissioning plan, a retention schedule, a revocation mechanism, a final export, a period of compatibility, an orderly bankruptcy, a public reauthorization vote, or a decision that this particular thing is valuable enough to keep indefinitely.

What changes when that question is asked early is not only the ending. The beginning changes too.

A team that knows somebody else will inherit the system may document it differently. A company that expects customers to export their data may resist unnecessary lock-in. A policy written with a review date may be clearer about the conditions that justified it. A service that expects credentials to be revoked will separate identity from permanent possession. A database with a real disposition schedule is designed differently from one whose retention policy is "storage is cheap." A capital project that must fund its own closure may reveal economics that looked attractive only because the tail was missing from the spreadsheet.

This is where the idea of an expiration economy becomes more than an argument about housekeeping. Endings change prices.

A cheap beginning can conceal an expensive tail. The price of a connected device does not necessarily include the future cost of maintaining its cloud service, migrating its data, securing it after the original team leaves, recycling the hardware, or supporting the last customer. A line of generated code can be almost free to produce while remaining expensive to understand years later. An automated agent can be easy to launch while its credentials, scheduled work, memory, and downstream permissions survive long enough to become somebody else's incident.

When those tails become common enough, work forms around them. Migration specialists, records managers, archivists, decommissioning engineers, security teams, e-waste processors, restructuring lawyers, digital-estate planners, lifecycle-governance teams, and whatever professions emerge around retiring autonomous software are all doing different jobs. It would be premature to pretend they already constitute one neat industry. They do, however, expose a shared economic fact: removal, transfer, preservation, and shutdown are productive activities. They consume skill and capital because the alternative is not free.

The generative-AI era makes that fact harder to ignore. A machine that can create a thousand plausible artifacts before lunch can also create a thousand future maintenance decisions. Most will never matter. Some will enter production, acquire dependencies, attract users, accumulate data, receive credentials, trigger other systems, or become the basis for decisions long after the person who initiated them has moved on. The interesting question is not whether artificial intelligence creates more stuff. It plainly can. The question is whether our capacity to govern the resulting stock grows at the same rate.

It may. That is an important possibility.

AI can help identify dead code, translate old formats, document systems, classify records, detect unused permissions, migrate workloads, plan decommissioning, summarize archives, and locate dependencies that humans would otherwise miss. If the same technology that accelerates creation also makes retirement dramatically cheaper, then the central scarcity described in this book may prove temporary. The expiration economy could contain the tools of its own dissolution.

That possibility is not a problem for the thesis. It is one of its tests.

The deeper claim is not that ending must always be scarce. It is that a civilization capable of creating at extraordinary speed has to treat ending capacity as part of productive capacity. If we become equally good at retirement, migration, preservation, succession, and deletion, then we will have solved the problem rather than disproved its importance.

The research trail kept with this working edition is deliberately more explicit than the prose. It separates claims from metaphors and records the sources behind the book's concrete examples. Among them are the National Institute of Standards and Technology's guidance on AI risk management and media sanitization; the Nuclear Regulatory Commission's reactor decommissioning process; the National Archives' records-scheduling rules; the IETF's formal deprecation of TLS 1.0 and 1.1; the European Union's bounded right to erasure; U.S. bankruptcy guidance; research on apoptosis and adaptive forgetting; Forest Service work on prescribed fire; global electronic-waste reporting from ITU and UNITAR; and legal work on fiduciary access to digital assets.

Those sources do not all point in the same direction. They should not. The important cases often contain the argument and its limit at once. The IETF can explain why an old protocol should disappear while admitting that some systems will stop interoperating. Records rules can require destruction of temporary material while protecting permanent records. A right to erasure can coexist with exceptions for law, public interest, research, and legal claims. Bankruptcy can offer a fresh start while preserving debts and rights that the law refuses to erase.

A credible theory of endings has to survive those exceptions.

There is another reason to leave the contradictions visible. Bad endings often begin with somebody declaring that the old thing has no remaining value. History is crowded with documents destroyed too soon, communities treated as obsolete, systems replaced before the replacement worked, institutions dismantled without a successor, and customers informed that their dependence was an implementation detail. The power to end something is power over whoever still relies on it.

So the final question is not simply whether a thing should end.

It is who gets to decide, who gets to object, what must be preserved, who pays for the transition, what evidence proves the ending happened, and what responsibilities survive it.

Beginnings naturally attract attention because they contain visible possibility. A launch has a date. A new building gets a ribbon. A new law gets a signing ceremony. A new model gets a benchmark. A new company gets a financing announcement. The end is usually a sequence of smaller, less photogenic actions carried out by people whose names are not attached to the original creation.

Those people are not cleaning up after the real work.

They are doing the real work that makes the next beginning possible.
