# The Forever Tax

The first cost of a new system is usually the easiest cost to see.

Someone approves a budget. Engineers estimate work. A vendor sends a quote. The project occupies a quarter, a year, or a capital plan. The organization can point to the amount spent creating the thing.

Then the accounting becomes less honest.

The system exists.

From that moment it begins collecting small claims on the future.

It needs hosting. It needs updates. It needs an owner. It may need security review, access control, documentation, backups, monitoring, audits, insurance, legal analysis, customer support, vendor management, training, migration work, and eventually decommissioning. Changes elsewhere must account for it. Incidents involving adjacent systems may require someone to check whether it is affected. New employees have to learn enough about it to avoid breaking it.

Some systems earn every dollar of that cost.

The problem is that the costs are distributed while the creation was concentrated.

A company can approve a $500,000 project with a visible return calculation and then absorb $100,000 of annual lifecycle burden across six departments where no one budget describes the whole amount. After five years, the organization's apparent one-time investment has become a continuing claim.

Call it the forever tax.

The name is intentionally unfair to things that should survive. A good bridge carries a maintenance burden because a useful bridge deserves maintenance. A customer database requires security because customers deserve security. A public institution can cost money indefinitely and still produce enormous value.

The tax is not maintenance itself.

It is maintenance detached from continuing judgment.

The forever tax appears when a system keeps collecting resources because existence became sufficient evidence for continuation.

This is difficult to measure because burden does not arrive as one invoice.

Consider security.

Every active account expands the set of identities that might be compromised. Every service introduces software that may contain vulnerabilities. Every dependency adds another supplier or codebase whose changes can matter. Every domain, certificate, key, endpoint, permission, and integration enters the organization's security model.

The cost is not simply the time spent patching that component.

It is the additional state the defenders must understand.

Complexity increases the number of possible configurations. Two systems can interact in ways neither team expected. A permission granted for one purpose can become dangerous after another system changes. An old API left enabled for compatibility can provide a path around a newer control.

The organization pays for possibility.

This is one reason reducing attack surface is a security goal. Fewer exposed services, unused protocols, unnecessary privileges, and obsolete components mean fewer places where failure can occur.

Deletion can improve security because it removes states the attacker might exploit.

The same logic applies to compliance.

A new dataset may require privacy assessment. A new geography may introduce legal obligations. A new product feature may alter disclosures. A new vendor may require diligence. A new automated decision may require monitoring and recourse.

Each addition can be manageable by itself.

The aggregate becomes a web.

Then a law changes.

The organization has to identify every place affected by the change. The cost of the law is partly the number of living systems to which the law might apply.

Accumulation turns policy change into archaeology.

The tax also appears in discovery and litigation. Records that no longer serve operational purposes may still be responsive to legal requests. Old communications can require review. Legacy systems may need specialists to extract data. A company that preserves everything has increased the universe it may someday need to search, protect, explain, and produce.

Again, preservation can be necessary. Destroying relevant evidence is worse. Records laws and litigation holds exist for reasons.

The principle is not “delete to avoid accountability.”

It is “do not create accountability burden by retaining material without a retention reason.”

The distinction is both ethical and economic.

Data-storage economics can hide the forever tax because the price per byte has fallen spectacularly over computing history. If another terabyte costs very little, deletion projects look irrational. Why spend expensive human time deciding what to remove when hardware can keep it?

Because storage is not stewardship.

A terabyte of unknown data can be cheap to possess and expensive to govern.

The cost arrives when someone asks a question.

Does it contain personal information?

Can we use it to train a model?

Can we send it to another country?

Was consent required?

Does a legal hold apply?

Can a customer ask us to delete it?

Is it authoritative?

Has it been manipulated?

Does it contain secrets?

Can we prove where it came from?

The byte is cheap.

The uncertainty is expensive.

Software has its own version through test matrices. A system supporting one operating mode can be tested relatively directly. Add versions, platforms, feature flags, customer-specific behavior, locales, interfaces, legacy formats, and optional integrations, and the number of possible combinations grows much faster than the visible feature count.

Organizations rarely test every combination. They sample, prioritize, automate, and use experience to focus risk.

Still, every supported variant makes the state space larger.

This creates a counterintuitive productivity effect. AI can make writing code cheaper while making validation more important.

If a coding agent produces five plausible implementations in the time a developer once wrote one, the bottleneck moves. Which implementation is correct? Which one fits architecture? Which one creates the fewest future dependencies? Which one can be retired? Which one has the smallest security footprint?

Cheap creation does not make the choice between creations cheap.

The forever tax therefore includes verification burden.

Every artifact that enters production becomes something future systems must distinguish from alternatives, drafts, superseded versions, and experiments.

Documents demonstrate this at human scale. A team uses generative AI to create proposals rapidly. Everyone can produce polished text. Soon there are six versions of the plan in different folders, three decks, two spreadsheets, and a message thread containing the decision that made all of them partially obsolete.

The organization saved drafting time and created authority debt.

Which document counts?

The question can consume more time than drafting used to.

This is why abundance shifts value toward canonicalization.

A canonical source is an anti-accumulation device. It allows many artifacts to exist while only one carries a defined authority for a particular purpose. Version control, package managers, registries, policy repositories, records systems, and approved model catalogs all perform forms of canonicalization.

They say: these are the living things.

The rest can remain history.

The forever tax also hits people through attention. Every active system generates notifications, alerts, renewal tasks, reports, dashboards, training requirements, and decisions. The company does not experience this as “maintenance cost.” Employees experience it as Tuesday.

A security tool sends another alert.

A vendor requests another renewal.

A dashboard needs another owner.

A policy requires another acknowledgment.

A legacy customer requires another exception.

A database certificate expires.

A service account needs review.

A compliance questionnaire asks about a system nobody remembers launching.

The future gets crowded by the administrative offspring of past decisions.

This is where the forever tax becomes strategic.

Organizations with lower lifecycle burden can change faster because fewer old promises need renegotiation.

They can adopt new security standards faster because fewer obsolete systems block the change.

They can move data more confidently because lineage is clearer.

They can reorganize because ownership is not buried in tribal knowledge.

They can shut down experiments because closure is normal rather than humiliating.

They have more of the future available.

That is the real cost of accumulation: it spends future optionality.

A decision made today can be profitable and still consume choices tomorrow.

This is not an argument against commitments. Durable commitments make civilization possible. A company cannot rewrite every contract weekly. A government cannot make every right provisional. A platform cannot break developers whenever a cleaner architecture appears. Reliability depends on promises that constrain future action.

The question is whether the constraint was purchased knowingly.

An indefinite API guarantee is an option granted to users. A long retention commitment is a liability as well as a service. A product with decades of support has a different cost structure from one designed for a short life. A law without review can constrain later lawmakers.

Duration belongs in the price.

Companies often underprice it because launch teams are rewarded before the tail arrives.

The employee who proposes a new tool can show license cost and implementation savings. The future migration cost belongs to another budget. The executive who acquires a company can show synergies. The duplicate systems may remain for years. The product team can launch a new plan. Billing, support, compliance, and data systems absorb another branch forever.

This is an organizational discount rate problem.

Benefits near the present receive attention.

Small recurring costs far in the future are discounted psychologically even when their cumulative value is large.

The same behavior appears in physical infrastructure. A city can find capital for a new facility more easily than a stable stream of maintenance funding. Politicians attend openings, not pipe replacements. Donors fund buildings whose operations require decades of budgets. The shiny capital asset creates an invisible future obligation.

Infrastructure professionals know this as lifecycle costing. The concept is not new.

What is new is how widely the logic will spread when AI turns many symbolic artifacts into maintainable infrastructure.

A generated report may be disposable.

A generated workflow can become operational.

A generated application can gain users.

A generated agent can acquire permissions.

A generated policy can become enforceable.

The line between artifact and infrastructure gets crossed faster.

That makes lifetime cost relevant to things that once would have been too expensive to create casually.

The obvious response is automation. If AI can create the system, AI can maintain the system. Agents can patch dependencies, renew certificates, answer support questions, migrate data, update documentation, and monitor usage. The forever tax shrinks.

Probably.

Then lower maintenance cost changes behavior.

When maintaining a system becomes cheaper, organizations will maintain more systems.

This is a version of the rebound effect found in other technologies: efficiency reduces the cost of an activity and total consumption can rise. Cheap storage created more stored data. Cheap compute created more computation. Cheap messaging created more messages.

Cheap maintenance can create more things worth maintaining.

The relevant quantity is not cost per system.

It is total lifecycle burden across the expanding population of systems.

If maintenance cost falls by ninety percent while the number of maintained systems rises twentyfold, the organization still becomes more complex.

AI may therefore make expiration more important precisely because it makes maintenance cheaper.

The systems never become painful enough individually to force cleanup.

They remain as low-grade obligations.

This is how a forever tax hides: every line item looks affordable.

The strategic response is to measure subtraction.

How many systems were retired?

How many permissions removed?

How many stale records dispositioned?

How many product variants consolidated?

How many dependencies eliminated?

How much future review burden disappeared?

These metrics can be gamed like any metrics. A team could delete useful things to hit a target. The aim is not to create a corporate cult of subtraction.

The aim is to make ending visible enough to compete with launching.

What gets counted earns status.

A launch has a dashboard.

A migration has a dashboard.

A decommissioning should too.

Not because dashboards create good judgment, but because invisible work loses budget fights.

The forever tax is paid whether the organization names it or not.

Naming it changes who can argue about the bill.

The next economic winners may not be the companies that create the fewest things. They may create astonishing amounts.

Their advantage will be metabolic.

They will know which things are still earning the future they consume.

RFC 8996 gives the forever tax a technical shape. When the IETF deprecated TLS 1.0 and 1.1, the argument was not simply that the versions were old. Continued support kept older cryptographic assumptions in the ecosystem, increased the set of configurations products had to handle, and preserved paths that modern deployments no longer needed. A protocol version does not send a maintenance invoice, but it expands the behavior a library, server, client, auditor, and security team may have to reason about.

That is the forever tax in its purest form: a small supported possibility multiplied across an ecosystem.

The important counterweight is that removal has a tax too. Systems incapable of newer TLS versions can lose interoperability. Customers may need replacement hardware or software. Engineers must plan migration. An organization can spend real money eliminating a compatibility path that cost very little to leave switched on.

Call this the exit tax.

Every lifecycle decision compares the forever tax with the exit tax.

The forever tax grows through maintenance, risk, attention, and constraint. The exit tax arrives through migration, disruption, write-offs, retraining, replacement, and sometimes political conflict. A system persists when the present value of the second looks larger than the first, or when the people paying them are different enough that no one has reason to optimize the total.

This is a better explanation for legacy persistence than laziness.

A platform team may bear the forever tax while a customer bears the exit tax. The customer rationally resists migration because the maintenance burden does not appear on its budget. The platform team rationally wants the migration because the customer's convenience exports costs into every future release. Neither side is confused. They are optimizing different ledgers.

The same distribution appears with data. The privacy and security teams pay part of the forever tax for old records. The product team may value the option to use them later. The customer pays some of the downside if data are breached. Legal pays discovery cost. The archive may hold historical value for people not yet present in the organization. A single retention decision spreads costs and benefits across time and owners.

This is why the forever tax cannot be measured as a neat percentage of revenue or engineering time. Any universal number would be theater. The category is heterogeneous. The useful work is to make cost paths visible enough that a specific decision can be compared.

For a software service, the ledger might include cloud spend, patching, incident exposure, old libraries, support labor, compatibility tests, special customer promises, documentation, access reviews, and the constraint imposed on future architecture. The exit ledger might include migration engineering, customer incentives, dual-running expense, lost features, contract renegotiation, and the probability of a failed cutover.

For a physical asset the categories are different. For a law they are different again.

The arithmetic changes, but the asymmetry remains: the cost of beginning is approved by the people who want the thing; the cost of ending arrives later, often to different people with less authority.

One way to correct the bias is to reserve something at birth.

Physical infrastructure sometimes does this literally through decommissioning funds, bonds, reserves, or closure obligations. Digital systems can do it institutionally by requiring export paths, lifecycle owners, retirement criteria, or budgets for migration. The reserve need not be cash. It can be design work that preserves future options.

The idea is simple: do not spend all of the project's economics on the launch.

Keep some capacity for the day usefulness ends.

This is where lifetime cost becomes strategy rather than housekeeping. A system with a cheap exit can be adopted more aggressively because failure is bounded. A vendor with credible portability may win customers who would avoid deeper dependence. A company whose technical estate can shed unused systems may take more experiments without accumulating paralysis.

The forever tax does not argue for owning less.

It argues for knowing the rent each thing charges on tomorrow.

The idea becomes more concrete during an acquisition. A buyer can value the target's customers, revenue, patents, data, software, and contracts while discovering after close that the technical estate contains another balance sheet: unsupported applications, duplicate tools, unowned service accounts, brittle interfaces, data that cannot easily be dispositioned, and products carrying promises nobody priced in the deal model.

Those liabilities may never appear under a line called forever tax.

They appear as delayed integrations, security remediation, migration programs, cloud bills, support head count, contract exceptions, and projects that cannot start because the old environment is not ready to move.

The cost can alter the economics of the acquisition without any single asset being catastrophic.

That is why lifecycle diligence belongs beside product and financial diligence. A buyer should not only ask what valuable systems exist. It should ask which systems cannot be removed, what obligations survive them, and whether the target has credible evidence about either question.

The same logic applies to a board reviewing an AI program. A count of deployed models is not enough. Neither is a count of generated productivity gains. The board should care about how many of those systems have owners, how many hold consequential data, how many can act, how many are tied to unsupported dependencies, and how much retirement capacity exists if the portfolio changes.

The relevant asset is not cleanliness.

It is maneuverability.

A company with a complicated estate can still be highly maneuverable if the dependencies are legible and exits are practiced. A company with fewer systems can be trapped if one proprietary platform holds all of its critical data and workflows.

This is another reason simple inventory counts are poor proxies for burden.

The forever tax is about constraints on future action.

A system earns its cost when the value of keeping it exceeds the options it consumes. The problem begins when nobody is still making that comparison.
