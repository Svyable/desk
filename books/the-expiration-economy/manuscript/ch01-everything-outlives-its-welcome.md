# Everything Outlives Its Welcome

A nuclear reactor does not stop existing when it stops generating electricity.

The control room can go quiet. The turbine can stop. The revenue associated with every megawatt-hour can disappear. The plant can be, in the ordinary language of business, closed.

The work has barely changed categories.

The United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission describes decommissioning as a process with formal stages: certification that operations have permanently ceased, removal of fuel, a post-shutdown decommissioning activities report, a license termination plan, implementation, cleanup, verification, and eventually termination of the license. The machinery that once produced a useful service becomes the object of another kind of industrial project. People still have to know where hazardous material is, what can be dismantled, what has to be monitored, what records must survive, what the site will become, and what conditions count as finished.

A reactor is an extreme example because the consequences make denial difficult. Nobody sensible can close the gate, mail the keys to the regulator, and announce that the plant has become history.

Most of the modern economy gets to pretend endings are simpler.

A software team launches a new service and calls the old one legacy. A company replaces a customer database and leaves the previous database running because three departments still query it. A city opens a new bridge while continuing to inspect the old bridge until demolition or conversion can actually happen. A family closes the account of a relative who died and discovers six other accounts that do not share the same definition of death. A legislature passes an emergency rule whose emergency ends before the rule does. A hospital changes a clinical system but preserves an older viewer because records created under the previous system still matter. An artificial-intelligence team replaces a model and learns that a dozen downstream applications quietly depended on outputs nobody had documented.

The thing can stop being central long before it stops being alive.

This is where the economics of endings begin.

We tend to measure creation because creation produces an event. A factory opens. A law passes. A product ships. A model is released. A program begins. The before and after can be photographed. The organization can assign credit. Somebody gets to say, with reasonable precision, that this did not exist and now it does.

Ending is rarely that clean.

The first version of an ending is usually a declaration. The real ending is a sequence of obligations.

The difference explains why organizations are full of things that everyone calls old but nobody has killed. Old software is not kept because engineers have an aesthetic preference for old software. It is kept because an invoice printer in a warehouse still uses it, because a partner's integration has not migrated, because a report required by a regulator is generated from its data model, because the one employee who understood a conversion script left, or because the business case for removal competes badly against projects promising new revenue.

New things arrive with advocates.

Old things leave with dependencies.

A dependency is a vote against death cast by something that may not know it is voting.

This makes persistence surprisingly rational at the local level. Imagine you manage a system that costs $200,000 a year to keep running. Replacing it will cost $1.5 million, create six months of migration risk, and produce no visible new feature. The old system is annoying, but it works. The replacement project can fail. The savings will accrue to future budgets. Your promotion may be decided this year.

Doing nothing can be the most defensible personal choice available.

Multiply that choice across a company and the company acquires a museum it cannot close.

The same bias appears outside software because human institutions are generally better at assigning owners to growth than to subtraction. There is a head of expansion. There is a vice president of product. There is a capital program for new construction. There is a legislative committee writing bills. There is a marketing budget for launches. There are grants for new research. There are venture funds seeking new companies.

There are fewer glamorous titles for the people who decide what to stop maintaining.

The work exists. It is just described as overhead.

That description will become increasingly expensive.

Generative artificial intelligence is often discussed as a machine for lowering the cost of production. That is true in a narrow and consequential sense. It can produce text quickly. It can create working code from plain-language requests. It can generate variants of images, interfaces, analyses, documents, and plans. Agentic systems can move beyond the first draft and operate tools, schedule tasks, inspect files, call services, and repeat work without waiting for a person to initiate every step.

The immediate economic question is what happens when one worker can create far more.

The slower question is what happens when one worker can leave far more behind.

Suppose a department that once maintained ten small internal applications can now produce fifty. The fifth application may be almost free to create because the expensive engineering work has been compressed into a prompt, a generated codebase, some testing, and a deployment. The fiftieth application's birth certificate can look wonderful in a productivity report.

Its future maintenance is not free.

It will need authentication. Dependencies will update. Users will ask for changes. Security findings may appear. The data it stores may become subject to new rules. The employee who requested it may leave. The vendor behind an API may change terms. An AI coding system may make future modifications cheap, but cheap modification can encourage even more variants, more integrations, and more locally rational exceptions.

The organization gains capability and inventory at the same time.

We have language for the first gain.

We need better language for the second.

Inventory is not automatically bad. A hospital should have redundant equipment. A library should preserve books nobody requested this month. A city should not demolish a water main because usage dipped for a season. An archive exists precisely because present demand is a poor measure of future value. The mistake would be to turn an argument for deliberate endings into a cult of minimalism.

The issue is whether the cost of continued existence is visible.

Physical inventory has a useful honesty. A pallet occupies floor space. A rusting vehicle occupies a yard. A warehouse charges rent. A machine consumes maintenance hours. A contaminated industrial site appears on maps and balance sheets even when accountants and lawyers debate the exact liability.

Digital inventory is more deceptive.

A million files can fit on hardware small enough to disappear inside a data center. Another copy can be cheap enough that nobody approves it. A dormant account looks like a row in a table. An old model looks like an object in storage. A deprecated service may consume little compute until the day an old customer calls it. The marginal cost of keeping any one thing can approach zero while the aggregate cost of keeping everything becomes large.

This is the paradox that makes the expiration economy difficult to see.

When storage is expensive, people delete.

When storage is cheap, they postpone the decision.

Postponed decisions accumulate into systems.

The physical world offers a parallel in electronic waste. The Global E-waste Monitor reported that the world generated 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, up sharply from 2010, and projected further growth by 2030. The obvious lesson is environmental: devices do not vanish when consumers replace them. Metals, plastics, chemicals, refrigerants, batteries, circuit boards, and casings enter collection systems, informal markets, drawers, closets, landfills, export channels, recycling plants, and sometimes places where their material value can be recovered.

But the more general lesson is organizational.

Replacement creates a second object.

The new phone does not metabolize the old phone. The new server does not erase the old server's data. The new policy does not repeal the old policy unless somebody writes the repeal. The new model does not discover and rewrite every dependency on the model it replaces. The new product does not close the accounts, contracts, warehouses, support obligations, documentation, inventory, and expectations associated with its predecessor.

Creation adds.

Replacement often adds too.

This seems obvious when stated plainly, which is one reason it can hide in practice. Nobody believes the old thing literally vanishes. They believe its remaining cost is somebody else's problem, or a later problem, or small enough to ignore.

Sometimes that belief is correct.

A cheap old file may be harmless. A little-used protocol can remain enabled for a while without catastrophe. A temporary program may quietly stop receiving funds. An old building can be safely mothballed. The expiration economy is not built on the claim that every lingering object becomes a crisis.

It is built on selection.

Which things become dangerous when they outlive their useful context?

Which things become costly because they must be maintained for the last one percent of users?

Which things become legally risky because the organization continues holding data it no longer needs?

Which things become strategically constraining because future systems must remain compatible with decisions made years earlier?

Which things should not be deleted because their evidentiary, cultural, or operational value outlasts their active use?

The ability to answer those questions becomes a source of advantage when the number of candidate things rises.

There is an old fantasy in management that a sufficiently good inventory system solves the problem. If every asset, account, dependency, service, document, model, contract, and workflow can be entered in a database, then the organization can know what it has. Better inventories do help. You cannot retire what you cannot find.

But the hard part arrives after discovery.

The inventory says the old service has fourteen users.

Now what?

One user may represent a million-dollar customer. Another may be an automated script that should have been turned off three years ago. A third may be a regulator. A fourth may be a forgotten test account. Ten may be genuine but able to migrate. The number fourteen is not the decision. It is the beginning of the decision.

Ending requires judgment about value, risk, obligation, and time.

This is why nature offers better analogies than warehouses.

A living system does not remain healthy by preserving every component indefinitely. Cells divide and die. Proteins are made and degraded. Immune responses rise and recede. Organisms shed material, heal wounds, remodel tissue, and replace components. Death is not the opposite of maintenance. In many biological processes, regulated death is part of maintenance.

The analogy has limits. Companies are not organisms, laws are not cells, and software deprecation is not apoptosis. Metaphors become dangerous when they smuggle moral conclusions across domains. A company that talks about employees as disposable cells has learned the wrong lesson.

The useful insight is narrower.

A system optimized only for addition eventually changes character.

You can see the result in a codebase where nobody removes flags because an unknown integration might depend on them. You can see it in a policy manual where exceptions accumulate faster than rules disappear. You can see it in a city where temporary barriers become permanent street furniture. You can see it in a cloud account full of resources with names that made perfect sense to people who no longer work there. You can see it in a household drawer containing five generations of charging cables because the cost of deciding which one can safely be thrown away is greater than the inconvenience of keeping them.

That last example is trivial, but trivial examples reveal behavior without institutional camouflage.

People preserve when uncertainty is greater than storage cost.

As storage cost falls, uncertainty wins more often.

Artificial intelligence lowers another cost: the cost of making substitutes. That shifts the balance again. If it takes six months to build a replacement system, teams are forced to confront migration before launch because running both forever is expensive. If a replacement can be generated in a week, the temptation is to launch first and discover the old system's dependencies later.

Cheap starts can produce expensive overlap.

The same pattern can appear in writing. When a memo took a day, the author had reasons to revise the existing memo. When ten variants can be generated in minutes, an organization can keep all ten, attach them to different threads, and later spend more time determining which one became authoritative than it saved producing them.

The abundance of artifacts creates an authority problem as well as a storage problem.

Which version counts?

Which policy is live?

Which model is approved?

Which contract language is current?

Which dataset may still be used?

Which automated agent is authorized to act?

The older world often answered those questions indirectly through scarcity. Producing an official artifact was expensive enough that fewer existed. That was not always good. Scarcity excluded people and slowed useful experimentation. But cost performed a filtering function whether anyone intended it to or not.

When creation becomes cheap, we have to build the filter deliberately.

Expiration is one such filter.

A date is the simplest form. Food expires. Credentials expire. leases end. Options mature. Temporary authorizations lapse. Certificates must be renewed. Some laws contain sunset provisions. A date forces a future encounter with the question of continuation.

Dates are useful because they reverse the burden of inaction.

Without an expiration date, a thing continues unless somebody acts.

With one, it stops unless somebody acts.

That is a profound governance difference disguised as a calendar setting.

Dates are also crude. A bridge should not become unsafe because a timer elapsed. A scientific dataset may become more valuable with age. A law can still be necessary at its sunset. A secure system may remain secure beyond an arbitrary review date. A beloved tool can retain a community long after its creator wants to move on.

The mature idea is not that every artifact should self-destruct.

It is that continuation should have a mechanism.

Sometimes the mechanism is inspection. Sometimes it is renewal. Sometimes it is a legal standard. Sometimes it is a maintenance budget. Sometimes it is an owner who must attest that the thing remains useful. Sometimes it is usage evidence. Sometimes it is a dependency test. Sometimes it is a public process because the thing has acquired a constituency that cannot be treated as a private implementation detail.

The opposite of immortality is not a kill switch.

It is an exit design.

Nuclear plants make this visible because their exit design is formal, regulated, expensive, and impossible to reduce to deleting a resource in a dashboard. The process forces the owner to distinguish cessation from decommissioning.

That distinction belongs everywhere.

A business can cease selling a product while still owing support.

A government can stop accepting applications while still administering benefits already granted.

A model can stop receiving traffic while its outputs remain inside decisions that matter.

A database can stop accepting writes while its records remain legally significant.

A person can leave an organization while credentials, scheduled tasks, documents, and informal authority continue moving through systems under their name.

The event is not the end.

The end is the completion of the obligations created by the event.

Once you notice that, a great deal of the modern economy looks different.

The abandoned system is not merely old technology. It is unfinished ending work.

The stale account is unfinished ending work.

The obsolete regulation is unfinished ending work.

The contaminated site is unfinished ending work.

The unreconciled archive is unfinished ending work.

The agent still holding credentials after its project ended is unfinished ending work.

This framing changes what counts as productivity.

If a team launches twenty systems and retires none, it may look more productive than a team that launches ten and safely removes eight old ones. The first team's gross output is larger. The second team's net increase in organizational complexity may be much smaller.

Gross creation is easy to count.

Net burden is harder.

That accounting problem will become more important as machines increase the rate of gross creation.

The economy of the next decade will contain extraordinary businesses built around helping people start. There will be systems that create software, companies, media, research, marketing campaigns, financial strategies, legal drafts, robots, synthetic data, personalized education, and agents able to create more agents.

There will also be a quieter class of businesses whose value comes from the opposite direction. They will inventory, revoke, reconcile, migrate, consolidate, archive, delete, decommission, verify, retire, and prove that the ending was completed safely. Some will look like security companies. Some will look like compliance tools. Some will look like data infrastructure. Some will look like waste-management firms, financial services, legal processes, or industrial contractors. Some will be product features that never receive a heroic launch.

They will all be working on the same second ledger.

What did we leave alive?

The question sounds pessimistic only if growth is imagined as a pile.

Healthy systems grow by adding what works and releasing what no longer belongs.

The world has spent centuries learning how to make more.

We are entering an era in which making more becomes easier than deciding what gets to stay.

The electronic-waste figures make one more point worth holding onto. Of the 62 million tonnes generated in 2022, the ITU reported that only 22.3 percent was documented as formally collected and recycled in an environmentally sound manner. The important word is documented. It separates an ending that can be observed and accounted for from one that consists merely of an object leaving the owner's field of vision.

A phone dropped in a collection box, a server handed to a contractor, a disk marked deleted, and an account disabled can all feel finished to the person initiating the action. Whether the material was recovered, the data was sanitized, the credential was revoked everywhere, or the obligation was actually discharged is a different question. Endings are full of this gap between local disappearance and system-level completion.

That gap explains why proof matters. A nuclear regulator does not accept the owner's feeling that a site is probably done. A records manager does not treat a file dragged to a desktop trash icon as equivalent to an authorized disposition. A security team cannot assume a user has lost access because one password was changed if tokens, service accounts, or delegated credentials remain live elsewhere. The work of ending includes evidence that the claimed state has been reached.

This is also why cheap disposal is not the same thing as good disposal. If the cost is shifted to an informal recycler, an abandoned customer, a future security team, a municipality, a successor employee, or a public archive that received records nobody sorted, the original creator has not eliminated the tail. It has moved it.

That observation complicates the clean managerial fantasy of subtraction. The expiration economy is not a program for making dashboards show fewer rows. It is an argument about completing obligations in a way that does not simply make them invisible to the person with the delete button.

The test for a real ending is therefore social as much as technical. Who can still be harmed? Who can still make a claim? Who still depends on the thing? What evidence must remain? What material or authority remains active? Who owns the last mile?

Those questions are slower than creation. They are also where the cost was hiding.
