# The Product That Will Not Leave

Companies love the phrase end of life because it makes a product sound biological.

A date is announced. Support ends. Security updates stop. A replacement is recommended. The old product joins an archive page and the company turns its attention to whatever comes next.

Customers experience a different event.

The machine is still on the desk.

The file is still stored in its format.

The workflow still depends on it.

The building still contains the controller.

The laboratory instrument still talks to the old computer.

The industrial line still runs a version of software the vendor would rather forget.

The account still contains photographs, messages, purchases, subscriptions, settings, and people who do not care that the product manager's road map moved on.

A vendor can declare an ending without completing one.

This is why product retirement reveals a basic conflict inside the expiration economy. The producer wants to concentrate resources on the future. The customer wants the past purchase to remain useful long enough to justify the trust placed in it.

Neither side is automatically unreasonable.

Supporting old products forever can become ruinously expensive. Engineers have to test new changes against old behavior. Security teams must investigate vulnerabilities in components that no longer fit the current architecture. Documentation branches multiply. Support staff maintain knowledge of devices and versions whose installed base is shrinking. Suppliers stop making parts. Certification requirements change. The product may depend on networks, cryptography, operating systems, or cloud services that cannot safely remain frozen.

The last user can be extraordinarily expensive.

So can abandoning that user.

The cost is not limited to goodwill. A product's useful life can be part of its economic value. If a company sells an expensive device on the implicit assumption that it will operate for ten years and withdraws essential support after three, the apparent purchase price was misleading. The customer did not buy a durable object. The customer bought a temporary permission to use an object whose continued function depended on the seller.

Connected products sharpen this issue because physical ownership and operational dependence can diverge.

A person may own the hardware and still need the manufacturer's servers for authentication, remote control, updates, media, maps, voice processing, device registration, or basic functionality. A business can own equipment whose software license, cloud endpoint, or certificate chain determines whether the machine remains useful. A product that looks durable can contain a service with a shorter life than the casing around it.

The cloud moves expiration upstream.

In an older product, failure was often local. A motor wore out. A battery degraded. A circuit failed. The owner could repair, replace, improvise, or accept the loss.

In a connected product, the manufacturer can retire a remote dependency and create failure across thousands of perfectly intact objects at once.

This is not always abusive. Services have real operating costs. Security standards change. Some products sell poorly. Companies fail. Acquisitions change priorities. A cloud architecture designed for one generation may be expensive to maintain for another decade.

The design question is whether the exit burden was considered before the dependency was created.

Could the product degrade gracefully if the service disappeared?

Can customers export their data?

Can another provider operate the function?

Can local control replace remote control?

Can the manufacturer publish enough information for maintenance after official support ends?

Is there a migration path that preserves the customer's investment?

What notice is owed before the ending?

These are not merely customer-service questions. They determine whether a product's retirement becomes waste.

The Global E-waste Monitor's estimate of 62 million tonnes of electronic waste generated in 2022 makes the physical stakes difficult to romanticize. Devices contain recoverable materials and hazardous materials. Some enter formal collection and recycling systems. Many do not. The report's projection of continued growth reflects more than consumer carelessness. It reflects a global system in which electronics multiply, product cycles shorten in some categories, repair can be difficult, and the economic path from unwanted device to recovered material remains uneven.

A product that loses software support can become waste before its hardware wears out.

That is expiration failure made physical.

Right-to-repair debates sit inside this conflict, but repair alone does not solve it. A replacement battery is useless if a service has shut down. Access to a part does not recreate an authentication server. A published schematic does not guarantee that specialized chips remain available. Conversely, keeping a cloud service alive forever is not a coherent repair policy.

The deeper principle is separability.

How much of the object's value can survive the withdrawal of one provider?

Products vary dramatically. A hammer can survive the manufacturer's bankruptcy. A conventional book can survive the publisher. A gasoline car can often be repaired by independent businesses years after the model leaves production because parts, manuals, diagnostic knowledge, salvage markets, and mechanical standards form an ecosystem beyond the original seller.

A tightly coupled connected product may not have that option.

This makes exit architecture part of durability.

A durable product is not only one made from strong materials.

It is one whose useful function is not unnecessarily hostage to dependencies with shorter lives.

The same idea applies to software subscriptions. Subscription models are often criticized because customers never finish paying. That criticism can be too broad. Ongoing services incur ongoing costs. Cloud storage, security monitoring, collaborative software, frequently updated tax systems, and many other products are genuinely continuous services rather than boxes that should be paid for once.

The expiration problem appears when the customer cannot leave cleanly.

Can data be exported in a useful format?

Can the export be interpreted elsewhere?

Do identifiers, histories, attachments, comments, permissions, relationships, and metadata survive migration, or does “export” mean a pile of files stripped of the structure that made them useful?

Does cancellation stop billing without destroying evidence the customer must retain?

Can an organization test a migration before committing to it?

Does the vendor make departure technically possible while economically punitive?

Exit is where product strategy becomes bargaining power.

A company that makes leaving easy appears to weaken its lock-in. It may also strengthen trust. Customers can adopt more confidently when adoption does not feel irreversible.

This is one reason open formats and interoperable standards can expand markets. They reduce the fear that choosing one product means handing the future to one vendor. Portability creates an option. Most customers may never exercise it. The option still changes the relationship.

The same was true in the software chapter: credible exit makes entry easier.

This relationship is underappreciated because businesses often measure retention without distinguishing loyalty from captivity.

A customer who remains because the product continues to earn the relationship is different from a customer who remains because migration is unbearable.

Both appear in the same retention metric.

Only one is a durable compliment.

Product endings also expose a coordination problem inside companies. The team responsible for launching a replacement is usually different from the people handling support for the old product. Revenue targets point forward. Support costs point backward. The replacement team can claim success when adoption rises even while another group absorbs the pain of stragglers.

The company sees one transition.

The organization chart sees two budgets.

This can produce irrational behavior even when every team is locally rational. A new product launches before migration tools are ready because delay threatens the road map. The old product remains because support owns customer risk. The company ends up operating both systems longer than planned.

Replacement again becomes addition.

The best product-retirement programs treat migration as part of the launch rather than cleanup after it. They identify who will be stranded, which features do not have equivalents, what data must move, which integrations will break, how long dual operation is acceptable, and who owns the final shutdown.

The details are unglamorous.

They are also the difference between a replacement and a second product.

Consider enterprise software with a large ecosystem of customizations. A vendor can create a modern platform that is better on nearly every dimension and still fail to move customers because the customers did not buy only the core product. Over years they embedded local workflows, reports, macros, integrations, permissions, and institutional memory around it.

The old system became habitat.

Replacing the software means relocating an ecosystem.

That can take longer than building the new software because the expensive asset is no longer the code. It is the accumulated fit between the code and the organization.

This is why some products never quite leave. Their installed base shrinks until support becomes a specialized business. Independent firms emerge to maintain old systems. Parts are harvested. Emulators preserve behavior. Compatibility layers keep files readable. Communities exchange manuals and repair knowledge.

What looks like technological stubbornness can be economically rational preservation.

The product still does a job.

The challenge is knowing when preservation crosses into avoidable burden.

A piece of medical equipment may remain because replacing it would cost far more than maintaining it and because the old system is clinically adequate. A consumer gadget may remain because the owner likes it. An industrial controller may run safely for decades. None should be retired merely because a newer model exists.

Age is still not the criterion.

Supportability is.

Security is.

Continued usefulness is.

Availability of parts and expertise is.

The cost imposed on surrounding systems is.

The risk of failure is.

The customer's ability to choose is.

These factors rarely point in the same direction at the same time.

That is why a good ending looks less like a date and more like a negotiated boundary.

End-of-sale, end-of-feature-development, end-of-standard-support, end-of-security-updates, end-of-cloud-service, end-of-spare-parts availability, and end-of-data access may reasonably occur on different dates. Collapsing them into one phrase—end of life—hides the actual transition.

Products have several lives.

A device can stop being sold while remaining well supported. It can stop receiving new features while still receiving security fixes. Official support can end while independent repair remains viable. The original vendor can disappear while an open standard keeps the ecosystem alive.

A humane retirement makes these states legible.

Ambiguity is expensive because customers delay decisions when they do not know what the future holds. A vague threat that “support will end eventually” can produce more fear than a clear schedule with a credible migration path. Certainty lets people plan.

This principle becomes more important with AI-enabled products because software can become highly personalized. A user may spend years teaching a system preferences, vocabulary, workflows, contacts, and context. The product's value is partly the accumulated relationship.

Retiring it is no longer equivalent to replacing a calculator.

The migration problem includes memory.

Can the new system inherit useful context without carrying unsafe or irrelevant history?

Can the user inspect what will move?

Can the old system produce an intelligible export rather than a proprietary blob?

Can the person choose to leave some history behind?

What happens when one vendor ends a companion-like product on which users formed emotional attachments?

These questions become more difficult as software behaves less like a tool and more like a continuing participant.

The temptation for companies will be to solve the problem through indefinite service. Keep the old model online. Keep the old personality available. Keep every previous agent, voice, interface, and memory schema because somebody prefers it.

That is not free either.

Old models can have security weaknesses. Safety techniques can improve. Infrastructure can change. Licensing can change. The system may depend on components the company can no longer maintain. Supporting every past behavior can block improvements for everyone else.

Customer attachment creates a real obligation without creating an infinite one.

That sentence will become important later when the book reaches artificial intelligence directly.

The product chapter provides the simpler version.

A company should be able to end a product.

A customer should be able to understand what the ending takes away.

Those statements can coexist.

The quality of the product is partly revealed by what happens when the company wants to leave it.

Did the design preserve customer agency?

Did the business model account for the tail?

Did the physical artifact become waste because a remote service vanished?

Did the customer receive enough notice to migrate rather than panic?

Did data remain portable?

Could independent repair or support continue where appropriate?

Was the final shutdown budgeted, or merely announced?

We usually judge products at the moment of adoption. Reviews compare features, price, speed, ease of use, appearance, and performance. Those things matter.

The expiration economy adds another review category.

What happens when this relationship ends?

For products that may live in homes, hospitals, factories, cars, schools, governments, and intimate personal routines for years, that question belongs near the beginning.

A product is not fully designed until departure has a shape.

The shutdown of third-generation cellular networks made the same problem visible at infrastructure scale. The Federal Communications Commission warned consumers that carriers' 3G phase-outs could affect not only older phones but other connected products using cellular links. The network dependency was embedded inside devices whose owners may never have thought of themselves as telecommunications customers: alarms, vehicle systems, monitoring equipment, and other machines whose useful life could exceed the life of the radio network they expected to call.

No carrier could reasonably be required to operate every generation of a mobile network forever. Spectrum, equipment, security, capacity, and operating economics all change. Newer networks can use scarce infrastructure more productively. The phase-out itself is not evidence of abuse.

What the transition exposes is the number of product lives hidden inside a single purchase.

A connected object has a hardware life, a battery life, a radio-standard life, a carrier-contract life, a certificate life, a cloud-service life, a software-support life, and perhaps a vendor life. The shortest one can become the effective life of the whole product if the design does not allow substitution.

A buyer sees one device.

The device may contain seven clocks.

This is why durability claims become slippery in connected markets. A manufacturer can build excellent hardware and still sell a fragile relationship. The casing may survive ten years while the server behind an API receives three years of funding. The radio may remain functional while the network generation disappears. A certificate embedded in firmware may expire. A licensing service may shut down after an acquisition. None of these failures looks like the cracked hinge or worn bearing that a buyer intuitively associates with product death.

They are institutional failures of continuity.

The best response is not to make every clock identical. It is to make the dependencies substitutable where the stakes justify it. A device that can use more than one network generation is less hostage to a particular sunset. A product whose local functions continue without the cloud can lose features without becoming a brick. A documented export format allows the data to outlive the application. An open protocol allows another provider to serve a function after the original seller leaves. A standard battery or connector lets a repair market form beyond one brand.

Separability does have costs. Supporting multiple standards can make a device more expensive and complex. Local processing can require more hardware. Open interfaces can create security and certification burdens. Portability can be difficult when competing systems have genuinely different data models. A company that promises graceful degradation must engineer and test the degraded state rather than assuming it will work.

Exit architecture is not free insurance.

The question is whether the premium is justified by the expected life and consequence of the product.

For a novelty gadget, perhaps not. For a medical monitor, industrial controller, school system, vehicle, building component, or piece of public infrastructure, the calculus changes. A long-lived physical installation deserves special suspicion when it depends on a short-lived remote service.

The economics of the last customer also become clearer here. As an installed base shrinks, the cost per remaining user of operating old infrastructure can rise. A million customers can support dedicated engineering, security, and operations. Ten thousand may not. A hundred almost certainly cannot support the same service at the original price. The producer sees declining scale. The last customer sees a product that still works.

That mismatch needs an exit path before it becomes a moral argument.

Sometimes the answer is migration assistance. Sometimes it is a final software update that enables local operation. Sometimes it is documentation sufficient for an independent maintainer. Sometimes it is a buyback, replacement credit, data export, escrow arrangement, or long notice period. Sometimes the honest answer is simply that the product was always a service with a finite supported life and should have been sold that way.

What is hard to defend is pretending durability at sale and discovering subscription-like dependence at shutdown.

The end of a product therefore belongs in the product's original value proposition. Not as a legal paragraph nobody reads, but as a property of the design. Can I leave? Can the manufacturer leave? What remains useful if either of us does?

Those are not gloomy questions.

They are how a buyer learns what they actually own.
