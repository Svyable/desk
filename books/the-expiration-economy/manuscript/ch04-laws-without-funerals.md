# Laws Without Funerals

A law usually enters the world with a date.

The vote is recorded. The executive signs. The regulation is published. The effective date is printed. Lawyers can identify the moment when an obligation that did not apply yesterday begins to apply today.

Its ending is often less precise.

Some laws contain expiration dates. Some authorities lapse unless renewed. Some emergency powers are written to terminate. Regulations can be rescinded. Courts can invalidate provisions. Legislatures can repeal statutes. Agencies can retire guidance. Appropriations can end even when the underlying authorization remains on the books.

But a great deal of law persists because persistence is the default.

This is not necessarily a flaw. Legal continuity is one of civilization's great achievements. People make investments, sign contracts, build homes, organize businesses, arrange families, and exercise rights partly because the rules are not expected to evaporate whenever political attention moves elsewhere. A legal system that automatically deleted yesterday's decisions would replace stability with roulette.

The problem is subtler.

Rules can remain after the conditions that justified them have changed, while the political machinery required to reconsider them becomes more expensive than the political machinery that created them.

Creation has a constituency.

Expiration has a burden of proof.

Congressional researchers use the term “sunset provision” for clauses that terminate a provision, authority, or program at a specified future time unless lawmakers act again. The mechanism is simple enough to sound almost administrative. It changes the politics of continuation.

Without a sunset, opponents of an existing rule generally have to assemble the votes, attention, legal authority, and institutional momentum necessary to remove it.

With a sunset, supporters may have to assemble enough of those things to keep it.

The clock changes who must move.

That is why expiration dates are never neutral. They redistribute inertia.

A rule can deserve a sunset because it was enacted under unusual conditions, because lawmakers want evidence before making it permanent, because the authority is exceptionally broad, or because the political bargain that produced it could not survive without a future review point. A sunset can also be bad design. Complex programs may need continuity. Long-term investments can be distorted by arbitrary deadlines. A worthwhile policy can be held hostage during renewal. Temporary tax provisions can create repeated uncertainty. Expiration can become a bargaining weapon rather than an evaluation mechanism.

The useful lesson is not that laws should expire.

It is that default duration is a policy choice even when nobody describes it as one.

The same is true of regulations.

A new rule usually arrives because somebody can describe the harm it is intended to reduce. The description may be compelling or contested, empirical or political, narrow or broad. Once implemented, however, the rule becomes part of the environment it changes. Firms redesign processes. Lawyers write compliance manuals. software vendors build features around it. Employees learn procedures. Inspectors develop routines. Trade groups emerge. Consultants develop expertise. People who benefit from the rule learn to defend it. People burdened by it learn to route around it.

The rule acquires dependencies.

By the time lawmakers ask whether the original problem still exists, the relevant question is no longer the one they faced at enactment.

The system has adapted.

This creates an evaluation trap. Suppose a safety regulation is followed by fewer accidents. One interpretation is that the rule worked and should remain. Another is that technology improved independently and the rule now adds little. A third is that the rule caused firms to shift risky activity somewhere else. A fourth is that compliance became embedded so deeply that repeal would save less than expected. A fifth is that the apparent improvement is unrelated to the intervention.

The post-rule world cannot simply be compared with the pre-rule world because the rule helped create the post-rule world.

That difficulty is not an excuse to avoid review. It is a reason to take review seriously.

A sunset provision can force the question without answering it.

That distinction matters because expiration mechanisms are often sold as self-cleaning government. Write enough sunsets, the argument goes, and obsolete rules will vanish automatically.

Real governance is not a kitchen timer.

An authority approaching expiration can create a rush of lobbying. Agencies can struggle to plan staffing. Regulated parties can postpone investment while waiting for renewal. Congress can reauthorize something in a larger package without much substantive review. A provision can lapse accidentally even when nearly everyone wants it to continue. The presence of a date does not guarantee judgment.

A review date without review capacity is theater.

This is the governmental version of the software team that marks an API deprecated but never invests in migration. The label announces an ending while the institution avoids the work required to complete it.

The harder question is what an actual legal funeral would involve.

First, somebody would have to know what the rule does in practice rather than only what its text says. That means understanding enforcement, compliance behavior, litigation, informal workarounds, costs, benefits, distributional effects, and interaction with other rules.

Second, somebody would have to know what depends on it. A requirement that looks redundant may anchor private contracts, state programs, technical standards, or administrative procedures. Removing the rule can expose assumptions elsewhere.

Third, somebody would need a theory of transition. A rule can stop applying prospectively while old cases remain. Licenses already granted may continue. Rights vested under an earlier regime may survive. Records may need preservation. Agencies may need to explain the change. Courts may spend years interpreting the boundary between old and new.

Repeal, like shutdown, is an event inside a longer ending.

This is why legal systems accumulate sediment.

Sediment is not the same as waste.

Older law can carry valuable constraints that later generations take for granted precisely because they work. Constitutional provisions, property rules, procedural protections, disclosure regimes, environmental standards, building codes, securities rules, and countless other legal structures can become so ordinary that their original controversies fade from memory. The absence of a recurring crisis may be evidence of success rather than irrelevance.

A society that mistakes unfamiliar origins for obsolete purpose can destroy institutional memory.

The reverse mistake is more common in bureaucracies: treating survival as evidence of necessity.

Survival proves only that the rule survived.

These two errors mirror one another. One fetishizes novelty. The other fetishizes continuity.

Expiration is valuable when it creates a disciplined encounter between them.

That encounter should ask a question law rarely asks at launch: what evidence would justify ending this later?

Policies are usually written around entry conditions. If unemployment rises, launch the program. If pollution exceeds a threshold, impose the standard. If a threat appears, grant an authority. If an industry behaves in a certain way, require disclosure.

Exit conditions are often vaguer.

The program ends when the emergency ends.

Which measure defines the emergency?

The authority ends when the threat has passed.

Who decides the threat has passed, and what evidence counts?

The subsidy ends when the industry is mature.

What does mature mean after firms have organized their economics around the subsidy?

Ambiguous endings turn temporary measures into political objects with no internal clock.

The most revealing examples are not necessarily notorious laws. They are mundane provisions that nobody would design from scratch today but that remain because repeal consumes scarce legislative time. Legislative agendas are finite. Every hour spent removing an obsolete requirement is an hour not spent on a current fight. The beneficiaries of cleanup are diffuse. The costs of one strange provision may be small.

So the provision stays.

This is the forever tax in political form: many individually minor survivals combine into institutional complexity.

Companies experience the downstream version. Compliance teams do not deal with statutes one at a time. They deal with interacting layers of federal, state, local, industry, contractual, and internal rules. A requirement introduced for one purpose can survive inside a company long after the external obligation changes because nobody wants to be the person who removes a control that might still matter.

The policy manual becomes an archaeological site.

“Why do we do this?”

“Because legal requires it.”

Legal may have required it in 2014.

The current lawyers may not know.

This is not a joke about lawyers. It is a knowledge problem created by institutional turnover. A control can remain because its rationale was never stored with it. The procedure contains the what but loses the why.

A system designed for eventual review would preserve both.

That design principle can be modest. A rule or internal policy can record its owner, authority, purpose, effective date, material dependencies, and a review condition. The condition does not have to be a hard expiration. It can be a change in the external law, a threshold, a recurring interval, an event, or a scheduled reassessment.

The important thing is to prevent “still exists” from becoming the only evidence for “still needed.”

This is easier to say than implement because organizations are asymmetrical about risk.

Adding a control is easy to defend after a failure.

Removing one is easy to blame after the next failure.

The person who creates a new requirement can point to caution. The person who removes an old requirement must own the possibility that the bad event returns.

This makes institutional deletion politically dangerous.

You can see the same asymmetry in cybersecurity. After an incident, organizations add alerts, approvals, scans, forms, and access restrictions. Some are necessary. Some are temporary compensating controls meant to survive only until a deeper fix is deployed. Years later, the deeper fix may exist while the compensating controls remain because removing a security measure requires more confidence than adding one.

Caution accumulates.

Eventually caution can impair the system it was meant to protect. Employees work around cumbersome controls. Critical alerts disappear inside noise. Engineers avoid improving architecture because the approval path is too expensive. Security itself becomes harder to see.

Rules have carrying costs.

That sentence can be abused by anyone who dislikes a particular rule. Cost is not a verdict. Seat belts have costs. Audits have costs. Due process has costs. Fire codes have costs. Rights can impose substantial costs on majorities and institutions precisely because protecting them is the point.

The economic question is not whether a rule is free.

It is whether the present cost remains justified by the present purpose.

Expiration mechanisms can make that question unavoidable without presuming the answer.

This is why temporary law is both attractive and dangerous in emergencies. Crises compress time. Governments act with incomplete information. Broad authorities can be easier to justify when delay itself carries large costs. The same compressed decision may be poorly suited to permanent governance.

An expiration date can acknowledge uncertainty honestly.

We are doing this now because the situation demands it. We will ask again later because we do not know enough to promise forever.

There is intellectual humility in that structure.

There is also political risk. Temporary measures can be repeatedly renewed until “temporary” becomes ceremonial. Legislatures can use deadlines to manufacture crises. Programs can become dependent on last-minute extensions. Institutions may spend resources lobbying for survival instead of improving performance.

A clock does not produce wisdom.

It produces a moment at which wisdom is required.

That may be the most useful way to think about expiration across the entire book.

An expiration mechanism is not a decision to kill.

It is a refusal to let continuation remain invisible.

The design can take many forms. A statute can sunset. A regulation can require retrospective review. An agency can publish termination criteria for a temporary authority. A company can attach a review date to a policy. A control can expire when the underlying vulnerability is fixed. A permit can require renewal. A budget can require reauthorization.

Each mechanism shifts continuation from assumption toward evidence.

The shift will become more important as artificial intelligence lowers the cost of generating rules as well as software. Organizations can already produce policies, procedures, contracts, compliance language, decision trees, and operational guidance faster than employees can absorb them. A model can draft a control after every incident. An agent can create a workflow to enforce it. Another model can rewrite the manual.

The danger is not that the language will be bad.

The danger is that the language will remain.

Imagine a company where every failure produces a new machine-generated rule, every exception produces a new branch, and almost nothing is removed because removal requires human accountability. The policy system will become technically precise and practically unusable.

Abundant rulemaking without abundant repeal is another form of accumulation.

Government will face the same temptation. AI can help agencies analyze comments, draft regulations, inspect statutes, compare jurisdictions, and identify inconsistencies. Those capabilities may improve policy. They may also make it easier to create detailed interventions faster than institutions can evaluate the pile already in force.

The old bottleneck was drafting.

The new bottleneck may be deciding what still deserves authority.

Law needs memory. Law also needs funerals.

The civilization that gets both right will not be the one that repeals the most.

It will be the one that knows why a rule is still alive.

The Congressional Research Service's treatment of sunset provisions is useful precisely because it is unromantic. A sunset is a drafting device: authority terminates at a future time unless the law provides otherwise or later legislation changes the result. That mechanical description protects the idea from a common political fantasy. The clause itself does not perform the evaluation. It only places an expiration event into the legal architecture.

What happens before that event determines whether the mechanism produces learning or merely a deadline.

Decades ago, proposals for broad federal sunset systems forced the same issue into view. A 1977 Government Accountability Office analysis of a proposed Sunset Act did not reduce the problem to inserting dates in statutes. Systematic review required schedules, information, scope, institutional responsibility, and enough capacity to examine what programs were actually doing. The interesting lesson is not whether that particular proposal should have become law. It is that review is itself an operating function.

If a legislature sunsets fifty complicated programs in the same month but gives itself no time, staff, data, or evaluation method to reconsider them, the clock can make judgment worse. Renewal becomes triage. Programs that happen to have organized constituencies and good lobbyists may receive attention. Quiet programs with diffuse benefits can be lost. Bad programs with politically powerful beneficiaries can survive. The date creates scarcity without creating knowledge.

This is the public-policy version of a company setting the same ninety-day retention period for every dataset because one number is easier to administer. Uniformity is not necessarily discipline.

A useful sunset therefore asks at least two questions before the deadline arrives. What was the original claim? What evidence would cause us to update it?

That second question is rarer than it should be. Policies often arrive with objectives but not falsification conditions. A tax preference is meant to encourage an activity. A reporting requirement is meant to improve transparency. An emergency authority is meant to address a specified threat. A program is meant to serve a population whose needs can be measured. Yet the law may never state what outcome would count as sufficient success, failure, obsolescence, or transition into a different instrument.

Without that information, renewal debates easily become identity contests. Supporters defend the program as a signal of commitment to the underlying goal. Opponents attack it as a symbol of government excess. The actual operating question—whether this mechanism is still the best way to achieve the goal—can disappear.

The same failure occurs inside corporations. A policy created after a customer incident remains because removing it would feel like becoming less serious about customers. A review step created after a security breach becomes equated with security itself. A reporting process survives because questioning it sounds like questioning accountability. The mechanism borrows moral authority from the value it was meant to serve.

That is another reason funerals are difficult. Institutions do not only depend on rules materially. They attach meanings to them.

A serious review must be willing to preserve the purpose while ending the mechanism. Sometimes the right result of a sunset is renewal. Sometimes it is replacement. Sometimes a temporary program has become valuable enough to deserve permanence, but permanence should be acknowledged rather than achieved through endless extensions. Sometimes the original problem has changed enough that the old authority should end even though the public objective remains.

This makes expiration less like destruction and more like constitutional maintenance. The question is not whether government can tolerate old law. It is whether law has a credible path for distinguishing inheritance from inertia.

There is no reason to expect that path to be cheap. It competes for legislative attention, analytical talent, political capital, and administrative knowledge. That cost explains why obsolete provisions can survive for years even when few people actively defend them. The cleanup benefit is spread across future transactions. The effort required to achieve it is concentrated in the present.

Cheap drafting may sharpen this imbalance. If AI makes it easy to generate precise, internally consistent regulatory text, policy organizations can produce more candidate rules, amendments, exceptions, and guidance. The scarce input does not disappear. It moves downstream into evaluation, implementation, litigation, interpretation, review, and repeal.

The state can become better at writing law faster than it becomes better at deciding which law deserves another decade.

That is the legal form of the expiration economy.
