# The Right to Disappear

There is a form of power that appears only when leaving is possible.

A customer can leave a company.

A worker can leave a job.

A country can leave a treaty under defined conditions.

A tenant can move.

A voter can remove an officeholder.

A user can delete an account.

An investor can sell.

The exit may be expensive, unfair, impractical, or incomplete. The mere existence of an exit still changes the relationship.

A relationship with no credible ending is not simply durable.

It is a form of dependence.

This is why the expiration economy eventually reaches beyond systems and into rights.

The ability to leave a system matters because systems accumulate knowledge about people. Employers know work histories. Platforms know messages and contacts. banks know transactions. Governments hold records. Health systems hold intimate information. AI assistants may know preferences, fears, habits, unfinished projects, and the ordinary details a person once told only to other people.

Participation leaves residue.

What rights follow the person out?

The first answer is familiar: access.

People should be able, in many contexts, to retrieve information they reasonably understand as theirs. Data portability laws and platform export tools reflect versions of this principle. The details differ by jurisdiction and product, and there are legitimate limits when records involve other people, security, proprietary systems, or legal obligations.

But access is only the beginning.

The second question is whether departure actually ends the relationship.

A user closes an account.

Does the company stop profiling the user?

Does the data remain in backup systems?

Do advertising profiles survive?

Do trained models retain influence from prior interactions?

Does the company preserve transaction records it is legally required to keep?

Can the user still be identified through records held by partners?

The truthful answer is usually not a clean yes or no.

Digital departure has layers.

This is frustrating because interfaces are binary. Delete account. Cancel subscription. Remove history. Turn off memory.

People reasonably interpret those verbs as endings.

The institution often implements transitions.

That gap creates distrust.

A mature system should explain what departure means without requiring the user to read an architecture diagram. The explanation can be plain.

Your active account will close now.

These records will remain for this reason.

These memories will stop influencing future recommendations.

These backups expire on their normal schedule.

These financial records must be retained.

These public contributions remain because other people rely on the record.

These items can be removed separately.

The specific language varies. The principle is legibility.

People should know whether leaving actually changes the institution's future behavior toward them.

That question becomes more important with AI because AI can make dormant records active again.

An old support conversation can become a training example.

A forgotten photo can become part of a face-search system.

An old email can become context for a workplace assistant.

A decade of purchases can become a behavioral profile much richer than the one available when the purchases occurred.

The institution does not merely remember.

It gains new ways to interpret memory.

This changes the moral meaning of retention.

A person who consented to one use years ago may not have consented to every future inference made possible by better models. Law will draw formal boundaries unevenly. Products will need to make practical choices before law resolves every edge case.

The simplest choice is not to treat possession as permanent permission.

That is a stronger principle than deletion.

It says continued use should remain connected to purpose, authority, and time.

The person may not have a right to erase every record. They may still have a legitimate claim against indefinite reinterpretation.

The difference between preserving a tax record and feeding the same record into a personalized persuasion system is not storage.

It is purpose.

This is why the right to disappear should not be understood literally as a right to erase history.

History matters.

Public officials should not be able to remove records of public acts because the record has become inconvenient.

Companies should not be able to destroy evidence of wrongdoing under the language of privacy.

A person cannot demand that every newspaper delete truthful reporting simply because time passed, subject to the legal rules of the relevant jurisdiction.

Victims can have legitimate interests in preserving records. Communities can have legitimate historical interests. Courts, auditors, scientists, and journalists depend on durable evidence.

The right at issue is narrower and more practical.

A person should sometimes be able to stop an old relationship from continuously reproducing itself.

That can mean closing an account.

Ending authorization.

Withdrawing from a recommendation profile.

Stopping a memory from influencing future interactions.

Moving data to a competitor.

Separating historical preservation from current targeting.

Removing stale identity from systems where continued presence has no justification.

The right is not to make the past untrue.

It is to prevent the past from claiming unlimited jurisdiction over the future.

Human societies have always created partial versions of this right.

Bankruptcy limits the future power of some old debts.

Statutes of limitation can limit the time in which certain claims are brought.

Expungement and sealing rules can reduce the future visibility or legal effect of some records.

Employment law in some places restricts how certain old information can be used. Credit-reporting regimes often place time limits on specific negative information. Different legal systems balance these questions differently because the interests conflict.

These mechanisms share one intuition.

Time can change what an old fact should be allowed to do.

That is not forgetting.

It is jurisdiction through time.

Artificial intelligence will make that idea unavoidable because models do not naturally experience social aging. A vector similarity score does not know that a remark was made by a twenty-year-old who is now forty. A recommendation system can rediscover an old preference without knowing it was temporary. A risk model can preserve a historical correlation after the conditions that produced it changed.

Machines need explicit help distinguishing history from destiny.

Personal AI makes this intimate.

Imagine an assistant that remembers every relationship the user described.

A friendship ends.

Years later the assistant still suggests the former friend when planning a trip because the old preference remains strongly represented.

The user corrects it.

The assistant says it understands.

Then another subsystem retrieves old conversations and reintroduces the association.

From the user's perspective, the system does not merely have memory.

It refuses to let a chapter close.

That is poor lifecycle design.

People change through endings.

They move cities.

They change careers.

They stop drinking.

They convert religions or leave them.

They marry, divorce, grieve, recover, learn, become ill, heal, become parents, become caretakers, discover new interests, abandon old identities, and revise stories about themselves.

A persistent assistant can be useful only if persistence is compatible with revision.

The right to disappear is therefore partly a right to become someone the system did not predict.

This is not an argument that systems should forget identity whenever a user asks. Fraud prevention, security, safety, legal obligations, and continuity can require durable records. A bank should not let a fraudster erase the existence of prior fraud simply by opening a new account. A medical system should not discard clinically relevant history because a recommendation feels old. A court should not destroy an order because one party dislikes it.

The distinction is between authority and salience.

A record can remain without dominating every future interaction.

This is the same separation introduced in the chapter on forgetting.

The new element is user power.

Who gets to decide that an old memory should lose influence?

Systems can infer some decay automatically. People should be able to intervene.

A user may say: this is no longer true.

A user may say: this is true historically but stop using it for recommendations.

A user may say: preserve this for my records but remove it from conversational memory.

A user may say: delete this if you are allowed to.

A user may say: export this and close the account.

These controls will be difficult to design well because ordinary people do not want to become database administrators.

The interface needs to match human concepts.

Relationships.

Preferences.

Projects.

Health information.

Work history.

Sensitive memories.

The system can manage underlying technical details while the user controls meaningful categories.

This will create tradeoffs. If the controls are too broad, useful context disappears. If they are too granular, nobody uses them. If automated summaries replace raw history, the summary can introduce errors. If old memory is merely hidden, future systems may still retrieve it unexpectedly.

There will be failures.

That does not make the principle optional.

Persistent AI without user-directed forgetting will become socially brittle.

The right to disappear also matters for workers.

Workplace systems increasingly preserve communication, performance data, security events, badge records, productivity signals, recorded meetings, and machine-generated assessments. Some of that information serves legitimate operational and legal purposes. Some can become a permanent shadow following employees through internal decisions.

A company that remembers every past weakness more efficiently than every past improvement creates an asymmetric memory institution.

This is partly a data-quality problem and partly a power problem.

Employees may not know what records influence decisions.

AI can make those records more actionable without making the process more visible.

The expiration economy asks a basic question.

When should workplace data stop being live evidence about who this person is now?

There is no universal answer. Safety incidents, fraud, qualifications, legal requirements, and contractual records differ. The point is that indefinite retention should not quietly become indefinite judgment.

The same is true for consumers.

A late payment can be relevant for some period without defining a person forever.

A failed business can be part of history without preventing every future attempt.

A teenager's public mistake can remain true without becoming the first fact every future stranger encounters.

Time does not make facts false.

It can change their proper weight.

The internet struggled with this long before modern AI. Search made old information newly discoverable. Social networks made old posts persistent. Cheap storage removed the practical forgetting produced by physical scarcity.

AI adds synthesis.

A model can combine weak traces into a strong narrative.

That narrative may be accurate, misleading, or somewhere between. The power to synthesize makes the governance of old traces more important because the system can create a present identity from records never intended to function together.

This is where the right to disappear becomes a right against forced coherence.

Human lives are contradictory.

A machine can make them look more consistent than they were.

It can connect the protest attended at nineteen, the job held at twenty-six, the purchase made at thirty-one, the medical search at thirty-seven, and the argument recorded at forty into a profile that feels explanatory.

The profile may reveal patterns.

It may also invent continuity where life contained change.

A healthy information system should leave room for discontinuity.

That is the human equivalent of deprecation.

Some versions of a person should be allowed to become historical.

This principle will meet resistance from businesses that profit from persistent profiles. Longitudinal data improve targeting, personalization, fraud detection, credit analysis, recommendation, and model performance. The economic incentive is to keep the relationship computationally alive even after the human believes it ended.

Regulation will handle some of the conflict. Competition will handle some. Product design can handle more than companies sometimes admit.

Privacy can be a feature because expiration can create trust.

A service that allows a genuine clean exit may attract users precisely because adoption feels less binding. A personal AI system that makes memory transparent and editable may feel safer to use deeply. A platform that lets people separate public history from active recommendation can offer persistence without surveillance by default.

Exit quality can become product quality.

That idea runs through the entire book.

Products are better when users can leave.

Organizations are healthier when projects can end.

Markets are more competitive when switching is credible.

Agents are safer when authority expires.

Memory is more useful when relevance can decay.

The right to disappear is one expression of the same general principle.

Nothing capable of knowing you should assume it owns the right to know you forever in the same way.

The past can remain.

The relationship can still end.

Death reveals the limits of our current digital exit language.

A person can die while accounts remain active, subscriptions renew, domains resolve, scheduled posts publish, cloud storage persists, photographs continue appearing in automated reminders, and authentication challenges are sent to a phone nobody should be expected to answer. The legal person has reached an ending that software may not recognize.

Digital-estate law has begun to address parts of this problem by giving fiduciaries defined routes to some digital assets and communications under state law and service agreements. The details are deliberately constrained because a deceased person's executor should not automatically receive every private message merely by receiving authority over the estate. Privacy, contractual terms, property interests, and fiduciary duties collide.

That collision is useful for the book because it proves that digital inheritance and digital disappearance are not opposites.

The estate may need access to one thing and deletion of another.

A family may need photographs while a work account should be closed.

A business may need control of a domain while private correspondence remains protected.

A recurring payment may need cancellation while tax records survive.

A personal assistant may hold household knowledge worth transferring and intimate conversations nobody should inherit by default.

Death is the hardest case because the user can no longer curate the exit.

The system has to rely on choices made earlier, law, authorized fiduciaries, and product policy.

This argues for digital estate planning as a normal feature of persistent AI rather than an edge case. A user should be able to say, while alive, what happens to certain data and services after death or incapacity: preserve, transfer, memorialize, close, or delete where law and product constraints permit.

The design should resist melodrama. Most people do not want to spend an afternoon specifying the afterlife of every settings record.

Defaults matter.

A financial account should not behave like a photo album.

A public body of work should not behave like a private journal.

A shared family archive should not behave like a one-person recommendation history.

One category called “digital assets” is too broad.

This becomes more urgent if AI systems act after the user's death. A household agent could continue renewing services. A small-business agent could continue sending invoices. A creator's system could continue publishing. An investment agent could continue operating within standing authority.

The appearance of continuity can mask the disappearance of the person whose judgment justified the authority.

The system should not interpret death as an invitation to keep the principal computationally present forever.

Authority needs succession.

Identity may need memorialization.

Memory needs disposition.

These are separate questions.

There is also a younger version of the same problem. Children and adolescents create data before they can realistically understand the future analytical power of the systems collecting it. A child's search history, school platform records, messages, photographs, game behavior, and AI conversations can become a longitudinal profile extending into adulthood.

The more capable later systems become at synthesizing those traces, the more consequential early accumulation can be.

A humane lifecycle should allow adulthood to create a boundary.

That does not mean every childhood record should disappear. Medical history, educational records, family archives, legal documents, and evidence of harm can have enduring value or legal retention requirements.

But the assumption that every behavioral trace collected from a minor deserves equal future salience is difficult to defend.

The question is not only consent at collection.

It is whether the later adult can revise the computational identity built before the adult existed as a legal and practical decision-maker.

This is another version of the right to become someone the system did not predict.

A serious counterargument comes from safety and accountability.

Bad actors also want to disappear.

A fraud network benefits when identities can be reset cheaply. A harasser benefits when abuse history cannot follow an account. A corrupt official benefits when public records lose salience. A company that caused harm benefits if records disappear before investigators arrive. A repeat offender can exploit systems that treat every new account as a complete fresh start.

The right to disappear can become the right to evade consequences if designed carelessly.

That risk is not incidental. It is the strongest reason the chapter must reject a universal deletion principle.

The appropriate boundary depends on who holds the record, why it exists, whose interests are implicated, what future use is proposed, and what harm deletion or continued use could create.

Sometimes continuity protects the vulnerable.

A platform may need to remember that a banned account was associated with serious abuse even if the user wants a fresh identity. A financial institution may need durable records to detect laundering or fraud. A professional regulator may need disciplinary history. A court may need to preserve public judgments.

These examples do not defeat the right to disappear.

They define it more accurately.

Disappearance should often mean ending ordinary participation, profiling, recommendation, or authority while preserving narrowly justified records under restricted purposes.

The institution can remember enough to enforce a boundary without continuing the relationship as though nothing ended.

This is similar to the distinction between a revoked credential and a deleted audit log. The system remembers that trust ended so that trust does not silently return.

The memory serves the ending.

That is different from using every preserved trace for future monetization, persuasion, or identity prediction.

Purpose limitation becomes the moral machinery of disappearance.

A person leaves.

The institution may keep what it must.

It should not quietly convert every remainder into a new relationship.

There is a practical product test for all of this.

Close an account and then ask what the company can still cause to happen to you.

Can it still target you?

Can it still recommend on the basis of your old behavior?

Can it still contact you beyond legally or operationally necessary messages?

Can old memories still shape a successor assistant?

Can the account still authorize action somewhere else?

Can a data broker or partner still receive fresh information generated from the old profile?

Can you understand what remains and why?

The quality of the exit is visible in those answers.

The test is more revealing than a delete button because it asks whether the relationship's power actually changed.

The expiration economy will need many such tests.

A system is not ended because the interface says goodbye.

It is ended when the rights, authority, memory, and obligations have changed into the states the ending promised.

For people, that promise carries unusual weight.

The object being retired is not merely a service.

It is one institution's claim on a human future.
