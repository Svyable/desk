# Zombie Organizations

Organizations are remarkably good at surviving the problem they were created to solve.

A committee is formed after a crisis. The crisis passes. The committee discovers adjacent concerns.

A task force becomes an office.

A pilot becomes a program.

A temporary reporting requirement becomes a quarterly ritual.

A product line loses strategic importance but retains customers, staff, internal systems, contracts, and an executive sponsor who can explain why the next year will be different.

The institution keeps moving.

Movement looks like life.

That is why the word zombie gets used so often in economics and management. Zombie firms, zombie projects, zombie processes, zombie meetings, zombie funds, zombie servers. The metaphor is imprecise but persistent because it captures a recognizable state: something no longer has a convincing future and still consumes resources as though the future were obvious.

The danger is using the label too quickly.

A company with weak profits today is not necessarily a zombie. A public agency that lacks a market test may still perform an essential function. A research program can appear unproductive for years before producing something valuable. A committee can preserve institutional coordination that is difficult to observe from outside. The long tail of usefulness is real.

The expiration problem is not how to identify everything that looks old and kill it.

It is how to distinguish continued purpose from continued motion.

Organizations make that distinction difficult because they can defend themselves.

A piece of software does not attend the budget meeting. A committee does.

Institutions produce narratives, metrics, constituencies, procedures, and careers. People learn the language of the program. Suppliers depend on it. Customers adapt. Internal status accumulates around ownership. Even when everyone agrees that the original mission changed, nobody approaches review as a neutral observer.

This is not corruption. It is what dependence looks like from inside.

A useful organization creates people with reasons to preserve it.

That is why expiration must be designed before preservation becomes the only constituency in the room.

Temporary organizations do this sometimes through charters. A task force can have a defined mission, membership, deliverable, and completion date. Project teams can dissolve when the project ships. special-purpose entities can exist for a transaction or asset. Campaign organizations can wind down after an election. Film productions assemble large temporary organizations and then disperse.

These structures prove that organizational death is not inherently failure.

Some organizations succeed by disappearing.

The confusion comes from management culture's preference for growth as proof of importance. A team that doubles head count looks successful. A department that hands its work to a durable operating process and shrinks can look weak. A program that needs more money has a story to tell. A program that solved the problem may be arguing for its own extinction.

Extinction has poor career incentives.

Imagine two managers.

The first grows a team from ten people to fifty, launches three new initiatives, and builds a visible internal brand.

The second automates routine work, eliminates duplicative processes, transfers a mature capability to another function, closes two outdated programs, and reduces the team from thirty people to twelve.

The second manager may have created more durable value.

The first manager looks bigger on the organization chart.

Organizations measure territory.

Expiration requires rewarding surrender.

This is one reason reorganizations so often move boxes without ending work. The company announces a new structure. Teams are renamed. Reporting lines change. Leaders leave. Responsibilities shift.

The old processes survive underneath because nobody had authority or time to remove them.

The reorganization becomes another layer.

Meetings continue because someone still expects them. Reports continue because no recipient wants to be the one who stops receiving information. Systems continue because migration was not part of the reorg plan. Policies continue because the legal review belongs to a different function.

The org chart changed faster than the organization.

This is the social version of a software rewrite that leaves the old database in production.

True organizational retirement has tail work.

Contracts must transfer or end.

Budgets must close.

Employees need new roles or exits handled responsibly.

Records need disposition.

Customers need continuity.

Authorities and approvals need reassignment.

Metrics should stop being produced if they no longer inform decisions.

Recurring meetings should stop appearing on calendars.

Systems should lose owners only after the systems themselves are transferred or retired.

The bureaucracy of death is real because an organization is a bundle of relationships, not a box on a chart.

This helps explain why acquisitions often produce persistence instead of consolidation. A buyer acquires a company partly for customers, technology, talent, distribution, or intellectual property. The acquisition thesis may assume overlapping systems and functions can be combined.

Then integration begins.

The target's billing system contains customer-specific rules. Its support team understands problems the buyer's team has never seen. Its data model does not map cleanly. Its contracts promise behavior the buyer's standard terms do not. Its brand still has value. Its employees fear losing autonomy. The buyer's own systems are not as universal as the deal model implied.

So both structures remain.

The expected synergies become an integration backlog.

Acquisitions are expensive lessons in the difference between legal ownership and operational unification.

A company can own two organizations long before it has one organization.

The same pattern appears after emergency responses. Crises create institutions rapidly because the cost of inaction is high. People accept duplication, unusual authority, manual work, temporary systems, and special reporting because speed matters.

After the crisis, temporary arrangements can persist because dismantling them receives less attention than creating them did.

This is understandable. The emergency created a visible objective. The post-emergency cleanup competes with the next objective.

Temporary institutions need post-emergency budgets.

Without them, the organization keeps paying smaller costs indefinitely.

This is one of the book's recurring themes: ending has to be funded.

A program cannot sunset if nobody pays to migrate beneficiaries.

A department cannot close if records and responsibilities remain ownerless.

A company cannot exit a product if the last customers have no replacement path.

A physical site cannot be decommissioned if the operating company spent everything on production and left no closure resources.

The elegant date is cheap.

The transition is expensive.

Zombie organizations often survive because the transition budget would expose the true historical cost of the original creation.

Suppose closing an internal system costs $3 million while leaving it alive costs $500,000 a year. The organization may defer the closure because the annual tax fits existing budgets while the retirement requires a special project. Over ten years the deferral costs more, but every annual decision can look locally sensible.

Organizations are bad at spending a large amount now to stop spending small amounts forever.

Capital budgeting favors beginnings because beginnings can be framed as investments.

Endings get framed as expenses.

The accounting language shapes strategy.

The same can happen with people, and here the moral boundary matters. An organization is not merely a portfolio of costs. Closing a facility can devastate a town. Ending a program can disrupt livelihoods. Consolidating teams can erase expertise and dignity if handled carelessly. A spreadsheet that shows long-run savings may ignore human transition costs that belong in the decision.

Graceful ending is not euphemism for ruthless downsizing.

A good expiration system makes obligations visible precisely so leaders cannot pretend subtraction is free.

Notice, severance, retraining, relocation, pension protection, benefit continuity, stakeholder consultation, and community effects can all be part of the real ending cost depending on the situation.

The book's argument becomes ethically hollow if it counts only the organization's burden and not the burden imposed on people.

That does not make continuation automatically right.

Keeping a failed organization alive can harm people too. Employees can spend years in a business with no investment or path forward. Public money can remain trapped in programs that no longer solve current problems. Customers can depend on products that receive minimal maintenance. Capital can be withheld from better uses. Younger workers can be denied opportunities because old structures never release authority.

Persistence distributes costs just as ending does.

The relevant question is not whether someone pays.

Someone always pays.

The question is whether the distribution is understood and justified.

Organizations sometimes solve this through periodic zero-based review: do not simply inherit last year's budget; justify activity again. The concept is appealing and difficult. Much institutional value does not fit a yearly proof exercise. Requiring every basic capability to reargue its existence can waste time and encourage short-term metrics.

A better expiration culture does not force constant existential theater.

It creates review where uncertainty is high and inertia is dangerous.

New programs can have earlier review points because their value is unproven.

Temporary crisis structures can have explicit transition triggers.

Mature essential functions can have longer horizons with monitoring appropriate to their risks.

Projects can have stop conditions tied to outcomes rather than only budgets.

Committees can close when the decision they were formed to make is completed.

The institution's lifetime should reflect the kind of uncertainty it contains.

Artificial intelligence will make organizational proliferation easier.

A person can already create a new channel, mailing list, dashboard, document repository, workflow, bot, or project space with a few clicks. Agents will make it possible to create whole micro-organizations around tasks: a set of agents assigned research, planning, negotiation, monitoring, coding, quality control, and reporting.

The cost of assembling a temporary synthetic team could approach zero.

That will be useful.

Then the synthetic organization will need to disappear.

Who closes the channels?

Who archives the output?

Who revokes the tools?

Who reconciles commitments made to outside parties?

Who decides which agent memories become part of the permanent organizational record?

Who terminates recurring jobs?

Who knows whether another workflow depends on the temporary team?

The future company may create organizations at the speed today's company creates documents.

Without expiration, its org chart will no longer be the main source of bureaucracy.

The bureaucracy will be invisible and executable.

Agents, automations, subscriptions, policies, credentials, memory stores, and recurring decisions can form durable institutional structures without a single employee being formally assigned.

That is a new kind of zombie risk.

The system keeps acting because action was scheduled.

No meeting is required.

No manager has to defend the process.

The automation persists beneath attention.

Human bureaucracy at least has birthdays, retirements, resignations, and reorganizations. Machine bureaucracy can survive all of them if nobody removes the job.

This changes the meaning of organizational hygiene.

Future leaders will need inventories not only of people and budgets but of delegated agency.

What processes can act?

Under whose authority?

For what purpose?

Until when?

What happens if the original sponsor leaves?

Can the process create successors or sub-agents?

What dependencies did it form?

What promises did it make?

These questions turn expiration into governance.

The organization that answers them well will be able to experiment aggressively because experiments will not automatically become permanent institutions.

That is the positive vision.

Temporary structures can be more ambitious when temporariness is credible.

A company can create a task force without fear it will become an eternal layer.

A government can use emergency capacity without normalizing emergency authority.

A research organization can spin up teams around hypotheses and dissolve them when evidence shifts.

A business can create hundreds of agentic workflows because the workflows carry lifetimes, ownership, and closure rules.

The ability to end increases willingness to begin.

We have seen this relationship in bankruptcy, contracts, products, and software.

Organizations are no different.

The best temporary institution is not the one that resists death heroically.

It is the one that can finish.

The long history of sunset proposals in government is useful here because it shows how quickly the idea of organizational expiration becomes more complicated than a deadline. The Government Accountability Office's analysis of proposed federal sunset legislation in the 1970s treated periodic review as an information and oversight problem. Review had to be scheduled, scoped, supported by evidence, and carried out by institutions capable of making decisions. Otherwise expiration could create a ritual without producing learning.

That same problem lives inside companies under gentler names: portfolio review, business-unit rationalization, application rationalization, operating-model redesign, cost transformation. The meeting is easy to schedule. The hard part is assembling enough knowledge to distinguish a weak quarter from a dead thesis.

A mature institution often looks inefficient to a reviewer because it contains capabilities whose value appears only during unusual events. Compliance expertise may be quiet until a rule changes. Incident-response capacity may look idle until a crisis. A laboratory may run many unproductive experiments before one result matters. A public-health capability can appear underutilized precisely when it succeeds.

Utilization is not purpose.

This is the institutional version of dead wood being habitat. Some apparent redundancy stores resilience.

The opposite is also true. An organization can learn to use its own continued activity as evidence of value. A report exists because a committee asks for it; the committee meets because the report gives it something to discuss. A program creates metrics measuring the volume of services it delivers, then treats rising volume as proof that the program should expand without asking whether the underlying problem is improving. A platform makes migration difficult and then cites its large installed base as evidence of indispensability.

Activity can become endogenous.

That makes a good review question different from "Are people using this?"

The better question is "What would happen if this stopped?"

The answer forces the organization to reveal whether the activity protects a real dependency, produces a valuable outcome, preserves an option, or merely keeps feeding itself. Sometimes the answer will strongly support continuation. A public institution may be so woven into rights and essential services that abrupt termination would be reckless. A team may carry tacit knowledge no replacement can reproduce cheaply. A mature program may have evolved far from its original mission and still be valuable in its current one.

Mission drift is not automatically failure.

Institutions are allowed to learn.

A task force created for one crisis may discover a durable coordination problem that deserves a permanent home. A research group can migrate from one scientific question to another because expertise compounds. A company can reinvent a product line rather than close it. The relevant failure is not change of mission. It is change without an explicit new bargain about purpose, authority, cost, and accountability.

That distinction matters because "stick to the original mission" can become as rigid as "never close anything." The world changes. Good institutions change with it.

The expiration discipline is not fidelity to origin.

It is legible renewal.

If a temporary structure deserves permanence, say so. Rewrite the charter. Reassign the budget. Clarify accountability. Stop pretending every extension is temporary. If a permanent organization has evolved into a new function, make the new function visible enough to evaluate on its own merits.

Perpetual temporariness is one of bureaucracy's strangest states. It avoids the scrutiny of permanence while enjoying the benefits of continuity.

AI could multiply this state dramatically. A manager may create a temporary agent team for a product launch. The launch ends, but one agent keeps monitoring customer feedback, another keeps updating a report, and a third retains credentials to renew a service. Six months later the agents are doing useful work, but no one has decided that this synthetic organization is permanent. Its authority grew through leftovers.

The correct response is not necessarily to shut it down.

It is to force a new organizational decision.

Who owns it now? What budget justifies it? What data can it retain? Which actions may it take? How will it be reviewed? What happens when its sponsor leaves? Which records should become institutional memory rather than agent memory?

An organization becomes less zombie-like not when it dies, but when its continued life can still be explained.

That is a much harder standard than age.
