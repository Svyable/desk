# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*The Expiration Economy* asks what becomes scarce when the cost of creating new things collapses.

The book's working claim is that modern institutions are optimized for beginnings and weak at endings. Software is deployed more easily than it is retired. Data is collected more easily than it is deleted. Organizations are created more deliberately than they are dissolved. Rules acquire constituencies after their original purpose fades. Infrastructure can keep imposing costs long after it stops producing value. Generative AI intensifies the imbalance because it lowers the cost of producing code, text, agents, workflows, decisions, records, and synthetic artifacts while leaving the future work of inspecting, maintaining, securing, migrating, archiving, revoking, or destroying them largely intact.

The thesis is not that short life is inherently good, that durability is obsolete, or that every system should carry a timer. Some records, institutions, buildings, protocols, relationships, and works deserve preservation. The stronger and more falsifiable claim is that **systems perform better when they distinguish deliberate durability from accidental persistence and make the cost, authority, and procedure of ending visible before the end arrives.**

The economic version of the thesis is that as initiation becomes cheaper, the ability to stop, unwind, transfer, decommission, erase, or safely abandon becomes a more valuable capability. That proposition should be tested rather than assumed.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Accumulation:** In domains where creating assets, records, rules, or services becomes cheaper, does the stock of legacy obligations actually rise faster than retirement capacity?
2. **Tail cost:** Do obsolete or low-value systems impose measurable maintenance, security, compliance, environmental, staffing, or opportunity costs after their primary usefulness declines?
3. **Designed endings:** Do explicit expiration dates, retention schedules, decommissioning plans, migration paths, revocation mechanisms, discharge processes, or sunset clauses improve outcomes compared with indefinite persistence?
4. **Premature endings:** Where do forced expiration, aggressive deletion, planned obsolescence, or rapid deprecation destroy value, rights, evidence, continuity, safety, or trust?
5. **Durability:** Which systems should be designed to last, and what differentiates legitimate permanence from inertia?
6. **Transfer and inheritance:** What obligations survive a founder, employee, owner, vendor, model, product, or institution, and who acquires authority and liability when the original steward disappears?
7. **AI retirement:** Can AI systems, agents, credentials, memories, datasets, and delegated authorities be retired without leaving active dependencies or orphaned decision rights?
8. **Market formation:** Are businesses, professions, standards, and regulatory regimes emerging around migration, decommissioning, data disposition, digital estates, secure deletion, lifecycle assurance, and cleanup?

## What would make the thesis false?

The book should change materially if the evidence shows that persistence is usually cheaper than retirement, that modern systems already internalize end-of-life costs effectively, or that explicit expiration mechanisms routinely produce more harm than indefinite continuity.

Specific counterevidence to preserve:

- Long-lived infrastructure can create enormous value precisely because replacement is expensive and continuity matters.
- Compatibility with old systems can protect vulnerable users and small organizations that cannot migrate on a vendor's preferred timetable.
- Records retained for legal rights, historical accountability, scientific reproducibility, safety, or cultural memory may become more valuable with age.
- Sunset clauses can produce performative review followed by automatic reauthorization rather than genuine evaluation.
- Deleting data can frustrate audits, litigation, fraud detection, public accountability, medical continuity, or the rights of other people represented in the same record.
- Rapid deprecation can transfer costs from a powerful vendor to customers who have little bargaining power.
- Ecological disturbance is context-dependent; fire, decay, and turnover are not universal prescriptions for human institutions.
- Apoptosis and other biological turnover mechanisms are analogies, not evidence that companies, laws, or software should behave like cells.
- Bankruptcy provides a structured fresh start only within specific legal rules and exceptions; it is not a moral license to discard every obligation.
- AI systems may require unusually long retention of logs, evaluations, model versions, and incident evidence for accountability even when the deployed service is retired.
- Founder dependence can sometimes be economically efficient; dispersing exceptional judgment into process can destroy value rather than preserve it.
- Physical reversibility, modularity, and design for disassembly can add cost, material, interfaces, and unused optionality.
- Platform legacy mechanisms can solve many ordinary digital-estate transitions without requiring a new institutional category.
- Mature identity and access-management controls may absorb much of the agent-offboarding problem if autonomous systems remain ephemeral, scoped, and enumerable.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Software, protocols, and security

Trace the lifecycle of legacy software, protocol deprecation, dependency retirement, credential revocation, compatibility burdens, migration, and the security costs of supporting old versions. RFC 8996 is a useful concrete case because it makes the maintenance and attack-surface argument for retiring TLS 1.0 and 1.1 while acknowledging interoperability costs for systems that remain on old versions.

### II. Data, records, and forgetting

Separate deletion, sanitization, archival preservation, legal retention, and human forgetting. Use GDPR Article 17 as a bounded legal example rather than a universal right to deletion. Use National Archives records scheduling to show that serious retention systems distinguish permanent records from temporary ones and specify disposition events instead of relying on "keep it until nobody cares."

### III. Physical infrastructure and end-of-life liability

Use nuclear decommissioning, electronic waste, design for adaptability and disassembly, and other long-tail infrastructure cases to examine what happens when the productive life of an asset ends but cost and responsibility continue. Distinguish funded decommissioning from abandonment and avoid treating all infrastructure as disposable. EPA design-for-disassembly guidance is useful because it contains both positive design practices and an explicit recognition that full deconstruction is not always feasible.

### IV. Biology and ecology

Use apoptosis, regulated cell death, prescribed fire, decomposition, and ecological succession only to sharpen questions about turnover. Preserve the limits of analogy. The point is not that nature "proves" expiration; it is that many living systems allocate real mechanisms to removal, recycling, and renewal.

### V. Law, finance, institutions, and succession

Study sunset provisions, repeal, bankruptcy discharge, liquidation, reorganization, dissolution, founder dependence, and institutional succession. Ask when formal endings make systems more adaptive and when they merely create procedural theater or shift losses onto weaker parties. Treat public-company key-person disclosures as evidence that person-bound dependence can be economically material, not as proof that founder leadership is inherently defective.

### VI. Products, customers, and market power

Follow end-of-life support, stranded customers, interoperability, export, repair, data portability, and vendor shutdowns. The critical question is who pays the tail cost when a producer wants to leave before its customers do.

### VII. Artificial intelligence, identity, and autonomous agents

Treat decommissioning as part of the AI lifecycle. NIST AI 600-1 explicitly includes safe decommissioning and identifies dependencies, retention/security, open-source components, and user attachment as considerations. Extend the inquiry to persistent agent identities, delegated credentials, scheduled tasks, memories, and downstream systems that may survive a model or vendor transition. Use NIST account-lifecycle controls to separate revoking access from reconciling consequences that survive the credential.

### VIII. The cleanup and succession economy

Look for observable demand around cloud and software migration, secure media sanitization, records disposition, e-waste processing, decommissioning, data lifecycle management, digital estates, AI governance, organizational succession, and physical adaptation. Avoid unsupported market-size claims. The relevant test is whether end-of-life work becomes a distinct source of cost, specialization, purchasing, and competitive advantage as creation grows cheaper.

## Source discipline

- Prefer statutes, regulators, standards bodies, government datasets, original academic papers, technical standards, court materials, SEC filings, and first-party lifecycle documentation.
- Keep legal claims jurisdiction-specific. A right to erasure in the GDPR is not a global right to disappear.
- Distinguish deletion from sanitization, de-indexing, revocation, archival transfer, and physical destruction.
- Distinguish a product's announced end-of-life date from the actual disappearance of installed systems and customer dependence.
- Distinguish credential invalidation from reconciliation of downstream tasks, commitments, and copied state.
- Distinguish digital property from private communications and from continuing authority to act in another person's name.
- Avoid invented market-size numbers for cleanup, decommissioning, data lifecycle, or AI retirement.
- Never use biology or ecology as proof of a social prescription. Use them to expose mechanisms and limits.
- Preserve cases where old systems continue to create net value and where rapid retirement causes avoidable harm.
- Verify every quotation against the original source. Prefer paraphrase when the exact wording is not necessary.
- For every chapter, record a specific answer to: **What evidence would force this chapter's argument to change?**

## Publication research gate

Before the book moves from `Drafting` to `Complete draft`:

1. Every chapter has at least one checkable primary or high-quality research source for its concrete factual claims, or in a synthesis chapter every concrete example traces to an already sourced earlier chapter.
2. Every chapter brief contains explicit counterevidence or a falsification test.
3. Quantitative claims in the prose can be traced to the source ledger.
4. No metaphor from biology, ecology, bankruptcy, or technical standards is presented as proof outside its domain.
5. No chapter relies on a fabricated quotation, composite anecdote, or unsupported historical detail.
6. The manuscript meets the Desk length standard through additional evidence and cases, not repetition.
