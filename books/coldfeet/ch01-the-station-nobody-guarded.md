# The Station Nobody Guarded

The Soviet Union left the door open because there was no door worth locking.

NP-8 was not a building in the usual sense. It was a settlement assembled on a moving geological accident: a drifting platform of Arctic ice carrying huts, instruments, radio equipment, scientific work and men who had learned to regard latitude as an address. Its runway existed only while the ice agreed to remain an airfield. When the ice stopped agreeing, the station's strategic geometry changed quickly. A place that could be supplied by aircraft became a place that could kill the people waiting to be supplied.

The Soviets evacuated.

This is the moment intelligence histories tend to hurry past because the next part contains an airplane with a hook on its nose. But abandonment was the invention that made COLDFEET possible. The Americans did not penetrate NP-8 while Soviet personnel defended it. They waited for physics to defeat the perimeter.

In 1961 American observers had noticed another abandoned Soviet drift station, NP-9. The Office of Naval Research saw an opportunity. The United States and Soviet Union were both learning how to operate in an Arctic transformed by nuclear submarines. Under the ice, a submarine could hide from many of the systems designed to find it in open water. Acoustics mattered. Ice mattered. The strange layered soundscape between seabed, water and frozen ceiling mattered. So did the practical knowledge accumulated by scientists who had spent years measuring a place most military planners knew mainly as blank white space on a map.

The first target drifted out of reach before the plan could mature. Then another station was abandoned.

NP-8 was better.

According to the CIA's later institutional account, the Office of Naval Research pursued the opportunity until its funding ran out, after which CIA took over. The eventual team joined military scientific interest to intelligence logistics: Major James F. Smith of the Air Force and Lieutenant (junior grade) Leonard A. LeSchack of the Naval Reserve would go onto the ice. A CIA proprietary, Intermountain Aviation, supplied a modified B-17 and pilots Connie Seigrist and Douglas Price. The extraction problem would be handled by Robert Fulton's surface-to-air recovery system, the Skyhook.

That list of organizations is easy to read as bureaucratic clutter. It is actually the operation's first important clue. COLDFEET did not begin because someone at CIA woke up wanting Soviet souvenirs. The target sat at an overlap. Naval researchers understood why the station might matter. Intelligence officers could fund and conceal a mission. Aviators could reach a place ordinary recovery methods could not. Fulton had spent years making the last absurd step—picking a person off the ground with a moving airplane—something closer to engineering than circus work.

On May 28, 1962, Smith and LeSchack parachuted onto NP-8.

The phrase is almost too clean. A parachute insertion onto a drifting ice station makes the Arctic sound like terrain. It was terrain only provisionally. The station existed because a sufficiently large piece of frozen ocean had been treated as infrastructure. Everything the men intended to inspect sat on a foundation that was migrating while degrading.

They had been given roughly seventy-two hours.

What does one steal from an abandoned laboratory?

The question is different from what to steal from an occupied one. In an occupied facility, the most valuable objects may be locked, watched or destroyed before capture. In a hurriedly abandoned facility, value has been sorted by the adversary under pressure. People take what they believe they need. They leave what they believe they can replace, what is too heavy, what seems routine, what has lost immediate importance, what they do not have time to classify as revealing.

That last category is intelligence gold.

A single exotic instrument can be less informative than twenty ordinary objects sitting in their working relationships. Cable runs reveal what talks to what. Spare parts reveal expected failures. Calibration sheets reveal tolerances. Logbooks reveal what operators bothered to measure every day. Empty racks can identify what was important enough to evacuate. Trash can reveal consumption. A station is an argument about priorities expressed in wood, wire, paper and habit.

Smith and LeSchack photographed the facility and collected documents, samples and equipment. CIA's public history puts the recovered haul at more than 150 pounds. Later agency accounts say it yielded information about Soviet Arctic research, including work on acoustic detection of under-ice American submarines and anti-submarine warfare techniques.

Those two sentences require different levels of confidence.

The recovery is well documented. The exact intelligence value is harder. Institutions writing their own histories have incentives to remember successful operations as consequential. Participants do too, although that does not make their recollections false. The useful question is not whether CIA says COLDFEET produced valuable intelligence. It plainly does. The useful question is what the recovered objects allowed analysts to know that they did not already know, how quickly that knowledge moved into American programs, and whether surviving records permit the claim to be tested.

That distinction will recur throughout this book because espionage stories are unusually vulnerable to the halo of access. Once we learn that an intelligence service obtained something secretly, we tend to assume the thing was important. Difficulty becomes a proxy for value. A dangerous collection operation feels as though it ought to have produced dangerous knowledge.

Sometimes it produces a filing cabinet.

A filing cabinet can be enough.

The Americans had their own drift-station experience. Naval researchers were not encountering Soviet Arctic science as primitives finding a mysterious machine. They had hypotheses about what Soviet researchers might be doing because the United States was wrestling with related problems. This is what made NP-8 useful. Intelligence is often less about encountering the unimaginable than about resolving uncertainty between several technically plausible possibilities.

Was a Soviet acoustic installation merely scientific oceanography? Was it supporting submarine detection? How mature was the instrumentation? Which frequencies, arrangements, recording practices or environmental assumptions interested the researchers? Were the Soviets solving the same under-ice problems Americans were solving, or had they found a different path?

The abandoned station could answer some of those questions because physical systems are bad at maintaining cover stories after their operators leave.

Paper can lie. Equipment has fewer options.

That sentence needs qualification too. Hardware is not self-interpreting. A component found at a site does not prove how it was used. A prototype does not prove deployment. A notebook does not prove a program succeeded. The history of intelligence exploitation is full of objects that acquired grander biographies after capture than they possessed before it. Chain of custody matters. Context matters. Analysts can overfit an adversary's debris as enthusiastically as civilians can.

But material has one virtue testimony lacks: it can sometimes be interrogated again.

A witness ages. An institution edits its memory. A recovered circuit can be measured. A document can be translated by another linguist. A component's manufacturer can be identified. A photograph can reveal something the original collector did not notice. Intelligence captured from a physical site can outlive the assumptions of the people who captured it.

This is one reason foreign materiel exploitation has such a long history. States collect enemy aircraft, radars, missiles, submarines, weapons, sensors and documents because possession changes the epistemic problem. Estimating a machine from reports is different from putting calipers on it. The Cold War generated elaborate systems for acquiring precisely those objects an adversary had no intention of supplying.

COLDFEET is the miniature version, almost comic in scale compared with later projects. There was no purpose-built deep-ocean ship masquerading as a mining venture. No billion-dollar recovery claw. No satellite constellation. Two men walked through a station that had been left to the Arctic and decided what to carry.

The extraction made the miniature operation cinematic.

Fulton's system used a balloon to raise a line from the ground. The aircraft approached low and fast enough to make every ordinary instinct about boarding an airplane irrelevant. A yoke on the aircraft caught the line. The person or cargo attached below was pulled from the surface and streamed behind the airplane before being winched aboard.

It had been tested. That did not make it gentle.

When the B-17 returned for the COLDFEET team, weather had already delayed recovery. CIA's account describes dense fog, poor visibility and winds around thirty knots at the surface. The aircraft first recovered the bag of collected intelligence. Then it came for LeSchack.

The wind dragged him hundreds of feet across the ice before the pickup. He had oriented himself incorrectly relative to the wind and had to correct his position. Smith also struggled against being dragged before his turn. The machine worked in the peculiar sense that matters most to engineers: the men ended up inside the airplane.

There is a photographically perfect version of this story in which audacity conquers environment. It is the version intelligence agencies understandably like to display. A B-17, an ice sheet, a line into the sky, men doing something that looks impossible until the frame after they have done it.

The less glamorous version is more useful.

A Soviet logistics failure created an American collection opportunity. An American research office recognized it. A funding problem nearly killed it. A new target appeared. A covert aviation network solved reach. An experimental recovery technology solved extraction. Weather nearly broke the timetable. Human beings improvised through the parts the engineering diagrams had tidied away. Then analysts had to determine whether 150 pounds of other people's abandoned work justified any of it.

That is intelligence in its natural state: not omniscience, but a chain of temporary advantages that can fail independently.

The Soviets had not intended to give the United States access to NP-8. They also had not built NP-8 to survive indefinitely without them. Those facts collided.

Secrecy usually imagines an adversary approaching the front door. Guard the document. Encrypt the message. fence the facility. Compartment the program. But every technical system has other boundaries. It has a maintenance boundary, a disposal boundary, a transportation boundary, a failure boundary. Aircraft crash. Submarines sink. Test articles wash ashore. Balloons descend. Laboratories evacuate. Contractors keep copies. Equipment is sold as surplus. Waste leaves secure sites in trucks driven by people who may not know what produced it.

Abandonment is one of those boundaries.

It is not automatically revelation. The Soviets did not leave a complete map of their military intentions under a coffee mug. The stronger proposition is narrower: the security model changes when the people who understand why an object matters are separated from the object itself.

NP-8 became legible because it became ownerless for a moment.

Not legally ownerless. Not politically ownerless. Operationally ownerless. Nobody was there to decide which drawer an American should not open.

The Arctic supplied the temporary permission.

For seven days in 1962, two Americans occupied that interval between Soviet evacuation and physical destruction. They photographed what they could, carried what they could, and waited for an airplane to pluck them out of a place that was disappearing underneath them.

The story is usually told as proof that the CIA could get men out of anywhere.

I am more interested in why anyone thought it was worth putting them there.
