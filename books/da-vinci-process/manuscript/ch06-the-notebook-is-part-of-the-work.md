# The Notebook Is Part of the Work

Leonardo's notebooks are usually presented as evidence of range.

Flying machines. Water. Anatomy. Fortifications. Optics. Geometry. Faces. Plants. Devices whose purpose is still debated. Pages so crowded with mirrored writing and drawings that the modern viewer experiences the pleasant shock of standing near a mind that seems unwilling to remain inside one category.

Range is the obvious thing to admire.

The more important thing is that the pages exist.

Leonardo externalized thought.

He drew to see. He wrote to hold questions still. He returned to problems across time. The Royal Collection describes drawing as the activity that unified his diverse interests: he used it to devise projects, record observations, explore ideas, and think. The Codex Atlanticus preserves an enormous body of drawings and notes spanning engineering, mechanics, architecture, mathematics, and other concerns.

The notebook was not storage after thinking.

It was part of thinking.

That distinction matters because our first interfaces with generative AI encouraged the opposite habit.

Open a blank chat.

Ask a question.

Receive an answer.

Close the chat.

The interaction feels intelligent because each turn is fluent. But a serious body of work cannot live comfortably inside isolated conversations. It has history. Decisions made three weeks ago constrain decisions today. A discarded approach matters because someone will rediscover it. Sources accumulate. Terms acquire local meanings. Exceptions become more important than the general rule. The project develops scars.

A blank chat has none of them.

This is why the next important step in human-agent collaboration is not a more eloquent model.

It is memory with structure.

Not merely machine memory. Shared working memory.

The workshop needs a place where the human and the agents can encounter the same evolving object.

For a book, that object includes the manuscript, research trail, chapter architecture, unresolved questions, source standards, rights constraints, prior edits, and the passages that establish the author's voice. For a software system, it includes code, tests, design decisions, incidents, deployment constraints, and conventions that may look arbitrary until their history is known. For a company, it includes goals, customers, metrics, contracts, failures, and the reasons one attractive strategy was abandoned.

Without this substrate, every agent interaction begins by reconstructing the room.

That reconstruction is expensive.

Worse, it is lossy.

People are already familiar with the human version. A new employee joins and asks why a system works a certain way. Nobody remembers. Someone says, "We tried that before," but cannot find the postmortem. A policy survives after the event that created it has disappeared from institutional memory. The organization becomes a building full of doors whose keys exist but whose purposes do not.

Agents can make this problem much worse because they increase the rate of change.

If one developer can produce ten times as many code changes, the decision history grows faster. If one researcher can examine a hundred sources, the evidence trail expands. If one executive can generate dozens of scenarios, the organization can make more decisions before anyone has time to metabolize the last ones.

Speed creates memory pressure.

The natural response is to ask the agent to remember everything.

That is not enough.

Everything is not memory.

Everything is an archive.

Useful memory requires selection.

Leonardo's pages make this visible. They are not a perfect transcript of his mind. They contain what he chose to put down: a drawing, a measurement, a question, a phrase, a diagram, a problem to revisit. The act of externalizing something changed its status. It became available for return.

A modern workshop needs the same discipline.

What deserves to survive the session?

This question is surprisingly hard because agent systems make recording cheap. One could preserve every conversation, intermediate file, tool call, draft, test output, critique, and branch. Storage is not the constraint.

Attention is.

The future master will need a theory of durable context.

Some information belongs in the permanent record because the cost of forgetting is high: the source of a factual claim, the reason a safety constraint exists, the decision that defines a product's architecture, the customer's stated requirement, the legal interpretation that governs the work.

Some information belongs in temporary scratch space: discarded phrasings, routine transformations, obvious failed attempts.

Some information deserves a warning label because it is unresolved rather than decided.

And some should be deliberately forgotten.

This last category matters. Memory can become clutter. A project that preserves every past assumption as if it were sacred becomes unable to change. Agents are especially vulnerable to inherited context because whatever is in the working set can become evidence of what should continue.

A mature workshop needs both memory and revision.

The notebook should remember that the decision existed.

It should also remember when the decision stopped being true.

This is where version control, one of software's great cultural inventions, becomes a model for knowledge work more generally. A version-controlled project does not merely preserve the current file. It preserves change as a first-class event. One can ask what changed, when, by whom, and often why.

Books deserve this.

Strategies deserve this.

Research deserves this.

Policies deserve this.

Agentic work will require it.

Consider the difference between an assistant that sees only the current draft of a plan and one that can see the decision history. The first may reopen a rejected path because it looks plausible. The second can learn that the path was rejected because a regulatory assumption failed, then ask whether that assumption still holds.

The second assistant is not necessarily more intelligent.

It is standing in a better workshop.

This is a crucial point because discussions of AI capability tend to focus on models. Models matter enormously. But once capability becomes broadly available, differences in local context can dominate.

Two organizations can use the same model and receive different outcomes because one has preserved a high-quality record of what it knows.

This record becomes a kind of proprietary cognitive infrastructure.

Not proprietary because it is secret in every case. Proprietary because it is specific.

A generic model may know everything public about an industry and nothing about the Tuesday in 2024 when your largest customer threatened to leave over a workflow nobody had documented. It may know the official architecture and not know which service has to be restarted manually after a specific failure. It may know the published research and not know which experiment your lab already ran unsuccessfully.

Local memory is where general intelligence becomes particular intelligence.

The notebook is therefore part of the work.

This sounds obvious once stated, but many organizations treat documentation as the thing people do after work if time remains. The incentives are almost perfectly designed to produce amnesia. Shipping is visible. Documentation is delayed value. The person writing the record often receives little immediate benefit because she already knows what happened.

Agents change the economics.

They can lower the cost of capturing context. A system can summarize a decision meeting, extract the assumptions, link the sources, record the rejected alternatives, and update a project history. It can compare a new proposal with old incidents. It can notice that two teams are solving the same problem under different names.

But again, automation is not the same as judgment.

A machine-generated record can be exquisitely useless.

It can preserve everything at the same level of importance. It can convert uncertainty into confident summary. It can omit the sentence everyone in the room understood was politically decisive because the sentence sounded informal. It can record the decision and miss the dissent that later turns out to have been right.

The human has to decide what the record means.

This is another form of mastery that looks clerical until the future depends on it.

Francesco Melzi understood something like this, though under radically different circumstances.

Melzi was a pupil and close associate of Leonardo who followed him in his later years and inherited his papers after Leonardo's death. The National Gallery emphasizes how important Melzi was to the survival of Leonardo's notebooks and drawings. That inheritance eventually became dispersed, but the simple fact of preservation changed what later centuries could know about Leonardo.

Think about the asymmetry.

A painting can survive as an object even if the process disappears.

An idea that remains only in a dead person's head cannot.

Leonardo's notebooks are famous partly because so much of his private process happened to become historical material. We can see the studies, the reconsiderations, the questions, the machines that were never built, the anatomy that was not published. His unfinished cognition became legible.

Modern agentic work will generate vastly more process and may preserve far less meaning.

The raw volume could be enormous: chat histories, branches, generated drafts, searches, code suggestions, evaluations, autonomous actions. Future researchers could theoretically know more about how a twenty-first-century project developed than we know about any Renaissance workshop.

They may instead find a landfill.

The difference will be curation.

A good workshop turns traces into institutional memory.

This has practical implications for how humans should work with agents today.

Do not make the model the only place a decision exists.

Do not let a chat transcript become the canonical specification.

Do not assume that because the agent can retrieve a document, the document contains the reason that matters.

Do not treat generated summaries as replacements for primary evidence.

Do not preserve a final answer without the source trail when the source trail will be needed to audit the answer.

And do not confuse the model's ability to remember a preference with a project's need to remember its history.

Preferences are convenient.

History is structural.

The most powerful human-agent systems will probably have several kinds of memory operating at once. There will be a current working set: what is relevant now. There will be durable project records. There will be source archives. There will be histories of decisions and failures. There may be personal models of how a particular collaborator thinks, what errors recur, what standards matter.

This sounds more complicated than chat because it is more complicated than chat.

A workshop is complicated.

The simplification will come at the interface. The person should not have to manually curate every bit. Agents can propose what to remember, connect records, surface conflicts, and compress old material. But the human must be able to inspect and correct the memory because a wrong memory can become a self-reinforcing instruction.

Imagine an agent that records, "The client prioritizes speed over accuracy."

Maybe that was true for one emergency. Maybe it was never true and emerged from a badly summarized meeting. If the statement becomes durable context, future agents will use it to justify a thousand decisions.

Memory can become policy without anyone voting.

The workshop therefore needs provenance for memory itself.

Where did this belief come from?

When was it last confirmed?

Who can change it?

What contradicts it?

These questions are not exotic. Databases have schemas. Science has citations. Courts have records. Aviation has maintenance logs. Medicine has charts. Institutions already understand that persistent action requires persistent evidence.

Agent systems are arriving faster than our memory architecture.

The organizations that fix that gap will have an advantage that looks, from the outside, like smarter AI.

It will actually be better continuity.

Continuity matters for learning too.

If an agent does not know what mistakes a person made last month, it cannot help that person develop. If the human cannot see which parts of a project the agent has repeatedly mishandled, trust never becomes calibrated. Every task resets the relationship.

A real apprenticeship does not reset.

The master remembers where the apprentice is weak.

The apprentice remembers the master's corrections.

Both carry history into the next attempt.

Current AI systems do not need to be conscious for an analogous loop to become useful. They need persistent, inspectable state tied to work.

That state changes the economics of collaboration because it reduces the cost of re-explanation. A person can build up a workshop over months and years. Examples accumulate. Standards sharpen. Agents inherit the corpus. The marginal interaction begins from a higher floor.

This is one reason the most consequential unit of AI adoption may eventually be the project rather than the person.

An individual subscription says little about whether intelligence compounds. A well-kept project can teach every future participant, human or machine, what earlier participants discovered.

The project starts to have memory beyond its members.

Organizations already aspire to this and usually fail.

Agents might make it easier.

They might also make failure less visible because an agent can synthesize a plausible answer from incomplete records, giving the impression that the institution remembers when it is merely improvising.

The distinction will matter most under stress.

During normal operations, improvisation can look like memory. In an incident, a lawsuit, a scientific dispute, a safety review, or a leadership transition, people need to know what actually happened.

The notebook becomes evidence.

This is where Leonardo's pages acquire a second meaning for our argument. They are not only instruments of thought. They are a chain of custody across time.

The page outlived the session.

It outlived the project.

It outlived Leonardo.

We can see a hand trying to understand because someone preserved the traces.

A human-agent renaissance will produce astonishing quantities of intellectual trace. The challenge is to keep enough of it coherent that the next mind can stand on it.

That next mind may be a person.

It may be an agent.

Usually it will be both.

The workshop begins to compound when neither has to begin again.
