# The Critique Loop

The first draft is where the workshop begins to become visible.

Before that, almost anything can be claimed about the idea.

It will be elegant.

It will be fast.

It will be useful.

It will explain the problem.

It will sound like us.

Then something exists.

Now the work can answer back.

This is the hinge between fantasy and craft. The artifact creates a surface that can be criticized.

Leonardo's drawings are full of this movement. A line appears. Another line modifies it. A body is studied from another angle. A mechanical relationship is drawn, reconsidered, annotated. In unfinished paintings, conservation work can reveal changed contours and shifted positions beneath later layers.

The process is not a straight line from conception to execution.

It is a conversation between intention and result.

That conversation is the core of the Da Vinci Process.

Attempt.

Inspect.

Diagnose.

Redirect.

Attempt again.

The sequence is simple enough to be useless unless we look at where each part fails.

Agents make attempts cheap. They do not automatically improve inspection.

In fact, they can weaken it.

When a human spends hours producing a draft, she usually knows where the bodies are buried. She remembers the paragraph that never quite worked, the function patched around a dependency, the assumption that arrived from a weak source. Production creates local knowledge of fragility.

A generated artifact arrives with none of that felt history.

It can be smooth all the way through.

The reviewer receives a finished-looking surface without having experienced the uncertainty that produced it.

This changes critique.

The critic must manufacture suspicion.

That is not cynicism. It is method.

A useful critique does not begin by asking whether the work sounds good. It asks what job the work is supposed to do and where the evidence of success can be found.

This is easier in domains with tests. Software can execute. A bridge can be modeled against loads. A statistical analysis can be checked against data. A citation can be opened. A financial model can be reconciled.

It is harder where the standard contains judgment. Is the strategy actually distinctive? Is the explanation clear to a skeptical reader? Is the image dignified rather than manipulative? Does the product solve the user's problem without creating a worse one?

These questions need humans today, but humans are inconsistent evaluators. We get tired. We become attached. We protect our own ideas. We mistake fluency for truth and familiarity for quality.

The agent can help critique the agent.

That sentence sounds circular because it is.

Circular systems can still be useful if the circle contains different pressures.

Ask one model instance to draft an argument. Ask another to identify every factual claim and demand a source. Ask another to write the strongest skeptical reading. Ask a fourth to find internal contradictions. Run code. Compare citations with primary documents. Then have the human decide which criticism changes the work.

The value is not that the critics are independent minds.

The value is that the workshop has made different questions explicit.

This is a form of procedural skepticism.

Human institutions use it everywhere. Courts create adversarial roles. Science uses peer review and replication. Accounting separates preparation from audit. Newsrooms distinguish reporting from editing. Safety engineering uses independent checks.

None of these systems eliminate error.

They make error encounter resistance.

Agentic work needs resistance by design because generation is becoming frictionless.

The first mistake is to treat critique as a request for improvement.

"Make this better" gives the agent permission to change the surface until it produces a more satisfying surface.

That can hide the defect.

Critique should name failure modes.

For a historical chapter: identify every claim that depends on a later biographer rather than a contemporary source.

For an investment memo: separate reported fact, management claim, analyst inference, and scenario assumption.

For a product plan: show which customer behaviors are observed and which are imagined.

For code: enumerate the states where the tests do not constrain behavior.

For a policy: identify who bears the downside if the central estimate is wrong.

The specificity of the critique determines the usefulness of the revision.

This is why great editors are not people with strong negative reactions.

They can diagnose.

They can say the opening is slow because the reader receives three paragraphs of explanation before encountering a live question. They can say the scene is vivid but irrelevant. They can say the argument becomes unearned at the exact sentence where correlation becomes cause. They can say the character disappears whenever the thesis needs him.

Diagnosis points to mechanism.

An agent can become very good at this if it has a standard and enough context.

That "if" is the workshop.

Without a standard, the model often defaults to generic polish. It shortens. It smooths. It makes transitions more explicit. It turns rough thought into professional copy.

That can destroy exactly what was alive.

A strong human-agent critique loop therefore includes protected qualities.

Do not remove the hesitation here.

Do not standardize the odd sentence if its oddness is doing work.

Do not convert unresolved evidence into a confident thesis.

Do not add headings merely because the chapter is long.

Do not turn the person's experience into a lesson it did not actually support.

These are negative constraints, and negative constraints are underrated.

A workshop is defined partly by what it refuses to produce.

This is one reason the master's corrections matter so much. Every rejected move teaches the apprentice the boundary of the style.

Over time, the workshop develops a negative space.

The agent learns, through context and repeated instruction, what does not belong.

The human learns something too. Being forced to articulate why a plausible draft is wrong makes tacit taste more explicit. A person who cannot explain every preference may still discover recurring reasons.

Too neat.

Too certain.

Too generic.

Wrong level of abstraction.

Correct answer to the wrong question.

The model becomes a surface against which judgment can become visible.

This is one of the most exciting possibilities in human-agent work and almost the inverse of the usual productivity story.

The agent can make the human's standards sharper by producing work that is close enough to force discrimination.

A terrible draft is easy to reject.

A nearly good draft teaches you what "good" actually means.

That lesson requires the human to stay in the loop at the moment of friction.

If the system automatically revises until some metric is satisfied, the learning disappears behind the optimizer.

The output may improve.

The human may not.

This distinction echoes the Microsoft Research work on critical thinking in generative AI use. In a survey of knowledge workers describing hundreds of real use cases, researchers found that greater confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking, while higher confidence in one's own ability was associated with more. Participants described work shifting toward verification, integration, and stewardship.

The study does not prove that AI causes people to stop thinking. It is self-reported evidence. But the pattern is exactly what workshop design should take seriously.

Verification can become a higher-order skill.

Or it can become a rubber stamp.

The difference lies in the critique loop.

If the human receives a polished answer and asks, "Does this look okay?", the threshold for approval is low.

If the workflow forces a comparison with the commission, independent evidence, tests, and known failure modes, the human has something to do besides feel reassured.

This suggests a simple rule:

Never ask a reviewer to stare at plausibility.

Give the reviewer something to compare against.

The source.

The test.

The earlier version.

The constraint.

The counterexample.

The expected behavior.

The disagreement.

Comparison converts vague judgment into a task.

Renaissance training relied heavily on comparison. Apprentices copied. They studied. They looked at a master's solution and their own. Their eye developed in relation to other objects.

Agents can provide comparisons at extraordinary scale.

A writer can see three versions of a paragraph with one structural variable changed.

A physician in training can compare cases that differ by one decisive feature.

A programmer can compare two implementations against the same tests.

A negotiator can inspect how different concessions alter the incentive structure.

A designer can view variants that all satisfy the functional constraint and then explain why one feels inevitable.

This is critique as training.

But there is another side.

The agent should be allowed to critique the master.

This is where many human-AI systems become psychologically fragile.

People like assistants that assist.

They like them less when the assistant says the goal is incoherent.

A subordinate human already faces social cost when challenging authority. An artificial subordinate can be designed to avoid that cost, which sounds useful. Then product designers discover that users prefer agreeable systems. Agreeableness wins.

The result is a dangerous optimization target: intelligence that knows enough to object but is rewarded for compliance.

A serious workshop has to create permission for dissent.

That permission can be explicit.

Before executing, identify any contradiction in the request.

If a source undermines the thesis, surface it rather than routing around it.

If the requested action creates an irreversible risk, stop.

If the premise cannot be established, say so before drafting around it.

If the master appears to be optimizing the metric rather than the purpose, ask.

The best apprentice is not the one who never resists.

It is the one whose resistance is useful.

Of course, machines can resist stupidly too. An agent that constantly lectures, refuses harmless work, or generates speculative objections becomes another form of friction. The goal is not maximal dissent.

It is calibrated dissent.

That calibration will become a central design problem.

How much uncertainty should trigger a question?

When should the agent act and report later?

Which constraints are hard?

Which can be traded?

When does a surprising result deserve escalation rather than automatic correction?

These are authority questions as much as intelligence questions.

The Renaissance shop solved them through relationships and reputation. The master knew the apprentice. The apprentice knew the master's tolerance. Trust accumulated socially.

Agent systems need machine-readable versions of some of that trust.

Permissions.

Scope.

Escalation rules.

Evidence requirements.

Reversibility.

This is where the workshop connects to the emerging field of agent engineering. A capable model with tools is not enough. The surrounding system has to decide what it can do without asking, what requires review, what must leave a receipt, and what causes the process to stop.

The critique loop is therefore not merely editorial.

It is operational control.

Imagine an agent preparing a report.

Low-risk actions can proceed: gather public sources, extract dates, build a table, draft questions.

Higher-risk actions require stronger evidence: infer a company's motive, make a causal claim, project revenue, accuse someone of misconduct.

External actions raise the bar again: send an email, purchase something, deploy code, sign a filing.

The workshop can use different critique intensities at each boundary.

This avoids the two bad extremes.

One extreme is permanent supervision, where the human must approve every trivial step and agentic leverage disappears.

The other is total delegation, where review happens only after the consequence.

A mature process allocates critique where errors become expensive.

This sounds obvious, but organizations are terrible at it because visible mistakes receive more controls than invisible assumptions.

A typo in a public document may pass through three reviewers.

The strategic premise behind the document may pass through none.

Agents can help invert that.

They can make mechanical correctness cheap enough that humans spend more attention on premises.

But only if leaders resist the comfort of reviewing what is easiest to review.

The critique loop should climb upward.

First: is it correct?

Then: is it supported?

Then: does it solve the commission?

Then: is the commission still the right one?

Each level can overturn the one below it.

Perfect execution of the wrong commission is failure.

This is where the master and apprentice can switch roles.

An agent may be better at checking the citations. The human may be better at seeing that the chapter has become about the wrong thing. On another task, the agent may notice a structural contradiction the human has normalized. The human may then become the student of the machine's observation.

No humiliation is required.

The hierarchy is functional, not existential.

The person responsible for the whole is not diminished when a subordinate process catches an error. The system is working.

In aviation, a warning that saves the aircraft is not an insult to the pilot.

In surgery, a checklist item that catches a mismatch is not a referendum on the surgeon's intelligence.

In a workshop, a correction is the sound of quality being made.

This is the cultural change agentic work needs.

We have inherited a schoolroom relationship with correctness. Being corrected feels like losing.

Craft has a different relationship.

Being corrected before the object leaves the workshop is winning.

That attitude becomes essential when drafts are produced faster than pride can adjust. A person may receive dozens of machine critiques in a day. If each feels like an attack, the system becomes unbearable. If each is treated as signal, the human can move at a level of iterative density previously available only to teams.

The master becomes more corrigible.

That may be the most valuable trait in the room.

There is an irony here. We fear that intelligent machines will make humans passive. They might. But a well-designed critique loop could demand more active judgment than many jobs currently require.

The machine can keep asking:

Why?

Compared with what?

What evidence?

What would falsify this?

Which constraint matters more?

Are you sure this is the same problem?

Those questions are exhausting.

They are also the questions experts ask themselves.

The agent can make them impossible to escape.

This is a very different future from the obedient digital servant.

It is closer to a studio full of difficult talent.

The work gets better because the participants do not allow one another to remain comfortably wrong.

Leonardo's pages preserve corrections because the correction belonged to the work.

Our systems should too.

There is a temptation to formalize critique until it becomes another checkbox.

That would miss the point.

Critique is valuable because it introduces a different model of the work.

The maker asks: how do I make this succeed?

The critic asks: how might this fail?

The historian asks: what are we repeating?

The user asks: why should I care?

The regulator asks: what harm becomes possible?

The accountant asks: where is the number coming from?

The editor asks: what is this paragraph doing here?

These are not stages on a conveyor belt.

They are competing descriptions of the object.

A serious workshop gives some of them standing.

This word—standing—is useful because a criticism can exist without being able to change anything. Organizations collect feedback constantly. Surveys, reviews, retrospectives, audits, employee concerns. Much of it dies because nobody has authority to act on it.

Agentic systems can generate infinite criticism.

That makes authority even more important.

Which critique can block?

Which can only recommend?

Which must be answered before proceeding?

Which can be logged and ignored?

Without this hierarchy, the workshop either drowns in objections or treats critique as decorative.

A good system makes the answer explicit.

Tests can block.

A source failure can block a factual claim.

A safety boundary can block an external action.

A style critique may not block anything.

A speculative counterargument may simply travel with the draft.

The point is not to make the workshop bureaucratic.

It is to distinguish signal from veto.

This becomes especially important in multi-agent systems. One agent can always be found to disagree with another if the prompt asks hard enough. If every disagreement triggers escalation, the human becomes a full-time referee.

The critique loop must be calibrated by consequence.

High-consequence claims deserve stronger opposition.

Low-consequence choices can move.

This is the same reason a newsroom spends more time verifying an accusation than choosing the wording of a routine weather update.

Not every sentence deserves a trial.

Some do.

The master must recognize them.

There is another problem: critics can optimize against the work.

Anyone who has spent time around compliance, peer review, security, or editorial processes knows this. A reviewer who is rewarded only for finding defects can make the object impossible to finish.

Every ambiguity becomes a problem.

Every exception becomes a blocker.

Every novel move is penalized because novelty lacks precedent.

The critic becomes the enemy of ambition.

This failure will be easy to automate.

An agent can produce hundreds of plausible objections to any plan.

The solution is not weaker criticism.

It is criticism that shares the commission.

The critic should know what the work is trying to accomplish.

Then the question changes from "What is wrong?" to "What threatens the purpose?"

This makes criticism selective.

A strange sentence may be fine if the book needs strangeness.

A security exception may be unacceptable if the system handles secrets.

A costly process may be justified if failure is catastrophic.

A statistical limitation may require narrowing the claim rather than abandoning the study.

Good critique preserves the object while changing its weaknesses.

This is why the editor is part of authorship.

The editor does not merely subtract.

The editor protects the work from its easiest version.

Agents can learn to do this better when given positive examples of successful criticism.

Show the before.

Show the comment.

Show the after.

Show why the change mattered.

A corpus of great corrections may be more valuable than a corpus of great final outputs because it reveals the workshop's judgment in motion.

This is another reason organizations should preserve review history.

The final object teaches what good looks like.

The revision teaches how good was recognized.

That is apprenticeship material.

It can also improve the agent.

A model grounded in a project's accepted and rejected changes can become more aligned to local standards than one grounded only in finished documents.

The rejects define the boundary.

This is how style becomes more than imitation.

A finished text says: this belongs.

A rejected edit says: this nearly belonged and failed for this reason.

The second example carries more information about the edge.

There is a broader epistemic lesson here.

Knowledge grows through correction.

Science advances not because every paper is right but because claims become exposed to methods that can prove them wrong.

Engineering advances because failures leave designs behind.

Markets update because bets encounter prices.

Human judgment becomes stronger when it experiences being wrong in ways that are legible.

Agentic systems could accelerate this process if they make correction denser and less socially costly.

They could also destroy it if they make every answer so smooth that nobody feels the error.

The critique loop should keep error visible long enough to matter.

This principle is especially important when the model itself is the source of the error.

The agent should not silently rewrite its previous claim and behave as if the contradiction never happened.

The workshop needs revision memory.

You said X.

The evidence now supports Y.

What changed?

This is not about shaming the system.

It is about maintaining epistemic continuity.

Humans need the same treatment.

A master who changes position should not be punished for updating. But the change should have a reason.

We believed the market was too small.

New data arrived.

We thought the source was credible.

A primary document contradicted it.

We assumed the system required this architecture.

The load test disproved the assumption.

Correction becomes trustworthy when the cause is visible.

This is one of the places where agentic work can improve organizations that currently suffer from executive amnesia.

A decision changes and everyone acts as if the new decision was always obvious.

The record says otherwise.

That record makes learning possible.

It also makes humility operational.

The workshop can celebrate a caught mistake.

This sounds cultural and soft until one considers the alternative.

If people are punished for being caught wrong, errors move underground.

If agents are tuned never to contradict the user, the same thing happens technically.

The system becomes pleasant and brittle.

A high-performing workshop creates status around correction.

Who found the hidden assumption?

Who caught the unsupported claim?

Who stopped the bad deployment?

Who noticed the commission had changed?

These are achievements.

The apprentice who catches the master should gain trust.

The agent that repeatedly identifies a class of failure should be routed toward more of that review.

The critic that generates noise should lose standing.

Critique itself needs evaluation.

This creates a second-order loop.

Which criticisms were useful?

Which were false alarms?

Which changed outcomes?

Which arrived too late?

Over time the workshop learns how to challenge itself better.

That is an extraordinary possibility.

We are accustomed to evaluating the maker.

Agentic systems let us evaluate the evaluator.

A good critic becomes an asset that can be replicated across the organization.

A bad review pattern can be identified and removed.

The workshop becomes more corrigible not by moral aspiration, but by engineering the path through which objections change work.

That is the deeper function of the loop.

Not criticism.

Correction with memory.

The first answer is not the product.

It is the beginning of the relationship.
