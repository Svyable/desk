# The Melzi Problem

Leonardo died in France in 1519.

His papers did not.

That fact seems ordinary until one considers how easily they might have.

Drawings are portable. Notes are fragile. Working papers have a low social status while their owner is alive because they are everywhere and unfinished. After death, they can become a burden: boxes without instructions, sheets whose value is unclear, material that heirs divide, sell, misplace, discard, or reorganize according to needs the author never anticipated.

Francesco Melzi became the hinge.

A younger artist from Milan, Melzi joined Leonardo's circle, remained close to him, followed him in his later years, and inherited his notebooks and drawings. The National Gallery's account of Leonardo's legacy emphasizes Melzi's role in their survival. The papers were eventually dispersed after Melzi's death, but the chain did not break at Leonardo.

Somebody cared for the memory.

We usually tell the history of ideas as if preservation were passive.

A work exists, therefore history receives it.

In reality, culture depends on custodians.

Someone keeps the letter.

Someone catalogs the specimen.

Someone copies the manuscript.

Someone pays the storage bill.

Someone refuses to throw out the boxes.

Someone creates the archive.

Someone migrates the file format.

Someone notices that the supposedly obsolete database contains the only record of why a decision was made.

Memory is labor.

Artificial intelligence is about to create more demand for that labor than most organizations understand.

The reason is simple. Agents increase the amount of consequential work that can happen without a corresponding increase in the number of people who remember it.

A small team can now conduct research, generate code, create marketing assets, evaluate contracts, build models, and make decisions at a speed that once required a larger organization. The team's output scales.

Human memory does not.

The same three people wake up the next morning.

This is the Melzi problem.

Who carries the work across time?

The obvious answer is the agent.

Let the system remember.

That answer contains truth and hides several different problems.

Which system?

Under whose control?

Remember what?

In what format?

For how long?

With what provenance?

Can the memory survive a provider change?

Can a successor understand it?

Can the agent distinguish a decision from a discarded idea?

Can the organization delete what should no longer persist?

What happens when two memories conflict?

What happens when the memory is wrong?

A context window is not an archive.

A profile is not institutional memory.

A transcript is not a knowledge base.

An embedding is not a provenance record.

The categories blur because all of them can help a model retrieve information. Organizationally, they do different things.

A workshop needs continuity at several layers.

It needs to remember the object: the current manuscript, codebase, design, contract, dataset.

It needs to remember decisions: why the object is shaped this way.

It needs to remember evidence: what supports the decisions.

It needs to remember failures: what was tried and what happened.

It needs to remember standards: what the workshop means by good.

It may need to remember people: who has authority, who owns a decision, who has domain knowledge.

These layers age differently.

The current object changes constantly.

A decision may remain stable for years.

Evidence may expire.

A failure can become relevant again after a technology changes.

A standard can evolve so slowly that nobody notices the old one has become absurd.

Memory therefore needs maintenance.

This is where the archive becomes alive.

The naive dream of AI memory is perfect recall. Ask anything and the system retrieves the right fact.

Perfect recall is not the objective.

Useful forgetting matters.

Suppose a company decided in 2027 that a certain market was unattractive because shipping costs made the unit economics impossible. Five years later, logistics economics change. An agent that remembers only the decision will keep steering the organization away. An agent that remembers the reason can notice the reason is stale.

The quality of memory depends on causal structure.

Do not remember merely that we said no.

Remember why.

Do not remember merely that the client prefers option A.

Remember when the preference was expressed, by whom, under what constraints.

Do not remember merely that this architecture is forbidden.

Remember the incident that made it forbidden and whether the underlying failure mode still exists.

This is how memory remains revisable.

Human experts do this imperfectly but naturally. A senior employee may say, "We don't do that because of the 2023 outage," and suddenly a strange rule has a story. The story creates a path to reconsideration.

Agent memory needs equivalent handles.

Otherwise the machine can turn old context into permanent law.

This is not hypothetical. Any system grounded in historical documents inherits the organization's past assumptions. The better it retrieves, the more efficiently it can reproduce them.

Memory can become conservatism at machine speed.

The Melzi role is therefore not librarian in the passive sense.

It is curator.

A curator decides what survives and how it is framed.

That power has ethical consequences. Archives shape history. Omissions matter. Labels matter. What gets described as a failed experiment instead of a dissenting argument changes how the future reads it.

In an agentic organization, whoever designs memory infrastructure will influence future decisions quietly.

This deserves governance.

People should know what classes of information become durable.

Sensitive personal information needs stricter rules than technical decisions.

Temporary brainstorming should not automatically become permanent performance evidence.

A corrected false statement should not continue to surface because the retrieval layer preserved every version without status.

Deletion needs to be real where deletion is promised.

Access should follow authority.

The workshop's memory cannot simply be "everything the model ever saw."

That would be both unsafe and cognitively useless.

Melzi had another advantage we do not.

He knew Leonardo.

That relationship supplied interpretation.

A page that looks obscure to us may have been legible inside years of shared work. Melzi could recognize handwriting, projects, recurring concerns, perhaps the context in which a sheet was produced.

Future custodians will often lack that intimacy.

This is why metadata matters.

Again, an unromantic word carrying a romantic amount of civilization.

Date.

Author.

Source.

Project.

Status.

Relationship to other records.

Confidence.

Permissions.

These small labels determine whether memory remains usable after the original people leave.

A folder named "final_final_v3_reallyfinal" is funny because everyone recognizes the failure. Agentic systems cannot build a renaissance on that.

We need durable identity for knowledge objects.

What is canonical?

What supersedes what?

Which record is a draft?

Which claim was retracted?

Which source is primary?

Which agent created the summary?

Which human approved the decision?

The answer does not have to be bureaucratically heavy. In many cases the system can capture it automatically.

The principle is heavier than the interface.

Preserve lineage.

This becomes especially important when agents begin acting over longer horizons. A single chat is easy to inspect. A long-running agent that modifies files, opens issues, sends messages, monitors systems, and makes recurring decisions develops a history.

That history is part of its competence.

It is also part of its risk.

If the agent inherits stale context, old permissions, or an obsolete objective, continuity becomes drift.

A human employee returning to a project after six months may ask what changed.

An agent may simply continue.

The more persistent the system, the more explicit the restart question must become.

Is the old commission still valid?

Are the permissions still appropriate?

Has the environment changed?

Does the evidence still hold?

Memory without reauthorization can become momentum.

This is the dark side of the Melzi problem.

Preservation is not always obedience.

A good custodian keeps the record while allowing the future to reject it.

This is why I prefer the workshop to the metaphor of an artificial "second brain." A second brain suggests one owner, one continuity, one cognitive identity. A workshop is social. Its memory must survive participants. It can contain disagreement. It has permissions. It has shared objects and private spaces. It can outlast the master.

The workshop frame also makes succession visible.

What happens when the person at the center leaves?

Most current human-agent systems are strangely personal. Prompts, chat histories, shortcuts, private examples, model preferences, and undocumented habits accumulate around one operator. That person becomes productive. The organization celebrates leverage.

Then the person departs.

The agents remain technically available and operationally useless because the real system lived in the operator's head.

This is not augmentation.

It is a new key-person risk.

A mature workshop can hand off.

Another human can enter and understand the commission, the current state, the important decisions, the open questions, the standards, and the capabilities assigned to each agent. The system should not require telepathy with the founder.

Leonardo's archive raises the stakes of handoff because we know what preservation can do. Work that remained unpublished or unfinished in his lifetime still influenced later understanding because the pages survived.

Organizations do not need five centuries of preservation for most projects.

They need Monday.

Can the next person pick up the work Monday morning?

That is the operational test.

The best memory systems will make this easier than it has ever been. Agents can generate handoff summaries, map dependencies, explain unfamiliar files, retrieve the decision behind a strange choice, and answer questions interactively.

The successor does not have to read the whole archive.

The archive can talk.

That is genuinely new.

It changes the value of keeping good records because retrieval becomes conversational. Documents once written for hypothetical future readers can now be interpreted dynamically for the person who actually arrives.

But the talking archive creates a new trust problem.

How do you know the archive's answer matches the archive?

The agent can summarize incorrectly.

It can merge two decisions.

It can infer a rationale that was never recorded.

It can fill the gap so elegantly that nobody notices the gap existed.

Therefore the archive must remain inspectable beneath the conversation.

The model should be a guide to evidence, not a replacement for evidence.

Show me the source.

Show me the decision record.

Show me the commit.

Show me the meeting note.

Show me the version that was approved.

The conversational layer makes memory accessible.

The underlying artifacts make memory accountable.

This principle may seem technical, but it reaches into culture.

An organization that can interrogate its past behaves differently.

People can challenge folklore.

"We always knew this would fail."

Did we?

"The customer demanded it."

Which customer? When?

"The model recommended it."

Based on what?

"The founder wanted it this way."

Is there a record?

Memory turns mythology into questions.

That is healthy.

Every institution produces myths about itself because humans are narrative animals and organizational history is messy. Agents can either harden those myths by repeating the most available story or weaken them by retrieving the underlying evidence.

The difference is design.

The Melzi role should therefore be recognized explicitly in agentic teams.

Not necessarily as a person with the title Chief Memory Officer. The function can be distributed. But someone must own continuity.

Someone has to care whether the workshop can remember itself.

This may be the same person who owns knowledge management today, or a technical platform team, or the project lead, or an agent supervised by a human curator. The structure will vary.

The function cannot disappear.

There is a final reason.

Memory shapes identity.

A person becomes a professional partly by remembering what happened across years of work. A workshop becomes a culture the same way. The stories of failure, the standards earned through pain, the examples everyone knows, the strange rule that has a reason—these form a shared sense of how things are done.

If agents mediate that memory, they will mediate culture.

That power should not be accidental.

We should choose what the future workshop carries forward.

Leonardo had Melzi.

Most of us will not be lucky enough to have a devoted human custodian standing beside the work for decades.

We can build the function.

The goal is not immortality.

It is continuity with the right to revise.

A workshop that cannot remember will repeat itself.

A workshop that cannot forget will fossilize.

Custodianship becomes more complicated once the archive can act.

A traditional archive waits.

A future agentic archive can surface a warning, update a brief, reopen a task, notify a person, or prevent an action based on remembered context.

Memory acquires agency.

That changes the risk.

A stale document sitting in a folder is a passive nuisance.

A stale rule inside an active agent can become behavior.

The organization decided three years ago that a certain vendor was prohibited.

The vendor changed ownership and controls.

Nobody updated the record.

The agent continues blocking the relationship.

Or the opposite: an old approval survives after the conditions that justified it have disappeared.

Active memory turns maintenance into governance.

Every durable instruction should therefore have an owner and, where appropriate, an expiration or review condition.

This is familiar in security.

Credentials expire.

Certificates expire.

Access can be revoked.

Agentic context needs analogous concepts.

A memory can be valid but no longer authoritative.

A preference can remain historically true and operationally obsolete.

A rule can be superseded.

A decision can be reopened.

These states prevent the archive from becoming a ghost executive.

The ghost executive is a real organizational phenomenon even without AI.

Someone once said no.

Nobody remembers who.

The no survives.

Someone created a process after an incident.

The incident disappears from memory.

The process remains.

A founder expressed a preference.

Years later employees still repeat it as doctrine even after the founder changed her mind.

Organizations are haunted by stale authority.

Agents could exorcise some ghosts by showing provenance.

They could also animate them.

This makes custodianship a live job.

Who is allowed to declare a memory superseded?

Which records must be retained for legal reasons even if they should stop influencing decisions?

How does an agent distinguish institutional history from current instruction?

These are architecture questions.

They are also political questions inside organizations.

Memory confers power because remembered reasons can constrain present choices.

The person who controls the archive can shape what counts as precedent.

This was true of courts, churches, states, companies, and families long before machine intelligence.

AI makes retrieval easier and therefore makes archival politics more consequential.

A buried memo that once required hours to find can now appear in seconds.

That can strengthen accountability.

It can also make selective retrieval a weapon.

An agent asked to justify a decision can search the archive for supporting precedent.

Another agent can search for contradictions.

The workshop should prefer the second posture.

Memory should be interrogable from more than one direction.

What supports this?

What contradicts it?

What changed since then?

Who objected?

What did the decision assume?

This turns the archive from precedent machine into reasoning substrate.

There is a useful analogy with legal systems. Precedent matters, but later courts distinguish cases. The old decision is not merely repeated. Its facts, reasoning, and authority are examined in relation to the current case.

Agentic memory needs the ability to distinguish.

A semantic match is not enough.

The same phrase under different conditions can mean the opposite.

The master should treat retrieved memory as an argument, not an instruction.

That is another reason human cognition remains important around persistent systems. Humans are good at social and temporal context because we inhabit it. The agent may know the date. The human may know that the chief executive who wrote the memo was leaving, that the policy was politically temporary, or that everyone in the meeting understood the language as compromise.

Some context will never make it into the archive.

This is not a defect that can be eliminated completely.

It is a reason to preserve people as living memory where possible.

Succession should transfer both documents and relationships.

The departing expert should be able to tell the successor which parts of the archive lie.

Every archive lies a little.

It contains what was recorded.

Reality contained more.

This humility matters when organizations become excited about "enterprise memory." No retrieval system can recover what nobody captured.

The model may compensate by inference.

That is precisely when it should become less confident.

A blank is safer than an invented history.

The Melzi function therefore includes protecting absence.

We do not know.

The record is incomplete.

The rationale was not captured.

The original participant disagrees with the written summary.

These are valid memory states.

An intelligent archive should be able to say them.

This creates another strange role for the human custodian.

The custodian preserves ignorance.

That sounds absurd until one considers how often false certainty enters institutions through retrospective summary.

A clean timeline is written.

The unresolved debate disappears.

The company now "knows" why it acted.

Five years later an agent cites the summary as fact.

The lie becomes durable.

A good archive should preserve competing accounts when the disagreement matters.

This is expensive for human readers.

Agents make it more manageable because the archive can carry complexity while the interface serves the relevant slice.

The future does not need one official story for every event.

It can retain several and explain where they conflict.

That is memory with depth.

This may have cultural value beyond organizations.

People increasingly live inside systems that record behavior continuously. Search histories, messages, photos, transactions, location traces, drafts, biometrics. AI can synthesize these into narratives about a person.

The temptation will be to call the narrative memory.

It is not.

It is an interpretation of traces.

A humane system must allow the person to contest it.

You misunderstood.

That event did not mean what the pattern suggests.

I changed.

I do not want this retained.

The right to revise identity that appeared in the notebook chapter becomes a governance requirement here.

A machine that remembers perfectly can become socially cruel if it denies the human right to move beyond an old self.

Melzi preserved Leonardo's papers.

He did not preserve Leonardo as a frozen prediction.

That difference should remain.

The archive serves the living.

The living do not serve the archive.

There is one more Melzi problem: format death.

Ideas survive only if future systems can open them.

Human history is full of records trapped in obsolete media, proprietary systems, damaged archives, lost indexes, and forgotten passwords. Agentic work will create new versions at astonishing speed.

A company can build years of cognitive infrastructure inside one vendor's memory layer.

Then the vendor changes.

The export is partial.

The retrieval behavior is different.

The carefully accumulated workshop becomes a stranded asset.

This is why durable memory should prefer portable evidence underneath intelligent interfaces.

Plain documents where possible.

Open formats.

Stable identifiers.

Version history.

Explicit source links.

Machine-readable metadata that another system can interpret.

The conversational layer can change.

The record should survive it.

This is less exciting than model choice and may be more important over a decade.

A master who builds a workshop should be able to change apprentices without burning the library.

That creates negotiating power too.

Organizations dependent on one model provider for memory, tooling, and workflow may discover that switching costs become cognitive rather than merely technical.

The provider does not only host software.

It hosts the organization's remembered way of working.

Portability becomes strategic.

The same is true for individuals.

A writer, researcher, programmer, or small business owner may spend years building local context around an assistant.

That history should not become a hostage.

The workshop belongs to the work.

The agent is a participant.

This principle preserves continuity through technological change.

Leonardo's papers survived the death of the master.

Our records should survive the death of an interface.

The custodian also has to protect chronology.

AI systems are unusually good at synthesizing across time and unusually capable of making different periods feel simultaneous.

A policy from 2024.

A customer preference from 2026.

A strategy from 2028.

All can appear together in one answer unless the system respects when each was true.

Temporal context is part of meaning.

What did we know then?

What changed afterward?

Was this document current when the decision was made?

A workshop that loses chronology can manufacture contradictions that never existed or preserve obsolete beliefs as if they were current.

The archive should therefore treat time as more than a timestamp.

Effective date.

Superseded date.

Decision date.

Observation date.

These distinctions help the agent reconstruct the world the record belonged to.

Memory without time is collage.

Custodianship turns collage back into history.

There is an institutional test that follows from all of this: can memory survive disagreement about the past?

Healthy organizations contain competing memories.

The engineer remembers a rushed launch.

The executive remembers a necessary launch.

The customer remembers a broken promise.

The finance team remembers the quarter that would have been missed.

A single official summary may flatten all four into a sentence that satisfies nobody and teaches little.

Agents make it possible to preserve the perspectives without forcing a future reader to absorb every transcript.

The archive can say: these accounts agree on the event and disagree on its meaning.

That is a sophisticated memory state.

It allows the next decision-maker to inherit the conflict rather than a fake consensus.

This is especially valuable after failures, when organizations have strong incentives to create a morally clean story.

One cause.

One lesson.

One responsible party.

Reality is often messier.

The workshop should preserve enough mess to prevent the lesson from becoming mythology.

A custodian does not guarantee that one story wins.

A custodian guarantees that future intelligence has access to the evidence required to judge.

That is why memory is not neutral storage.

It is the preservation of future agency.

Melzi's importance was not that he explained Leonardo once and forever.

It was that enough of the material survived for later people to keep looking.

The best agentic archive should have the same ambition.

Not to end interpretation.

To keep interpretation possible.

The future workshop needs custodians who understand both halves of that sentence.

A workshop that knows why it remembers can learn.
