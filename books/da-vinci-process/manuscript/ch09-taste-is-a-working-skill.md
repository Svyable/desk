# Taste Is a Working Skill

Taste has acquired the reputation of a private luxury.

Someone has it or does not. The word appears when the reasons become inconvenient to explain. This room has taste. That product does not. The editor has taste. The investor has taste. The founder has taste. The model has no taste, or perhaps it does now, depending on which demonstration we have just watched.

The vagueness is useful to people whose status depends on possessing it.

It is less useful to a workshop.

A workshop has to teach.

The moment you try to teach taste, the mystical version begins to break apart.

Taste is not one faculty. It is accumulated discrimination.

The painter sees that the hand is wrong before the pupil does.

The editor hears that the sentence is false to the voice even though every word is grammatical.

The engineer distrusts the architecture because one clean abstraction is carrying too many unrelated responsibilities.

The musician knows that the technically perfect take is emotionally dead.

The investor recognizes that the management team has answered the question without addressing it.

These judgments may arrive as feeling before explanation. That does not mean they arrived from nowhere. The feeling is compressed experience.

A master has seen more.

More good work.

More bad work.

More almost-good work.

More failures that looked good at first.

More expensive exceptions to the rule.

Taste is memory with a preference function.

That makes it trainable.

It also makes it vulnerable.

The new machines create a paradox. They can give a person access to far more examples than any historical apprentice could see. They can compare, classify, imitate, criticize, and generate variations. That should be a machine for developing taste.

They can also remove the need to choose.

That is a machine for losing it.

The difference is whether the human remains an evaluator or becomes a requester.

A requester says, "Give me the best one."

An evaluator sees the candidates before the ranking closes.

The distinction sounds small. It changes the learning signal.

Suppose an agent generates ten names for a product and highlights its top three. If the human picks among the three, the model has already shaped the field of attention. That may be helpful. It also means the human is learning the model's taste more than developing an independent one.

Now invert the order.

Show the ten names without ranking. Ask the human to choose three and explain why. Then reveal the model's ranking. Compare. Where do the judgments differ? What constraint did one side notice? Which rationale survives contact with customers?

The agent has become a sparring partner rather than a concierge.

This matters because much of expertise develops through prediction before feedback.

A radiologist forms an interpretation before seeing the confirmed diagnosis.

A chess player chooses a move before checking the engine.

An editor marks a sentence before hearing another editor's view.

A student solves before reading the solution.

If the answer arrives first, the mind becomes very good at recognizing explanations after the fact.

Recognition feels like knowledge.

It is not the same thing.

Generative AI can flood professional life with answers-first interactions.

The system proposes before the person has formed a view. The proposal anchors judgment. Even when the person disagrees, the space has been shaped.

This is not a new cognitive problem. Humans anchor on suggestions from other humans, defaults, prices, first estimates, headlines, and authority figures. The novelty is the frequency.

A person may now receive hundreds of machine suggestions in the same amount of time that an earlier worker received a handful of colleague suggestions.

Anchoring can become the atmosphere.

Taste needs air.

This is one reason the Microsoft study of knowledge workers and generative AI deserves more attention than its headline about critical thinking. Participants described their work shifting toward verification and integration. That shift can be intellectually demanding. It can also become shallow if the verifier never creates an independent reference point.

The problem is confidence.

When users trusted the AI more, they reported less critical-thinking effort. When they had more confidence in their own ability, they reported more. The study is not proof of a causal mechanism, but it describes a relationship any master should recognize: the less capable you feel, the more attractive the fluent answer becomes.

This creates a brutal loop for novices.

The people who most need practice are the people most likely to accept the substitute.

The system can raise immediate output while slowing the development that would make them confident enough to challenge it.

A workshop has to break that loop deliberately.

The old masters had a natural advantage. Their apprentices could not summon a better draft from nowhere. They had to look, try, fail, compare.

We need artificial versions of that exposure.

One is delayed assistance.

Let the person commit to a judgment before the agent contributes.

Another is blind comparison.

Mix human and machine candidates without labels and force evaluation on the work.

Another is rationale testing.

The human explains the preference, then the agent searches for cases where that preference would fail.

Another is adversarial imitation.

Ask the agent to generate something that looks tasteful at first glance but violates one deeper standard. Make the learner find the defect.

This is the difference between teaching a rule and teaching an eye.

An eye learns edges.

The almost-good example is therefore more valuable than the obvious failure.

A bad sentence announces itself.

An almost-good sentence requires an editor.

A ridiculous business plan requires no strategy skill to reject.

A plausible plan with one fatal incentive problem does.

A terrible model hallucination is easy.

A correct-looking answer built on a slightly wrong assumption is the real frontier.

Agents can generate those almost-good cases endlessly.

This may become one of their greatest educational uses.

The irony is that product interfaces are optimized to reduce exactly this discomfort. The model is supposed to be helpful. Friction is treated as a defect. Suggestions are ranked. Answers are polished. The interaction aims for completion.

Taste grows in incompletion.

It grows while something remains unresolved long enough for the person to care about the difference.

This is why artists stare.

Why editors reread.

Why scientists repeat.

Why engineers argue over a design that either option could technically satisfy.

The time looks inefficient because the output is not changing quickly.

The evaluator is changing.

That is invisible productivity.

Organizations are not good at paying for it.

A junior employee who spends two hours comparing five approaches may look slower than one who asks the agent for the best answer in two minutes. The second employee wins today's metric.

A year later, the first may be able to tell when the "best answer" is wrong.

That future value rarely appears in the productivity study.

This does not invalidate measured gains from AI. It changes what else should be measured.

If a company deploys agents and sees throughput rise, it should ask a second question: what happened to unaided judgment?

Not because unaided work is morally superior.

Because judgment is the backup system.

When the machine fails outside its frontier, the user must know enough to notice.

The BCG consultant study makes the issue concrete. On tasks where GPT-4 was strong, consultants using it performed better. On a task designed outside the model's capability frontier, those relying on AI were more likely to produce incorrect answers.

The lesson is not to distrust AI.

It is that trust has to be conditional on a map of competence.

Taste is partly that map.

An experienced person senses which kinds of outputs deserve suspicion. She knows where the domain becomes adversarial, where data is thin, where incentives distort the source, where small wording changes have legal meaning, where a visual pattern can mislead.

A general model may know those risks abstractly. The local expert knows which one is alive now.

This is why taste and context are inseparable.

The same sentence can be good in one book and ruin another.

The same architecture can be elegant in a startup and reckless in a bank.

The same medical threshold can mean something different in a frail patient.

The same negotiation tactic can be clever with a transactional counterparty and destructive in a relationship that must last ten years.

Taste is not a global score.

It is judgment inside a world.

This creates a problem for benchmarking machine taste.

A model can be preferred by large numbers of evaluators on standardized tasks and still fail the particular workshop because the workshop's standard is not the average preference.

This is especially important in creative work.

Generative systems are trained and tuned toward broad usefulness. Broad usefulness exerts pressure toward recognizable quality. Recognizable quality can converge.

The sentence becomes smooth.

The image becomes cinematic.

The interface becomes clean.

The strategy becomes legible.

The presentation becomes polished.

Nothing is obviously wrong.

Nothing is unforgettable either.

The workshop needs a way to defend the weird thing that works.

That requires a master who knows why the weird thing belongs.

Otherwise agents will sand it away.

This danger is not unique to AI. Large organizations have been sanding away idiosyncrasy for centuries through committees, style guides, brand standards, legal review, and fear. AI simply makes the average easier to manufacture.

The cheapest competent output will tend toward convention.

Distinctive work requires a reason to depart.

Taste supplies the reason.

Leonardo is useful again, but we should resist making him a mascot for eccentricity. His departures mattered because they grew from obsessive looking. He studied light because painting demanded it. He studied anatomy because bodies were not symbols when seen closely enough. He drew movement until the conventional outline became insufficient.

His strangeness had evidence.

That is a high standard for originality.

Not different because difference sells.

Different because the object refuses the convention.

This is exactly the standard human creators should demand when an agent proposes novelty.

Why is this different?

What does the difference solve?

What observation requires it?

If the answer is merely that the model generated an unusual combination, novelty is decoration.

Taste converts surprise into necessity.

This is why the future master needs a richer relationship with examples.

The model can bring thousands.

The human should build a canon.

That word can sound grand, but every serious practitioner has one. A set of works that establish the ceiling. A set of failures that remain instructive. A set of local examples that define what the workshop means by good.

For a software team, the canon might be exemplary internal services, incident reports, design docs, and external systems admired for specific properties.

For a newsroom, it may be investigations whose sourcing, pacing, fairness, and explanatory discipline are studied.

For a product group, it may be interactions that respect the user's time, plus failures that demonstrate what manipulation looks like.

For a writer, it is not "write like these authors." It is a set of passages examined for what they do.

The canon gives the agent something better than a vibe.

It gives the human something better than an impression.

Now critique can refer to actual choices.

This opening earns attention because it withholds the explanation until the reader has a question.

This chart is good because the uncertainty remains visible.

This interface is good because the dangerous action cannot be mistaken for the routine one.

This paragraph works because the author stops before explaining the line to death.

Taste becomes articulate.

Not fully. There will always be a remainder.

That remainder matters.

Experts often know before they can say. The right response is not to dismiss the intuition or worship it. It is to test it.

An agent is unusually useful here.

It can ask the master to defend the feeling.

It can propose several hypotheses for why something feels wrong.

It can search for counterexamples.

It can change one variable at a time.

The human's tacit reaction becomes material for inquiry.

This is the apprentice teaching the master to understand his own craft.

It is one of the places where the human-agent relationship becomes genuinely reciprocal.

The human supplies preference shaped by lived context.

The agent supplies variation and articulation at scale.

Each can expose what the other cannot see.

But the reciprocity collapses if the human's first move is deference.

Taste has to be exercised to remain available.

This suggests a practical discipline for any agentic workshop: maintain zones of unaided judgment.

Not entire jobs.

Moments.

Before seeing the model's score, choose.

Before reading the summary, inspect one primary source.

Before accepting the patch, state what you expect the bug to be.

Before asking for the rewrite, identify what is wrong.

Before requesting options, write down the constraint that will decide among them.

These moments keep the human predictive rather than merely reactive.

They are small.

Over years, they may determine whether the person becomes a master or a supervisor of surfaces.

There is a larger cultural problem.

AI is arriving in institutions that already undervalue taste because taste is difficult to quantify. Performance systems prefer throughput, conversion, response time, error rate, utilization, and other numbers that can be compared cleanly.

Those metrics matter.

They also become dangerous when the work's quality includes things the metric cannot see.

A university can improve graduation rates while lowering intellectual demand.

A newsroom can increase engagement while losing trust.

A software company can ship features faster while making the product less coherent.

A hospital can optimize documented throughput while clinicians become less able to see the patient outside the workflow.

Agents amplify the metric because they can optimize what is legible.

Taste protects what is not yet legible.

This is not an argument against measurement.

It is an argument for masters who know what the measurement leaves out.

Every serious craft has such people.

They are often annoying.

They say the dashboard is green and the thing is bad.

They say the tests pass and the design is wrong.

They say the campaign is performing and the brand is being cheapened.

They say the sentence is clear and should still be cut.

Their value is easiest to see after they leave.

Then the institution discovers that the rules were never enough.

Artificial intelligence will force us to decide whether we want to preserve this kind of human.

We should.

Not because machines can never develop sophisticated evaluative capability. They already exhibit surprising evaluative strength in many domains, and future systems will be better.

We should preserve human taste because people still have to live inside the worlds our evaluations create.

Taste is where experience becomes a standard.

If the standard is outsourced completely, the world may become extraordinarily optimized for criteria nobody remembers choosing.

Taste also has an economic history that becomes more important when production costs collapse.

Scarce production hides weak selection.

If a company can afford to make only three television commercials, almost any process will appear selective because scarcity imposes a filter. If a publisher can acquire only a certain number of books, choices become unavoidable. If a photographer has thirty-six exposures on a roll, each frame carries a small tax.

Digital production removed many of these taxes.

Generative production removes more.

This means taste stops being partially enforced by budget.

The workshop has to supply the filter consciously.

That filter can become a competitive advantage.

When everyone can generate competent work, the scarce institution may be the one that can refuse competent work.

This is already familiar in luxury, publishing, venture capital, design, and research. Selection creates value because the field is too large to consume indiscriminately.

Agents will enlarge the field.

The ability to say no becomes more valuable.

But "no" can harden into conservatism.

A person with strong taste can become a prisoner of the canon that trained it.

This is the hidden weakness of expertise.

The expert recognizes quality partly because quality resembles successful precedent. Radical work may initially look incompetent.

The master must therefore distinguish between violation and failure.

Leonardo's own career makes the point symbolically. Techniques that now seem obviously "Leonardesque" were once deviations from local convention. A standard that cannot be violated cannot produce a new standard.

This is where an agent can help the master most by locating unfamiliar precedent.

Something that feels wrong may belong to another tradition.

A design rejected as confusing in one domain may have a successful analogue elsewhere.

A narrative move that seems too abrupt may resemble a form from journalism, theater, or oral storytelling.

The agent can widen the canon faster than the human could.

This does not replace taste.

It gives taste more evidence before it closes.

The sequence matters.

First reaction.

Then inquiry.

Then decision.

If inquiry comes first every time, the human loses the raw signal.

If reaction becomes final every time, the human becomes provincial.

The workshop preserves both.

This suggests a richer use of recommendation systems inside creative work. Instead of asking the model to recommend "the best examples," ask it to bring examples that challenge the current standard.

What respected works violate this rule?

Where has the opposite choice succeeded?

Which culture, field, or period solves this problem differently?

Now the agent acts as an anti-canon.

That can keep taste alive.

Taste should have adversaries.

There is another problem: machine-generated work can be optimized to human preference faster than humans can articulate why they like it.

If a system produces hundreds of variants and learns from clicks, selections, edits, dwell time, and conversion, it can infer a preference surface.

The human may begin seeing more of what the human tends to choose.

This is convenient.

It can also become aesthetic self-enclosure.

A master whose workshop only serves familiar preference loses contact with surprise.

The recommender becomes a mirror that gradually removes everything the mirror has not seen.

This is already a problem in media and commerce.

Agentic creation brings it inside the act of making.

The model remembers your sentences.

Your colors.

Your pacing.

Your argument structures.

Your favored analogies.

Soon "write like me" becomes easy.

Then the danger is that you become easier to predict than to surprise.

The workshop should deliberately inject outside influence.

Another person's eye.

A different model.

A source from a field the current canon ignores.

An adversarial constraint.

A human collaborator who dislikes your defaults.

Taste needs contamination.

Renaissance workshops were not sealed. Artists moved. Designs circulated. Patrons imported expectations. Techniques crossed cities. Rivalries created pressure.

Cultural fertility often comes from contact.

The human-agent workshop can simulate some contact, but it should also seek real contact.

Go look at the object.

Talk to the reader.

Work with the designer.

Ask the scientist.

Bring the agent into a world with people who do not share the prompt.

This protects taste from becoming a closed-loop preference engine.

There is also a moral dimension.

Taste often masquerades as neutrality while encoding class, culture, training, and power.

What counts as "professional" can exclude voices.

What counts as "clean" design can privilege one audience.

What counts as "serious" prose can erase humor, dialect, anger, or intimacy.

Agents trained on broad patterns may reproduce dominant standards with extraordinary fluency.

A master should know when the standard itself deserves critique.

This is where taste becomes responsibility.

The question is not simply: do I like this?

Who is this standard serving?

What does it make invisible?

What does it punish?

What human difference does it smooth away?

This does not require turning every aesthetic decision into politics.

It requires remembering that standards came from somewhere.

A workshop can preserve distinctive voices only if it knows which irregularities are errors and which are identity.

This is one of the hardest editorial tasks in machine-assisted writing because the model is often good at normalization.

The author writes a strange but exact line.

The system offers a smoother one.

The smoother line receives a higher generic readability score.

The book becomes worse.

The master needs the courage to keep the difficult sentence.

That courage comes from evidence accumulated in the craft.

The sentence may be harder because the thought is harder.

Or it may simply be bad.

Taste is knowing which.

No benchmark can settle every case.

That is why taste remains a working skill rather than a final rulebook.

The master keeps practicing it.

The workshop keeps challenging it.

The canon expands.

The standard changes slowly enough to remain a standard and quickly enough to stay alive.

This is not mystical.

It is difficult.

That is better.

A master is someone who remembers.

And then, when the work proves the memory wrong, changes it.
