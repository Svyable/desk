# Da Vinci Process

*Masters, Apprentices, and the Human-Agent Renaissance*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

© 2026 Sven Hardy Benson. All Rights Reserved.

There is a version of the future in which artificial intelligence makes human beings smaller.

It is the version in which every difficult thing becomes a button. The first draft arrives before the writer has decided what he thinks. The code appears before the programmer has understood the system. The diagnosis is suggested before the doctor has framed the uncertainty. The student submits an answer without acquiring the question. The executive receives a strategy assembled from patterns she can no longer interrogate. Work gets faster. People become less able to tell whether it is good.

There is another version.

In that version, capable agents do not end the need for human craft. They force us to become more explicit about what craft was doing all along. The work moves through a living system of commission, attempt, demonstration, correction, comparison, revision, delegation, refusal, memory, and judgment. Some parts are performed by the person whose name is on the work. Some are performed by others. Some are performed by machines. What matters is not that one pair of hands touched every surface. What matters is whether the workshop became capable of making something none of its participants could have made alone.

Five hundred years ago, Florence already understood part of this arrangement.

The great Renaissance workshop was not a temple to solitary genius. It was a place of contracts and pigments, chores and drawings, reputations and deadlines. A master could take a commission larger than one person could execute. Apprentices learned by working near consequential work, then on it. Assistants became trusted with larger portions. Designs moved through multiple hands. The best pupils did not remain pupils.

One of them was Leonardo da Vinci.

That fact has been hidden by his fame. Leonardo now arrives in the imagination already finished: the notebooks, the machines, the anatomical drawings, the Mona Lisa smile, the old man with the beard. But before the myth there was a young worker in Andrea del Verrocchio's shop, learning inside an institution built to turn novices into practitioners and practitioners into masters.

This book uses that workshop as an analogy for a new one forming around us.

It does not claim that Leonardo anticipated artificial intelligence. It does not turn Renaissance Florence into a management parable with better costumes. It does not assume that an AI agent is a human apprentice in silicon. The differences matter too much for that.

The proposition is narrower and, I think, more useful.

When execution becomes cheap, the architecture around execution becomes more valuable.

Someone still has to decide what is worth making. Someone has to supply context that does not fit inside the task. Someone has to notice when the request itself is wrong. Someone has to compare ten plausible answers and know why the eleventh is better. Someone has to protect the purpose when the work fragments into subtasks. Someone has to preserve the evidence. Someone has to decide when the apprentice should be trusted, when it should be challenged, and when the apprentice has seen something the master missed.

Those are workshop problems.

They are also becoming human-agent problems.

The future of intelligence may therefore look less like one machine replacing one worker and more like a return, in strange new form, to the studio: one person surrounded by capable assistants, critics, researchers, draftsmen, testers, archivists, and specialists that can be summoned, redirected, duplicated, and dismissed at extraordinary speed. The person at the center will have more reach than any historical master. That does not guarantee better work. It only raises the ceiling and lowers the excuse.

The Renaissance that matters now will not be a renaissance of output.

It will be a renaissance of interaction.
