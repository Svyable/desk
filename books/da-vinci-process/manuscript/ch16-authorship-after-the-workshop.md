# Authorship After the Workshop

A museum label can contain a small crisis in two words.

"Workshop of."

The phrase tells the viewer that the object belongs near a master without claiming the master's own hand made all or any of the crucial passages. Other labels are even more careful: "attributed to," "circle of," "follower of."

We read these qualifiers as uncertainty.

They are also honesty about production.

Art history has spent generations trying to reconstruct who touched what because the name on the object changes meaning, value, interpretation, and price. A painting by Leonardo is not economically equivalent to a painting made by someone in his orbit, even if the images are superficially close.

The obsession with the hand can look quaint in an era when digital work is infinitely reproducible.

Then generative AI arrives and everyone becomes an attribution scholar.

Who made this?

How much?

Which model?

What was the prompt?

Was it edited?

Does the human deserve the byline?

Did the machine copy someone else's style?

Should the result be protected?

Should it be disclosed?

We are back in the workshop, except the apprentice is software and the traces are harder to see.

The legal answers vary by jurisdiction and are changing. This chapter is not an attempt to settle copyright doctrine.

The cultural question comes first.

What do we mean by authorship when execution is distributed?

The easiest answer is labor.

The author is whoever did most of the work.

This fails quickly.

Editors can do enormous labor and remain editors.

A director may do less physical production than hundreds of crew members and still be properly understood as the director.

A founder can be responsible for a product without writing most of the code.

Labor matters morally and economically. It does not map cleanly onto authorship.

The next answer is originality.

The author supplied the original idea.

This is also unstable.

Ideas are cheap.

Execution often changes the idea beyond recognition.

Many works begin from prompts, commissions, genres, inherited forms, factual events, or questions other people have asked before.

The person with the first idea is not automatically the author of every result downstream.

The next answer is control.

The author is whoever determines the final form.

Closer.

But control can be superficial. A person can click "accept" on machine output without understanding it. Technically the human had final authority. Practically the human contributed little.

The next answer is responsibility.

Now we have something.

The author is the person who can answer for the work.

Not only legally.

Intellectually.

Why is this claim here?

Why was this source trusted?

Why did this structure win?

What was rejected?

What would cause you to revise it?

What do you mean by this sentence?

If the person cannot answer because the machine did the thinking and the person never inspected it, the byline becomes weak.

Responsibility alone is still insufficient. A corporate executive can be responsible for a document without being its author.

Add intention.

The author sets the work's purpose.

Add selection.

The author chooses among possibilities.

Add integration.

The author maintains coherence across contributions.

Add revision.

The author changes the work in response to evidence and judgment.

Now authorship begins to look less like typing and more like a workshop role.

This is not a legal test.

It is a craft standard.

A person can satisfy it while using enormous machine assistance.

A person can fail it while personally typing every word.

This second fact is important.

Human execution does not guarantee authorship in the meaningful sense.

A writer can assemble clichés without thought.

A manager can produce a deck by copying last quarter.

A student can paraphrase a source line by line.

The fingers move.

The mind is absent.

We should not let the arrival of AI cause nostalgia for keystrokes.

The goal is not to preserve human effort.

It is to preserve human authorship where humans claim authorship.

That requires contact with the object.

A master who delegates every passage can still be an author if the master is deeply engaged in commission, selection, critique, integration, and responsibility.

At some point delegation can become so total that the claim weakens.

Where is the line?

There is no universal percentage.

Counting tokens will not solve it.

The better test is counterfactual.

If the human were removed, would the work still have substantially the same purpose, standard, shape, and final choices?

If yes, the human's authorship claim is thinner.

If no—if the machine's output becomes this work because the human supplied the question, evidence, constraints, judgment, and repeated selection—then the human contribution is substantial even if the machine produced many literal words.

This is why an author's project history matters.

The final prose does not reveal the architecture of control.

The workshop record can.

That record should not become an exercise in defensive disclosure. Readers do not need a transcript of every tool use any more than readers need a list of every spell-check suggestion accepted.

The level of disclosure should match the context.

A school assignment may require strict disclosure because the purpose is to assess the student's own capability.

Scientific work may need methodological transparency because reproducibility and provenance matter.

Journalism may need policies around generated material because trust and source integrity matter.

A novelist may use tools differently.

A company report may need internal records even if the public document does not enumerate the process.

The point is to align disclosure with the promise.

What does the audience believe it is receiving?

That question is older than AI.

Ghostwriting, studio production, anonymous collaboration, editors, researchers, assistants, and work-for-hire arrangements have always complicated authorship.

AI expands the scale and reduces the visibility.

A person can now create the appearance of solitary expertise without the social traces that once accompanied assistance.

There may be no junior analyst to thank.

No research assistant.

No visible team.

Just a document and a name.

This makes self-discipline more important.

The easiest abuse of agents will not be plagiarism in the narrow sense.

It will be counterfeit competence.

A person will present work in a way that invites others to infer abilities the person does not possess.

The report will imply research that was not understood.

The code will imply engineering judgment that was borrowed.

The essay will imply a command of the material that disappears under questioning.

The strategy will imply conviction where there was only generation.

This matters because institutions allocate trust based on demonstrated work.

If artifacts cease to signal capability, trust systems have to change.

Hiring will change.

Education will change.

Professional credentialing will change.

The interview may become more important than the portfolio.

Live reasoning may become more important than the take-home exercise.

References to process may matter more than polish.

The master-apprentice model offers a healthier response than surveillance.

Do not try to prove that the human performed every step.

Evaluate whether the human can own the whole.

Can the person explain the commission?

Can the person critique the output?

Can the person make a change when the context changes?

Can the person detect when the agent is wrong?

Can the person say what is uncertain?

Can the person defend the decision without asking the model to do it?

This is authorship as capability.

It is more demanding than proof of manual labor.

It also creates room for genuinely ambitious collaboration.

A writer should be able to use agents for research assistance, structural critique, counterargument, source checking, and variation without pretending the tools do not exist or conceding that the resulting book has no human author.

An engineer should be able to use code generation extensively while remaining responsible for architecture and behavior.

A scientist should be able to use machine assistance while preserving the evidentiary standards that make the work science.

The important thing is not purity.

It is traceable judgment.

This phrase—traceable judgment—may become central to authorship in an agentic world.

The person made consequential choices and the path of those choices can be reconstructed where needed.

This is also how we should think about machine contribution.

Current AI systems are not legal persons and should not be anthropomorphized into Renaissance apprentices with human claims simply because the metaphor is useful. They do not need credit in the social sense a human collaborator does.

But provenance still matters.

Which model and system shaped the result can matter for reproducibility, rights, bias, security, and interpretation.

A machine contribution can require disclosure without requiring personhood.

This distinction keeps the workshop analogy grounded.

The agent is like an apprentice in the structure of delegated cognitive work.

It is not an apprentice in biography, moral status, or legal identity.

Metaphors become dangerous when they carry more than the argument asked them to carry.

Authorship has another dimension the Renaissance workshop makes difficult to avoid.

Credit.

A master could receive reputation for a work containing substantial assistant labor. That asymmetry helped build the master's brand and could obscure the subordinate's contribution.

Modern agentic work may create the reverse temptation around human teams.

A senior person surrounded by AI can reduce the number of visible human contributors and increase personal output. The economic gains may concentrate around the person who owns the agentic system.

This could be extraordinarily empowering for individuals.

It could also become a machine for appropriating other people's expertise if the underlying data, examples, or internal work are used without appropriate recognition and rights.

The workshop has to take credit seriously.

Human contributions should remain visible where norms or contracts require it.

Sources should be cited.

Research should retain provenance.

Ideas that came from colleagues should not become ownerless because an agent transported them.

The ease of synthesis should not become a license for erasure.

This is another reason source trails matter. When a machine can remix many inputs smoothly, attribution must be designed upstream rather than reconstructed from memory later.

The Renaissance workshop did not give us a clean moral model here.

It gives us the problem.

The future workshop should do better.

There is a further challenge.

What happens when an agent begins to contribute not merely execution but direction?

It proposes the thesis.

It discovers the evidence that changes the thesis.

It invents the structure.

It writes the strongest passage.

It identifies the flaw the human could not see.

At what point does the human author's claim become ceremonial?

Again, percentages will not save us.

The human can still be the author if the human has adopted, tested, and integrated those contributions into a work whose purpose and responsibility the human genuinely owns.

Humans receive decisive ideas from other humans all the time.

An editor can suggest the ending.

A colleague can identify the central argument.

A teacher can supply the conceptual frame.

The author does not cease to be an author merely because the best idea arrived from outside.

But a pattern matters.

If the human repeatedly accepts machine-generated purpose without independent judgment, the center has moved.

The honest response may eventually be a different kind of credit.

Culture will invent language for these arrangements.

We should not rush to settle it before the practices stabilize.

What we can demand now is honesty.

Do not claim solitary execution when it matters and was not solitary.

Do not claim expertise you cannot exercise.

Do not hide machine involvement where disclosure is part of the audience's legitimate expectation.

Do not diminish human collaborators because a machine made their contribution easier to absorb.

Do not let the existence of a tool erase responsibility.

This is a strong standard because it survives changing technology.

The tools will improve.

The norms will move.

The legal rules will evolve.

Responsibility remains legible.

The master can delegate the brushstroke.

The master cannot delegate the meaning of putting a name on the work.

That is the bond.

A byline is not a receipt for keystrokes.

It is a promise that someone is home.
