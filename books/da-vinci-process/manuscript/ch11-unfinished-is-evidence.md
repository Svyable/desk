# Unfinished Is Evidence

The finished surface is a liar.

Not because it is false. Because it conceals how many other objects almost existed.

A completed painting hides the figures that moved before the final position, the hand repainted three times, the line abandoned under a layer of color, the argument between the first composition and the last. The viewer receives decision as destiny.

Unfinished work is less discreet.

Leonardo's *Adoration of the Magi* is one of the great exposures of process because the painting stopped while decisions were still visible. Commissioned in 1481 for the Augustinian monks of San Donato a Scopeto, it remained unfinished when Leonardo left Florence for Milan. The Uffizi's restoration work has made the object even more revealing. Different parts sit at different levels of development. Underdrawing survives. Contours multiply. Positions change. Washes and tonal passages coexist with areas that remain closer to construction than completion.

The panel is not a tutorial.

It is better.

It is evidence that the work moved.

For centuries, finished objects encouraged a mythology of creation in which great people had great ideas and then executed them. Unfinished objects show the machinery: uncertainty, revision, replacement, contradiction.

This matters now because generative systems create an opposite visibility problem.

They can make unfinished thought look finished.

A model can take a vague instruction, infer a conventional structure, fill missing transitions, and return prose that has the visual confidence of completion. It can produce code with the indentation of intention. It can generate a strategy deck in which every slide has an answer.

The result may be useful.

It can also erase evidence that the problem is unresolved.

Fluency is a finishing layer.

A workshop needs ways to see underneath it.

One of the simplest is to preserve intermediate artifacts.

The initial commission.

The first outline.

The discarded hypothesis.

The failed test.

The critique that forced the change.

The source that did not support the claim.

The alternative design that lost.

The unresolved question.

Again, not every token. The useful traces.

This is why version history will become more valuable as generation becomes easier. The final answer tells us what survived. The history can tell us why.

Software engineers understand this instinctively because code changes create operational consequences. A good commit is more than a snapshot. It is a claim about a coherent change. A pull request can preserve discussion, tests, alternatives, and review. When the system fails six months later, that history becomes part of diagnosis.

Knowledge work needs similar habits.

A policy should have a record of the assumptions that made it reasonable.

A forecast should preserve the range that existed before the point estimate won.

A research conclusion should carry the analyses that changed the interpretation.

A book should remember the evidence that forced a chapter to become less certain.

The agentic workshop can generate these traces automatically.

That is an enormous advantage if the traces are treated as evidence rather than exhaust.

Consider a strategy produced with several agents. One scans competitors. One models unit economics. One researches regulation. One attacks the thesis. One synthesizes. The final document may be twenty pages. The underlying process may contain thousands of claims and decisions.

If the final twenty pages are all that survives, the organization loses most of what it learned.

It loses which source was weak.

It loses which assumption changed.

It loses the alternative that almost won.

It loses the critic's objection after the objection has been resolved in the prose.

The polished artifact becomes a memory compressor with no decompression key.

This is dangerous because certainty increases as process disappears.

Six months later, somebody opens the report and reads the recommendation as if it were obvious.

The unresolved becomes precedent.

A visible revision history protects against that.

Leonardo's unfinished panel does something similar involuntarily. It refuses to let us believe that the composition arrived whole.

We can see thought under construction.

There is another lesson in the *Adoration*, less flattering.

Leonardo did not finish it.

The unfinished state has become art-historical treasure. For the patron in 1481, it was also a failure to receive the commissioned painting.

We should not let posterity romanticize non-completion.

The agentic era will produce a new cult of infinite refinement if we are not careful.

The machine can always make one more pass.

One more research sweep.

One more alternative opening.

One more test.

One more scenario.

One more critic.

The workshop can become so good at iteration that it loses the ability to deliver.

This is especially dangerous for intelligent people because every additional pass can be justified.

There is always a real weakness.

There is always a missing source.

There is always another edge case.

There is always a stronger phrase.

The question is not whether more work could improve the object.

It is whether more work is the best use of the next unit of attention.

That is a portfolio decision.

Masters make portfolio decisions even when they are working on one object.

Every hour spent improving one area is an hour not spent elsewhere. Every revision carries a risk of damaging what already works. Every delayed release postpones contact with the world, which may contain information no internal critique can generate.

Perfectionism hides opportunity cost by making the current object feel morally unique.

A workshop cannot afford that illusion.

This is where completion criteria become part of craft.

A completion criterion is not the same as a deadline.

A deadline says time is over.

A criterion says the object has crossed a threshold.

The thesis is supported to the standard we promised.

The dangerous test passes.

The customer can complete the core task.

The evidence is strong enough for the claim we are making.

The known defect is below the agreed risk tolerance.

The work may still improve, but further internal iteration is less valuable than external contact.

Agents can help enforce this if the criterion is written down.

They can also destroy it if success is defined vaguely as "make it better."

Better has no stopping condition.

The machine will comply forever.

A serious workshop therefore distinguishes revision from churn.

Revision resolves a known deficiency or tests a meaningful uncertainty.

Churn changes the artifact because change is available.

This distinction becomes visible in the history.

If version seven exists because version six failed a source check, the change has a reason.

If version eight exists because the agent was asked to "polish," the burden should be higher.

Polish is not free. It can remove character, precision, and informative roughness.

The unfinished trace is sometimes better than the polished sentence because the trace reveals where certainty ends.

Scientists know this problem in another form. Clean figures can conceal researcher degrees of freedom. The final paper presents one path through many analytic choices. Good research practices try to make more of those choices visible through preregistration, code, supplementary material, data-sharing, and replication.

The principle generalizes.

When a result depends on a path, preserve enough of the path to audit the result.

Agents increase the number of possible paths enormously.

That means provenance must become stronger, not weaker.

A machine can now search, transform, and synthesize information so quickly that a person may not remember where a claim entered the project. If the source link is not attached at the moment of use, later reconstruction becomes expensive. A generated explanation can migrate across documents until nobody knows whether it was observed, inferred, or invented.

The workflow should preserve epistemic status.

Observed.

Reported.

Inferred.

Speculative.

Rejected.

Unknown.

These labels are less glamorous than artificial intelligence.

They are how intelligence avoids becoming fiction.

Leonardo's scientific notebooks contain their own mixtures of observation, analogy, hypothesis, engineering design, and speculation. Modern readers can separate them partly because the raw pages remain. A finished treatise might have clarified some distinctions and concealed others.

This is the paradox of unfinished work.

It can be epistemically richer because it has not yet been forced into one coherent story.

The final story is necessary.

So is respect for what it removed.

A human-agent workshop can keep both.

This is one place where digital abundance is genuinely superior to the physical workshop. We do not have to paint over the underdrawing permanently. We can ship the finished object and preserve the process privately or publicly as appropriate.

The hard part is deciding what deserves preservation.

One rule is to keep traces where the consequence of a future misunderstanding is high.

Safety decisions.

Legal interpretations.

Scientific analyses.

Architectural choices.

Major strategy pivots.

Claims likely to be challenged.

Anything the organization expects future people or agents to inherit without the original participants present.

Another rule is to preserve disagreement when it materially affected the decision.

Institutions have a bad habit of rewriting consensus backward. Once a decision succeeds, everyone remembers supporting it. Once it fails, everyone remembers their warning.

A contemporaneous trace resists that vanity.

Agents can help by preserving alternatives mechanically.

This may improve institutional learning because postmortems become less dependent on memory.

What did we know?

What did we believe?

What did the agent recommend?

What did the human override?

Which test was unavailable?

Which uncertainty was acknowledged?

These questions turn failure into training data for the actual organization rather than a generic lesson about "communication."

The trace is the apprenticeship of the institution.

This is another reason unfinished work should not be hidden automatically.

A junior employee can learn more from a marked-up draft than a perfect final memo. The edits show where judgment entered.

A young programmer can learn more from the rejected pull request and review comments than from the clean implementation.

A new researcher can learn from the null result that never became a headline.

The old workshop was full of visible corrections because work happened in shared space.

Digital systems often hide correction by presenting only the current state.

Agents could reverse that.

Imagine opening a project and being able to ask:

Show me the three decisions that changed this most.

Show me where the human overruled the agent.

Show me where the agent caught the human.

Show me the assumptions that remain unresolved.

Show me the strongest discarded alternative.

Now history becomes navigable.

This is not merely recordkeeping.

It is pedagogy.

The workshop can teach from its own scars.

There is a risk, of course, that total traceability becomes surveillance. People need private thought. Early speculation should not always become permanent organizational evidence. A culture that records every unfinished idea can make people afraid to think badly in public.

Leonardo's notebooks were private working material, not performance logs for a manager.

We should preserve that distinction.

The goal is not total observability of people.

It is sufficient observability of consequential decisions.

A healthy workshop has scratch space.

It also has a record.

Confusing the two will either destroy privacy or destroy memory.

Agents complicate this because their scratch work can be voluminous and difficult to interpret. Some systems expose hidden reasoning; others do not; in either case, raw internal process is not automatically reliable explanation. What matters operationally is the evidence and actions we can inspect: sources consulted, tools used, tests run, files changed, decisions escalated.

The workshop should preserve externally meaningful traces.

That is enough to reconstruct accountability without pretending we possess a transcript of cognition.

There is another reason unfinished work matters.

It protects possibility.

A polished final answer exerts authority. People hesitate to reopen it. A visible sketch invites intervention.

This means the workshop should choose the right finish level for the stage.

Do not bring a board-ready deck to the meeting where the strategy is supposed to be questioned.

Do not show a pixel-perfect interface when the team still needs to debate the workflow.

Do not ask an agent for final prose when the argument is unsettled.

The polish changes social behavior.

People critique rough work differently.

They feel permission.

The Renaissance sketch had this property physically. Charcoal and ink announce provisionality in a way a completed altarpiece does not.

Digital work needs equivalent signals.

Draft.

Hypothesis.

Simulation.

Unverified.

Candidate.

Ready for decision.

Approved.

These states sound mundane, but they prevent one of generative AI's most dangerous tricks: making every stage look final.

A fluent model collapses the visual distance between ignorance and expertise.

The workflow must put that distance back.

This may be one of the defining design challenges of the next decade. We need interfaces that show epistemic maturity, not just textual polish.

A draft should look like a draft because it behaves like one.

A source-backed conclusion should show its sources.

A high-risk uncertainty should not be hidden by typography.

A machine recommendation should carry the evidence needed to challenge it.

The status of the work becomes part of the work.

Leonardo's unfinished panel gives us this gift accidentally. We cannot mistake it for complete. The object tells us where the artist stopped.

Our agentic systems should be equally honest.

They should know the difference between something generated and something earned.

Humans should know it too.

The unfinished is not automatically noble.

Sometimes it is procrastination.

Sometimes it is abandonment.

Sometimes it is the last surviving record of the moment when a mind changed direction.

The master's job is to know which one is in front of him.

Finish what needs to be finished.

Preserve what needs to be remembered.

And never let polish erase the evidence that made the final work trustworthy.
