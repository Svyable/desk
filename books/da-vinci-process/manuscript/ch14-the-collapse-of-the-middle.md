# The Collapse of the Middle

Every profession has work it secretly depends on and publicly disrespects.

The junior work.

The first draft.

The note-taking.

The simple research.

The routine code.

The basic analysis.

The customer question everyone senior has answered a hundred times.

The task is low status because an experienced person can do it easily.

The task is valuable because an inexperienced person cannot become experienced without doing some version of it.

This is the apprenticeship ladder.

Artificial intelligence is arriving with a saw.

The first jobs to become easy to automate are often exactly the jobs organizations have used to train people.

This is not because AI is malicious.

It is because the economics are obvious.

If a senior lawyer can ask an agent for a first-pass research memo, why pay a junior associate to spend six hours assembling one?

If a software engineer can ask an agent to implement a routine feature, why give it to the new hire?

If a consulting team can generate the first deck structure in minutes, why have an analyst build it overnight?

If customer-support software can suggest the answer immediately, why make a new representative struggle through the knowledge base?

The short-run answer is compelling.

Do the work faster.

The long-run question is harder.

Where do seniors come from?

The old system was inefficient in ways that accidentally trained people. A junior worker did low-value production while absorbing high-value judgment. The organization paid for output and received development as a side effect.

Agents separate the two.

They can eliminate the production without automatically replacing the development.

This is one of the least discussed risks of successful automation because the damage arrives late.

The first year looks wonderful.

Senior people become more productive.

Head count grows more slowly.

Margins improve.

The junior pipeline shrinks.

Nothing breaks because the institution is living on experts trained by the old system.

Five years later, there are fewer people ready for the work that was never automated.

Ten years later, the organization discovers that the ladder was also a supply chain.

This is not a prediction that every profession will face a shortage. Some roles will change completely. Some senior tasks will automate too. New entry paths will appear. Workers may become capable faster with AI assistance.

The point is structural.

Whenever automation removes a task, ask whether the task was doing hidden developmental work.

If it was, replace the development intentionally.

Do not assume it comes free with the tool.

The Renaissance workshop makes this obvious because training was visible in the organization. Apprentices were apprentices. The fact that they were not yet masters was not embarrassing. Their assignments reflected a developmental state.

Modern professional life has become oddly ashamed of apprenticeship.

We hire "analysts," "associates," "coordinators," "junior engineers," "residents," "fellows," but often evaluate them as smaller versions of fully formed professionals. Then we complain that they need mentoring.

The agentic era may force us to recover explicit developmental design.

A new worker with a capable agent is not simply a cheaper senior worker.

The pair is a different system.

The human may be able to produce senior-looking artifacts immediately. That can accelerate learning if the senior-looking artifact is used as a comparison, a critique target, and a doorway into harder questions.

It can also conceal that the human has not built the underlying model.

This is the synthetic seniority problem.

Output outruns formation.

A firm that mistakes the output for competence will promote people into decisions they cannot audit.

A firm that distrusts all AI-assisted work will waste leverage and train people on obsolete scarcity.

The middle path is harder: separate performance support from capability assessment.

Let the agent help do the work.

Periodically test whether the human can reason about the work.

This does not require theatrical closed-book exams. It requires moments where the employee has to demonstrate judgment without leaning on the same support that produced the artifact.

Explain why this source is credible.

Show where this code will fail under a changed assumption.

Reconstruct the model.

Defend the recommendation against an expert objection.

Identify what the agent did badly.

Choose between two plausible outputs before seeing the ranking.

The test should resemble the responsibility the person is expected to hold.

If the future job is to supervise agents, then "can perform every subtask manually" may be the wrong standard.

If the future job requires detecting agent failure, then "can explain the system well enough to catch one" may be essential.

This is how apprenticeship evolves rather than disappears.

The old ladder was task-based.

The new ladder may become judgment-based.

Beginners first learn to distinguish correct from plausible.

Then they learn to frame commissions.

Then to allocate work.

Then to critique.

Then to integrate conflicting evidence.

Then to carry responsibility for increasingly consequential outcomes.

The production underneath those stages can be heavily agent-assisted.

This could create a faster path to mastery.

It could also create frauds.

The difference will be difficult to see because both produce impressive output.

Organizations will need new signals of development.

One signal is error recognition.

Show a flawed machine output and see what the person notices.

Another is transfer.

Can the person apply the principle to a case that looks different?

Another is explanation under pressure.

Can the person state what is known, what is inferred, and what is uncertain when a senior stakeholder wants a simple answer?

Another is refusal.

Can the person say the evidence is not strong enough to act?

These are not soft skills.

They are the hard layer of agentic work.

The irony is that automation may increase their economic value while making them harder to train.

This is the collapse of the middle.

At the bottom, machines can make novices productive.

At the top, experts can become extraordinarily leveraged.

The missing region is the long sequence of tasks through which novices used to become experts.

We need to build a new region.

Education will be tempted to solve this by banning assistance.

That is understandable and insufficient.

Students should sometimes work without AI for the same reason musicians practice scales without an automated accompanist doing the difficult passage. But a school that trains entirely without the tools people will use at work risks teaching an obsolete form of purity.

The better objective is dual competence.

Can you think without the agent where unaided thinking builds the necessary model?

Can you think with the agent where collaboration creates greater reach?

Can you tell which mode the situation requires?

That third skill is new.

It should be taught.

Professional firms face an even harder version because they have economic incentives to skip training. A law firm can bill or price work in ways that make junior development visible. A software company may feel direct pressure to replace entry-level tasks. A consulting firm may discover that clients no longer want to pay for teams of analysts doing work a model can accelerate.

The business model of apprenticeship is changing.

Someone still has to finance formation.

Historically, clients often paid indirectly. The junior worked on the matter while learning. Firms absorbed some inefficiency because the pipeline produced future partners.

If clients refuse to pay for training and automation removes the productive justification, firms may have to treat apprenticeship as explicit capital investment.

That will feel expensive because the old subsidy disappears.

It may be necessary.

A company that would spend millions training a model may need to ask why training a human expert is treated as avoidable overhead.

The comparison is not sentimental.

Experts are infrastructure.

If the organization needs human judgment at the top, it must maintain the pipeline that creates it.

There is a second route.

Agents themselves can become apprenticeship infrastructure.

This is where the story becomes more hopeful.

A human mentor is scarce. A good one has limited time. Feedback arrives late because reviewing junior work competes with the mentor's own work.

An agent can review instantly.

It can create exercises from real organizational problems.

It can adapt difficulty.

It can remember recurring mistakes.

It can simulate clients, adversaries, examiners, and edge cases.

It can expose the learner to far more variation than a human mentor can generate.

The human expert then spends time where human expertise matters most: diagnosing the learner, transmitting tacit standards, discussing ambiguous cases, modeling responsibility.

The ratio of master attention to apprentice learning can improve.

This is a major opportunity.

It will be missed if companies deploy AI only to remove juniors.

The same technology can increase the output of the existing experts or increase the rate at which new experts are formed.

The obvious financial return favors the first.

The long-run institutional return may depend on the second.

This is a capital allocation problem.

How much current productivity are we willing to spend on future judgment?

The answer will vary by domain.

In some tasks, preserving human skill is irrational. Nobody needs an apprenticeship in manual invoice matching if machines can do it reliably and the skill has little transfer value.

In other tasks, the underlying practice trains perception that the senior role still needs.

A young reporter who never calls sources may become excellent at summarizing documents and weak at detecting when a person is lying.

A young physician who sees fewer patients because the system mediates every interaction may know more information and develop less bedside judgment.

A young engineer who never debugs may become fast at feature generation and weak during an incident.

A young investor who never builds a model may be able to query every ratio and fail to feel where the assumptions are doing the work.

We need to know which repetitions matter.

That is a research question, not a nostalgic opinion.

Professions should study their own formation.

Which junior tasks predict later expert performance?

Which can be removed with no loss?

Which can be simulated?

Which require real consequence?

Which can be compressed by AI?

Which should be protected precisely because they are slow?

The answers will surprise people because professions contain rituals disguised as training and training disguised as drudgery.

Agents give us the chance to separate them.

The historical apprenticeship system did not get this distinction right automatically. Apprentices performed chores because workshops needed chores done. Exploitation and formation were intertwined. Nobody should recreate bad labor merely because it once trained skill.

The goal is not to preserve junior suffering.

It is to preserve the mechanism by which judgment is made.

That mechanism often includes exposure, correction, variation, responsibility, and time.

Time is the hardest part.

There is a fantasy that AI will compress years of experience into weeks because information can be delivered faster.

Some learning will compress dramatically.

Consequence does not always.

You can simulate a negotiation. It is not identical to having the relationship depend on your next sentence.

You can study a hundred incidents. It is not identical to being responsible during one at 3 a.m.

You can generate a thousand diagnostic cases. It is not identical to looking at a real patient whose family is waiting.

Humans learn differently when the outcome matters.

That weight should not be fetishized or manufactured irresponsibly. It does mean that some forms of apprenticeship require progressive real responsibility.

The workshop has to let the apprentice touch the work.

This returns us to Verrocchio.

The young Leonardo was not placed in a classroom adjacent to the workshop until he passed a mastery exam. He entered the production environment.

He learned inside consequence.

The new workshop should do the same.

Give novices real commissions at a scale where failure is recoverable.

Let agents support them.

Let masters inspect.

Increase scope when judgment earns it.

This is how the middle can be rebuilt.

Not as a museum of obsolete tasks.

As a designed gradient of responsibility.

The most dangerous organization will be the one with enormous machine leverage at the bottom and a thin layer of aging human expertise at the top.

It will look efficient.

It will also be consuming its seed corn.

A healthier organization will use agents to widen access to difficult work while preserving the developmental sequence that makes a person capable of owning it.

That may require slower moments inside a faster system.

Protected practice.

Independent judgment before assistance.

Rotation through failures.

Time with experts.

Real decisions small enough to survive mistakes.

These are investments in future masters.

The language matters.

If we call every junior task "work that AI should eliminate," we will eliminate some training without noticing.

If we call every old task "essential apprenticeship," we will defend waste.

The master has to distinguish.

That is becoming one of the master's central jobs.

A Renaissance workshop measured success partly by whether it could continue beyond one pair of hands.

The modern workshop should too.

The labor-market evidence is still early, but the pressure is no longer purely theoretical.

The 2026 Stanford AI Index reports that the effects of AI are appearing unevenly and points in particular to hiring pipelines and younger workers in exposed occupations. It notes a sharp decline in employment among young software developers over the preceding period while also emphasizing that broad economy-wide job loss has not appeared in the same way.

That is exactly the kind of signal that should make apprenticeship a live research problem.

It does not prove AI caused every change in young developer employment. Labor markets move for many reasons. Technology hiring went through a broader cycle. The data does not allow a simple story.

But the location of pressure matters.

Entry points are where institutions can change first.

A company does not have to fire the senior workforce to weaken the apprenticeship ladder.

It can simply stop hiring the next cohort.

Nothing looks broken immediately.

The age distribution changes quietly.

This is why organizations should track formation metrics alongside automation metrics.

How many juniors are entering?

What work are they doing?

How quickly are they acquiring independent judgment?

Which senior roles depend on experience the new pipeline no longer supplies?

A workforce plan that counts only current productivity will miss the depletion.

This is especially important in professions with long formation periods.

Medicine.

Advanced engineering.

Research.

Skilled trades.

Law.

Fields where ten years of apparent efficiency can create a future bottleneck that takes another decade to repair.

The agentic workshop needs a succession model.

Not because every existing job should be preserved.

Because every required capability should have a formation path.

This is a more precise way to think about workforce transition.

Do not ask only which jobs disappear.

Ask which capabilities the future system still needs humans to possess and how those humans will acquire them.

Maybe the senior software engineer of 2035 writes far less code manually.

Fine.

How does that engineer learn architecture, debugging, operational judgment, security instinct, and the social reality of maintaining systems?

Maybe the radiologist of 2035 spends less time screening normal images.

Fine.

How does the physician develop the perceptual model needed to challenge the system on the rare case?

Maybe the lawyer generates routine research instantly.

Fine.

How does the lawyer learn which authority matters and how facts change doctrine?

These are design questions.

Professions can answer them deliberately.

One method is simulation.

Another is rotation through real cases with narrow responsibility.

Another is apprenticeship around exceptions rather than routine.

Another is periodic unaided work.

Another is human review of machine failures specifically selected for training value.

The future apprentice may spend less time doing the ordinary case and more time studying the boundary.

This could create better experts.

Routine volume has always been a crude teacher. A physician sees thousands of ordinary cases to gain enough exposure to recognize the unusual. An agent can help select and explain cases more intelligently.

The training distribution can be designed.

But selection creates another danger.

The learner can become a connoisseur of edge cases without understanding normal reality.

A security engineer who studies only attacks may design unusable systems.

A doctor trained only on pathology can lose calibration about prevalence.

An investor obsessed with spectacular failures may see catastrophe everywhere.

The workshop needs representative experience and adversarial experience.

The mix matters.

Again the master becomes curriculum designer.

This is one reason the phrase "AI replaces junior work" is too crude.

Junior work contains several components.

Some are clerical.

Some are exposure.

Some are repetition.

Some are trust-building.

Some are socialization into professional norms.

Some are simply exploitation justified as training.

Agents allow the components to be separated.

That is an opportunity for reform.

The law firm no longer needs to pretend that endless document formatting is character-building.

The hospital can question whether residents need certain administrative burdens to become better doctors.

The consulting firm can stop confusing sleep deprivation with apprenticeship.

Remove the waste.

Keep the formation.

This may make early careers better.

It will require institutions to pay explicitly for development rather than hiding the cost inside low-status production.

That is a difficult transition because training has always been easier to fund when it also generates billable or sellable output.

The economic model may need to change.

Firms could treat apprenticeship as capital expenditure.

Professional associations could share training infrastructure.

Schools and employers could cooperate more closely.

Agent-based simulation could reduce the marginal cost of practice enough to make deliberate training financially plausible.

The important thing is to recognize that formation is an asset.

Accounting rarely captures it well.

The workshop cannot afford to ignore it.

There is also a distributional question.

If the best apprenticeship environments become proprietary, the labor market could split between people who have access to high-quality agentic formation and people who have access only to generic tools.

The first group gets real cases, expert feedback, organizational memory, calibrated simulations, and increasingly consequential responsibility.

The second gets answers.

Both appear AI-enabled.

Their trajectories diverge.

This is the new apprenticeship inequality.

It will not show up simply in who pays for a premium model. The differentiator is the surrounding workshop.

Data.

Examples.

Mentors.

Feedback.

Permission to touch real work.

A company with strong formation infrastructure can become a talent compounding machine.

A company that uses agents only to reduce head count may become a talent liquidation machine.

The short-run financial statements may favor the second.

The long-run capability may favor the first.

This is exactly the kind of tradeoff markets can underprice because the asset being consumed is intangible and delayed.

Executives will need to make the cost visible deliberately.

What expert roles are we consuming faster than we are replenishing them?

What judgment depends on tacit experience?

Where has the junior pipeline already narrowed?

Which skills can be simulated and which require real responsibility?

These questions belong in workforce planning as much as salary and head count.

There is no reason to preserve a twentieth-century career ladder if a better one can be built.

The new ladder may be shorter.

It may be steeper.

It may branch.

It may allow a young worker to reach consequential problems earlier because the agent handles the clerical perimeter.

That could be extraordinary.

Imagine a junior scientist spending less time formatting data and more time thinking about experimental design.

A young lawyer spending less time locating cases and more time understanding arguments.

A new programmer spending less time on boilerplate and more time inspecting failure.

The collapse of the middle can become the reconstruction of the middle.

But only if the institution notices that a gap has opened.

The market will automate the old ladder automatically.

Nobody will build the new one automatically.

That is the master's responsibility.

A system that produces great work today and nobody capable of judging it tomorrow is not productive.

It is liquidating.
