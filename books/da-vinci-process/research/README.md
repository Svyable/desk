# Research trail — Da Vinci Process

This research trail separates four kinds of material that the manuscript deliberately brings into contact:

1. documented Renaissance workshop practice and conservation evidence;
2. later biographical stories about Leonardo and Verrocchio;
3. empirical research on contemporary human-AI work;
4. Sven Hardy Benson's interpretive synthesis about a future human-agent workshop.

The fourth category is the argument of the book. It should never be retroactively attributed to Leonardo or presented as something Renaissance artists "already knew" about artificial intelligence.

## Historical spine

### Verrocchio, Leonardo, and the Baptism

**Uffizi Galleries — The Baptism of Christ**  
https://www.uffizi.it/en/artworks/verrocchio-leonardo-baptism-of-christ

The Uffizi dates the work to about 1470–1475 and identifies it jointly with Andrea del Verrocchio and Leonardo da Vinci. Its description explicitly attributes to Giorgio Vasari the story that the young Leonardo painted the angel on the left with such skill that the older Verrocchio was upset. Use this as an attributed sixteenth-century story, not direct evidence of Verrocchio's inner reaction.

**Uffizi — In the Light of Angels**  
https://www.uffizi.it/en/online-exhibitions/in-the-light-of-angels

Useful for the workshop context and the identification of Leonardo's contribution to the left-hand angel. The page also notes that multiple students are said to have collaborated on the painting.

**National Gallery — Verrocchio unveiled**  
https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/learn-about-art/paintings-in-depth/paintings-in-depth-verrocchio-unveiled

Useful corrective to the lone-genius story. The National Gallery emphasizes Verrocchio's importance as a teacher and notes Leonardo, Perugino, Lorenzo di Credi, and probably Botticelli among the painters associated with his training circle. It also emphasizes the attribution difficulty created by workshop collaboration.

**Metropolitan Museum of Art — Workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, Madonna and Child**  
https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/437892

The Met describes Verrocchio as running the most prestigious Florentine workshop of the last third of the fifteenth century and names Leonardo among those associated with it. Useful for the shop as an institution whose designs could circulate through other hands.

### What a workshop and pupil were

**National Gallery glossary — Workshop**  
https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/glossary/workshop

Concise definition: the workshop was the place where a medieval or Renaissance painter carried out work with apprentices and assistants; a chief assistant might execute a painting without the master's participation.

**National Gallery glossary — Pupil**  
https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/glossary/pupil

Useful for the master-apprentice training relationship, the role of the studio, fixed terms of apprenticeship, and the guild-regulated path by which a pupil could become a master.

**Getty — The Art of Learning to Draw**  
https://www.getty.edu/news/history-of-drawing-classes/

Useful for concrete apprenticeship practice: day-to-day shop work, material preparation, instruction, access to live projects, informal hierarchies in larger studios, and the progression toward independent practice. Also useful for the broader point that large projects were possible because masters supervised capable teams.

**National Gallery — Room 60, Florence 1470–1500**  
https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/visiting/floorplans/level-2/room-60

The Gallery states directly that collaboration was key to workshop success and that masters and juniors often worked in partnership even on small-scale projects.

### Drawing, notebooks, and process

**Royal Collection Trust — The Life of Leonardo da Vinci**  
https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/leonardo-in-the-royal-collection/the-life-of-leonardo-da-vinci

Useful for Leonardo's incessant drawing as a way to devise projects, explore nature, and record imagination, and for the transfer of drawings and notes to Francesco Melzi.

**Royal Collection Trust — Leonardo da Vinci: A Life in Drawing**  
https://www.rct.uk/collection/publications/leonardo-da-vinci-a-life-in-drawing

The Royal Collection describes drawing as the activity that unified Leonardo's varied interests: he drew to develop projects, record perceptions, explore imagination, and think. Useful for the book's claim that external artifacts are not paperwork around cognition; they are part of the process.

**Biblioteca Ambrosiana — Codex Atlanticus**  
https://www.ambrosiana.it/en/discover/masterpieces/codex-atlanticus/

Primary collection context for the largest surviving collection of Leonardo's writings and drawings. Useful for concrete examples of engineering, measurement, machines, and visual thinking.

**Royal Collection Trust — Leonardo's Study of Anatomy**  
https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/leonardo-in-the-royal-collection/leonardos-study-of-anatomy

Useful for Leonardo's iterative anatomy work, the integration of drawing and investigation, and the important counterexample that extraordinary private work can fail to compound socially when it is not completed and published.

### Unfinished work and visible revision

**Uffizi — Adoration of the Magi**  
https://www.uffizi.it/en/artworks/leonardo-adoration-of-the-magi

Documents the 1481 commission, Leonardo's departure for Milan, the unfinished state, and the visibly different levels of completion across the panel.

**Uffizi / Opificio restoration note — Understanding the non-finito**  
https://www.uffizi.it/news/il-restauro-dell-adorazione-dei-magi-di-leonardo-da-vinci-capire-il-non-finito

Detailed conservation account, in Italian, describing repeated contours, revisions, changed positions, underdrawing, washes, and alterations directly on the panel. This is a particularly strong source for the chapter on iteration because the physical object preserves decisions that a finished surface would have concealed.

### Melzi and transmission

**National Gallery — Leonardo's Legacy: Francesco Melzi and the Leonardeschi**  
https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/whats-on/leonardo-s-legacy-francesco-melzi-and-the-leonardeschi

Useful for Melzi joining Leonardo's Milanese workshop around 1508, following him to Rome and France, and inheriting Leonardo's notebooks and drawings. The Gallery emphasizes Melzi's role in their survival.

## Modern human-AI evidence

### Productivity and diffusion of expertise

**Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond — Generative AI at Work, NBER working paper / QJE publication**  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161  
https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/generative-ai-work

Study population: customer-support agents. The published Stanford summary reports a roughly 15 percent average productivity increase, with larger gains among less experienced and lower-skilled workers and smaller gains among the most experienced. The authors find evidence consistent with AI helping disseminate patterns associated with stronger workers. This is highly relevant to apprenticeship and knowledge diffusion, but it is not a universal estimate for knowledge work.

**Dell'Acqua et al. — Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier**  
https://aiinstitute.hbs.edu/navigating-the-jagged-technological-frontier/

Field experiment with 758 BCG consultants. On tasks inside the model's capability frontier, the HBS summary reports faster completion and higher human-rated performance; on tasks outside it, reliance can hurt. The paper introduced the memorable "centaur" and "cyborg" patterns of dividing versus interweaving human and AI work. The book uses this as evidence that workflow configuration matters.

**Harvard Business School AI Institute — The Cybernetic Teammate**  
https://aiinstitute.hbs.edu/the-cybernetic-teammate-how-ai-is-reshaping-collaboration-and-expertise-in-the-workplace/

Field-experiment research at Procter & Gamble on generative AI, collaboration, and expertise. Useful for examining whether AI can reproduce some benefits of team composition and cross-functional knowledge sharing. Claims in the manuscript should follow the study's actual task design rather than generalize from the headline.

### Critical thinking, oversight, and skill

**Microsoft Research — The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking**  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/

Survey of 319 knowledge workers contributing 936 real-world examples. Higher confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking; higher self-confidence with more. Participants described a shift toward verification, integration, and task stewardship. Because this is self-reported observational evidence, the manuscript should not treat it as proof that AI causally erodes critical thinking.

**Microsoft Research — Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI**  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/shifting-work-patterns-with-generative-ai/

Cross-industry randomized field experiment involving roughly 6,000 workers. Useful for measured changes in email and document work and for the distinction between individually changeable behaviors and coordination-dependent organizational routines.

### Real-world usage and agents

**Anthropic Economic Index — February 2025**  
https://www.anthropic.com/news/the-anthropic-economic-index

Based on anonymized Claude.ai usage in Anthropic's product population. The initial report found more augmentation than automation in its classification and heavy concentration in software and writing-related tasks. Treat as vendor telemetry, not a census of the economy.

**Anthropic Economic Index — Cadences, June 2026**  
https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report

Useful for the shift from chat sessions toward longer-running agentic work in Claude Code and Cowork and for the report's continued distinction between automation and augmentation. Again, the population is users of Anthropic products.

**Stanford AI Index 2026 — Technical Performance**  
https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/technical-performance

Useful current constraint on the future-facing argument. The report describes substantial improvement in agentic computer-use benchmarks while also documenting persistent failure rates. The manuscript should use this combination — rapid capability growth plus unreliability — rather than a simple "agents are here" claim.

**Stanford AI Index 2026 — Economy**  
https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy

Useful for adoption, productivity evidence, early workforce effects, and the still-limited deployment of agents across many business functions. This prevents the book from narrating a completed transition that is still unfolding.

## Claims to avoid

- "Verrocchio stopped painting forever because Leonardo was better" as an established historical fact.
- "Leonardo invented human-agent collaboration."
- "Renaissance masters merely signed work their apprentices made." Workshop participation varied by object, commission, master, assistant, and period.
- "AI makes novices experts." Some studies show disproportionate gains for less experienced workers on specific tasks; expertise is broader than task performance.
- "AI always augments rather than automates." Usage classifications change by product, task, and period.
- "The human will always be the master." This is exactly the proposition the book tests rather than assumes.
