# Da Vinci Process

*Masters, Apprentices, and the Human-Agent Renaissance*

The lone-genius version of Leonardo da Vinci is almost perfectly wrong for the age arriving now.

Before the notebooks, machines, anatomy, and paintings that made his name shorthand for genius, Leonardo was an apprentice inside Andrea del Verrocchio's Florentine workshop—a place where commissions, materials, technique, correction, reputation, and several pairs of hands had to become one finished object. The famous Vasari story in which Leonardo paints an angel so beautifully that Verrocchio gives up painting is treated here as what it is: a later story. The documented workshop beneath it is more consequential.

*Da Vinci Process* argues that the Renaissance bottega offers a better model for human-agent collaboration than either the obedient-tool story or the replacement story. Capable agents can research, draft, code, compare, test, criticize, remember, and increasingly act. The human role does not survive by pretending to be superior at every local task. It becomes more demanding: set the commission, cultivate standards, allocate work, preserve context, detect drift, integrate competing truths, decide where friction belongs, remain teachable when the apprentice is better, and carry responsibility for the whole.

The book treats "Da Vinci Process" as Sven Hardy Benson's modern synthesis, not a historical term. It separates documented Renaissance workshop practice from later biography, current empirical human-AI research from vendor telemetry, and present evidence from forward-looking interpretation.

Its central wager is that the next renaissance will not be measured by the quantity of automated output. It will be measured by whether human-machine workshops make people and institutions capable of attempting harder work without surrendering judgment, apprenticeship, memory, authorship, or responsibility.
