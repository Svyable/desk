# The Advisory

Every U.S. Surgeon General inherits a peculiar kind of authority: enough institutional gravity that an advisory bearing the office's seal gets covered by every major news outlet in the country, and almost none of the enforcement power that would let the office do anything about what the advisory describes. Surgeons general have used that gravity before to reshape how Americans think about a risk hiding in plain sight — the 1964 report on smoking and health is the standard reference point, credited with beginning the decades-long process that turned a habit practiced by nearly half of American adults into one practiced by roughly one in ten. That earlier report, issued under Surgeon General Luther Terry after a specially convened advisory committee spent more than a year reviewing thousands of studies on tobacco's health effects, did not itself ban anything or regulate anything; it was, like Murthy's advisory nearly sixty years later, a document with no direct legal force. What it did was establish an official, evidence-reviewed government position authoritative enough that Congress could not easily ignore it, and within roughly a year lawmakers had passed the Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act requiring warning labels on cigarette packages, with a ban on broadcast cigarette advertising following in 1971. The comparison matters for understanding what kind of document Murthy's advisory actually is and is not: it is the opening move in a slower institutional process rather than a policy in itself, a formal, evidence-anchored statement designed to give later, more concrete interventions — by Congress, by employers, by insurers, by the ministries examined later in this book — an authoritative foundation to build on, its influence measured over years and decades rather than in the news cycle immediately following its release.

A different precedent from the same office suggests what more direct, immediate government communication about a stigmatized health condition can look like, for comparison against the more conventional advisory format Murthy chose. In May 1988, Surgeon General C. Everett Koop mailed an eight-page pamphlet titled "Understanding AIDS" to all 107 million households in the United States, the largest single mailing in American history and the first time the federal government had sent explicit information about sexual transmission and condom use directly into every home in the country, over considerable objection from social conservatives within the same administration that had authorized it. Koop's mailer was possible in part because AIDS by 1988 was understood, correctly, as directly and acutely fatal in a way that made blunt, universal distribution feel proportionate to the stakes; loneliness, whatever its measured mortality associations, does not present with the same visible urgency, and no comparable household-by-household mailing has accompanied Murthy's advisory or seems likely to. The comparison is offered here not to suggest Murthy's office made the wrong strategic choice — a mailer is a blunt instrument suited to an emergency with a clear, single preventive behavior to communicate, and loneliness has neither the acuity nor the simple behavioral fix that made Koop's approach fit its subject — but to mark, honestly, how much more institutionally cautious and diffuse the loneliness advisory's mode of communication has been relative to the most forceful tool the same office has used before, a caution that likely reflects the underlying condition's genuinely different character as much as it reflects any lack of institutional seriousness about the problem. In May 2023, Vivek Murthy, serving his second, non-consecutive term as Surgeon General, used the same institutional tool on a subject that had never before received it: loneliness.

The document, titled "Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation," runs to roughly eighty pages and reads less like a press release than like a literature review with a public mandate attached. It compiles decades of peer-reviewed research into a single narrative arc: loneliness and social isolation are common among American adults; they are measurably associated with worse physical and mental health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, stroke, dementia, depression, anxiety, and premature death; and the country's civic, economic, and technological infrastructure has been moving, gradually and mostly without anyone deciding to do it this way, in a direction that produces more of both. Murthy did not invent any of the underlying findings. His contribution, and the reason the document became a genuine cultural event rather than another government report filed and forgotten, was to gather research that had been sitting in separate academic silos — cardiology, gerontology, sociology, workplace psychology — and assert, with the weight of his office, that it described one coherent problem large enough to deserve a name and a coordinated response.

The single line from the advisory that traveled furthest, reprinted in nearly every news story about the report and repeated in commencement speeches, corporate wellness newsletters, and dating-app marketing copy in the years since, is the claim that the mortality impact of being socially disconnected is comparable to smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day. It is worth tracing that number back to its actual source, because the number's own history is a small case study in how a careful piece of research becomes a much blunter public fact by the time it finishes traveling.

The comparison originates with Julianne Holt-Lunstad, a psychologist at Brigham Young University, and her coauthors' 2010 meta-analysis in *PLoS Medicine*, "Social Relationships and Mortality Risk." Holt-Lunstad's team pooled data from 148 separate studies, covering more than 308,000 participants followed for an average of seven and a half years, and found that people with stronger social relationships had, on average, a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the study period than people with weaker ones — an effect that held up after controlling for age, sex, initial health status, and cause of death. Fifty percent is a large effect in epidemiological terms, comparable in scale to some of the strongest known mortality risk factors, and Holt-Lunstad's team reached for a familiar benchmark to make that scale legible to a general audience: they compared it to the well-established mortality risk associated with smoking. That is where the cigarette figure comes from. It is not a measurement of loneliness specifically. It is a rhetorical benchmark applied to a broad measure of social relationship strength — network size, frequency of contact, perceived support, and more — bundled together in the 2010 analysis rather than isolated into loneliness alone.

Holt-Lunstad returned to the question five years later with a more precise instrument. In a 2015 paper in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, she and her coauthors separated the bundled 2010 construct into three distinct risk factors and calculated an effect size for each: an odds ratio of 1.29 for objective social isolation, 1.26 for subjective loneliness, and 1.32 for living alone. Translated out of the statistical shorthand, these numbers mean something closer to a 26 to 32 percent increase in mortality risk for each factor individually — real, replicated, worth taking seriously, and also meaningfully smaller than the aggregate 50 percent figure that produced the fifteen-cigarette comparison in the first place. The 2015 paper additionally found something that rarely makes it into the popular retelling: the effects were consistently observed across sex, follow-up duration, and world region, but were actually *larger* in study samples with an average age under 65 than in older samples — the opposite of the intuitive assumption that loneliness is primarily a problem of old age, and a detail this book will return to when it examines companion technologies later, most of which are marketed to, and used disproportionately by, younger people.

The most useful correction to the popular fifteen-cigarette framing, though, comes from Holt-Lunstad herself. In 2023, writing with Timothy Smith and Ichiro Kawachi in the *American Journal of Epidemiology*, in a paper titled "Benchmarking Social Isolation, Loneliness, and Smoking: Challenges and Opportunities for Public Health," the researchers who had originated the comparison published an explicit caution about how it had come to be used. The smoking analogy, they wrote, is useful for raising public awareness of an underappreciated risk, but it "often oversimplifies the evidence" — flattening a set of distinct, differently sized effects into one viral-ready number, and risking a framing that treats loneliness primarily as an individual habit to be corrected, similar to quitting cigarettes, rather than as a population-level condition shaped by housing costs, work schedules, city design, and family structure, requiring population-level responses. It is a rare thing in public-health communication: the authors of a statistic publicly asking the public to hold their own famous finding a little more loosely than the public has been holding it. This book tries to honor that request rather than repeat the simplified version because it makes a better opening line.

Beyond the aggregate mortality figures this chapter has already dissected, the advisory also drew on separate strands of the epidemiological literature to attach specific, disease-level risk estimates to social disconnection, and these narrower figures are worth reporting alongside the more famous aggregate ones because they show the association holding up across genuinely distinct biological pathways rather than resting on a single statistical artifact. The advisory cites research associating inadequate social connection with roughly a 29 percent increased risk of heart disease, a 32 percent increased risk of stroke, and a 50 percent increased risk of developing dementia — three separate conditions, driven by different underlying physiology, each independently studied by different research teams using different cohorts, converging on broadly similar elevated risk. The dementia figure in particular sits at the center of a growing, still-developing area of research into whether sustained social engagement functions as a form of cognitive reserve, exercising neural pathways in ways that may help the brain compensate for age-related decline, though the advisory itself was careful to frame this as an area of continuing investigation rather than a settled causal mechanism, consistent with the same caution this book has tried to apply throughout to every claim about mechanism rather than mere association.

None of this qualification changes the advisory's central, harder-to-dispute claim, which the 2015 paper's separated effect sizes actually support on their own, more modest terms: loneliness, social isolation, and living alone are each independently associated with a meaningfully elevated risk of death, at effect sizes epidemiologists take seriously for far less well-known risk factors. The Surgeon General's advisory built its recommendations on that more careful foundation — a six-pillar framework for rebuilding social connection through strengthened social infrastructure, public policy, workplace and educational reform, and a shift in cultural norms around prioritizing relationships — while its most quoted line, ironically, was the one furthest from that careful foundation. That irony is itself instructive: a genuinely precise finding rarely survives contact with a news cycle looking for a single sentence, and the sentence that survives is usually the most dramatic simplification available, not the most accurate one.

Beyond its epidemiological review, the advisory itself is organized around a specific set of recommended domains for action rather than a single prescription, and it is worth describing what those recommendations actually asked for rather than leaving the advisory as an abstract source of statistics. It called on employers to treat workplace connection as a measurable dimension of organizational health rather than an incidental perk, on schools to build structured opportunities for relationship-building into curricula rather than treating socialization as something that simply happens in the gaps between instruction, on health systems to screen patients for social isolation the way they already screen for other risk factors, on technology companies to design digital platforms that measurably strengthen rather than merely occupy their users' time, and on community and civic institutions and everyday individuals to actively rebuild the kind of associational density Putnam's data, examined in the previous chapter, showed eroding. Follow-on guidance from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, building directly on the advisory's foundation, went further than the advisory itself in one practical respect: it recommended specific, validated clinical screening instruments physicians could actually use in a routine visit, rather than leaving loneliness as a vague concern with no standardized way to measure it consistently — the Berkman-Syme Social Network Index for assessing objective social isolation, and a shortened three-item version of the UCLA Loneliness Scale examined earlier in this chapter, for assessing subjective loneliness specifically, each brief enough to administer within an ordinary appointment's time constraints. Physician groups have since begun echoing this recommendation directly: reporting in 2026 found doctors describing loneliness and social isolation as deserving a place alongside blood pressure and other routine vital signs in standard primary-care intake, treating the two brief screening instruments as low-cost, low-friction tools capable of surfacing a risk factor a doctor might otherwise never learn about during a visit focused on an unrelated complaint. None of these recommendations came with binding force or dedicated federal funding attached; the advisory is a diagnosis and a set of suggested directions, not an appropriations bill, and Murthy's own public remarks around its release were explicit that turning the document's recommendations into concrete outcomes would depend on employers, schools, health systems, and communities choosing to act on it rather than on any authority the Surgeon General's office itself possesses to compel them.

Murthy's advisory did not appear in a vacuum, and it is worth situating it inside a broader institutional shift that had already been underway internationally for several years by the time it was published. The World Health Organization, which typically reserves its highest-profile coordinated attention for infectious disease, chronic illness, and injury prevention, launched a Commission on Social Connection in November 2023, six months after Murthy's advisory, co-chaired by Murthy himself alongside Chido Mpemba, the African Union's Youth Envoy. The commission's mandate was explicitly modeled on how global health bodies treat other major cross-national risk factors — assembling an international panel of experts, gathering evidence across regions with very different cultural norms around family and community, and producing recommendations meant to travel beyond any single country's policy debate. Its flagship report, released in June 2025 under the title "From Loneliness to Social Connection," estimated that roughly one in six people worldwide experience loneliness, with prevalence highest among adolescents and young adults and in lower-income countries, and offered an estimate — modeled rather than directly measured, and worth treating with the same caution this chapter has just applied to the smoking comparison — that loneliness-associated conditions contribute to roughly 100 deaths per hour globally, on the order of 871,000 deaths a year. In May 2025, the World Health Assembly adopted Resolution WHA78.9, "Fostering social connection for global health," its first resolution specifically addressing the subject, co-sponsored by Spain and Chile and explicitly designed to establish social connection as a standalone global health priority rather than treating it, as global health policy more typically had, as a secondary consideration folded into broader mental-health programming. The resolution formally directs the WHO to develop recommendations and guidance member states can incorporate into their own national health policies, and its framing leaned on the commission's own headline finding — that loneliness and social isolation contribute to an estimated 871,000 deaths worldwide annually — explicitly comparing that death toll to other risk factors global health governance already treats as first-tier public-health priorities, including air pollution, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption, a comparison meant to signal that social disconnection deserved comparable institutional seriousness. In the months following the resolution's adoption, individual member states began citing it directly in their own domestic policy: Ireland, for instance, has pointed to the resolution in aligning its national wellbeing plan, whose six stated goals include tackling loneliness and social exclusion, with a specific nationwide public communications campaign aimed at combating isolation among older residents. A single WHO resolution obligates no member state to any specific spending or program, and this book does not want to overstate its binding force; but the speed with which individual governments began citing it in their own domestic planning within months of adoption suggests the resolution functioned, much as this chapter argued the original 1964 Surgeon General's report on smoking did, as an authoritative reference point other actors could build more concrete policy on top of, rather than as an empty diplomatic gesture.

It would be easy to read all of this institutional activity — the advisory, the commission, the World Health Assembly resolution — as proof that the underlying scientific question is fully settled, and that would be a mistake this book is not going to make, here or later. Public-health institutions issue advisories and convene commissions in response to evidence they judge sufficient to act on, which is a real and meaningful threshold, but it is a different threshold from scientific consensus on every specific mechanism, every specific number, and every specific policy remedy. Chapter sixteen returns to this question directly and takes seriously a genuine body of scholarly pushback: researchers who argue the "epidemic" framing itself may overstate a trend that shows real generational and national variation rather than a uniform global surge, and at least one economist's reanalysis, discussed later with its own significant caveats, suggesting some of the underlying time-use data shows a smaller shift than the more dramatic headline framings imply. Holding both of those things at once — real, replicated, institutionally significant findings, and real, legitimate scientific disagreement about their scale and framing — is more honest than resolving the tension in either direction for the sake of a cleaner story.

What the advisory did accomplish, whatever one makes of its most famous sentence, was to convert a subject that had mostly lived in academic journals and occasional magazine features into an object of formal government attention, with a named cause, an institutional home, and — as the chapters ahead will show — a growing set of businesses, insurers, and technology companies treating it as a durable, addressable market rather than a passing cultural mood. The advisory itself makes no claims about markets. It makes claims about mortality, cardiovascular disease, dementia risk, and the eroding institutions this book's earlier chapters have already begun to trace. But an advisory that reaches this many desks, this many newsrooms, and this many corporate wellness budgets does not stay confined to the register in which it was written. It becomes raw material for everything that gets built on top of it, and the next several chapters follow that raw material into the specific American institutions — the household, the neighborhood, the workplace, the dating market — where the erosion the advisory describes in aggregate terms is actually, individually, being lived.

It is worth naming, before this chapter closes, exactly why a document carrying no direct enforcement power was able to generate this much downstream institutional activity across the chapters ahead. An advisory's influence operates less like a law and more like a shared reference point: once the Surgeon General's office had assembled and formally endorsed a specific body of evidence, that evidence became available for other, independently motivated actors to build on without each one needing to conduct its own literature review from scratch — insurers developing underwriting research examined in chapter ten, employers designing return-to-office policy examined in chapter six, state legislatures debating school phone restrictions examined in chapter fourteen. This is the same mechanism this chapter's earlier comparison to the 1964 smoking report described: the document's job was never to solve the underlying problem directly, but to lower the evidentiary cost for every subsequent actor who wanted to act on it, a form of institutional infrastructure investment whose returns accrue gradually, across many separate downstream decisions, rather than all at once in the advisory's own immediate aftermath.
