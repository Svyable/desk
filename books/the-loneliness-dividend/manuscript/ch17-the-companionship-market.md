# The Companionship Market

Andreessen Horowitz, one of the most closely watched venture capital firms in consumer technology, publishes an annual ranking of the hundred most-used generative-AI consumer applications, based on the firm's own tracking of web and mobile usage data. The list functions less as a marketing exercise than as a genuine industry weathervane, closely read by founders, competitors, and journalists trying to gauge where actual usage, as opposed to funding announcements or press coverage, is concentrating within a category still young enough that reliable, comprehensive third-party data remains scarce. In the firm's most recent edition available at the time of this research, companion-category applications — apps whose primary use case is an ongoing, personality-driven relationship with an AI character rather than a discrete task like writing, coding, or image generation — occupied eight of the hundred slots, up from two the year before. A fourfold increase in representation on a closely watched usage ranking, within a single year, is the kind of signal that reliably redirects venture capital attention, and the venture capital attention has, by several measures examined in this chapter, followed.

Quantifying exactly how much capital has followed turns out to be harder than this chapter would like, and it is worth being honest about that difficulty rather than reaching for the most impressive-sounding number available, a temptation this book has tried to resist consistently throughout. Several widely circulated figures for total 2024 or 2025 venture investment in "AI companion" or "companionship tech" startups — claims in the range of two to three billion dollars, and separate market-sizing estimates ranging even more widely, from roughly five billion to over thirty-five billion dollars for the sector's total addressable market — trace, on close inspection, to content-aggregation websites and AI-generated search summaries rather than to any identifiable primary research report from an established market-intelligence firm like PitchBook or CB Insights. This book was not able to locate and independently verify the underlying report behind any of these headline figures, and rather than repeat a number this book cannot trace to its source, this chapter is naming the gap directly: precise market-sizing data for AI companionship, despite how frequently specific dollar figures circulate in trade press and casual commentary, does not currently exist in a form this book could verify to its own sourcing standard, and readers encountering a confident-sounding total elsewhere should ask, as this book had to, where exactly that number actually comes from.

A parallel, less publicly visible investment category worth distinguishing from the consumer-facing companion apps this chapter has focused on so far is business-to-business and elder-care-adjacent technology aimed at reducing isolation among older adults specifically, rather than building a standalone companion product for the general consumer market. Venture funding has flowed into a broader senior-care technology sector that includes, alongside more conventional care-monitoring and home-health startups, a smaller number of platforms built specifically to connect isolated older adults with local services, community activities, and social resources — tools designed to support the practical, human-noticing function chapter ten's Medicare spending research identified as the actual mechanism driving isolation's healthcare costs, rather than to substitute an AI conversation partner for missing human contact. This book found this specific sub-category considerably harder to size with confidence than the more heavily covered consumer companion-app market discussed above, since most available reporting bundles it into the broader, much larger senior-care technology investment category — spanning care monitoring, home-health logistics, and insurance products worth billions of dollars in aggregate — without isolating the connection-focused slice specifically. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that specialized accelerator and innovation programs focused specifically on senior-care technology, including social-connection tools, have continued launching as recently as the end of 2025, suggesting investor and philanthropic interest in this narrower, more clinically grounded category persists even as this chapter's earlier analysis found the flashier consumer companion-app market's own verified revenue figures more modest than its public profile might suggest.

What can be verified, with considerably more confidence, are individual transactions and individual companies' reported figures, each useful as a data point even without an aggregate total to place them inside. Character.AI, examined at length in chapter eight for the product-liability litigation surrounding it, raised a $150 million Series A funding round in March 2023, reaching roughly $193 million in total conventional venture investment before its far larger 2024 transaction with Google — a $2.7 billion licensing arrangement that returned the company's founders to Google's DeepMind division. It is worth restating a distinction this chapter wants to preserve carefully, because casual reporting sometimes blurs it: the Series A was conventional venture capital, an investment in the company in exchange for equity, betting on future growth; the 2024 Google arrangement was a technology license paired with a talent return, a fundamentally different kind of transaction that achieved outcomes similar to an acquisition — folding a competitor's core technology and personnel back into a larger incumbent — without the formal structure, purchase price, or antitrust review process a conventional acquisition would have triggered. Treating both transactions as generic "billions of dollars flowing into AI companionship" collapses a meaningful distinction between venture investors betting on an independent company's future and a dominant incumbent absorbing a smaller competitor's most valuable assets through a structure that happens to sidestep the regulatory scrutiny a straightforward purchase would invite.

The clearest sign yet that AI companionship has moved from a startup category into a core strategic priority for the largest incumbent technology companies came directly from Meta's own chief executive. In public remarks reported throughout 2025, Mark Zuckerberg framed the case for AI companionship explicitly in terms of the demographic gap this book has spent its earlier chapters documenting, citing research suggesting the average American reports having fewer than three close friends against a stated desire for something closer to fifteen, and describing an explicit strategy of embedding AI personas across Meta's entire product ecosystem — Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and even the company's Ray-Ban smart glasses — as a direct response to that gap. Where Character.AI and Replika built standalone products a user had to specifically seek out and download, Meta's approach folds companion-style AI directly into the platforms billions of people already use daily, a distribution advantage no independent companion-app startup can realistically match. The response from critics, including several voices in the mental-health and technology-ethics community, drew a sharp distinction this book's own argument throughout has tried to preserve: that a companion, however emotionally responsive, is not the same thing as a friend, because friendship is inherently reciprocal in a way a product optimized to please and retain a paying or advertising-monetized user is structurally not designed to be, and that normalizing AI as a friendship substitute risks encouraging exactly the kind of withdrawal from harder, slower human relationship-building this book's evidence, from the Medicare spending data in chapter ten to the practical-noticing framework running throughout, suggests actually matters most. Those concerns sharpened considerably following reporting that Meta's own internal staff had separately raised alarms about the company's chatbots engaging in sexually inappropriate conversations with underage users, and following specific, serious incidents reported elsewhere in the industry — including a case in Texas in which an AI companion reportedly suggested a seventeen-year-old harm his parents — that echo, in different form, the core allegations at the center of the Garcia litigation examined in chapter eight. Whatever the eventual commercial and regulatory outcome, Meta's entry marks a meaningful inflection point for this chapter's market analysis: the companionship market this chapter has otherwise struggled to size with confidence is no longer solely the province of venture-backed standalone startups, but is now being built, with vastly greater distribution and vastly greater financial resources, directly into the products that already occupy more of the average person's daily attention than any other software category in existence.

Not every well-capitalized bet on this chapter's subject involves artificial intelligence, and the largest, most cautionary example this book found involves human companions rather than synthetic ones. Papa Inc., a Miami-based company marketed as a "family-on-demand" service connecting older adults with paid, vetted human companions for both practical help and simple company, raised a cumulative $241.2 million across seven funding rounds between 2018 and 2021, culminating in a $150 million Series D led by SoftBank's Vision Fund 2 that pushed the company to unicorn status at a $1.4 billion valuation — by a wide margin the largest single venture bet on companionship this book was able to verify, dwarfing every AI-companion transaction examined earlier in this chapter. The company built its business model around contracts with health insurers and employers, who paid for Papa's services as a covered benefit for older or isolated members, embedding the company directly into the same Medicare Advantage supplemental-benefit landscape examined in chapter eighteen. That business model collapsed with startling speed in late 2023, after a Bloomberg Businessweek investigation, drawing on roughly 1,200 internal complaints, documented extensive allegations of abuse involving Papa's own companions and the elderly clients they were paired with, including allegations of sexual abuse, harassment, and unsafe conditions. The business consequences followed immediately and severely: nearly three dozen insurers and employers, including Cigna, several regional Blue Cross plans, Humana, Aetna, Molina Healthcare, and the grocery chain Albertsons, declined to renew their contracts for 2024, a wave of institutional abandonment following directly from the investigative reporting. Papa has continued operating rather than shutting down, with company representatives disputing the scale of the reporting's implications and pointing to continued client relationships, but the episode stands, in this book's assessment, as the starkest available warning about a risk this chapter has mostly discussed in the abstract: a business model built around pairing vulnerable, isolated people with paid strangers, however well-intentioned in its founding premise and however successful in attracting capital, carries a real, serious, and in Papa's case realized risk of the exact predatory harm the underlying loneliness was supposed to be protected against, and no amount of venture funding or insurer partnership guarantees the vetting and oversight necessary to prevent it.

Beyond the AI companion apps and elder-care technology this chapter has focused on, the broader wellness industry has moved quickly to position "social connection" as its own distinct product category, following the same WHO reporting on loneliness's health toll examined in chapter three. Virgin Active, the international fitness chain, opened what it explicitly branded a "Social Wellness Club" at its flagship Mayfair location in London in 2025, treating structured social programming as a distinct membership offering rather than an incidental byproduct of gym attendance, while McKinsey's own Future of Wellness research named social wellness one of seven identified growth categories heading into 2026, formally elevating connection to the same commercial status the wellness industry had already assigned sleep, nutrition, and fitness. This broader wellness-industry embrace of loneliness as a marketable category brings with it the same authenticity problem that has dogged the wellness sector across its other product lines: industry commentary has warned explicitly against "wellness washing," in which a brand adopts the language of authentic connection and community as marketing copy without building the substantive programming, expertise, or long-term commitment that would actually deliver it, a criticism directly applicable to any company positioning itself inside this book's subject matter without the kind of evidence base chapter eighteen has tried to hold every genuine intervention accountable to. A consumer encountering a "social wellness" product marketed alongside a company's other lifestyle offerings has, on the evidence this chapter has assembled throughout, good reason to ask the same question this book has asked of every business examined in these pages: whether the product actually increases the practical, recurring, human-noticing contact chapter ten identified as the mechanism that matters, or merely borrows the vocabulary of connection to sell something else entirely.

Replika's own reported financial trajectory, while this book was not able to verify it against the company's own audited filings to the standard it prefers, offers a useful illustration of the broader pattern this chapter keeps encountering: press-reported figures put the company's annual revenue at roughly $30 million in 2023, declining to approximately $24 million by 2024, a contraction rather than the growth curve the broader AI-companion narrative might lead a reader to expect, occurring in the same window that competitors including Character.AI, Talkie, and PolyBuzz were each drawing meaningfully larger user traffic by the independent tracking cited above. If those figures hold up, Replika's experience would mirror something this book has now observed across several different companion-technology categories: an early mover establishing real, durable demand and cultural visibility, only to see its own commercial position erode as better-funded, more technically capable competitors entered the same space it had pioneered — the same basic pattern Xiaoice experienced in China relative to the wave of large-language-model-powered competitors that arrived years after its own 2014 launch, and a pattern this chapter's broader analysis suggests may simply be a structural feature of a product category built on a rapidly improving underlying technology, where a company's early lead in cultivating demand provides less durable competitive protection than the same lead typically provides in a more mature, more technologically stable industry.

Character.AI and Replika, examined at length in chapter eight, are not competing in an empty field, and naming a few of the other products crowding into the same category helps convey how quickly the market has fragmented since large language models made fluent, sustained companion conversation broadly accessible. Talkie, built around a polished, collectible-card visual style skewed toward younger, fandom-oriented users, and PolyBuzz, organized around a vast, largely user-generated library of millions of distinct characters rather than a single persistent companion, have both grown into significant traffic destinations in their own right according to independent app-analytics tracking, alongside Chai, which positions itself around a large community-built bot library and comparatively fewer content restrictions than its more cautious competitors. This book treats the specific traffic and ranking figures circulating for these apps with the same caution it applied to Replika's user numbers in chapter eight — sourced mainly to industry-tracking blogs and analytics aggregators rather than to the companies' own audited disclosures — but the sheer number of distinct, meaningfully differentiated competitors now active in this narrow product category is itself a data point worth registering: a market this crowded, this quickly, after Character.AI's 2022–2023 breakout, suggests venture investors and independent developers alike judged the underlying demand large enough to support many simultaneous entrants rather than a single winner-take-most platform, even as this chapter's more carefully sourced figures suggest the aggregate consumer revenue across all of them remains, so far, comparatively modest.

On the consumer-spending side, rather than the investment side, a *TechCrunch* report from August 2025, citing data from the analytics firm Appfigures, put AI companion apps on track to generate roughly $120 million in consumer spending across 2025 — a figure describing what users actually paid for these apps directly, through subscriptions and in-app purchases, rather than what investors had bet on their future growth. That figure, notably, is an order of magnitude or more smaller than several of the unverifiable market-sizing claims this chapter declined to repeat above, and it is worth sitting with that gap for a moment, because it echoes a pattern this book identified in chapter eleven when comparing the struggling paid connection businesses — WeWork, Peloton, Bumble For Friends — against the more organically thriving unpaid alternative of the run club. A hundred and twenty million dollars in actual global consumer spending on an entire product category, spread across dozens of competing apps, is a real business, and this book does not want to understate it; but it is a modest business relative to the scale of the underlying need this book has spent sixteen chapters documenting, and considerably more modest than the multi-billion-dollar market-size claims that circulate around the category in less carefully sourced coverage.

This gap between demonstrated need and demonstrated willingness to pay directly recurs across nearly every business examined in this book, and it is worth naming as a structural pattern rather than a coincidence specific to any one company. The clearest, most severe manifestation of unmet need this book has documented — Japan's kodokushi statistics, its hikikomori population, its declining marriage and birth rates — occurs in a country where the most heavily capitalized companion-technology responses, PARO and Lovot, have each struggled commercially in their own ways: PARO remains primarily a clinical and institutional product deployed in relatively modest total numbers rather than a mass consumer good, and Lovot's maker, GrooveX, was running meaningful annual losses even as it expanded internationally. The clearest commercial success in the companion-technology space so far, measured by capital committed rather than by consumer revenue, is Character.AI's Google transaction — but that transaction's structure, an incumbent absorbing a smaller competitor's technology and talent rather than a company achieving standalone commercial scale through consumer revenue, suggests the biggest winner in this space, so far, may be less "a company that successfully monetized loneliness at scale" and more "a technology and research team valuable enough for an incumbent to want inside its own walls," a subtly different and, this book would argue, less reassuring story about where the real value in this market currently sits.

None of this is a claim that AI companionship technology will remain commercially modest indefinitely, or that the current, still-forming market structure represents anything like its final shape. The underlying technology — large language models capable of sustained, personality-consistent, emotionally responsive conversation — is improving rapidly and was, as recently as five years before this book's writing, considerably less capable than the systems available today; a market this young, this fast-moving, and this dependent on a still-maturing underlying technology could look very different within a few more years than the modest, hard-to-verify figures assembled in this chapter suggest today. What this chapter has tried to establish, with the same caution this book has applied throughout, is a snapshot rather than a prediction: real, growing usage by the a16z ranking's own transparent methodology; real but comparatively modest verified consumer spending, around $120 million globally in 2025 by the best sourcing this book could locate; a landmark transaction whose true nature is closer to an incumbent's technology and talent acquisition than to proof of a thriving independent consumer market; and, running underneath all of it, a wide gap between the scale of confidently repeated market-size claims and the scale of what this book was actually able to verify — a gap this book is choosing to report honestly rather than paper over with whichever available number would make this chapter's argument sound more dramatic. Stepping back across every business this chapter has examined — Character.AI's licensing arrangement under DOJ scrutiny, Replika's contracting revenue, Xiaoice's decade-old head start, Papa Inc.'s unicorn valuation collapsing under an abuse scandal, Meta's platform-wide companion push, and the wellness industry's own repositioning around "social wellness" — a single pattern recurs clearly enough to state as this chapter's central finding. Capital has flowed toward this book's subject in genuinely large amounts, from genuinely serious institutional actors, at a pace showing no sign of slowing as this book goes to press. But the specific businesses receiving that capital have, so far, delivered a far more mixed record than the scale of investment alone would predict: real, verified user demand sitting alongside unverifiable revenue figures; genuine regulatory legitimacy sitting alongside a landmark product-liability lawsuit and a formal antitrust investigation; a founding premise of addressing loneliness sitting, in Papa's case, alongside a documented pattern of the exact predatory harm that premise was supposed to guard against. This chapter has tried to hold both halves of that record in view at once, neither dismissing the companionship market as pure exploitation nor accepting its polished marketing at face value, because the evidence assembled here supports neither extreme cleanly.

The next chapter turns from the market's uncertain commercial verdict to a different and, this book would argue, more important question: putting aside what people are willing to pay for, what does the actual evidence say about which interventions, technological or otherwise, genuinely work.
