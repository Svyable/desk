# Bowling Alone, Twenty-Five Years Later

In 1995, a Harvard political scientist named Robert Putnam published an eleven-page article in the *Journal of Democracy* with a title that would eventually become one of the most recognized phrases in American social science, and one of its most misunderstood. "Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital" did not argue that Americans had stopped bowling. Bowling, Putnam noted, had never been more popular; more Americans bowled in the early 1990s than at any point in the sport's American history. What had collapsed was league bowling — organized, recurring, socially structured bowling, played with the same people on the same night year after year — which fell by roughly 40 percent between 1980 and 1993 even as the total number of individual bowlers rose by about 10 percent over a similar stretch. People had not stopped doing the activity. They had stopped doing it together, on a schedule, with people who would notice if they didn't show up.

Putnam used the bowling statistic as a synecdoche for something much larger, which he set out to measure with the tools of a social scientist rather than the impressions of a cultural critic. He wanted to know whether "social capital" — his term for the networks, norms, and trust that let people cooperate for mutual benefit — was rising or falling in the United States, and he built his case out of decades of membership records from exactly the kinds of organizations most Americans, in the mid-twentieth century, took for granted as part of ordinary adult life. Parent-Teacher Association membership had fallen from more than 12 million members in the 1960s to roughly 5 million by the early 1980s, before a partial recovery. Membership in the General Federation of Women's Clubs had dropped by 59 percent since 1964. The League of Women Voters was down 42 percent since 1969. Membership in fraternal and civic organizations — the Lions, the Elks, the Shriners, the Jaycees, the Masons — had each fallen by double-digit percentages over comparable periods, in some cases by well over a third. None of these declines happened because someone announced that community organizations were now unfashionable. They happened one lapsed membership, one skipped meeting, one generation that simply never joined in the numbers its parents had, at a time.

Putnam's technical vocabulary distinguished two different kinds of social capital, a distinction this book has been using implicitly throughout without naming it directly, and naming it now clarifies something important about which kind of connection each institution examined across this book's chapters actually supplies. "Bonding" social capital describes close-knit, homogeneous networks of people who already resemble each other — a family, an ethnic or religious community, a tight circle of old friends — networks Putnam described as inward-looking, reinforcing an existing shared identity, and good, in his own phrasing, for "getting by": providing the deep trust and reliable mutual aid that lets someone weather a personal crisis. "Bridging" social capital describes looser, weaker ties connecting people across different backgrounds, professions, and social positions — a public school drawing students from an entire community, a mixed-membership civic organization, a workplace employing people who would never otherwise cross paths — networks Putnam described as outward-looking and, in his phrasing, good for "getting ahead": expanding a person's access to information, opportunity, and perspectives beyond their own immediate circle. The institutions this book's earlier chapters described eroding supplied both kinds in different proportions — a religious congregation leans toward bonding, a workplace or public library toward bridging — and the businesses and technologies examined in this book's later chapters vary considerably in which kind, if either, they actually replace: an AI companion app, however emotionally supportive, supplies neither bonding capital with an existing community nor bridging capital connecting a user to people unlike themselves, while a run club or a Meetup group, bringing together strangers around a shared low-stakes activity, functions closer to bridging capital in Putnam's original sense, mixing people who would not otherwise have met rather than merely reinforcing bonds that already existed.

Putnam's explanation for why that generational handoff broke down centers on what he called America's "long civic generation" — men and women who came of age during the Depression and the Second World War and who carried into peacetime an unusually high, and unusually durable, propensity to join, volunteer, and vote, forged by a shared experience of collective sacrifice and coordinated national mobilization that had no equivalent for the generations born after it. That generation did not simply join more organizations than its successors; it kept joining them at high rates for decades, propping up membership figures across the middle of the twentieth century in a way that made the eventual reversal, once the generation began to age out of active civic life without being replaced in equivalent numbers, look sudden even though the underlying generational transition had been building for years. Layered onto that generational story, Putnam pointed to the more mundane erosions of everyday informal socializing that his surveys tracked alongside the formal membership numbers: declining rates of card-playing, of hosting or attending dinner parties, of simply having friends over on an ordinary evening — habits that, unlike joining a fraternal lodge, required no dues and no meeting minutes, and whose decline is correspondingly harder to document with the same precision as membership rolls, but which Putnam's survey data traced falling in step with the more formal associational decline rather than compensating for it.

The book that grew out of the 1995 article, *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community*, published in 2000, extended the argument across nearly every domain of American associational life Putnam could find records for: church attendance, union membership, dinner parties, card games, even the frequency with which Americans reported having friends over. His explanation for the decline was deliberately multi-causal rather than a single culprit story — he pointed to generational replacement, as the unusually civic-minded generation shaped by the Depression and World War II gradually gave way to less joiner-prone successors; to suburban sprawl and longer commutes, which ate into the discretionary time and neighborhood density that associational life depends on; to the entry of women into the paid workforce, which was good for the economy and for gender equity but also removed a demographic that had historically done a disproportionate share of unpaid community-organizing labor; and, receiving the most attention in later commentary, to television, which Putnam calculated absorbed enough leisure time, especially among the generations that grew up with it as a constant household presence, to crowd out the associational activities that had filled those hours before.

The book was a massive commercial and academic success, and it was also, almost immediately, contested — and it is worth pausing on that contest here, at the start of this book rather than waiting for a designated "skeptics' chapter" later, because a book that only introduces its counter-evidence once, in one quarantined chapter, risks implying that everywhere else it has left the counter-evidence out. It has not, and will not. The sociologist Claude Fischer, at the University of California, Berkeley, mounted one of the most sustained empirical challenges to Putnam's decline thesis. In a 2005 paper in the journal *Social Networks* and later in his 2011 book *Still Connected: Family and Friends in America Since 1970*, Fischer used compiled survey data — much of it independent of the membership rolls Putnam had relied on — to argue that Americans' interpersonal ties with family and close friends had actually remained fairly stable since 1970, even as formal, dues-paying organizational membership fell. Fischer's point was not that Putnam had fabricated anything; he called *Bowling Alone* "a major contribution" even while disputing several of its interpretations. His point was that Putnam had measured one real thing — participation in a specific set of mid-century civic institutions — and then extrapolated from that specific decline to a much broader claim about the health of American social life as a whole, when other measures of that broader social life, examined on their own terms, did not tell the same story of uniform collapse. A parallel line of criticism came from journalist Nicholas Lemann, whose 1996 *Atlantic* piece "Kicking in Groups" questioned both the strength of Putnam's evidence and the speed with which politicians across the ideological spectrum had adopted "declining social capital" as a ready-made explanation for whatever problem they already wanted to solve.

A different kind of response to Putnam came not from an academic critic but from inside the U.S. Senate. In 2017, Senator Mike Lee established the Joint Economic Committee's Social Capital Project, a research initiative that, unlike Fischer's work, did not dispute the underlying decline thesis at all — if anything, the project's own compiled data affirmed it in even starker terms than Putnam's original figures, finding that the share of American adults living with a spouse had fallen from 71 percent to roughly 50 percent, that the share of births occurring outside marriage had risen from 11 percent to around 40 percent, and that religious membership had fallen to roughly 55 percent by the project's own accounting, tracking closely with the Gallup figures examined later in this book. What distinguished the Social Capital Project from Fischer's critique was not a disagreement about whether social capital had declined but a disagreement about what kind of response the decline called for: where progressive readings of Putnam's work tended toward public investment in replacement institutions, Lee's project framed the same data as a case for policies supporting marriage, family formation, and religious and civic institutions directly, treating the decline as evidence of a retreat from voluntary associational life that government programs could support but not substitute for. The project is useful to this book not because it resolves anything, but because it demonstrates something else worth noting early: acceptance of Putnam's basic empirical picture is not confined to any single part of the American political spectrum, even where the prescribed remedy diverges sharply along exactly the lines one might expect.

There is also a version of the counter-argument that takes Putnam's data at face value but disputes his interpretation of what replaced the lost associational forms. Pew Research Center studies from the early 2000s, examining the first wave of mass internet adoption, found that internet use was associated with higher, not lower, rates of community and organizational involvement — evidence, to researchers making this case, that new technologies were supplementing older forms of social capital rather than simply cannibalizing them, connecting people to communities of interest that geography alone would never have assembled. A reader in 2026, two and a half decades into a smartphone- and social-media-saturated culture that did not exist when those Pew studies were conducted, may reasonably wonder whether that early, hopeful reading of the internet's civic effects has aged as well as Putnam's original decline thesis did. Later chapters in this book — particularly the one on remote work and the one on the empirical debate around teenagers, smartphones, and mental health — take up that question directly, because the honest answer, at this point in the research, is contested rather than settled in either direction.

Putnam himself revisited the thesis two decades later, and it is worth taking his revision seriously rather than treating *Bowling Alone* as his final word on the subject. In *The Upswing: How America Came Together a Century Ago and How We Can Do It Again*, published in 2020 with coauthor Shaylyn Romney Garrett, Putnam extended his data back roughly 125 years, into the Gilded Age, and found something that reframed the entire 1995 argument: rather than a single, one-directional decline from a golden age of civic engagement, American social capital appeared to trace something closer to an inverted U, or in the book's own shorthand, an "I-we-I" curve. Around the turn of the twentieth century, by Putnam and Garrett's account, the United States looked a great deal like it does today — atomized, unequal, politically polarized, and thin on cross-class associational life, a period historians call the Gilded Age for a reason. Over the following six decades, on four separate and only loosely coordinated dimensions — economic equality, political cooperation across party lines, social cohesion, and a shift from individualist toward more communitarian cultural values — the country moved steadily toward the dense, joiner-heavy civic culture that Putnam's 1995 statistics captured near its peak, sometime around the middle of the twentieth century. Then, beginning in the 1960s and 1970s, all four dimensions reversed, more or less in sync, back toward the individualist, unequal, polarized starting point — a reversal *Bowling Alone* had captured mid-collapse, in 1995 and 2000, without yet knowing whether it represented a permanent new equilibrium or one leg of a longer cycle.

It is worth pausing on what organizations like the Elks, the Masons, and similar fraternal lodges actually did for their members, beyond the social calendar Putnam's membership statistics track, because the specific function they served connects directly to a theme this book returns to repeatedly in later chapters. Many of these fraternal orders originated in the nineteenth century not primarily as social clubs but as mutual-aid societies: members paid regular dues into a shared fund that paid out death benefits to a member's family, covered funeral costs, or provided a modest income during illness or unemployment, at a time before employer-provided health insurance, life insurance as a mass consumer product, or government social insurance programs like Social Security existed in anything like their modern form. The social gathering — the weekly or monthly lodge meeting, the fraternal rituals, the shared meals — was, in a real sense, the mechanism that made the insurance pool function: it kept members paying in, kept the organization aware of which members needed support, and supplied the recurring, in-person contact that let a lodge brother's illness or a member's death become quickly known rather than quietly overlooked. As commercial insurance, employer benefits, and government programs gradually took over the financial function these organizations originally existed to provide, membership in many of them declined even faster than the purely social explanations Putnam offered would predict on their own — a reminder, worth carrying into this book's later chapter on Japan's kodokushi insurance market, that the line between an institution built around genuine communal care and connection and an institution built around a financial product has never been as clean as it might first appear, and that losing the social infrastructure sometimes precedes, or accompanies, the arrival of a more purely commercial substitute for what it used to provide for free.

Putnam and Garrett's account of how the country climbed out of its first Gilded Age is worth dwelling on briefly, because it is the closest thing this book has to a documented precedent for the kind of deliberate rebuilding its later chapters examine happening today, in far more tentative form. The turn-of-the-century rise in American associational life did not happen spontaneously; it followed a wave of Progressive Era reform and organizing that included the settlement house movement, which built physical institutions in immigrant and working-class neighborhoods explicitly designed to generate the kind of cross-class contact and mutual aid an increasingly industrialized, urbanized society was failing to produce on its own; the rapid growth of labor unions, which supplied not only collective bargaining power but also lodge halls, mutual insurance funds, and recurring social gatherings; and a substantial expansion of public infrastructure — libraries, parks, playgrounds, public schools — built and funded with explicit civic as well as educational purposes in mind. None of this happened quickly, and none of it happened without serious political conflict; Putnam and Garrett's data shows social capital rising gradually across roughly six decades, not springing up in response to a single reform. But the precedent matters for a reader tempted to treat the erosion this book documents as simply an unstoppable feature of modern, technologically advanced, individualist societies. The United States built dense associational infrastructure once, deliberately, in response to an earlier period that looked, on Putnam and Garrett's own measures, strikingly similar to the present one, and it did so through a combination of organizing, institution-building, and public investment that this book's later chapters will show smaller, more recent, and more tentative versions of already being attempted.

The distinction matters enormously for how this book proceeds, because it changes the shape of the claim being made. A pure decline narrative implies an irreversible loss — a golden age behind us, receding, with no mechanism offered for getting it back. A cyclical narrative, the one Putnam and Garrett ultimately settled on, implies something closer to a policy and cultural choice: American social capital rose once through a combination of Progressive Era reforms, labor organizing, expanded civic infrastructure, and a shared crisis in the form of two world wars and a depression, and there is no structural law of physics saying it cannot rise again, deliberately, through a comparable combination of institutional reform and choice. This book leans toward that second, more contingent reading, not because it is more comforting — comfort is not a criterion for choosing between competing empirical claims — but because the historical record Putnam and Garrett assembled genuinely does show the reversal happening once already, driven by identifiable, partly reproducible causes rather than an unrepeatable accident of history.

None of that historical framing changes the scale of what has to be explained in the meantime, and it is worth stating the scale plainly before this book moves into more recent, more granular evidence in the chapters ahead. The Survey Center on American Life, the same research organization behind Daniel Cox's skeptical work on the "loneliness epidemic" framing examined later in this book, has independently produced some of the starkest contemporary evidence that the underlying decline Putnam documented in formal associational membership has continued into the more intimate register of close personal friendship itself, not merely into dues-paying club membership. Cox's own research center found the share of American men reporting at least six close friends had fallen by roughly half since 1990, from 55 percent to 27 percent, while the share of men reporting no close friends at all had risen roughly fivefold over the same period, from 3 percent to 15 percent — a change the center's own researchers have taken to calling a "friendship recession." The effect concentrates most sharply among a specific subgroup: roughly one in five American men who are both unmarried and not in a romantic relationship report having no close friends whatsoever, a population sitting at the precise intersection of the household and associational trends this book's earlier chapters have already traced separately. The center's own analysis attributes the male-specific friendship decline to substantially the same mechanisms this book has been assembling from other angles — weakening ties to religious congregations, examined in chapter thirteen, changes in the nature and location of work, examined in chapter six, and a broader reallocation of limited social time toward immediate family at the expense of the more peripheral friendships that used to fill out a fuller social network — while explicitly noting the trend predates, and is not simply explained by, the COVID-19 pandemic many casual accounts reach for as a default explanation. This data matters for how this chapter's argument should be read going forward: Putnam's 1995 statistics described the decline of organizations built around a shared activity or cause. The Survey Center's more recent numbers describe something closer to the bone, a decline in the raw count of people a person can name as genuinely close to them — evidence that whatever replaced the lost associational structures Putnam catalogued has not, at least for American men specifically, replaced their underlying social function either.

Whatever one makes of Fischer's methodological critique or Putnam's own cyclical revision, the specific, dues-paying, meeting-attending, weekly-recurring form of associational life that Putnam measured in 1995 — the PTA chapters, the bowling leagues, the fraternal lodges, the Federation of Women's Clubs branches — really did shrink by the double-digit and sometimes majority percentages his original data showed, and in most cases it has not meaningfully rebuilt itself in the three decades since. Whatever replaced those specific organizations, if anything did, was not more of the same kind of organization. It is one of this book's central jobs to figure out what, if anything, actually did replace them — a run club instead of a bowling league, a Discord server instead of a PTA meeting, a Bumble BFF match instead of a fraternal lodge brother — and whether those replacements supply the same underlying good that Putnam's institutions supplied almost as an accident of their real purpose: the simple, unglamorous fact of showing up in the same room as the same people, often enough and reliably enough, that someone would notice if you stopped.

Putnam's argument has outlived its original moment enough to become the subject of an Emmy-nominated 2023 documentary, *Join or Die*, directed by Rebecca and Pete Davis, which follows Putnam's half-century engagement with the question of American civic decline and connects it directly, through interviews with figures including Surgeon General Vivek Murthy, economist Raj Chetty, and a range of political and cultural voices spanning Hillary Clinton to Pete Buttigieg, to the more recent, more medicalized loneliness research this book examines in the next several chapters. One line from the film captures the throughline this book has been building toward since its opening pages, spoken as a description of the stakes rather than as a policy recommendation: a warning that a society has to treat loneliness as a threat to its own health security, not a private misfortune to be managed individually. That a film built around a thirty-year-old sociology book could earn a major American streaming platform's distribution and an Emmy nomination in the mid-2020s is itself a small data point worth noting: Putnam's specific argument about bowling leagues and PTA membership has, if anything, gained cultural urgency in the decades since its original publication rather than fading into academic history, evidence that the phenomenon it first named continues to feel more, not less, relevant to the audiences encountering it fresh.

That fact — showing up reliably enough to be missed — connects Putnam's 1995 statistics directly back to the scene this book opened with, an ocean and three decades away, in the apartment of a Japanese pensioner found weeks after death. Putnam was not writing about kodokushi, and he was not writing about mortality at all; his subject was democratic participation and civic trust, measured through the proxy of who joined what. But a bowling league, a PTA chapter, and a fraternal lodge share one structural property with the informal neighborhood density that used to make Japan's solitary deaths rare: all of them require a person's regular, recurring physical presence, tracked by other specific people who would ask where you'd gone if you simply stopped coming. That is the actual load-bearing function this book is interested in — not civic virtue in the abstract, and not nostalgia for a particular decade's clubs, but the practical, mostly unnoticed job those clubs did of keeping people inside a web of others who would notice. The next chapter turns from Putnam's historical, organization-counting method to a newer and more directly medical one: the public-health researchers and government agencies who have spent the past fifteen years trying to measure, with the tools of epidemiology rather than membership rolls, exactly how much that noticing is worth to the people who have it, and how much its absence costs the people who don't.
