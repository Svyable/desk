# Going Solo

Eric Klinenberg opens his 2012 book *Going Solo* with a fact that startled reviewers more than it probably should have, given how quietly it had accumulated: for the first time in recorded American history, people who live alone had become one of the most common household types in the country, outnumbering the nuclear family arrangement — two parents and children under one roof — that most twentieth-century social policy, most advertising, and most architectural convention had been built around. Klinenberg, a sociologist at New York University, was careful throughout the book not to frame this as a tragedy. Living alone, he argued from extensive interviews across age groups and income levels, is disproportionately a choice made by people with the resources to make it, and it correlates with real, well-documented benefits — more time for friendships pursued on one's own terms, more autonomy over daily rhythms, a hedge against the specific unhappiness of a bad marriage or an unwanted roommate. *Going Solo* is, on balance, an optimistic book about a demographic shift most commentary treated with alarm.

But Klinenberg's optimism came with an explicit qualifier that is easy to lose in summary: living alone works out well specifically when the person living alone still has a dense surrounding network of other relationships — friends nearby, a walkable neighborhood, a workplace or community that supplies regular contact — to draw on. The arrangement Klinenberg was describing and largely defending was solo *housing*, not solo *life*. A person who lives alone in a walkable Copenhagen neighborhood, with a short commute to coworkers they see daily and cafés full of familiar faces, is having a very different experience of solitude than a person who lives alone in a car-dependent American suburb, working remotely, without nearby family, in a neighborhood built for maximum privacy between houses rather than casual contact between neighbors. This book's argument through its early chapters has been that the second set of conditions — thinning third places, more remote work, fewer dense associational memberships — has been advancing in the United States at the same time the first trend, single-person households, has been advancing too. Klinenberg's optimistic case for solo living assumed the surrounding social infrastructure would hold up around it. This book's project, in significant part, is documenting what happens to that optimistic case when the surrounding infrastructure doesn't hold.

The scale of the underlying demographic shift is not seriously in dispute, whatever legitimate uncertainty exists around the precise decimal figures in any given year's release. The U.S. Census Bureau's household-composition data, tracked annually through its Current Population Survey and reaching back to the mid-twentieth century in its historical tables, shows single-person households rising from roughly one in eight American households around 1960 to somewhere close to three in ten households in the most recent counts — call it a rough tripling of share over sixty-odd years, even allowing for the small variations across different release years and survey methodologies that make any single precise percentage a moving target rather than a fixed fact. The trend is not uniquely American. Klinenberg's book pointed to Sweden, Norway, Finland, and Denmark as having among the highest shares of one-person households in the world, in some cases approaching two in five, suggesting that rising rates of solo living track with rising national wealth and social-safety-net generosity more than with any specifically American cultural pathology — an important complication for anyone tempted to read this book's American case studies as evidence of uniquely American social failure.

American household composition has not moved in only one direction, and a genuine countertrend deserves equal billing alongside the rise of solo living this chapter has spent most of its length documenting. Multigenerational households — homes containing two or more adult generations, most commonly adult children living with parents or grandparents living with their children's families — grew from about 7 percent of American households in 2011 to roughly 18 percent by the early 2020s, according to Generations United's tracking, with nearly six in ten people in such arrangements citing the COVID-19 pandemic as a factor in starting or continuing to live together. The economic drivers behind this growth are explicit and well documented: about two-thirds of people in multigenerational households cite the broader economic climate as a factor, with eldercare needs, childcare costs, job loss, and family healthcare expenses each cited by roughly a quarter to a third of respondents as specific reasons for doubling up. The growth is also unevenly distributed in ways that echo other patterns this book has traced: multigenerational living has grown fastest among adults aged 25 to 34, the same cohort facing the delayed marriage and housing-cost pressures examined earlier in this chapter, and varies sharply by ethnicity, with roughly 29 percent of Asian American households and 27 percent of Hispanic households multigenerational, compared with 16 percent of white households — a gap that likely reflects both differing cultural norms around family living arrangements and differing exposure to the economic pressures driving the trend. This countertrend complicates any simple narrative of American households uniformly shrinking toward solitary living: the same economic and social forces this book has described eroding some forms of connection appear to be simultaneously pushing a meaningful, fast-growing share of American families toward a denser, more traditionally extended household structure, even as the aggregate single-person-household share this chapter opened with continues its own separate long-term climb — two real trends moving in opposite directions within the same national population, driven substantially by the same underlying economic pressure operating differently on different households.

South Korea's more recent trajectory adds a useful data point precisely because it complicates the tidy story that rising solo living simply tracks rising national wealth. Statistics Korea's own household counts show single-person households rising from 5.6 million in 2017 to 7.5 million by 2022, reaching roughly 34.5 percent of all households — a share now comparable to the Nordic countries Klinenberg singled out, but arrived at over a compressed span of only five years, far faster than the multi-decade drift the U.S. Census data traces. Korean media and marketing have coined an entire vocabulary around the shift, most prominently *honjok*, literally "alone tribe," describing the culture of solo dining, solo drinking, and solo travel that has grown up around the demographic change, complete with restaurants explicitly designed with single-seat counters and apartment developments marketed on their suitability for one occupant. The honjok phenomenon has been received in South Korean popular culture with a notably more ambivalent mixture of celebration and concern than Klinenberg's optimistic American framing — celebrated, in some coverage, as liberation from restrictive family obligation and Confucian-inflected social expectation, and worried over, in other coverage, as one visible face of the same demographic pressures behind South Korea's godoksa statistics discussed in chapter one, where a 2024 study found that nearly four in five people in single-person households showed at least some measurable risk profile for solitary death. South Korea, in other words, is running something close to a natural experiment in what happens when a rise in solo living arrives faster than the surrounding culture, commercial infrastructure, and social safety net can adjust to it — a compressed, more visible version of the slower-motion version this book has been tracing across the United States.

What has moved alongside single-person households, and what connects this demographic shift more directly to the institutions Putnam and the Surgeon General were both describing from different angles, is the American marriage rate. The Centers for Disease Control's National Center for Health Statistics has tracked marriage and divorce rates through its National Vital Statistics System for over a century, and the broad shape of the data is not in dispute even though exact per-year figures vary somewhat across data-release vintages: American marriage rates, which sat in a fairly stable band through most of the twentieth century, roughly halved between 1970 and the present, falling from somewhere in the range of ten to eleven marriages per thousand people to roughly six per thousand in the most recent complete data. Divorce rates followed their own arc — rising sharply through the 1960s and 1970s, peaking around 1979 to 1981, and then declining meaningfully in the decades since, though the NCHS itself flags recent divorce-rate figures as provisional, since not every state, California prominent among them, reports comparable divorce data to the federal system. People are marrying later, marrying less often, and, when they do divorce, doing so at somewhat lower rates than the peak — three separate trends that combine into a population spending a larger share of adult life outside a marriage than at almost any point in the twentieth century's data.

Solo travel offers one final, genuinely interesting complication to this chapter's account of rising solo living, because it shows the same underlying population actively engineering its way back toward connection rather than simply accepting solitude as a fixed condition. The global solo travel market, valued at roughly $550 billion in 2025 and projected to grow at 13 to 15 percent annually through 2033, has grown into one of the travel industry's most significant segments, driven disproportionately by women, who make up roughly 60 percent of solo travelers by some industry counts. Hostelworld's own 2025 survey of its solo-traveling customer base found 71 percent actively seeking social connection during their trip rather than solitude for its own sake, and 68 percent reporting they had actually formed a meaningful friendship while traveling alone — even as 28 percent separately reported concern about feeling lonely and 24 percent reported difficulty meeting people, showing the same population holding both the aspiration and the anxiety simultaneously. The hostel format itself, industry commentary suggests, has become a deliberately engineered solution to exactly this tension: travelers choose hostels specifically because the format surrounds a person who has arrived alone with other people who are also, at that moment, unaccompanied, producing what one report characterized as the specific, sought-after condition of being "solo but not alone." Solo travel, read this way, is not really solo at all in the sense this book has used the term elsewhere; it is a deliberately chosen, temporary, structurally engineered version of solo *arrival* that still aims squarely at social connection as its destination — evidence that even the population most visibly embracing individual, unaccompanied living retains, and often actively pursues, exactly the underlying social need this book has argued the rest of modern life has grown worse at supplying by default.

A specific and growing subset of the marriage-decline data deserves separate attention: divorce occurring late in life, now common enough in the United States that researchers have given it its own name, "gray divorce," describing separations occurring at or after age fifty. More than a quarter of all American divorces now fall into this gray-divorce category, a demographic shift driven by longer life expectancy and greater social acceptance of divorce later in life among a generation that, unlike its parents, does not treat an unhappy marriage as something to simply endure until death. The research literature on gray divorce's social consequences finds a specific, gendered pattern worth flagging directly, since it echoes findings this book has already reported from other angles: divorce after fifty is associated with higher loneliness than widowhood for men specifically, and older men living alone following a late-life split are considerably more likely to report social isolation, and considerably less likely to report satisfaction with their remaining friendships, than women in the equivalent situation — a pattern researchers attribute partly to men's documented tendency, examined at greater length in chapter nine's discussion of Japanese and Korean isolated men, to rely more heavily on a spouse as their primary or sole source of emotional support and social coordination, leaving them with a thinner independent social network to fall back on once that specific relationship ends. Gray divorce also frequently severs a person from an entire secondary social network built up around the marriage — mutual friends who continue socializing as a couple, in-laws, a spouse's extended family — compounding the loss of the primary relationship with a broader contraction of the surrounding social world that a younger divorced person, with more years remaining to rebuild an independent network, is often better positioned to recover from.

The marriage-rate decline this chapter has been describing is also not a simple story of romantic partnership disappearing outright; a meaningful share of the decline reflects couples substituting cohabitation for formal marriage rather than living entirely apart, a distinction the Census Bureau's household counts capture only imperfectly, since an unmarried cohabiting couple registers in the same household-composition data as a married one for many purposes, while showing up quite differently in the CDC's marriage-rate statistics. This matters for how strictly a reader should interpret the marriage-rate figures as evidence of declining partnership itself, as opposed to declining formal marriage specifically: two adults sharing a household, a bed, and daily routines without a marriage certificate are not, by most reasonable definitions, "alone" in the sense this book cares about, even though they register as a falling marriage-rate statistic. What the aggregate data cannot cleanly separate is how much of the marriage decline this chapter has described represents genuine loss of partnership and how much represents the same underlying partnership continuing in an administratively different form — a genuine limitation of the available statistics that this book flags rather than papers over, even as it notes that cohabitation's rise does not, on its own, account for the parallel and more clearly documented rise in single-person households this chapter opened with, which by definition excludes cohabiting couples entirely.

It would be a mistake, and this book is trying hard throughout not to make it, to treat every one of these trends as straightforwardly bad news, or to treat marriage as the only legitimate structure for producing social connection. The social psychologist Bella DePaulo, a Harvard-trained researcher who has spent nearly two decades studying single life in America, has documented a persistent and largely unrecognized bias she terms "singlism" — a pattern of stereotyping and discrimination against unmarried people that runs through the workplace, the housing market, tax and benefits policy, media representation, and everyday social assumptions, often without the people expressing it recognizing it as bias at all. In one of DePaulo's own studies, examining scenarios where members of various socially disadvantaged groups offered above-asking-price bids on a home, participants readily recognized discrimination when a landlord favored a white applicant over an equally qualified Black one, or a male applicant over an equally qualified woman, but did not recognize the same pattern as discrimination when a landlord favored a married couple over a single applicant offering the identical terms — evidence, in DePaulo's reading, that bias against single people occupies a genuine analytical blind spot even among people who consider themselves alert to other forms of discrimination. DePaulo's research complicates this chapter's own framing in a useful way: if a meaningful share of the social and institutional penalty attached to living alone stems not from the arrangement's inherent loneliness but from other people's unexamined assumptions about what living alone signifies, then part of what this book's later chapters would need to "rebuild" is not only the practical infrastructure of connection but also the cultural assumption, still widespread by DePaulo's own account, that a person living alone is, by definition, missing something a coupled or married person has already secured. Klinenberg's interviews are full of people who describe living alone, after an unhappy marriage or a difficult family household, as one of the healthiest decisions of their lives — a chance to build friendships and routines suited to who they actually are rather than who a shared household required them to be. Delayed marriage correlates in the research literature with more education, more financial stability entering a marriage, and, in some studies, more durable marriages once they do occur, for people who marry later rather than earlier. None of that is this book's target. This book's target is narrower and, this book would argue, more empirically grounded: not marriage rates or household composition in themselves, but what happens to the *density of a person's ordinary, recurring social contact* when the household they live in shrinks to one person at the same time that the surrounding institutions — the third places, the workplaces, the associational memberships — that used to supply contact independent of household size are also thinning. A single person embedded in a dense, walkable, association-rich community and a single person embedded in a sparse, dispersed, association-poor one are having categorically different experiences of the same Census category, and the aggregate household statistics, on their own, cannot tell those two experiences apart.

One specific slice of the household data deserves separate treatment before this chapter moves on, because it complicates the overall rising-solo-living narrative in a way this book wants to preserve rather than smooth over: older Americans specifically. The Census Bureau's 2023 Current Population Survey found about 28 percent of Americans aged 65 and older living alone — nearly 6 million men and just over 10 million women, with the gender gap widening sharply after age 75, when 43 percent of women live alone compared with 24 percent of men, a divergence driven substantially by women's longer average life expectancy outliving male spouses. That 28 percent figure is a dramatic rise from 1950, when only about one in ten older Americans lived alone. But a separate Pew Research Center analysis of the same underlying trend found something that cuts against a simple ever-rising narrative: the *share* of older adults living alone actually peaked around 1990, at roughly 29 percent, and has since declined slightly, to about 26 percent by 2023 — even as the *absolute number* of older Americans living alone has kept climbing to historic highs, because the overall population of people over 65 has grown so much faster than the rate of solo living among them has fallen. Both facts are true simultaneously, and holding them together matters for how a reader should weigh the kodokushi-style risk this book's opening chapter described: more elderly Americans than ever before are living alone in raw numeric terms, which is what actually determines how many households a failing social safety net has to reach, even though the proportion of any given older American's chance of living alone has modestly declined since its own 1990 peak. This is exactly the kind of nuance chapter sixteen's skeptics would want preserved rather than flattened into a single directional claim, and this book is preserving it here rather than waiting until that later chapter to admit the complication exists.

This is where the American story diverges most sharply from the story Klinenberg told about Scandinavia, and where the earlier chapters of this book's American evidence — Putnam's declining associational memberships, the Surgeon General's mortality data — start to compound with the household data rather than simply sitting alongside it. A rising share of Americans living alone would matter less, from a public-health standpoint, in a country where solo living coexisted with a still-thriving associational culture: strong unions, full churches, well-attended PTA meetings, busy bowling leagues, walkable neighborhoods with reliable third places within a short walk. The trouble this book is tracing is that the American version of rising solo living has been arriving at the same time as the decline in exactly those other supports, rather than alongside their preservation — two trends moving in the same direction, at the same time, in the same country, each making the other's effects larger than either would be alone. A person living by themselves in a neighborhood with a functioning corner bar, an active congregation, and a bowling league that still meets on Tuesdays has real backstops against isolation even without a housemate. A person living by themselves in a neighborhood where the corner bar closed, church attendance fell below half the population for the first time in Gallup's eighty-plus years of tracking, and the bowling league dissolved decades ago is living inside a much thinner net, and the Census's household count alone cannot show which situation any given single-person household is actually in.

That distinction — between living alone inside a rich social infrastructure and living alone inside a thin one — is also the distinction the kodokushi statistics from this book's opening chapter were implicitly making, without using this book's vocabulary to make it. Japan's rate of solitary death did not rise because more Japanese people started living alone in the abstract; plenty of countries have high rates of solo living without comparable rates of undiscovered deaths. It rose because rising solo living arrived in Japan at the same time as declining multigenerational cohabitation, declining neighborhood density in rapidly depopulating regions, and a culture of employment loyalty and long working hours that had, for decades, substituted for some of the informal neighborhood checking that thinner family networks used to provide, and which itself came under pressure as Japan's economy and labor practices shifted after the 1990s. The household arrangement was never, by itself, the risk factor. The household arrangement combined with a thinning surrounding infrastructure was.

The United States is not Japan, and its demographic clock runs on a different schedule — Japan's population began aging and its household sizes began shrinking earlier and more steeply than America's, giving Japan an unwanted head start on the more severe end of these trends. But the direction of travel on the specific variables this chapter has traced — a rising share of single-person households, a falling marriage rate, a thinning associational culture running in parallel — is the same direction, and nothing about American demographic or economic policy over the past several decades has meaningfully reversed any of the three. The next several chapters turn from the household, which this chapter has treated mostly as a static Census category, to the specific institutions whose erosion determines what living inside that category actually feels like: the third place that used to sit a short walk from home, the workplace that used to guarantee daily contact with the same colleagues, and the dating market that, for a large share of the unmarried adults this chapter has just described, is now the primary mechanism by which a solo household might, eventually, become something else.
