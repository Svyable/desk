# Pews Emptying

Gallup has been asking American adults whether they belong to a church, synagogue, or mosque since 1937, which makes its religious-membership tracking one of the longest continuously run survey series on any single social behavior in American polling history. For most of that eighty-plus-year run, the number moved within a fairly narrow band: 73 percent of Americans reported formal religious membership in Gallup's first measurement, and the figure stayed close to or above 70 percent through the end of the twentieth century, dipping only gradually across the 2000s and 2010s. Then, in Jeffrey M. Jones's March 2021 analysis of Gallup's own tracking data, the figure crossed a threshold it had never crossed before in eight decades of measurement: 47 percent, the first time in the poll's history that fewer than half of American adults reported belonging to a religious congregation.

The decline had been visible in Gallup's own data for years before it crossed fifty percent, and Jones's analysis, along with an earlier Gallup piece from 2019 tracking the sharper drop across the preceding two decades specifically, breaks the aggregate trend down by generation in a way that clarifies what is actually driving it. Among the oldest cohort Gallup calls Traditionalists, born before 1946, membership remained relatively high, around 66 percent. Baby Boomers came in at 58 percent. Generation X fell further, to 50 percent. Millennials, the generation that had by the early 2020s become the largest working-age cohort in the country, reported religious membership at just 36 percent — not a gradual softening from the generation before them, but a substantial, generation-scale drop. The share of Americans identifying with no religion at all, a category pollsters call religious "nones," grew from roughly 8 percent in the late 1990s to a three-year average around 21 percent by the early 2020s, more than doubling in a generation.

Pastors Jim Davis and Michael Graham, working with sociologists Ryan Burge and Paul Djupe on what they describe as the largest comprehensive study of its kind, surveying more than 7,000 participants across three phases, made the scale of this shift explicit in their 2023 book *The Great Dechurching*: roughly 40 million American adults have stopped attending church in the preceding quarter-century, a movement the authors argue is larger, in raw numbers of people who changed religious behavior, than the First Great Awakening, the Second Great Awakening, and every other American religious revival combined — run, in this case, in the opposite direction. The book's central empirical contribution, beyond the headline number, is its attempt to disaggregate the dechurched population by actual reason for leaving, rather than treating the 40 million as a single undifferentiated bloc of the newly secular. The authors' own survey work found that the largest share of people who left had not lost religious belief at all; they cited practical, logistical, and life-stage disruptions — a move to a new city without an established congregation, a change in work schedule that conflicted with service times, a general drift during a busy period of life that was never actively reversed — as their primary reason for stopping attendance, a pattern that reframes a meaningful share of America's dechurching as closer to an unintentional casualty of the same mobility, scheduling pressure, and disrupted routine this book's earlier chapters have described eroding other associational institutions, rather than as a deliberate ideological rejection of religion itself.

A closer look at who exactly makes up that growing "none" category, from Pew Research Center's own January 2024 breakdown of religiously unaffiliated Americans, reinforces this chapter's specific focus on institutional membership rather than belief. Among the roughly 28 percent of U.S. adults Pew classified as religiously unaffiliated, only a minority describe themselves in terms that suggest a considered secular philosophy: 17 percent identify as atheist and 20 percent as agnostic, together accounting for well under half of the unaffiliated population. The clear majority, 63 percent, describe their religion using the residual category "nothing in particular" — not a rejection of religious belief so much as an absence of religious institutional identity, a category that Pew's own researchers have found includes many people who report holding spiritual beliefs, praying occasionally, or believing in a higher power, without belonging to, attending, or identifying with any specific congregation or tradition. That distribution matters directly for this chapter's argument: if the rise of religious "nones" were driven overwhelmingly by growing atheism or agnosticism, it would support a story about declining belief itself. Instead, the data suggests something closer to this book's broader thesis about institutional rather than ideological erosion — a large and growing population that has not necessarily concluded religion is false, but has simply drifted out of the specific, recurring, membership-based structure that used to translate whatever belief a person held into regular in-person contact with other people who shared it.

It matters, for the argument this chapter and this book are making, to be precise about what Gallup's question actually measures and what it does not. "Do you belong to a church, synagogue, or mosque" is a question about formal institutional membership, not a question about private religious belief, prayer, spiritual practice, or even attendance at services by people who might still describe themselves as members of a faith tradition without holding formal membership in a specific congregation. A person could, in principle, hold deep personal religious conviction while never joining a specific congregation, and Gallup's declining-membership figure would count that person the same as someone with no religious belief at all. This book is not making, and does not need to make, any claim about the state of American religious belief in the abstract — that is a separate and much larger question with its own extensive research literature, much of it genuinely contested, that falls outside this book's scope. What this book is interested in, consistent with every other institution examined in its earlier chapters, is specifically the formal, recurring, membership-based social infrastructure that congregations have historically supplied, whatever theological content accompanied it — and on that narrower, more measurable question, Gallup's eighty-year data series is about as clean and as consistent a signal as social science produces.

The specific social infrastructure a congregation supplies, independent of its theological content, overlaps substantially with the functions this book's earlier chapters have already described eroding elsewhere. A weekly congregation gathers the same group of people, on a predictable schedule, across a wide range of ages and, in many congregations, a wider range of economic backgrounds than most other American institutions manage to bring into regular contact with each other — closer, in that specific respect, to Oldenburg's description of a genuine third place than almost any of the commercial alternatives examined in chapter five. It generates informal caretaking networks nearly as a byproduct of its stated religious purpose: members who notice when another member has missed several weeks in a row, meal trains organized for a family after a birth or a death, visits to members who are sick or hospitalized, coordinated through channels that exist because the congregation already exists, without requiring anyone to build new social infrastructure from scratch in a moment of crisis. Putnam's original *Bowling Alone* research, examined in chapter two, found religious congregations were, by a wide margin, the single largest generator of associational membership and volunteer activity in American civic life, larger than every other category of organization his data tracked combined, which is part of what makes their decline structurally significant to this book's argument in a way that the decline of, say, fraternal lodges alone would not be: congregations were not simply one civic institution among many equally weighted alternatives. They were, by Putnam's own accounting, the largest single piece of America's associational infrastructure, and its ongoing decline removes more raw social-connective capacity than the decline of almost any other single category this book has examined.

Political scientist Ryan Burge, whose survey work also underpins the dechurching research discussed above, published further research in 2025 that complicates the "nothing in particular" category even more than this chapter's earlier discussion suggested. Surveying more than 15,000 Americans through Qualtrics, with roughly 12,000 identifying as atheist, agnostic, or "nothing in particular," Burge found that within the specific "nothing in particular" subgroup — nicknamed "NiNos" in his research shorthand — a genuinely striking share retain conventional religious practice and belief despite rejecting any formal affiliation: over half report praying daily and say they believe in God without doubt, and roughly a third report attending religious services at least once a year, findings that would surprise anyone assuming "nothing in particular" describes a population that has simply stopped believing. Burge's separately tracked secularism index among Americans who never attend religious services at all — a stricter population than the broader "nones" category — shows its own complicated trajectory: after rising steadily from 1988 to roughly 2002, the index plateaued for approximately fifteen years before resuming its climb in the most recent data, reaching an all-time high of 0.58 in 2024. Read together, Burge's findings reinforce this chapter's central claim in an even more granular form than the earlier Pew breakdown alone supported: the decline this chapter has documented is overwhelmingly a decline in formal religious belonging and practice, not a uniform decline in belief itself, with a meaningful share of the specific population driving America's rising "unaffiliated" numbers continuing to pray, believe, and even occasionally attend services, while simply no longer identifying with, or regularly participating in, any specific congregation.

The aggregate Gallup and Pew figures this chapter has cited also flatten a meaningful racial disparity worth naming on its own terms, because it complicates any account of religious decline as a uniform, evenly distributed national trend. Pew Research Center's surveys have consistently found Black Americans attending religious services at higher rates than white, Latino, or other American respondents, with 47 percent of Black respondents in one recent Pew study reporting weekly attendance compared with 39 percent of Latino and 34 percent of white respondents, and members of the historically Black Protestant tradition reporting among the highest participation rates of any American religious group Pew tracks — 66 percent taking part in services at least monthly, trailing only Latter-day Saints and evangelical Protestants. Predominantly Black congregations have, by Pew's own comparative analysis, declined more slowly than the national average this chapter's earlier Gallup figures describe, a resilience with a documented historical basis: Black churches in the United States have functioned, since well before the twentieth century, not merely as sites of worship but as centers of mutual aid, political organizing, and community institution-building in ways white American Protestant congregations more rarely had to, born of a history in which Black Americans were frequently excluded from other civic and financial institutions this book's earlier chapters have described eroding, making the congregation, for long stretches of American history, one of the only stable associational structure available. That resilience is not, however, permanent or uniform across generations within Black congregations either: Pew's generational breakdowns found Black Millennials and members of Generation Z measurably less likely than older Black Americans to have grown up attending a Black church, less likely to rely on prayer, and less likely to describe religion as personally important — the same generational divergence chapter fourteen's discussion of youth mental health traces through an entirely different set of institutions, suggesting the youngest American adults, across racial and denominational lines that otherwise show real differences in the pace of religious decline, share a broadly similar relationship to formal religious participation regardless of which specific tradition their parents and grandparents belonged to.

Pew's own research directly tested the assumption underlying much of this chapter's argument — that declining religious membership should translate into declining civic and community participation more broadly — and found a more qualified answer than a simple confirmation. Religious "nones" as a whole are only modestly less likely than religiously affiliated Americans to report current involvement in a nonreligious volunteer or community-service organization, a smaller gap than the sharp decline in formal congregational membership might predict on its own. But that modest aggregate gap conceals a sharper divide within the "nones" category itself: atheists and agnostics, the more ideologically committed secular minority within the broader unaffiliated population, participate in civic life at rates matching or even exceeding religiously affiliated Americans, while the lower overall civic-engagement rates among "nones" are concentrated almost entirely among the "nothing in particular" majority this chapter examined earlier — the same population Burge's research found retaining meaningful private religious practice despite rejecting formal affiliation. Read together with the earlier NiNo findings, this suggests the specific population driving America's declining religious membership numbers is not primarily composed of committed secularists building alternative civic institutions of their own, but of people who have simply drifted out of organized participation altogether, in religious and non-religious institutions alike — a population whose declining congregational membership looks less like substitution, trading one associational form for another, and more like straightforward attrition, precisely the pattern this book's earlier chapters on Putnam's associational decline and the American friendship recession would predict.

This chapter is not arguing that religious decline is simply bad, any more than earlier chapters argued that rising rates of solo living or remote work are simply bad. Declining religious membership tracks with genuine, well-documented liberalizing social trends many Americans experience as positive: greater acceptance of religious and secular pluralism, reduced social pressure toward conformity with a single congregation's specific moral and social expectations, and, for people whose lived identity or life circumstances put them at odds with a particular tradition's teachings, real relief from institutions that could be exclusionary or actively harmful to them. The honest accounting this book is trying to offer throughout is not that any single eroding institution — the union hall, the bowling league, the office, the congregation — was an unqualified good that should be nostalgically restored exactly as it was. It is that each of these institutions was doing real, measurable connective work, largely as an unplanned byproduct of its other stated purposes, and that American social life has not yet built adequate replacements for the specific, practical function — regular, cross-generational, geographically anchored contact, with built-in mechanisms for noticing when someone stops showing up — that these institutions, congregations very much included, used to supply almost without anyone deciding to supply it.

At least one explicit attempt has been made to reconstruct the congregation's connective function while deliberately stripping out its theological content, and its trajectory is worth reporting honestly rather than only at its moment of greatest promise. The Sunday Assembly, founded in London in January 2013 by two standup comedians, Pippa Evans and Sanderson Jones, built what its founders called a "godless congregation," gathering attendees in a deconsecrated church for a service format that consciously mimicked traditional worship — communal singing, largely drawn from popular music rather than hymns, along with reflective talks on ethical themes — while replacing religious content with secular humanist values, under the motto "Live better. Help often. Wonder more." The movement's early growth was genuinely striking: its first gathering drew more than 300 people, and within roughly two years it had expanded to 185 gatherings across multiple countries, spreading through additional UK cities and into the United States via a founder-led tour through Boston, Washington, San Jose, San Diego, and Los Angeles among other stops. That initial trajectory did not sustain itself at the same pace: by the later 2010s, reported counts of active Sunday Assembly locations had settled into a considerably smaller range, variously reported between roughly 48 and 70 ongoing chapters worldwide, suggesting the format found a genuine, durable niche audience without achieving anything close to the scale of the religious institutions it was explicitly designed to replace. The Sunday Assembly's arc — rapid early growth followed by a plateau well short of its founders' evident early ambitions — echoes a pattern this book has now observed across several deliberately constructed replacement institutions, from WeWork to Bumble For Friends: manufacturing, on purpose and from scratch, the specific combination of ritual, regularity, and community that older institutions accumulated gradually and mostly by accident has proven, again and again, to be considerably harder than identifying the underlying need would suggest it should be.

It is worth widening the lens beyond the United States and Britain one final time before this chapter closes, because the pattern this chapter has described — declining religious membership as a proxy for declining associational infrastructure — is far from globally uniform, and treating it as a universal wealthy-world phenomenon would repeat exactly the overgeneralization chapter sixteen's skeptics warned against. Pew Research Center's long-range demographic projections show global Christianity's center of gravity shifting decisively away from exactly the regions this book has focused on: the share of the world's Christian population living in Europe is projected to fall from roughly 26 percent in 2010 to about 16 percent by 2050, even as Europe's own religiously unaffiliated population continues growing in both share and absolute number despite the continent's overall population decline. Sub-Saharan Africa runs in the opposite direction entirely, with its Christian population projected to roughly double, from 517 million in 2010 to 1.1 billion by 2050, driven by a combination of fertility rates and sustained institutional religious vitality that shows little resemblance to the American and British decline this chapter has documented in detail. This divergence matters for how a reader should weight this chapter's argument going forward: the specific mechanism this book cares about — congregational membership functioning as recurring, cross-generational, geographically anchored social infrastructure — is eroding sharply in the wealthy, historically Christian-majority countries this book has focused its research on, while remaining robust, and in some regions actively strengthening, across large parts of the global South. A reader in Lagos or São Paulo encountering this chapter's argument should not assume it describes their own community's trajectory; it describes a pattern specific to the aging, secularizing, historically Protestant and Catholic societies of North America and Western Europe, not a universal law of modernization this book has any basis for extending to the rest of the world.

This chapter's overall account — a sharp, well-documented American decline in formal religious membership, a global picture that runs in the opposite direction across large parts of the Global South, a "nones" population more religiously and spiritually active than its label implies, and a racial disparity in decline rates rooted in Black congregations' distinct historical role — resists compression into any single sentence, and this book is deliberately not offering one. What survives all of that complexity, consistently, is the narrower claim that has anchored this chapter throughout: whatever a reader concludes about American religious belief itself, the specific, recurring, cross-generational, geographically anchored associational structure congregations once reliably supplied has thinned considerably across the wealthy, historically Christian societies this book has focused on, and nothing examined in this chapter has yet rebuilt that specific structure at anything close to its former scale.

What has moved into the space religious decline has left behind is uneven and, in most cases, considerably thinner than what it replaced. Some of the businesses examined in chapter eleven — run clubs prominent among them — have begun to take on some of a congregation's structural role: recurring, geographically anchored, cross-demographic gatherings that meet on a predictable schedule and generate informal community around a shared, non-religious activity. Online communities, examined more critically in the next chapter's discussion of the debate over social media and teenage mental health, have taken on another piece of the same function for some users, with much more contested evidence about whether they actually deliver a comparable version of the underlying good or merely its surface appearance. Neither replacement, on the evidence assembled across this book so far, comes close to matching the scale, the built-in caretaking mechanisms, or the cross-generational reach that Putnam's data showed American congregations once supplied at their mid-twentieth-century peak. The next chapter turns to the most contested and most consequential of the replacement candidates: the smartphone and the social-media platforms that have, for the youngest generation examined throughout this book, arrived at precisely the moment the older institutions were emptying out.
