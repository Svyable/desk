# The Rebuild Economy

Strava, the fitness-tracking app that has become the closest thing endurance athletes have to a shared social network, publishes an annual "Year in Sport" report drawn from its own platform data — billions of logged activities from more than 135 million users across roughly 190 countries, supplemented by a survey of several thousand active users. The 2024 edition, released in December of that year, documented something that had not shown up clearly in the company's data before: a 59 percent increase in run-club participation on the platform globally over the course of the year, alongside an 18 percent increase in group runs involving ten or more participants. When Strava asked its surveyed users why they had joined a run club, the single most common answer was not fitness. It was social connection — more than a third of respondents specifically described run clubs as a good place to meet people, and Strava's data showed Generation Z users were nearly 40 percent more likely than Generation X users to say they used fitness activities specifically to meet people who shared their interests. The following year's report described the trend accelerating rather than plateauing: the total number of Strava-tracked clubs of all kinds nearly quadrupled over the course of 2025, with running clubs specifically growing roughly three and a half times, and Brazil, in particular, recording club growth on the order of 800 percent.

Women-specific friendship clubs have grown rapidly enough in the mid-2020s to constitute their own identifiable category within this chapter's broader rebuild economy, built around a specific, well-documented life-stage problem: the observation, common enough among the organizers themselves to function as an informal founding thesis, that a person's twenties and early thirties are frequently the hardest years of adult life in which to build new friendships, once school-based social structures have ended and before a stable, long-term community has had time to form. OC Women's Group, founded in 2024, grew to more than 25,000 members within its first year; the Women's Social Club, structured around private local chapters offering curated in-person events alongside an app-based member directory and message board, expanded to more than 50 chapters over roughly six years of operation. Smaller, more local operators — Houston's Gorls Club HTX and HTX Girls Social Club, both launched in 2024 with programming ranging from yoga classes and coffee tastings to bouquet-making workshops and watch parties — illustrate how quickly this specific format has proliferated at the city level, each following a broadly similar model of low-commitment, activity-anchored gatherings explicitly marketed around friendship formation rather than romance or professional networking. The women's-club wave shares the structural profile this chapter has identified across its other successful examples: modest per-event cost, recurring rather than one-off programming, and an explicit design intention — friend-making as the stated purpose rather than an incidental benefit — that distinguishes it from the older civic organizations Putnam catalogued, which supplied friendship formation as a byproduct of some other stated purpose rather than as their organizing principle.

Adult summer camp represents perhaps the most nostalgically explicit version of the rebuild economy examined in this chapter, reconstructing a specific childhood institution — themed activities, cabin assignments, communal dining, evening campfires — for a customer base old enough to remember, and evidently miss, the original. Operators like Camp Social report a core demographic ranging from the mid-twenties through mid-forties, with a meaningful contingent of repeat attendees in their fifties and beyond, and describe solo attendance not as an exception but as common practice, with programs deliberately structuring "friend-maker" cabin assignments, first-day icebreaker activities, and interest-based groupings specifically to help attendees arriving alone form connections over the course of a multi-day retreat. Yelp's own 2025 trend forecast identified "dopamine-spiking, community-boosting experiences" as a defining consumer category for the year, and industry coverage has framed the adult-camp boom explicitly in terms of burnout and isolation relief rather than simple leisure, with operators including Hot and Social's Camp Social brand announcing further expansion of their programming into 2026. What makes adult summer camp analytically interesting alongside this chapter's other examples is how directly it monetizes something the run club and Meetup do for free: a structured, time-limited, deliberately engineered version of childhood's easiest social conditions — assigned bunkmates, forced proximity, low-stakes shared activity — repackaged as a paid weekend retreat for adults who have concluded, correctly by this book's own evidence, that ordinary adult life no longer reliably supplies those conditions on its own.

American employers, unlike the British businesses examined through the Co-op's £2.5 billion figure in chapter ten, do not yet have an equivalently rigorous, independently sourced national estimate of loneliness's cost to their own workforces; this book found American figures circulating in the range of $150 billion to $300 billion annually, traced mainly to corporate wellness vendors and HR-consulting content rather than to a peer-reviewed or government-commissioned study comparable to the British research, and is therefore treating those specific totals with the same skepticism it has applied throughout to unverifiable market-sizing claims, rather than repeating either figure as settled fact. What is better documented, and more directly useful to an individual employer weighing whether to invest in exactly the kind of program examined throughout this chapter, is the basic arithmetic of employee replacement cost: industry benchmarking consistently finds that replacing a single departed employee costs somewhere between fifty and two hundred percent of that employee's annual salary, once recruiting, onboarding, and lost-productivity costs are included, a range this book considers more reliably sourced than the more dramatic aggregate national totals. Combined with chapter two's finding that lonely employees are roughly five times more likely to leave a job within a year, that replacement-cost arithmetic gives an individual employer a concrete, calculable reason to invest in exactly the kind of low-cost, high-frequency connection programming this chapter has documented working elsewhere — not because a company should care about its employees' loneliness as an abstract moral matter, though it might reasonably do so anyway, but because the specific, well-established cost of losing and replacing a lonely employee gives the investment a business case that does not depend on any single contested national estimate holding up under scrutiny.

The humble book club, an institution old enough to have existed in some form for centuries, offers this chapter's clearest example of an existing, low-cost social format experiencing a genuine contemporary revival rather than a manufactured one. A 2025 reassessment of U.S. book club membership — updating a stale estimate of roughly 5 million that had gone unrevised since 1999 — put current membership closer to 13 million, a more than twofold increase once the outdated baseline was corrected for. Event-listing data from Eventbrite showed book club events proliferating by 31 percent in a single year between 2023 and 2024, with silent book clubs — gatherings where members read individually in shared company rather than discussing a single assigned text — more than doubling, and romance and fantasy-genre book clubs specifically quadrupling, reflecting a shift toward genre-specific and format-flexible variations on the traditional model. Coverage of the trend has described book clubs becoming genuine "social scenes" for younger readers, with participants describing the format as a lifeline for building friendships in a post-pandemic landscape, consistent with Eventbrite's own finding that roughly 79 percent of surveyed Gen Z and millennial respondents were actively seeking events blending multiple interests rather than a single narrow activity. The book club's growth is notable specifically because it runs counter to the trend in the underlying activity it is nominally organized around: average American reading time has fallen from roughly 8.3 hours a week in 2004 to about 4.2 hours by 2024, meaning book club membership is rising even as the amount of actual reading Americans do on their own has roughly halved — further evidence, alongside the sober-bar trend examined above, that the specific commercial or cultural activity nominally organizing one of these gatherings may matter considerably less to its participants than the recurring, structured social contact built around it.

Board game cafés represent another small but genuinely growing category worth naming alongside run clubs and the sober-bar movement, though this book applies its usual skepticism to the specific market-size figures circulating for the category, most of which trace to industry-report aggregators of the kind this book has treated cautiously elsewhere rather than to independently audited data. What is more directly verifiable is the specific, named expansion of individual venues explicitly built around Oldenburg's third-place model, monetizing a modest food-and-beverage markup rather than charging directly for the social connection itself: The Uncommons in New York and Snakes & Lattes in Toronto, among a growing number of similar operators opening across North American cities, combine an extensive board-game library with café or bar service, deliberately designed to attract customers who show up specifically to play with strangers or acquaintances rather than to eat or drink as the primary purpose of the visit. The demographic these venues report attracting — millennials, Generation Z, and families specifically seeking an affordable, structured, low-pressure social outing — overlaps closely with Strava's own reported run-club demographic, reinforcing this chapter's broader observation that the businesses succeeding at rebuilding something like Oldenburg's third place tend to share a specific structure: a modest, secondary commercial transaction (a coffee, a game rental fee, running shoes) functioning as the entry ticket to an experience whose real value lies in the recurring social contact surrounding it, rather than a business model that tries to charge directly for the connection as the product itself.

A parallel platform, older than Strava and organized around a broader range of interests than running specifically, tells a compatible story. Meetup, the event-organizing platform founded in 2002, reports roughly 60 million users organized into more than 300,000 groups spanning over 10,000 cities worldwide, with the company's own tracking showing the share of its events held in person, rather than virtually, rising from 71 percent in 2022 to 79 percent in 2023 — a shift toward physical gathering running directly counter to the pandemic-era assumption that virtual events had become the platform's durable default. The company's own measurement research found the average user pursuing about 2.4 distinct hobbies or interests and belonging to roughly five different groups simultaneously, suggesting that for the population actively using the platform, community-seeking behavior is neither a rare, one-off activity nor concentrated in a single interest area, but a recurring, multi-pronged habit spread across several simultaneous group memberships — closer, in its texture, to the dense, overlapping associational membership Putnam's 1995 data described American adults having in the mid-twentieth century than to the thin, single-membership participation more typical of recent decades.

Strava's numbers come with an obvious caveat this book has applied to every self-reported company statistic so far: they describe Strava's own platform, self-reported by a company with a clear commercial interest in framing its user base as growing and socially engaged, not an independently audited measure of run-club participation across the population at large. But the underlying activity-log data behind those percentages — actual GPS-tracked group runs, not survey opinions about intentions — is harder to fabricate or exaggerate than a pure sentiment survey would be, which gives the trend more evidentiary weight than a marketing claim built entirely on stated preference. And the trend fits a pattern this chapter has been assembling from several independent directions: something that looks very much like a deliberate, informal rebuilding of exactly the kind of low-cost, recurring, geographically anchored social infrastructure this book's earlier chapters described eroding — a Tuesday-night bowling league's modern replacement, arguably closer in spirit to Oldenburg's original third place than most of the paid, venture-funded alternatives this book has examined so far, precisely because a run club typically costs nothing to join, requires no subscription, and monetizes, if it monetizes at all, only incidentally through gear sales or occasional gym partnerships rather than through a direct charge on the desire for company itself.

The businesses examined earlier in this book occupy the opposite end of that spectrum, and their comparative struggles are worth revisiting here, side by side with the run club's apparent success, because the contrast is instructive. WeWork, examined in chapter six, tried to sell workplace community as a standalone, subscription-priced product and filed for bankruptcy in November 2023 carrying more than $18.6 billion in debt, a collapse driven primarily by its underlying real-estate economics rather than by any failure of its community programming specifically, but a collapse that nonetheless demonstrates how difficult it has proven to build a durable, profitable business around manufacturing connection as a purchasable good rather than as an unplanned byproduct of something else. Peloton, examined in the same chapter, built a genuinely large and, for years, genuinely profitable subscription business around simulated fitness-class community, only to see its subscriber base contract meaningfully in the years following the pandemic, falling to roughly 2.66 million paid Connected Fitness subscriptions by the quarter ending December 31, 2025, down 7 percent year over year, as the specific value proposition of paying a recurring fee for a screen-mediated version of a shared workout lost ground once in-person alternatives reopened and cheaper competitors entered the market. WeWork's own collapse is worth distinguishing carefully from the fate of the coworking industry as a whole, a distinction easy to blur if WeWork's bankruptcy is read as a verdict on shared workspace generally rather than on one company's specific financial structure. The broader flexible-workspace sector has, in fact, continued growing substantially since WeWork's 2023 filing: industry tracking places the global coworking market in the range of $26 billion in 2025, expanding at a compound annual rate approaching 15 percent, with profitability among operators improving sharply — one 2025 industry survey found 58 percent of coworking operators reporting profitability, against only 18 percent operating at a loss, a marked turnaround from the sector's earlier, WeWork-defined reputation for burning cash indefinitely in pursuit of growth. Commercial real estate giant CBRE's 2025 move to fully acquire Industrious, a coworking operator it had already partnered with, reflected rising demand specifically from large, established employers — Fortune 500 companies increasingly treating flexible office space as a standard component of hybrid-work strategy rather than a startup-culture novelty. Total flexible office inventory in the industry's own tracking climbed 16 percent in a recent year, adding more than 22 million square feet of new flexible workspace to the market even as WeWork itself was restructuring. The lesson this book draws from the contrast is narrower and more useful than "coworking failed" or "coworking succeeded": WeWork's specific model — aggressive lease-driven overexpansion paired with community programming as a marketing differentiator rather than a disciplined cost structure — failed, while a more financially conservative version of the same basic product, oriented around established corporate clients rather than a mission of manufacturing community for its own sake, has continued growing at a healthy pace, suggesting that shared workspace itself supplies real, durable value even where the specific company that pioneered its most visible, most connection-forward version could not survive on the strength of that value alone.

Bumble For Friends, examined in chapter seven, launched as a standalone app in 2023 explicitly betting that dating-app fatigue would translate into appetite for a platonic version of the same swipe-based matching mechanism, and by Morgan Stanley's 2023 estimate generated roughly $1 million in revenue against Bumble's total company revenue of just over $1 billion — real evidence of demand, by the company's own reported engagement data, but so far strikingly modest evidence of anyone's willingness to pay for it directly.

A more specific and somewhat unexpected rebuild is happening inside the very category chapter five described losing ground fastest: the neighborhood bar. Rather than abandoning the bar as a social format, a growing wave of operators has begun reconstructing it around a different core product, responding to a documented generational shift in alcohol consumption. Industry surveys of Generation Z adults have found roughly three in five reporting they drink rarely or not at all, with just over a fifth reporting no alcohol consumption whatsoever, a pattern young adults and marketers alike have taken to calling being "sober curious" — not full abstinence as a fixed identity, but an active, ongoing experimentation with drinking less, or not at all, without necessarily swearing off alcohol permanently or judging peers who still drink. Alcohol-free venues explicitly modeled on the bar as social format — spaces like No More Cafe and Mockingbird in New York, among a growing number of similar operators in other cities — have opened specifically to serve this demand, offering elaborate mocktails, structured social programming, and the same after-work or weekend-evening gathering function a conventional bar provides, minus the product that used to define the category. The broader no- and low-alcohol beverage market, valued at more than $11 billion in 2022, was projected to grow by roughly a third by 2026, a trajectory that runs directly counter to the closure statistics chapter five documented for conventional, alcohol-centered bars and pubs. Read alongside this book's earlier chapters, the sober-bar trend suggests something genuinely interesting about which specific element of the traditional third place was actually load-bearing: not the alcohol itself, which an entire emerging category of successful venues has now proven dispensable, but the reliable, low-commitment, recurring social gathering the alcohol had simply been the historical excuse for — further evidence, alongside the run club and Meetup data examined above, that the underlying appetite for exactly the kind of institution Oldenburg described has not disappeared even where its most traditional commercial form has been struggling.

Reading these cases together against the run club's apparent momentum suggests a pattern worth stating plainly, because it recurs across nearly every business this book examines and will matter again in the chapters ahead on AI companionship's commercial prospects. The connection businesses that have struggled hardest share a common structure: they ask users to pay a direct, recurring fee specifically for access to connection or its simulation, decoupled from any other clearly useful thing the payment also buys. The connection-adjacent institutions that appear to be growing most robustly — run clubs prominent among them, but also, by less rigorously documented anecdotal and trade-press accounts, coworking-adjacent "third workspace" cafés, hobbyist meetup groups organized informally through free platforms, and neighborhood mutual-aid networks that expanded rapidly during the pandemic and have persisted since in many cities — tend to charge little or nothing directly for the social component, instead monetizing, if at all, an adjacent good: running shoes, coffee, gym membership, a shared skill or hobby that would have value even without the social element attached. This is not a universal law, and this book does not have the systematic, controlled evidence to claim it as one with full confidence; it is a pattern visible across the specific cases this book has been able to document, worth naming as a hypothesis future research on the "connection economy" could test more rigorously than a single trade-nonfiction book examining a handful of high-profile cases can manage on its own.

There is a less comfortable reading of the same pattern worth sitting with before this chapter moves on, because a book arguing that unpaid social infrastructure has eroded should be honest about what it means that the most visible replacement, so far, appears to work best specifically when it stays unpaid or nearly so. If the businesses charging directly for connection keep struggling to sustain themselves at scale, that could mean the market has simply not yet found the right product — a real possibility, and one several well-funded startups examined in the next two chapters are actively betting on. Or it could mean something closer to a structural mismatch: that the specific good this book has been describing throughout — regular, low-stakes, unplanned contact with people who would notice your absence — resists direct monetization in a way that makes it a poor fit for a venture-capital-backed subscription model no matter how well designed, because the moment connection becomes explicitly transactional, priced, and optimized for engagement metrics, it stops functioning, for at least some meaningful share of users, as the same kind of good that a free bowling league, a free run club, or a free neighborhood association used to supply. This book does not have a confident answer to which of these two readings is closer to correct, and treats the question as genuinely open rather than resolving it for the sake of a tidier argument. What the evidence assembled in this chapter does support, more confidently, is that the informal, low-cost, community-organized version of the rebuild — people showing up to run together on a Tuesday morning for no reason other than that other people will be there — currently shows the clearest signs of actually working, at a moment when several much better-funded, much more heavily marketed paid alternatives have struggled to demonstrate the same.

None of this settles the larger question the next several chapters take up directly: what role government, rather than either the market or informal community organizing, should play in rebuilding what has eroded. A run club can flourish without anyone deciding, from a position of policy authority, that it should. The institutions examined next — a British cabinet ministry, a Japanese national law, a World Health Organization commission — represent a different kind of response entirely, one that treats the erosion this book has documented not as a gap for the market to fill, profitably or otherwise, but as a public problem serious enough to warrant the deliberate machinery of government.
