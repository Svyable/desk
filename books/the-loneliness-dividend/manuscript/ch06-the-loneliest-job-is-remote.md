# The Loneliest Job Is Remote

For most of the twentieth century, a job supplied social infrastructure whether or not the employer thought of it that way. It put a person in a fixed physical location, on a predictable schedule, alongside the same colleagues for years at a stretch — long enough for acquaintance to deepen into something closer to friendship, for office culture to develop its own private jokes and unwritten norms, for a coworker's absence to be noticed by lunchtime rather than by the end of a fiscal quarter. Putnam's earlier chapters showed what happened to the associational institutions that used to supply connection outside of work. This chapter examines what has happened to the connective function of work itself, as a large and growing share of the workforce has stopped sharing a physical room with colleagues at all.

The scale of the shift toward remote and hybrid work following the COVID-19 pandemic is well documented and does not need extensive relitigation here; what matters for this book is a narrower and more recent finding, drawn from the most comprehensive workplace survey instrument that exists. Gallup's "State of the Global Workplace" report, in its 2024 edition covering 2023 data, asked employees around the world how they had experienced the previous day, including whether they had felt lonely. Roughly one in five employees globally — about 20 percent — reported experiencing loneliness "a lot" of the previous day, a substantial baseline on its own. But the figure that matters most for this chapter is how sharply that number varied by work arrangement: fully remote employees reported loneliness at 25 percent, hybrid employees at 21 percent, and fully on-site employees at 16 percent — the largest gap Gallup found across any variable it measured in that year's survey, larger than the gaps by age, income, or industry. Loneliness was also more prevalent among employees under 35, a detail that lines up with the age pattern Holt-Lunstad's 2015 meta-analysis found in the mortality data discussed two chapters earlier: contrary to the common assumption that loneliness is chiefly an old-age problem, younger workers, disproportionately represented in remote-capable knowledge industries, appear to be carrying more of this particular burden than their older colleagues.

Remote knowledge work is not the only, or even necessarily the most severe, form of employment isolation this book's research turned up, and it would be a mistake to let the Gallup data's focus on white-collar remote versus in-office work obscure a parallel and in some ways starker pattern among gig and platform workers, whose isolation is built into the structure of the job rather than into a choice about where to sit while doing it. Sociologists Paul Glavin, Alex Bierman, and Scott Schieman, in a 2021 study published in the journal *Work and Occupations* titled "Über-Alienated: Powerless and Alone in the Gig Economy," found that gig workers were almost twice as likely as other workers to report frequently experiencing a sign of loneliness, alongside lower reported happiness and a diminished sense of control over their working lives — with ride-share driving specifically showing a stronger association with powerlessness and isolation than other forms of gig or platform-mediated crowdwork, a difference the researchers attributed in part to algorithmic management structures that route, price, and evaluate a driver's work without the ordinary peer contact, supervisory relationships, or workplace culture that even a traditional low-wage service job typically provides. A more recent 2026 study in the *Journal of Business Ethics*, titled "Lonely Work: Gig Work, Workplace Technology and Working-Class Loneliness," extended this line of research further, examining how the specific technological architecture of platform work — apps that route assignments algorithmically, discourage direct communication between workers, and in some cases actively engineer distance between a driver and their passenger, including ride-hailing features that let a rider select a "quiet preferred" option turning a driver into what the researchers describe as an effectively silent chauffeur — can actively manufacture isolation as a structural byproduct of how the work itself is designed, rather than as an incidental side effect of where the worker happens to be physically located. Where the Gallup data examined above raises an open question about whether remote work causes loneliness or merely selects for workers already prone to it, the gig-economy research points toward a more direct mechanism: platform design choices that measurably increase a worker's isolation as a side effect of optimizing for efficiency, rider preference, or algorithmic simplicity, with the affected workers holding little power to negotiate a different arrangement.

Gallup's own data cannot settle the question of causation, and this book is not going to pretend it can either. The nine-point gap between fully remote and fully on-site workers is consistent with at least two very different underlying stories. The first is straightforwardly causal: removing daily physical proximity to colleagues removes a source of casual, low-stakes social contact — the conversation waiting for the coffee machine, the small talk on the walk to a meeting room, the shared lunch that requires no scheduling because it simply happens — and workers who lose that contact become measurably lonelier as a direct result. The second is a selection story: people who are already more prone to loneliness, for reasons unrelated to their job, may be more likely to choose or accept remote positions in the first place, precisely because remote work removes an obligation — daily in-person social performance — that a person already struggling with connection might find draining rather than sustaining. Gallup's survey design, a single cross-sectional snapshot rather than a study that tracks the same workers before and after a change in work arrangement, cannot distinguish between these explanations, and neither can most of the similar workplace research this book reviewed in preparing this chapter. What the data can establish, cleanly, is the correlation itself: work arrangement is now one of the strongest single predictors of workplace loneliness that a major global survey instrument has found, regardless of which direction the underlying causal arrow runs, or whether it runs in both directions at once, reinforcing itself over time.

The economic infrastructure built around the assumption of in-person work has reorganized itself in response, and nowhere more visibly than in the fate of the companies that tried to sell a substitute for the traditional office rather than a replacement for it. WeWork, the coworking company that had, at its 2019 peak private valuation, been worth roughly $47 billion, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in a New Jersey federal court on November 6, 2023, listing more than $18.6 billion in debt against just over $15 billion in assets. The proximate causes were straightforwardly financial — years of aggressive lease expansion, locking the company into long-term fixed rent obligations that collided badly with a wave of tenants downsizing or abandoning office space after the pandemic accelerated remote work — but the underlying business model is worth examining on its own terms, because WeWork had marketed itself from its earliest days not merely as a real-estate arbitrage play, subletting office space at a markup, but explicitly as a community product: shared kitchens, company-wide events, a promise that renting a desk at WeWork bought access to a network of fellow entrepreneurs and professionals, not just a chair and a Wi-Fi password. The company's implosion does not, on its own, prove that the community pitch was hollow; WeWork's core financial problems were structural and would likely have sunk the company regardless of how successful its community programming was. But the fact that a company built explicitly to sell workplace connection as a standalone product, decoupled from a specific employer, could not make the economics work at scale is itself informative about how difficult it is to manufacture, as a purchasable product, what used to arrive as an unplanned byproduct of simply having a job that required showing up somewhere.

This chapter's analysis of remote work's effect on colleague contact was largely written on the assumption that the colleagues in question are other human beings, an assumption already beginning to erode by the time this book was researched. Since April 2026, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google have each released tools explicitly marketed around managing teams of autonomous AI agents — software marketed, in some cases quite directly, as "digital colleagues" capable of reasoning through problems, coordinating across departments, and making independent decisions without constant human supervision, rather than as the simpler conversational chatbots examined in chapter eight. The economic logic driving this shift is already visible in real corporate decisions: Atlassian announced in March 2026 that it would cut roughly 10 percent of its global workforce, about 1,600 jobs, partly to self-fund further investment in AI systems, and industry researchers have argued that AI-leveraged organizations should converge toward smaller teams augmented by agents rather than larger teams of humans, with some projecting a team of five engineers equipped with frontier AI agents could match the output of a team of fifty without them. Early research on the human side of this transition has found a consistent and troubling pattern: workers who adopt AI tools earliest and most heavily report a growing loss of connection to their actual human coworkers, alongside a diminished, rather than enhanced, sense of their own productivity — a finding that, if it holds up under further study, would extend this chapter's central argument into genuinely new territory. Remote work, examined throughout this chapter, removed a colleague's physical proximity while leaving the colleague, and the underlying human relationship, intact. A workplace increasingly staffed by AI agents removes something more fundamental: not the location of the coworker, but the coworker's existence as a person capable of being missed, checked in on, or built into exactly the kind of practical-noticing relationship this book's chapter ten identified as the mechanism that matters most, a shift this book can document emerging but cannot yet, at this early stage, fully assess.

It would be incomplete, and arguably unfair to remote work as a category, to close this chapter's account without examining a genuinely positive countercurrent this same shift has produced at a different geographic scale. While this chapter has focused on remote work's effect on the individual worker's daily social contact, the same shift has measurably reshaped where Americans choose to live, with real benefits for the kind of small-town and rural third-place infrastructure examined in chapter five. Census data shows some of the smallest American metro areas, those under 250,000 residents, including many classified as rural, reversing years of population decline among working-age adults, gaining at least 100,000 net new residents in each of several recent years as remote-capable workers relocated away from expensive, dense urban cores. That inbound population has brought real downtown investment with it: Main Street America, the national network supporting small-town commercial-corridor revitalization, reported $5.68 billion in local reinvestment into downtowns and commercial corridors in a single recent year, helping open more than 6,600 new businesses and create over 35,000 new jobs, much of it explicitly aimed at building exactly the coffee shops, coworking spaces, and small commercial third places this book's chapter five described struggling in denser, more expensive markets. Remote work, in other words, is not a uniformly isolating force once the analysis moves from the individual worker's daily experience to the aggregate effect on where people choose to live: the same flexibility that chapter six's Gallup data shows correlating with higher individual loneliness has, at the level of entire small communities, funded a wave of exactly the kind of physical third-place investment this book has spent its earlier chapters describing as scarce, a genuine complication worth holding alongside this chapter's more pessimistic individual-level findings rather than resolving in either direction.

A parallel pattern shows up in a very different corner of the pandemic-era connection economy: at-home fitness. Peloton built its business on a similar theory to WeWork's, applied to exercise rather than office space — the company's stationary bikes and treadmills came bundled with a subscription to live and on-demand classes designed to simulate the social energy of a studio class, complete with instructors calling out riders' names during live sessions and a leaderboard meant to create a sense of shared, simultaneous effort among strangers exercising alone in their separate homes. The company's paid Connected Fitness subscriptions grew explosively during the pandemic, when gyms were closed and home workout equipment briefly became one of the most sought-after consumer purchases in the country. Growth has not held. By the quarter ending December 31, 2025, Peloton reported roughly 2.66 million paid Connected Fitness subscriptions, down 7 percent from the same quarter a year earlier, part of a longer decline from the company's pandemic-era peak that has also seen its standalone digital-only subscriber tier — the version of the product with no hardware purchase required, priced accordingly lower — shrink even more sharply as competition from cheaper alternatives and a broader return to in-person gyms eroded the specific value proposition Peloton had built its subscription around. Peloton's numbers do not, by themselves, prove that its simulated-community model failed to deliver real connection to the people who used it; many riders report genuine attachment to specific instructors and to the leaderboard community, and a shrinking subscriber base is also a story about post-pandemic gym reopening and general fitness-market competition, not solely a referendum on whether a screen can replace a shared room. But both WeWork and Peloton share a structural lesson worth carrying into the rest of this book: businesses explicitly built to manufacture, as a standalone purchase, the connective tissue that used to arrive bundled into an ordinary job or an ordinary gym membership have, so far, proven harder to sustain at scale than the underlying human need for connection would suggest they should be.

The gap between what return-to-office mandates like Amazon's are trying to accomplish and what they actually produce in practice shows up most vividly in a workplace behavior that has acquired its own name: "coffee badging," in which an employee complies with an in-office attendance requirement in letter rather than spirit, swiping their building badge to register attendance, spending perhaps an hour or two in the office making brief small talk near the coffee machine, and then leaving to do the actual substance of the day's work from home. Owl Labs' 2025 State of Hybrid Work report found 44 percent of hybrid employees admitting to the practice, down somewhat from 58 percent in 2023 but still describing a substantial share of the ostensibly in-office workforce, with 70 percent of self-reported coffee badgers admitting they had at some point been caught by their employer doing it. The economics behind the behavior are straightforward and, in their own way, illuminating for this chapter's argument: workers cited in the same reporting estimated spending an average of roughly $51 a day in commuting, parking, and food costs simply to be physically present in an office, a real financial cost weighed against a benefit — the connective tissue this chapter has spent its length describing — that many employees, based on their own revealed behavior, do not judge worth a full workday's commitment to earn, even while apparently judging it worth a shortened, ritualized version of the same commitment. Coffee badging is, in a strange way, the clearest empirical evidence available that employees themselves recognize some real value in physical office presence — enough to bother showing up and making the effort of a brief, in-person social interaction — while simultaneously judging that value insufficient to justify the full cost, in time, money, and lost flexibility, of a genuine full return to the office as originally practiced. It is connection valued highly enough to perform, but not highly enough to fully fund, a compromise that captures this entire chapter's central tension in a single, quietly rebellious daily habit.

This does not mean remote work is simply bad, or that every remote worker is quietly suffering, any more than the earlier chapters on solo living meant every person who lives alone is quietly suffering. Remote and hybrid arrangements have delivered real, well-documented benefits — reduced commuting time returned to workers rather than lost to traffic, greater flexibility for people managing caregiving responsibilities or disabilities that made traditional office attendance genuinely difficult, and, for some workers, the ability to live in a lower-cost or more personally suitable location entirely disconnected from where their employer happens to be headquartered. Several companies that adopted remote or flexible arrangements report improved retention and employee satisfaction on other dimensions even as workplace loneliness rose on this one. The honest picture, consistent with how this book has tried to handle every trade-off so far, is not that remote work is a mistake to be reversed, but that it removed a source of unpaid, unplanned social contact that most employers never had to think about supplying deliberately, because a shared physical office supplied it automatically, and few organizations have yet found a substitute that works as well as the thing it replaced.

Some employers have tried, and Amazon's experience shows both the ambition and the friction involved in trying at the largest possible scale. In September 2024, Amazon announced that its roughly 350,000 corporate employees would be required to work in the office five days a week beginning in January 2025, reversing the hybrid arrangements many had settled into since the pandemic, with company leadership framing the change explicitly around rebuilding the kind of spontaneous collaboration, mentorship, and cultural cohesion executives argued had eroded under distributed work. The employee reaction, measured through the anonymous professional platform Blind, was sharply negative: 91 percent of Amazon employees responding to a poll on the platform said they were dissatisfied with the new schedule, and 73 percent said they were considering looking for another job as a result, while more than 500 employees signed a formal letter to an Amazon Web Services executive arguing the mandate lacked supporting data and would fall hardest on employees with caregiving responsibilities or particular accessibility needs. Amazon's own public defense of the policy rested substantially on an argument about connection rather than mere productivity — the idea that certain kinds of trust, mentorship, and cultural transmission depend on physical co-presence in ways video calls cannot fully replicate — which makes the case a genuinely useful, if contested, test of whether mandating a return to the pre-pandemic default can actually rebuild what this chapter's Gallup data shows eroded, or whether it simply reproduces the same physical proximity without the voluntary, unforced quality that made the original office culture feel like community rather than compliance.

Remote work's displacement onto third places has produced its own small, telling conflict, worth reporting here because it shows the tension between chapters five and six colliding directly in a single physical space. As offices thinned out under hybrid and remote arrangements, many displaced knowledge workers relocated their laptops to coffee shops, seeking exactly the kind of ambient, peopled backdrop this book's earlier chapters described a home office lacking. Some cafés have welcomed the resulting business. Others have pushed back directly, arguing that a room full of silent laptop workers, each individually present but not interacting with anyone else, actively destroys the social atmosphere a café depends on to function as a genuine third place rather than merely a room full of individually isolated remote offices that happen to share a coffee machine. A café in Oakland, California, banned laptops entirely on weekends and reported a markedly livelier dining room as a result; a London café called Lumberjack posted small signs reading "No laptop tables" on roughly half its seating specifically to preserve space for people who wanted to sit and talk with each other rather than work in silence; and a wider wave of similar restrictions — some banning laptops outright, others disabling Wi-Fi during peak hours, still others limiting laptop use to specific days — spread across cafés in multiple cities during 2025. One owner, quoted in coverage of the trend, put the underlying tension in terms directly relevant to this book's argument: having someone sit for hours instead of paying for a proper coworking desk serves neither the business's finances nor its community, and preserving "equity in terms of customer enjoyment" required actively defending the room's social function against exactly the kind of remote-work displacement chapter six has described happening at the level of the individual worker's daily routine. The café laptop-ban trend is, in miniature, this book's entire argument playing out inside a single room: a business owner discovering that the third-place function Oldenburg described does not survive automatically just because bodies are physically present, and that defending it sometimes requires actively excluding the very activity — solitary remote work — that filled the seats in the first place.

Mandatory in-office days, company retreats, structured team-building programming, and dedicated "culture" budgets have all become more common in hybrid workplaces specifically framed, in internal communications this book reviewed secondhand through public reporting on corporate return-to-office policies, as attempts to rebuild the connective tissue that daily physical proximity used to supply for free. Whether these interventions work as well as the thing they are trying to replace is an open, under-researched question this book does not have a definitive answer to, and it is worth being honest about that gap rather than filling it with confident-sounding speculation. What is not in doubt, from the Gallup data this chapter opened with, is the size of the gap they are trying to fill: a nine-percentage-point difference in loneliness between the most and least physically connected forms of employment, in the largest workplace survey instrument that exists, at a moment when a meaningful and likely permanent share of the knowledge-economy workforce has moved into the more isolated category. The next chapter follows a related but distinct erosion — not the workplace relationships that used to form without deliberate effort, but the romantic ones, and the industry that has grown up specifically to replace the courtship infrastructure that used to surround them.
