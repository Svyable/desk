# The Cost of Being Alone

Somewhere inside the actuarial departments of Medicare's administrators, there is a version of this book's entire argument reduced to a spreadsheet. In November 2017, researchers Lynda Flowers, Ari Houser, Claire Noel-Miller, and their coauthors, working through the AARP Public Policy Institute in collaboration with researchers at Stanford, published a study attempting to answer a narrow, tractable version of a much larger question this book has been circling since its opening chapter: not whether loneliness feels bad, or whether it correlates with mortality in the way Holt-Lunstad's meta-analyses had already established, but specifically how much more the federal government's largest health insurance program spends on beneficiaries who are socially isolated compared to those who are not.

The answer, linking objectively measured isolation — small social networks, infrequent contact with others, not the subjective feeling of loneliness, a distinction the study's authors were careful to draw and this book is equally careful to preserve — to Medicare claims data, was $6.7 billion in additional annual spending nationally, or roughly $1,608 more per year for each isolated beneficiary compared to a socially connected one. The study estimated that about 14 percent of Medicare beneficiaries, or roughly 4 million people, met its criteria for having "meager" social networks. It is worth dwelling on the distinction the study's own title makes explicit — "Social Isolation and Medicare Spending: Among Older Adults, Objective Isolation Increases Expenditures While Loneliness Does Not" — because it is exactly the kind of finding that gets flattened in translation. What predicted higher Medicare spending was not how isolated people said they felt; it was the measurable size and frequency of their actual social contact. A person who feels lonely despite having a large, active network did not, in this data, cost the system more. A person with an objectively small network, whether or not they described themselves as unhappy about it, did. The finding complicates any simple story in which loneliness is primarily a subjective mood problem, suggesting instead that the health consequences trace more directly to the practical function a social network serves — someone who notices a missed appointment, someone who catches a worsening symptom early, someone who can drive a person to urgent care before a manageable problem becomes an emergency-room admission — than to the emotional experience of isolation on its own.

The Surgeon General's 2023 advisory, discussed at length in chapter three, cited the Flowers et al. figure directly, which is worth noting because it illustrates how a single 2017 study, rather than an independently replicated body of newer research, has come to anchor much of the public conversation about loneliness's healthcare cost. A more recent attempt to widen that evidentiary base arrived in 2025, in the form of a peer-reviewed systematic literature review that pooled findings from fifteen separate studies across multiple countries — six cost-of-illness studies, four formal economic evaluations, and five studies using a "social return on investment" methodology — attempting to establish an updated, more internationally representative picture of loneliness and isolation's economic burden. The review's finding, honestly reported, is less tidy than a single headline number: excess costs across the pooled studies ranged from roughly $2 billion to $25.2 billion annually, a range spanning more than an order of magnitude, reflecting genuinely different countries, populations, time periods, and methodologies rather than a single converged estimate. This book is choosing to report that wide range rather than pick the most dramatic figure within it and present it as settled, because the range itself is the more honest finding: the field has established, with reasonable confidence, that social isolation carries a real, measurable healthcare cost large enough to matter at a national-budget scale, without having converged on a single authoritative dollar figure for what that cost actually is in any given country in any given year. A reader encountering a specific, precise-sounding number for "the cost of loneliness" elsewhere should treat that precision with some skepticism; the underlying research, read honestly, supports the direction and the rough order of magnitude, not a decimal-point-accurate total.

Healthcare spending is not the only channel through which this cost has been quantified, and a parallel line of research, commissioned by the Co-op — a British retail and financial cooperative that was itself a founding partner of the Jo Cox Commission on Loneliness examined in chapter twelve — and compiled by the New Economics Foundation, priced the cost of employee loneliness directly to British businesses at roughly £2.5 billion a year. The breakdown behind that total is worth walking through because it identifies a different, more purely economic mechanism than the healthcare-utilization pathway the AARP-Stanford research traced: the largest single component, accounting for about 64 percent of the total, or roughly £1.62 billion, came from increased staff turnover, since lonely employees, by the researchers' own finding, were roughly five times more likely than their colleagues to leave a job within a year. A further 26 percent, or about £665 million, was attributed to reduced wellbeing and productivity among employees who stayed, with smaller shares attributed to the burden loneliness placed on colleagues taking on caregiving-adjacent support roles and to sickness absence directly. The researchers themselves described their assumptions as conservative and their scope as limited to the more direct, easily quantified costs falling on employers specifically, suggesting the true figure, inclusive of harder-to-measure costs, could run considerably higher. Read alongside the AARP-Stanford Medicare data examined above, the Co-op figure extends this chapter's central finding into a second, entirely separate economic domain: loneliness is not only a cost the healthcare system absorbs once a person's isolation has progressed far enough to generate medical claims, but also, well before that point, a cost employers absorb directly, in the mundane, ordinary form of higher recruitment expenses and lost institutional knowledge every time a lonely employee decides the job, like everything else in their life, is not worth staying in.

A more recent and, in several respects, more rigorously designed study offers a British parallel to the American Medicare research this chapter opened with, using a considerably larger and more current dataset than the 2017 AARP-Stanford study had available. Researchers at the University of Exeter, publishing in *PLOS One* in September 2025, analyzed data from 23,071 participants in the Understanding Society UK Household Longitudinal Study, linking self-reported loneliness between 2021 and 2023 to actual National Health Service resource use, priced using standard NHS unit costs of roughly £49 per general-practitioner visit, £217 per outpatient visit, and £1,111 per inpatient case. The study found people who reported feeling lonely "often" — about 8 percent of the sample, with a further 32 percent reporting loneliness at least some of the time — incurred approximately £900 more in annual NHS costs than people who did not report loneliness, a finding broadly consistent in direction, if not in exact magnitude or methodology, with the American Medicare data this chapter opened with. The Exeter study's most analytically interesting finding, though, is its age pattern: rather than a simple linear relationship in which cost rises steadily with age, the loneliness-cost association traced what the researchers described as a U-shaped curve, elevated among young adults aged 16 to 24 and rising again among older adults, with a comparative trough in cost impact across the middle of the adult lifespan. That U-shape lines up suggestively, though the two studies are different enough in design and population that this book stops short of calling it confirmation, with the Cigna generational data examined below showing the highest reported loneliness among the youngest adults surveyed, and with Holt-Lunstad's 2015 finding, discussed in chapter three, that loneliness's mortality effect was actually stronger in younger study populations than commonly assumed — three separate, methodologically distinct bodies of research, spanning American mortality data, British healthcare cost data, and insurer-sponsored prevalence surveys, converging on a similar and genuinely counterintuitive point: whatever this book's title suggests about loneliness accumulating with age, several of the best-designed studies available find its most acute costs concentrated disproportionately among the young.

Insurance companies, whose entire business model depends on pricing risk accurately enough to remain solvent while remaining competitive, have taken loneliness seriously enough to fund their own primary research into it, rather than simply relying on academic literature — a choice that is itself a form of evidence about how seriously the industry takes the underlying phenomenon, independent of what any given survey finds. Cigna's U.S. Loneliness Index, first published in 2018 based on an Ipsos-fielded online survey of more than 20,000 American adults using the widely validated twenty-item UCLA Loneliness Scale, found 54 percent of U.S. adults reporting loneliness at a level the scale classified as lonely — already a striking baseline finding on its own. A follow-up survey in 2020 found the figure had risen to 61 percent, with nearly eight in ten Generation Z respondents, then the youngest cohort surveyed, reporting loneliness, the highest rate of any generation the survey measured, and generally declining loneliness by generation moving from Gen Z through Millennials, Gen X, and Baby Boomers — a generational pattern that lines up with the age-related finding in Holt-Lunstad's 2015 meta-analysis, discussed in chapter three, showing stronger mortality effects in younger study populations than older ones, and with the Gallup workplace data in chapter six showing higher loneliness among workers under 35.

It is worth noting, in the interest of the same transparency this book has tried to apply to every source it relies on, that Cigna's survey is not a neutral academic instrument. It is commissioned by a health insurer with an obvious commercial interest in publicizing findings that support the value of its wellness-oriented product lines, fielded through a private polling firm using a specific loneliness cutoff on the UCLA scale that some researchers in this space have argued sets the bar for counting as "lonely" lower than other commonly used instruments, which would tend to inflate the reported prevalence relative to studies using a stricter threshold. None of that means the Cigna figures are wrong, and the direction they point — rising loneliness concentrated most heavily among the youngest adults, a group popularly assumed to be the most digitally connected generation in human history — is broadly consistent with the other, more independently sourced data this chapter and earlier chapters have already presented. But a reader should hold the specific percentages more loosely than the percentages from a peer-reviewed academic meta-analysis, understanding them as industry-sponsored market research that happens to be methodologically legitimate, rather than as independent science.

The single longest-running body of evidence behind this chapter's entire argument predates every other study cited in this book by decades and deserves its own direct mention before this chapter turns to its positive inverse. The Harvard Study of Adult Development, now in its ninth decade of continuous operation, began in 1938 by tracking 724 initial participants — Harvard undergraduates on one hand, boys from disadvantaged Boston neighborhoods on the other — and has since expanded to include roughly 2,500 people, including the spouses and children of the original cohort, in a research effort now old enough to be tracking multi-generational outcomes within the same families. Robert Waldinger, the study's fourth director and a professor of psychiatry at Harvard Medical School, has summarized the study's central, decades-validated finding in terms that align precisely with this book's own argument: good relationships, ranging from casual acquaintance to lifelong partnership, are the factor most consistently associated with participants being happier, physically healthier, and longer-lived than their peers, outperforming wealth, professional achievement, and fame as predictors of a good later life, an association the study's researchers have been able to observe directly through decades of blood draws, brain scans, videotaped interviews, and family interviews rather than through a single retrospective survey. Waldinger's public presentation of these findings, delivered originally as a TED talk, has become one of the most-watched talks in the platform's history, with tens of millions of views — a reminder, worth sitting with given this book's earlier chapters on the struggles of manufactured connection businesses, that the underlying finding clearly resonates with an enormous public audience even though translating "invest in your relationships" into a purchasable, scalable product has proven, across nearly every business this book has examined, considerably harder than the finding's popularity would suggest it should be.

It is worth pairing this chapter's cost data with its positive inverse, because a book built entirely around the price of isolation risks implying that connection's only value is the cost it prevents rather than a good in its own right. Okinawa, Japan, has drawn sustained research attention as one of the regions researcher Dan Buettner popularized under the term "Blue Zones" — areas with an unusually high concentration of centenarians and unusually low rates of chronic disease, alongside Sardinia, the Nicoya Peninsula in Costa Rica, and the Greek island of Ikaria. Okinawan researchers and longevity scientists studying the region have pointed specifically to the *moai*, a traditional social support structure in which small groups, often formed in childhood, commit to meeting regularly for life, pooling financial, emotional, and practical support across every stage of a member's life course, as one of the leading candidate explanations for the region's exceptional longevity, alongside diet and other lifestyle factors. This book flags, in the same spirit of honest qualification it has applied throughout, that Blue Zones research more broadly has faced real methodological scrutiny in recent years, including questions raised by demographers about the reliability of extreme-age record-keeping in some of the identified regions, and this book is not treating the *moai*-longevity connection as definitively proven causation any more than it has treated any other single-mechanism explanation in these pages as settled. What the Okinawan case does usefully illustrate, even under appropriately cautious interpretation, is the flip side of this chapter's Medicare and NHS cost data: if isolation's absence of practical, reliable, other-person contact measurably shortens life and inflates healthcare costs, then a lifelong, formally structured version of the opposite — guaranteed group membership from childhood, with explicit mutual obligations rather than the informal, easily eroded social contact this book's earlier chapters described disappearing — represents close to the theoretical maximum expression of the practical-noticing standard this chapter has been building toward, a useful reference point for evaluating how far short of that maximum the more modest interventions examined in chapter eighteen actually fall.

Life insurers, whose underwriting decisions depend on estimating an individual applicant's mortality risk decades into the future rather than a population's aggregate healthcare utilization in the near term, have begun treating this same body of research as directly relevant to their own core business. RGA, one of the world's largest life reinsurers — a company that insures the risk other insurance companies carry, giving it an unusually broad, cross-market view of mortality trends — has published its own research literature reviewing the association between loneliness, social engagement, and all-cause mortality specifically for an underwriting audience, citing the same kind of longitudinal cohort evidence this chapter has already drawn on, including UK Biobank data finding social isolation associated with a hazard ratio for all-cause mortality of 1.73 before adjusting for other factors, falling to roughly 1.26 after controlling for socioeconomic status and health behaviors — a reduction that itself illustrates how much of loneliness's apparent mortality effect is entangled with, though evidently not fully explained by, other risk factors that already correlate with social isolation. That an industry built entirely around the accurate long-term pricing of individual death risk has begun treating social connection as a legitimate, evidence-worthy underwriting consideration, alongside more traditional factors like smoking status, cholesterol, and family medical history, represents a different and in some ways more consequential form of institutional validation than a government advisory or an academic meta-analysis: insurers have a direct, immediate financial incentive to get mortality risk assessment right, and an industry actuary's professional judgment that a factor is worth pricing carries a kind of market-tested seriousness that a policy document, however well-researched, cannot fully replicate on its own.

Taken together, these several strands of evidence — the AARP-Stanford Medicare spending study, the 2025 international systematic review, the Exeter NHS cost analysis, Cigna's insurer-sponsored survey work, and the life-reinsurance industry's own underwriting research — point toward a conclusion this book wants to state carefully rather than dramatically. Social isolation carries a real, multiply-documented cost to healthcare systems, large enough in absolute dollar terms to appear in federal budget analyses and large enough in relative terms, on a per-person basis, to be actuarially significant to an insurer pricing risk across millions of policyholders. The exact size of that cost remains genuinely uncertain, varying by an order of magnitude across the best available international research, and the mechanism running from isolation to cost appears to route more through the practical, unglamorous function of having someone nearby to notice a problem early than through the subjective unhappiness of feeling lonely, a distinction with real implications for what an effective intervention should actually target. A policy or product aimed at making isolated people feel less unhappy, without also increasing the practical density of people checking in on them, may be addressing the wrong variable entirely, at least by the narrow but consequential standard of what the Medicare spending data actually found to matter.

It is worth being explicit about how this chapter's cost data should, and should not, change a reader's view of the businesses and policies examined in the rest of this book. A dollar figure, however uncertain its precise magnitude, gives a policymaker or an employer a language for justifying investment in connection that a purely humanitarian appeal cannot always secure on its own — the Co-op's £2.5 billion figure, cited later in this book's account of the rebuild economy, did real work in British policy circles precisely because it translated an emotional argument into a budgetary one. But a reader should resist the temptation to treat the largest available number as automatically the most persuasive, or the most accurate; this chapter has shown repeatedly that the more rigorously sourced figures tend to be more modest, more heavily caveated, and less rhetorically satisfying than the numbers that circulate most widely, a pattern this book has now documented enough times — the fifteen-cigarette comparison in chapter three, the AI-companion market-size claims examined in chapter seventeen, the American workplace-loneliness totals this book declined to repeat in chapter eleven — that it amounts to something close to a general rule for how to read any dramatic statistic anywhere in this space: the more precisely a number is stated, the more its source and methodology deserve to be checked before it is repeated further.

That distinction — between an intervention that changes how someone feels and an intervention that changes how often someone else notices — turns out to be the single most useful lens for evaluating almost every business and technology this book examines in the chapters ahead, from AI companions and therapeutic robots to run clubs and government loneliness ministries. Some of what has been built genuinely increases the practical density of people, or systems functioning as reasonable substitutes for people, checking in on someone at risk. Some of it, this book suspects without being able to fully prove it from the available evidence, may be better understood as addressing the feeling without addressing the underlying practical gap the Medicare data suggests actually drives the cost, and possibly the deaths, this book's opening chapter began with. The chapters ahead try to sort the businesses examined into those two categories as honestly as the available evidence allows, starting with a cluster of companies and community movements explicitly trying to rebuild something closer to the practical, checking-in kind of connection this chapter's data suggests matters most.
