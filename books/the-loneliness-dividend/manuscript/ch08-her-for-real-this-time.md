# Her, For Real This Time

On October 22, 2024, Megan Garcia filed a lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Florida, both individually and as personal representative of the estate of her son, against Character Technologies, Inc. and several individual and corporate defendants, including the company's founders and, notably, Google LLC and its parent, Alphabet Inc. Garcia's son, Sewell Setzer III, had died by suicide in February 2024, at fourteen years old, in Orlando. The complaint alleges that in the months before his death, Setzer had engaged in extensive, often deeply personal conversations with chatbot personas on Character.AI, an app that lets users create and converse with AI characters built on large language models, including one persona modeled on a character from the television series *Game of Thrones*. The suit brings claims including wrongful death, negligence, and product liability, alleging that the app was designed in ways that encouraged compulsive, addictive engagement, that some conversations had turned sexual despite the user being a known minor, and that the company was aware of, or should have been aware of, the risks its product posed to young users.

The psychological vocabulary for understanding why a text-based conversation with software could come to feel like a genuine relationship predates AI companion apps by nearly seventy years, and it is worth borrowing that vocabulary before this chapter goes further, because it clarifies what is and is not actually new about the current wave of products. Sociologists Donald Horton and Richard Wohl, writing in 1956 about the then-novel experience of television and radio audiences forming what felt like personal, reciprocal bonds with broadcast personalities who had no idea the audience member existed, coined the term "parasocial relationship" to describe this one-directional, mediated form of attachment. A viewer who felt they "knew" a favorite news anchor or talk-show host, despite the relationship existing only in the viewer's own mind and never being reciprocated in any way the broadcaster could perceive, was engaging in exactly the psychological pattern Horton and Wohl identified, decades before any computer could hold a conversation. AI companion apps intensify this dynamic in a way broadcast media never could, because unlike a television host, a chatbot actually responds, in real time, in ways that are tailored specifically to the individual user's own words, creating an experience of reciprocity that, on a surface behavioral level, is difficult for the human brain to distinguish from a genuinely two-way relationship, even though the underlying computational process generating that response has no continuous memory, no independent inner life, and no stake in the user's wellbeing beyond whatever the app's designers have chosen to optimize for. Whether this technologically intensified parasocial bond represents a fundamentally new psychological phenomenon or simply a much more convincing version of a pattern Horton and Wohl already understood in 1956 is a genuinely open question this book does not resolve; what seems clear from the litigation, research, and regulatory response examined throughout this chapter is that the intensification itself — the jump from a one-way broadcast relationship to a responsive, individually tailored, always-available one — is large enough to have produced consequences broadcast-era parasocial attachment never did.

This chapter is not going to attempt to adjudicate, on its own authority, exactly what role the app played in Setzer's death, and it is worth being explicit about why. Litigation over the case proceeded through 2025, and in May of that year, Senior Judge Anne Conway denied the defendants' motion to dismiss on First Amendment grounds — a ruling with real significance beyond this single case, because it held that the outputs generated by an AI chatbot do not automatically qualify as protected speech reflecting human intent in the way a human author's words would, a first-of-its-kind decision that let product-liability and related tort claims proceed into discovery rather than being dismissed at the threshold. That ruling settled a legal question about whether the case could proceed at all; it did not settle the underlying factual and causal questions the case was filed to litigate. In January 2026, this suit and four related family lawsuits, filed in Florida, Colorado, New York, and Texas against the same company over similar allegations involving other young users, were resolved through a settlement, on confidential terms and without any admission of liability by the defendants. A confidential settlement is, definitionally, not a public factual finding. It means the parties agreed to end the litigation on terms neither has disclosed, which this book has no independent way to verify and will not speculate about. What can be stated as documented fact — the filing, the specific allegations, the First Amendment ruling, and the eventual settlement — is what this chapter relies on, and where the record runs out, this chapter says so rather than filling the gap with a more dramatic or more reassuring version of events.

What the case does establish beyond dispute, regardless of how the underlying facts of Setzer's death are ultimately understood, is that a serious court took seriously the premise that an AI companion product could be designed, marketed, and deployed to minors in ways that carry the kind of liability exposure previously associated with physical consumer products — the same category of claim, structurally, that has been brought against automakers, toy manufacturers, and pharmaceutical companies. That premise did not exist, as a live legal theory with a judicial ruling behind it, before this case. It exists now, and it will shape how every company in the companion-AI industry, examined in the rest of this chapter, designs its products and discloses its risks going forward, independent of how the Garcia litigation itself is ultimately understood by history.

The business relationship tangled up in the Garcia lawsuit's defendant list is itself worth untangling, because it illustrates something particular about how this industry has grown. In August 2024, Google struck a licensing arrangement with Character.AI valued at roughly $2.7 billion, under which the company gained a non-exclusive license to Character.AI's underlying technology while Character.AI's cofounders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, along with roughly thirty additional staff, returned to Google, this time within its DeepMind AI research division — both men had originally left Google years earlier, reportedly out of frustration that the company was moving too cautiously on the kind of conversational AI products they wanted to build. The structure of that deal — a licensing payment plus a return of key personnel, rather than a conventional acquisition of the company itself — has drawn scrutiny from observers who note it achieves much of what an acquisition would achieve, folding a competitor's talent and technology back into a larger company, without triggering the antitrust review a formal acquisition might invite. That characterization question moved from press speculation to formal government inquiry: the Department of Justice opened an antitrust investigation into the arrangement, examining whether its specific structure — a licensing payment paired with a talent return, rather than a conventional stock or asset purchase — was designed specifically to let Google absorb a competitor's core technology and research team while sidestepping the merger-review process a straightforward acquisition would have triggered. Google has maintained publicly that Character.AI remains an independent company with no Google ownership stake, while regulators scrutinize whether the deal's practical effect is functionally indistinguishable from an acquisition regardless of its legal form. The DOJ's inquiry does not stand alone: reporting has linked it to parallel scrutiny of similarly structured licensing-plus-talent arrangements elsewhere in the AI industry, including Microsoft's roughly $650 million agreement with Inflection AI and Amazon's absorption of AI talent from Adept, suggesting regulators have identified this general deal structure — call it acquisition by other means — as a pattern worth investigating across the industry rather than a one-off curiosity specific to Character.AI. Whatever the deal's ultimate regulatory characterization, it establishes that the largest technology companies in the world consider AI companionship products, even ones facing the kind of litigation described above, valuable enough to pay billions of dollars to bring in-house.

Character.AI is not the only, or the first, large-scale entrant in this category, and looking at its predecessors clarifies how long this business model has actually been developing, well before large language models made today's more fluent versions possible. Replika, an app developed by the company Luka, Inc. and led by Eugenia Kuyda, built an early and durable user base around AI companions users could customize and converse with, marketed explicitly around emotional support and, for some users, romantic or intimate roleplay — a design choice that drew the attention of Italy's data-protection regulator, the Garante, which in February 2023 ordered Luka to immediately stop processing Italian users' personal data. The Garante's order, which this book treats as its primary and most reliable source in this section because it comes directly from a regulatory body rather than from press aggregation, found that Replika lacked a legal basis for processing user data under several provisions of the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation and, critically, lacked adequate age verification to prevent minors from accessing an app the regulator judged could expose them to responses inappropriate for their age. Subsequent reporting has described a further fine and a continuing regulatory investigation into how the company trains its underlying models, though this book was not able to independently verify the exact fine amount or filing date to the same standard of confidence as the original 2023 order, and flags that uncertainty rather than presenting a specific figure as settled.

Replika's American prominence somewhat obscures the fact that the largest and earliest mass-market AI companion product was Chinese, not American, and predates the current generation of large language models by half a decade. Microsoft launched Xiaoice in China in 2014, initially as a chatbot integrated into platforms like WeChat and Weibo, built to hold open-ended, personality-driven conversations rather than to answer factual queries the way Microsoft's other assistant products were designed to do. In July 2020, Microsoft spun Xiaoice out into an independent company, retaining an investment stake and a licensing relationship, with reporting at the time citing 660 million cumulative online users and deployment across 450 million third-party smart devices — figures that should be understood as a snapshot of the product's scale at the moment of its spinoff, not as a current user count, since this book was unable to locate reliable, recent reporting on Xiaoice's user base in the years since. What the 2020 figures do establish is that a companion-style conversational AI product had already reached hundreds of millions of users, in China, years before ChatGPT's 2022 public release introduced most Western audiences to the idea that a chatbot might feel worth talking to for its own sake rather than merely useful for looking something up. The current wave of American AI companion apps is not, in other words, a genuinely novel invention responding to a newly discovered market. It is, in significant part, a belated Western version of a product category a Chinese subsidiary of an American technology company had already proven out at enormous scale nearly a decade earlier.

Grindr, the dating and social-networking app serving gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men and examined in chapter seven for its role expanding courtship access to a historically underserved population, announced its own move into this chapter's territory in October 2024: an "AI wingman" feature, built using technology licensed from Amazon and Anthropic, designed to track a user's ongoing conversations across matches, recommend date locations, and, in its most ambitious proposed form, let two users' respective AI assistants communicate directly with each other to establish compatibility before the humans themselves ever exchange a message. Grindr's own stated rationale for the feature explicitly invoked this book's subject matter directly, framing the wingman partly as a tool for addressing loneliness and depression, conditions the company noted are particularly prevalent within the LGBTQ+ community it serves, alongside the more conventional matchmaking-efficiency pitch. The company built in privacy safeguards it described as a particular priority given that a meaningful share of its roughly 14 million users live in countries where being openly LGBTQ+ remains illegal or socially dangerous, a design consideration with no real equivalent in the more mainstream companion products examined earlier in this chapter. Grindr's wingman was still moving through a staged rollout as this book was being researched, expanding from a small initial test group toward a stated goal of full functionality by 2027; its ultimate reception and effectiveness remain, like most of the products examined in this chapter, genuinely unknown rather than something this book can responsibly forecast.

Character.AI's own reported user demographics complicate any assumption that this chapter's concerns apply narrowly to a small population of vulnerable minors. Independent traffic analytics tracking put the platform's user base, which crossed 20 million globally in early 2024, at roughly 51 to 60 percent within the 18-to-24 age bracket specifically — technically adult users, but barely so, spanning exactly the age range this book's chapter three noted showed the strongest mortality association with loneliness in Holt-Lunstad's research and the highest reported loneliness in Cigna's generational data. The company's own platform restrictions have tightened considerably since the period covered by the Garcia litigation: an under-18 model introduced in December 2024 added filters against violent and explicit content alongside a sixty-minute engagement notification, before the more sweeping November 2025 restriction, examined earlier in this chapter, barred open-ended conversations for minors entirely, replacing self-reported age with selfie-based age verification for any user seeking to continue open conversational access. That specific enforcement mechanism — verifying age through a photograph rather than a simple birthdate checkbox — represents a real technical escalation in age-verification rigor relative to the industry's earlier, easily circumvented self-attestation standard, though this book notes, without further independent verification, that photograph-based age estimation carries its own accuracy limitations and privacy tradeoffs that fall outside this chapter's scope to evaluate fully.

The Garcia litigation did not remain a private dispute between one family and one company; it helped catalyze a broader regulatory response that reached well beyond Character.AI specifically. On August 25, 2025, a bipartisan coalition of forty-four state attorneys general, led by officials from Tennessee, Illinois, North Carolina, and South Carolina, sent a formal joint letter to the major American companies building consumer-facing AI products, including Google, Meta, and OpenAI, raising concerns about chatbot safety for minors across the industry rather than at any single firm. Roughly two weeks later, on September 11, 2025, the Federal Trade Commission formally opened a Section 6(b) inquiry — a specific statutory investigative tool the agency uses to compel information disclosure without needing to allege a violation of law first — into the effects of AI companion chatbots on children and teenagers, issuing formal orders to seven companies: Alphabet, Character.AI, Meta and its Instagram subsidiary, OpenAI, Snap, and Elon Musk's xAI, giving each company forty-five days to produce detailed records on how it measures, tests, and monitors potential harm to young users, what steps it has taken to limit product use by or negative effects on minors, and how it discloses those risks to users and parents. The inquiry's scope, spanning seven of the largest and most differently positioned companies in consumer AI rather than targeting Character.AI alone, signals that American regulators had concluded, by late 2025, that the risks raised in the Garcia case were not specific to one company's product design choices but reflected a structural feature of companion-style AI products more broadly — a regulatory judgment this book's own evidence, spanning products as different as Character.AI, Replika, and Xiaoice, tends to support.

Character.AI's own product changes since the Garcia litigation began offer a useful, concrete measure of how seriously the company came to take the concerns the lawsuit raised, independent of whatever the confidential settlement itself contains. On October 29, 2025, following continued pressure from the litigation and a critical investigative report from the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, the company announced it would bar users under eighteen from open-ended chat conversations with its AI characters entirely, a restriction that took effect on November 25, 2025. In place of unrestricted conversation, the company began developing an alternative under-18 experience focused on more constrained activities — generating videos, stories, and short interactive content with AI characters rather than sustained, open-ended dialogue of the kind at the center of the Setzer case. Alongside the age restriction, the company added a crisis-intervention feature that detects language associated with self-harm and redirects the conversation toward the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, introduced notifications warning users after extended continuous sessions, and launched a parental-monitoring feature it called Parental Insights. That feature's limits are worth noting alongside its existence: reporting on the rollout found it did not allow a parent to block specific characters, review the actual content of a teenager's conversations, or filter by topic, giving parents visibility into usage patterns rather than the conversations themselves. Taken together, these changes represent a real, substantively different product than the one Setzer used in 2023 and early 2024 — evidence that litigation, media scrutiny, and regulatory pressure can shift a company's design choices meaningfully even before a court reaches a final verdict on the underlying legal claims — while also showing, in the specific gap between monitoring usage and monitoring content, how much a company can still leave unaddressed even while responding visibly to the most serious criticism it has faced.

This chapter has moved through a lawsuit, a regulatory order, a decade-old Chinese product, a licensing deal under federal antitrust scrutiny, and a dating app's own AI experiment, and it is worth stating plainly what connects all five before this chapter closes. Each represents the same underlying wager, made by a different company in a different country for a different specific product: that a sufficiently responsive, sufficiently available, sufficiently personalized software system can meaningfully substitute for the specific good this book's opening chapters described eroding — reliable, recurring, human attention. Some of the evidence assembled in this chapter suggests that wager pays off for at least some users, some of the time, in ways worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as a lesser, fake version of real connection. Some of the evidence — the Garcia litigation chief among it — suggests the same wager can go catastrophically wrong for the most vulnerable users encountering it under the least amount of adult oversight. This book does not have a verdict that resolves that tension for every product, every user, and every context examined in this chapter, and it would be dishonest to manufacture one for the sake of a cleaner ending. What it has instead is the standard this book has applied consistently since chapter ten: whether a given product increases the number of actual humans positioned to notice when its user is struggling, or merely substitutes a responsive simulation for that noticing while leaving the underlying practical gap exactly as wide as this book's opening chapters described it.

The question this chapter has been circling, and that the rest of this book will keep returning to without fully resolving, is what these products actually do to the loneliness this book has spent its earlier chapters documenting. It is possible, and the companies' own marketing leans hard on this possibility, that an AI companion genuinely reduces the acute pain of isolation for at least some meaningful share of its users — providing a low-stakes space to practice social interaction, offering support to people who have no other outlet for it, filling hours that would otherwise be spent in genuinely painful solitude. It is also possible, and the Garcia litigation's core allegation raises this possibility at its most serious, that for some users, particularly vulnerable minors, a sufficiently persuasive and always-available synthetic companion displaces rather than supplements the harder, slower, more frustrating work of building real human relationships, in a way that leaves the underlying isolation not just unaddressed but actively deepened by a product engineered, deliberately or not, to maximize engagement. Rigorous, long-term, independent research comparing these outcomes across large user populations is still genuinely limited, and this book is not going to claim otherwise or pretend that either possibility has been definitively ruled out by the evidence available as of this writing. What is not limited is the market's own judgment about where the opportunity lies: a licensing deal worth billions of dollars, a decade-old Chinese product with hundreds of millions of users, a European regulator issuing formal orders over age verification, and an American court allowing wrongful-death claims to proceed past a First Amendment defense all point toward the same conclusion from different institutional angles — this is not a niche experiment. It is one of the largest, most heavily capitalized responses this book will examine to the underlying gap in human connection its earlier chapters have spent so much space documenting, and, as the next chapter shows through the specific and extreme case of Japan's socially withdrawn young men, it is not the only technology being built to substitute, imperfectly and at scale, for the human contact a thinning social infrastructure no longer reliably supplies.
