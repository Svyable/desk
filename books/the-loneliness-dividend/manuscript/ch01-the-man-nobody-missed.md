# The Man Nobody Missed

The cleaning crew that arrives at a Japanese apartment weeks after a solitary death rarely knows the name of the person who lived there before the landlord tells them, and sometimes not even then. What they know is the smell, which reaches the stairwell before the door is open, and the outline on the tatami or the carpet where a body lay long enough for fluids to stain the flooring beneath it — a shape the cleaning trade calls, with a bluntness that took outsiders decades to get used to, a "body map." They know which insects arrive first and which arrive later, in a rough sequence experienced workers can read the way a geologist reads sediment. They know that the refrigerator is usually still running, stocked with food for a person who expected to eat it, and that the mail has usually kept arriving, a small paper record of a life that the outside world assumed was still going on. Sociologists who have interviewed workers in this trade, in a 2021 study published in the journal *Social Science History*, found that the workers themselves have built an entire vocabulary and set of professional norms for turning what could be an unbearable job into a manageable one — treating the site with the procedural calm of a hazmat crew rather than the emotional register of a funeral, because the emotional register of a funeral would make the work impossible to repeat forty times a month.

Forty times a month is not an exaggeration for the busiest firms. Japan calls this *kodokushi*, "lonely death," and by the country's own police statistics the practice of dying alone and being found late has gone from a footnote to a mainstream category of mortality. The National Police Agency, which began systematically tracking the phenomenon only in 2024, recorded roughly 76,000 to 77,000 people found dead alone in their homes in 2025, according to reporting on the agency's own figures in *The Japan Times* and other outlets in early 2026. People sixty-five and older accounted for about 76.6 percent of those cases — nearly 59,000 individuals — a share that had actually risen slightly from the year before. Of the total, 28.9 percent were not discovered for eight days or more after death; at least 7,148 people had been dead for over a month; 208 had been dead for over a year. A year is long enough for an apartment building to hold an annual meeting, replace a superintendent, and repaint a hallway without anyone walking past a door behind which someone had already been gone for most of that time.

None of this is uniquely Japanese, though Japan is unusually good at counting it. Comparable figures are harder to find in the United States, the United Kingdom, or most of the European Union, partly because agencies elsewhere track cause of death rather than the circumstances of discovery, and partly because "died alone, discovered late" is not a category most mortality-reporting systems were built to ask about. Japan started asking because Japan noticed the pattern first — driven by a combination of the world's oldest population, one of the highest single-person-household rates among wealthy nations, and a culture that had, within living memory, taken multigenerational cohabitation and dense neighborly surveillance for granted. When those arrangements eroded, the country ended up with an unusually clear, unusually early view of what happens at the far end of a process every rich country is now living through to some degree: a growing number of people whose absence, when it finally happens, is not noticed by anyone in particular, because no one in particular was checking.

South Korea offers a second, independently useful data point, because its government began tracking the phenomenon under its own name at almost exactly the moment Japan's National Police Agency did. Korean law defines *godoksa* — its own term for solitary death — as a death occurring in isolation from family and friends, with the body undiscovered for at least seventy-two hours, a lower discovery-time threshold than the informal Japanese usage of kodokushi, which makes direct numerical comparison between the two countries imprecise even though the underlying phenomenon and its trajectory look similar. South Korea's Ministry of Health and Welfare recorded 3,924 such deaths in 2024, up 7.2 percent from 3,661 the year before, continuing a rise that had been climbing every year since the government began compiling the data in 2021; the rate had grown from roughly 7.2 to 7.7 cases per 100,000 people between 2023 and 2024 alone. The demographic pattern diverges from Japan's in a way worth noting rather than smoothing over: where Japan's solitary deaths skew heavily toward the elderly, South Korea's 2024 figures show a striking concentration among men in their fifties and sixties, who accounted for nearly 63 percent of cases, and men outnumbered women by more than five to one — 3,205 men against 605 women — a gap large enough that South Korean researchers and journalists have specifically framed godoksa as a crisis disproportionately affecting middle-aged men, often following divorce, job loss, or estrangement from adult children, rather than a general feature of old age. One widely cited Korean study found that 78.8 percent of people living in single-person households met criteria putting them at some level of risk for godoksa, a figure this book treats as a screening-instrument estimate of vulnerability rather than a prediction that four in five solo Korean households are headed toward this specific outcome. Seoul's city government responded in 2024 by committing roughly 451 billion won, or about $327 million, over five years to a program explicitly aimed at what officials called building "a city where no one is lonely," including a twenty-four-hour hotline staffed by dedicated loneliness counselors — a striking dollar figure for a single metropolitan government to commit to a problem this book's opening pages have been describing, and further evidence, alongside the National Police Agency's Japanese figures, that the pattern this chapter opened with is not confined to one country's peculiar demographics.

This book is not a book about Japan, and it is not, centrally, a book about death. It is a book about what happens in the decades before that point — the long, mostly invisible attrition of the relationships, institutions, and physical spaces that used to make a missing person conspicuous within days rather than months. It is also a book about the fact that this attrition has become large enough, and stable enough, to be worth money. Somewhere between the sociable, densely networked mid-twentieth-century societies that Robert Putnam would go on to eulogize in *Bowling Alone* and the socially thinner ones many of us now live in, a gap opened up — a gap between the amount of connection a person needs and the amount their surrounding institutions, workplaces, cities, and default household arrangements actually supply. That gap does not stay empty. It gets filled, imperfectly, by things people buy: apartment-death insurance policies now being developed for landlords in Japan; a professional cleaning industry, *tokushu seisou*, that did not meaningfully exist thirty years ago and now operates as a standard line item in Japanese property management; run clubs and Bumble BFF and companion chatbots and coworking memberships in the United States; government ministries in London and Tokyo whose entire mandate is to notice what families, congregations, and unions used to notice on their own. The gap is the subject of this book. The businesses and governments now filling it are the argument.

Start with the Japanese property market, because it makes the mechanism unusually legible. When a tenant dies alone and is not discovered for an extended period, the unit they lived in becomes what Japanese real estate law and custom classify as a *jiko bukken* — a "stigmatized property." Landlords are legally obligated to disclose the death to future tenants for a period of time, and the disclosure predictably depresses what the unit can rent for, by anywhere from roughly 20 to 50 percent depending on the severity of the case and the local market, according to industry reporting on the *jiko bukken* system. A landlord facing that kind of loss has an obvious incentive to buy insurance against it, and insurers have begun offering exactly that product. Tokio Marine & Nichido Fire Insurance, one of Japan's largest general insurers, began selling a policy in July 2022 designed specifically for apartment buildings housing elderly residents living alone, paying out up to one million yen to cover cleanup, disinfection, funeral costs, and the legal fees of tracking down a deceased tenant's next of kin, with an annual premium of roughly fifty thousand yen for a hundred-unit apartment complex — pricing a discrete, actuarially modeled risk of solitary death directly into the ordinary cost of running a residential building. The policy covers the lost rent, the discounted resale value, and the cost of the specialized cleaning crew, priced against the statistical likelihood that any given elderly, isolated tenant will die unnoticed long enough to trigger a claim. It is a strange thing to sit with: a financial instrument whose underlying risk is the erosion of the informal social checking — a daughter's weekly phone call, a neighbor noticing the newspapers piling up, a building superintendent who still knows tenants by name — that used to make this kind of loss rare enough not to need pricing. The insurance did not create the isolation. But the insurance is a precise, numeric admission that the isolation is now common enough, and stable enough as a pattern, to underwrite.

The special-cleaning industry tells a version of the same story from a different angle. In interviews collected for the 2021 *Social Science History* study on how cleaning workers narrate their trade, workers described the profession's evolution from an ad hoc, stigmatized task that families and building managers handled quietly and with considerable shame, into an organized service industry with training programs, professional associations, and a set of shared occupational norms for managing the psychological weight of the work — what the researchers describe as workers reframing a "dreadful" event into a "manageable incident" through repetition and professional distance. An industry does not develop training programs and professional associations for a problem that shows up once a decade. It develops them for a problem that shows up on a predictable schedule, at a predictable scale, that someone is willing to pay to have handled. The scale, again by the National Police Agency's own count, is now tens of thousands of cases a year in Japan alone, and the reporting suggests it has been climbing rather than plateauing: the agency's first-half 2025 count of roughly 40,900 cases in the first six months already ran nearly 3,700 ahead of the same period in 2024.

It would be a mistake to read the Japanese numbers as evidence of a uniquely Japanese failure, and this book is going to spend real effort resisting that reading, because the more useful and more uncomfortable claim is the opposite one: Japan is not an outlier so much as an early instrument reading. The country combines, more intensely than most, three trends that are visible in every wealthy society this book examines — a rapidly aging population, a rising share of people living in single-person households, and a decline in the density of everyday social contact that used to make a missing person's absence obvious quickly. The United States and the United Kingdom are behind Japan on the demographic curve but moving in the same direction on all three variables, and later chapters will walk through the American and British data in the same rigor this chapter has just applied to Japan's — the Census Bureau's own tracking of single-person households, the U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 accounting of the public-health cost of loneliness, the UK's decision in 2018 to create, for the first time anywhere, a cabinet-level Minister for Loneliness. None of those responses would exist if the underlying condition were rare, or transient, or confined to one country's peculiar demographics.

Here is the shape of the argument this book is going to make, stated plainly before the evidence for it, because plain statement up front is more honest than letting readers discover the thesis by inference. For most of the twentieth century, in most wealthy countries, ordinary social connection was produced as an unplanned byproduct of other institutions people participated in for other reasons: a job that put you in a physical workplace with the same colleagues for years, a church or synagogue or mosque that gathered a stable community weekly whether or not anyone thought of it as "socializing," a union hall, a bowling league, a PTA meeting, a neighborhood dense enough that the corner store owner recognized your face, an extended family that lived close enough to visit without booking a flight. None of these institutions existed primarily to combat loneliness. Loneliness prevention was a side effect, largely unnoticed because it was so reliably supplied that no one had to think about supplying it deliberately.

Over the past several decades, a long list of forces — some technological, some economic, some the result of choices made for entirely separate reasons — thinned out that supply. Remote and gig work loosened the tie between employment and a stable physical community of coworkers. Rising urban housing costs and car-dependent suburban design made the old, walkable "third places" that sociologist Ray Oldenburg described in *The Great Good Place* — the cafés, bars, barbershops, and diners where people gathered informally and often — harder to build and easier to lose. Religious membership in the United States fell, according to Gallup's long-running tracking poll, below 50 percent for the first time on record around 2020. Marriage rates fell across the wealthy world as people married later or not at all, while the share of adults living in single-person households climbed steadily, a trend sociologist Eric Klinenberg documented at length in *Going Solo*. None of these shifts was engineered with the goal of producing more solitary lives. Each one was a reasonable response, by individuals and institutions, to its own separate set of incentives — cheaper commercial real estate, more flexible work arrangements, greater individual autonomy, later and more selective marriage. But their combined, largely unplanned effect was to dismantle the unpaid social infrastructure that used to make loneliness rare and to leave, in its place, an unmet need on a scale large enough for markets and governments to notice.

That is the "built" in this book's title, and it is doing double duty on purpose. Modern life was built, gradually and mostly without anyone deciding to do it, in a way that produces more solitary hours, more thinly populated calendars, and more people whose disappearance would take longer to notice than it once did — not through malice or a single villain, but through the compounding, unglamorous logic of a thousand separate decisions about zoning, commuting, work schedules, household formation, and technology, each locally sensible and collectively corrosive. And now, because the resulting gap in ordinary human contact is real, measurable, and large, a second wave of building has begun on top of it: apps, devices, subscriptions, ministries, insurance products, and entire new industries engineered specifically to sell some version of connection, or its closest available substitute, back to the people the first wave left without it. Some of what is being built in that second wave is genuinely good — this book will spend real time later on evidence-based interventions, from the UK's National Health Service prescribing social activities the way it prescribes medication, to community design that appears to work when it is tried seriously. Some of what is being built is a synthetic, machine-generated stand-in for connection whose long-term effects on the people using it are still an open, actively contested empirical question, and this book will not pretend that question is settled in either direction. All of it, good and uncertain alike, shares one structural fact: it exists because the first, older layer of unpaid social infrastructure eroded enough to leave room for it.

It is worth being honest, this early, about what this book is not claiming. It is not claiming that solitude itself is a disease, or that every person who lives alone, works from home, or spends an evening with a chatbot instead of a friend is a casualty of civilizational decline. Chapter sixteen takes the counter-case seriously and at length, because the counter-case deserves to be taken seriously: solitude and loneliness are not the same thing, some solitude is chosen and restorative rather than imposed and painful, some of the data behind the "loneliness epidemic" framing has real methodological limits that researchers who study this professionally have pointed out, and at least one widely circulated statistic in this space — the claim that loneliness carries a mortality risk "equivalent to smoking fifteen cigarettes a day" — turns out, on close inspection of its origin, to be a looser paraphrase of the underlying research than the phrase as usually repeated implies. This book tries to use the real number, with its real confidence intervals and its real caveats, rather than the version of the number that travels best on a conference slide. Precision costs some rhetorical punch. It is worth the trade, because a reader who catches this book overstating one number will reasonably wonder what else has been oversold, and everything that follows depends on being trusted with the harder, more qualified version of the evidence.

What is not seriously contestable, across the sources this book relies on, is the underlying direction of the trend and its scale. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory, discussed in full in chapter three, documented loneliness and social isolation as a widespread condition among American adults with measurable associations to cardiovascular disease, dementia, and early death, drawing on decades of peer-reviewed research including Julianne Holt-Lunstad's meta-analyses of the relationship between social connection and mortality risk. The World Health Organization convened a Commission on Social Connection in 2023, explicitly modeled on the way global health bodies treat other major, cross-national risk factors, because the evidence had accumulated to the point that the WHO judged loneliness worth treating as a global public-health priority rather than a private unhappiness. The United Kingdom appointed the world's first Minister for Loneliness in January 2018, following the Jo Cox Commission's 2017 report, which had itself been prompted by a murdered member of Parliament's unfinished work on the subject. Japan followed with its own Minister of Loneliness and Isolation in 2021 and later passed dedicated legislation. Governments do not create cabinet positions and commission international reports over statistical noise. They create them in response to something they have concluded is real, large, and getting worse quickly enough to require an institutional response — the same signal, read from a different angle, that the *jiko bukken* insurance market and Japan's booming special-cleaning trade are reading from theirs.

The chapters that follow move outward from that signal in roughly the order this introduction has sketched. The next two chapters lay the historical and demographic foundation — Putnam's original argument about American social capital and what has happened to it since, followed by the specific advisory, meta-analyses, and Census data behind the claim that this is a measurable, growing phenomenon rather than a cultural mood. From there the book moves through the erosion of specific institutions — the third place, the workplace, the dating market, religious membership, and the built environment itself — before turning to the businesses and technologies now responding to that erosion, from AI companion apps and therapeutic robots to run clubs and coworking spaces, and the genuine, sometimes disturbing questions raised by a market that profits from unmet human need. It closes with the skeptics' case in full, an accounting of what interventions the evidence actually supports, and a synthesis of where this leaves a reader trying to make sense of a phenomenon that is simultaneously a public-health crisis, a demographic inevitability, a business opportunity, and, for tens of thousands of people every year, from Osaka to Ohio, an ending nobody was watching for closely enough to prevent.

The cleaning crews in Japan have a term for the period of time between a death and its discovery, drawn from the length of that interval rather than any medical fact about the death itself: they measure a case not by cause but by how long it took. A short case might mean two or three days. A long case, the kind that requires a full-strength odor-neutralizing protocol and specialized equipment before anyone can safely enter the room, might mean a season. The number that should trouble a reader most, out of everything the National Police Agency's 2025 count contains, may not be the 77,000 total, striking as it is. It may be the 208 people who were gone for over a year before anyone came looking — a length of time that says less about how those individuals died than about how completely the ordinary, unpaid machinery of noticing another person's presence had already stopped running around them, long before their deaths made the absence official.
