# The Herbivores and the Shut-Ins

Japan's Cabinet Office has, since the early 2000s, run periodic national surveys attempting to count a population that resists easy counting by its very nature: people who have withdrawn from social participation so completely that reaching them for a survey is itself part of the phenomenon being measured. The Japanese term for this population is *hikikomori*, and the most recent Cabinet Office estimate, drawn from a survey of 30,000 people fielded in November 2022 and released in 2023, put the number at roughly 1.46 million people between the ages of 15 and 64 — about 2 percent of that entire age range, split almost evenly between a younger cohort (15 to 39, at just over 2 percent) and an older one (40 to 64, at just over 2 percent as well), a detail that itself complicates the popular image of hikikomori as a strictly youth phenomenon. The single most common reason respondents gave for their withdrawal was not shyness, trauma, or any of the more dramatic explanations that dominate international coverage of the phenomenon; it was job loss, cited more often than any other factor, with the COVID-19 pandemic cited as the second most common reason — 18 percent among the younger cohort and 20 percent among the older one.

It is worth pausing on that finding because it cuts against a common misreading of hikikomori, one this book wants to avoid reproducing. Western media coverage has often treated Japan's socially withdrawn young men as an exotic cultural pathology, evidence of something uniquely broken in Japanese social expectations around work and masculinity. A 2024 paper in the academic literature, surveyed for this chapter, argued instead for treating hikikomori as a heterogeneous phenomenon — not a single diagnosis but a spectrum of related conditions with different causes, severities, and trajectories, some meeting criteria closer to clinical social anxiety or depression, others better understood as an extreme, prolonged version of ordinary labor-market discouragement, where a person who lost a job, or never successfully entered the workforce after school, gradually withdrew further as each unsuccessful attempt to reengage made the next attempt feel less worth making. Read this way, hikikomori looks less like an exotic cultural artifact and more like an unusually visible, unusually extreme endpoint of a dynamic this book has already traced in less dramatic form throughout wealthy countries: a labor market and social infrastructure that offers fewer reliable, low-barrier paths back into participation once someone has fallen out of it, whether through job loss, illness, family rupture, or simple accumulated disuse of social skills that, like any skill, atrophy without practice.

The specific mechanism by which hikikomori has, so far, mostly avoided producing a kodokushi-scale solitary-death crisis of its own is worth naming directly, because it exposes both the resilience and the fragility of Japan's current arrangement. Japanese researchers and journalists have coined a specific term, the "8050 problem," to describe what happens when a hikikomori individual reaches middle age while their parents, who have continued providing housing, food, and financial support throughout the withdrawal, reach old age alongside them — a household in which parents in their eighties are still supporting an adult child in their fifties who has not held employment or independent housing for years or decades. Nearly 80 percent of middle-aged hikikomori, by the same Cabinet Office data examined above, live with their elderly parents, meaning the family home functions, for this population, as an informal and unplanned welfare system, absorbing a social-support burden that in a different institutional context might otherwise fall to formal government services. Japan's government introduced a "Stratified Support System" in 2021 specifically aimed at this population, attempting to coordinate services across the housing, financial, and social-welfare needs the 8050 problem creates. But the arrangement's dependence on aging parents' continued survival creates an obvious and unresolved structural fragility: Japanese media has its own grim shorthand for what happens when that support finally ends, describing cases of "parent and child collapsing together," *oyako-tomodaore*, in households that had remained functionally invisible to any outside support system for years, right up until a parent's death or incapacity suddenly left a middle-aged adult child without either income or the informal caretaking network their family home had quietly provided. The 8050 problem is, in miniature, a preview of exactly the mechanism this book's opening chapter described in the kodokushi statistics: a population currently protected from acute crisis by an informal, unpaid support structure that is itself aging out of its capacity to provide that protection, with no clearly established formal replacement yet in place to catch what happens once it does.

A related but distinct phenomenon, sometimes conflated with hikikomori in casual Western coverage but analytically separate, is the loose cultural category Japanese media has labeled *soshoku-danshi*, or "herbivore men" — a term describing young Japanese men reportedly less interested in traditional markers of masculine ambition, including aggressive pursuit of romantic and sexual relationships, than previous generations. Unlike the Cabinet Office's hikikomori surveys, the herbivore-men discourse rests on softer, more contested survey and media evidence, and this book treats it with correspondingly more caution — as a real and widely discussed cultural narrative in Japan rather than as a precisely measured demographic trend on the same evidentiary footing as the hikikomori figures above. What connects the two phenomena, loosely rather than through any single confirmed causal mechanism, is a broader pattern Japan's declining marriage and birth rates have made increasingly visible: a growing share of Japanese adults, across several overlapping categories, disengaging from the traditional social and romantic participation that used to be close to a default expectation, for reasons that likely include economic precarity, changing gender norms, and the same thinning of associational infrastructure this book has documented in the American and British context throughout its earlier chapters, but operating on a Japanese timeline that in several respects runs a decade or more ahead of the equivalent Western trends.

South Korea, tracking its own version of hikikomori under a national survey rather than importing the Japanese term directly, has taken a more directly financial approach to the same underlying problem. A 2023 Ministry of Health and Welfare survey of more than 20,000 Koreans aged 19 to 39 estimated roughly 540,000 young adults, about 5 percent of the country's 11 million people in that age range, experiencing a level of social withdrawal severe enough to meet the government's own criteria. Rather than relying solely on outreach centers of the kind Japan has built since 2009, South Korea's government passed an amendment making isolated youth eligible for a direct monthly stipend of up to 650,000 won, or roughly $475, specifically to support the practical costs of re-entering society — job searching, transportation, basic living expenses during a period when a withdrawn young adult has typically had no independent income for months or years. Seoul's city government opened the country's first dedicated institution for this specific population, the Seoul Youth Gijigae Center, in 2024, offering a coordinated pathway from initial identification and diagnosis through program support, social reintegration, and follow-up care, and by January 2025 had renamed and expanded the effort into a broader Seoul Isolation Prevention Center, alongside two new dedicated helplines the city launched that year. South Korea's cash-stipend approach represents a genuinely different intervention philosophy from Japan's more relationship-and-outreach-centered support-center model examined below: a direct financial transfer addresses the practical, material precarity that Japanese researchers have already identified as the single most commonly cited reason for withdrawal in the first place, betting that removing the immediate financial barrier to reentry may do more, for at least some share of this population, than outreach and counseling alone.

Japan's response to hikikomori and its adjacent phenomena includes government outreach programs, dedicated support centers, and, increasingly, a technology sector building products specifically designed to meet extremely isolated people where they already are — and the outreach infrastructure predates the robot-companion industry by well over a decade. Since 2009, Japan's national government has provided financial support to a network of prefectural and municipal hikikomori support centers, which had grown to nearly seventy locations across the country by the time the Cabinet Office conducted the 2022 survey discussed above. These centers function as a first point of contact for a population that, by definition, rarely seeks help on its own initiative: staff field calls from parents rather than from the withdrawn individuals themselves, host support groups for family members trying to understand and respond to a relative's withdrawal, and coordinate outreach efforts including home visits for people who have not left their residence in months or years. In 2018, Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare formalized this patchwork into a more coordinated regional support structure, expanding the range of services available to include specific housing support aimed at middle-aged and older hikikomori, a population the 2022 Cabinet Office data showed constitutes a larger share of the total than the popular image of hikikomori as a strictly youth phenomenon suggests. The support-center model represents a genuinely different intervention strategy from the robot-companion technologies examined below: rather than providing a substitute for human contact that a withdrawn person can access without ever leaving their room, it is built around the harder, slower work of reestablishing an actual human outreach relationship, one home visit or one parental phone call at a time, an approach that scales far less easily than a consumer app but that, following the logic chapter ten's Medicare spending data established, targets the practical, human-noticing variable this book has argued matters most, rather than only the subjective experience of the isolation itself.

Japan's response to hikikomori and its adjacent phenomena also includes, increasingly, a technology sector building products specifically designed to meet extremely isolated people where they already are — inside their own homes, often unwilling or unable to leave, sometimes for years at a stretch. Therapeutic and companion robots represent Japan's most distinctive contribution to this book's broader subject, developed years before the current wave of American AI-companion apps, and grounded in a meaningfully more rigorous evidentiary base than most of what chapter eight examined.

PARO, a robotic harp seal roughly the size of a large housecat, was developed at Japan's National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology under researcher Takanori Shibata, with development beginning in the early-to-mid 1990s and commercial deployment following in the following decade. PARO was designed specifically for use with elderly people, particularly those living with dementia, in residential care settings, responding to touch, sound, and light with seal-like movements and vocalizations intended to evoke the calming effect of animal-assisted therapy without the practical and hygienic complications of using a live animal in a care facility. Unlike the more commercially driven companion products examined in the previous chapter, PARO has accumulated a genuinely substantial peer-reviewed evidence base: a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis published in the *International Journal of Nursing Studies*, along with a 2024 randomized controlled trial published in the *Journal of the American Medical Directors Association*, both reported reductions in behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, improved mood and sociability among residents using PARO, and, in some studies, reduced use of antipsychotic and pain medication — while both sets of reviewers also noted real methodological weaknesses across the underlying literature, including inconsistent randomization and blinding procedures that make the size of the true effect harder to pin down with confidence than a simple summary of the positive headline findings would suggest.

American military veterans, among the domestic populations PARO's own U.S. deployment specifically targets through Department of Veterans Affairs facilities, carry their own well-documented and distinct isolation burden worth naming before this chapter turns to PARO's international reach. Survey research has found 56.9 percent of U.S. veterans reporting loneliness at least sometimes, with 19.7 percent reporting loneliness often — figures running well above general-population baselines cited elsewhere in this book. Researchers studying the specific mechanism behind veteran loneliness point to the abruptness of the military-to-civilian transition itself: leaving military service severs an existing, often intensely bonded social network essentially overnight, while the civilian community a veteran reenters frequently lacks the shared frame of reference, workplace culture, and lived experience that made the military network cohesive in the first place, a mismatch compounded by practical transition difficulties including unsuitable civilian job placement and a lack of transferable civilian work experience. One experimental study using a social-exclusion simulation found veterans reported significantly more distress when excluded by civilian participants specifically, compared with equivalent exclusion by fellow veterans, suggesting the felt loneliness of military transition may relate less to social contact in the abstract than to a specific, felt loss of belonging to the particular community whose culture and experience a veteran actually shares. Programs like the Equus Effect, developed in 2008 as an alternative therapeutic approach using structured interaction with horses to help veterans process the transition, represent one small-scale attempt to address this specific population's needs, though this book found no evidence the intervention had been evaluated with anything approaching the rigor applied to PARO's own clinical trials, leaving veteran-specific loneliness as a population this book's own survey of interventions suggests remains comparatively underserved relative to the scale of the problem researchers have documented.

PARO's international deployment, beyond its home market, offers a useful window into how a therapeutic technology built around isolation moves from one country's care system into another's, shaped by very different funding structures. Denmark, whose national and municipal governments fund elder care far more comprehensively than most systems this book has examined, began publicly funding PARO units for use in nursing homes starting in 2008, and by various counts roughly 110 units were in active use across Danish care facilities, integrated into standard dementia-care protocols in a way that reflects the country's broader willingness to treat welfare-technology purchases as a routine public-health expenditure rather than a discretionary add-on. Secondary reporting, treated here with the same moderate confidence this book has applied to figures it could not independently verify against a primary source, suggests PARO's total international reach had grown to somewhere in the range of seven thousand units across more than thirty countries by the early 2020s, alongside continued use in United States Department of Veterans Affairs facilities — a far smaller absolute footprint than any of the consumer-facing AI companion apps examined in the previous chapter, but one grounded, as this chapter has already shown, in a considerably more rigorous evidentiary base of peer-reviewed clinical research than most of those consumer products can currently claim for themselves.

Living animals, rather than robotic or software substitutes for them, represent the largest and best-documented companion-acquisition wave examined anywhere in this book, and it is worth placing alongside PARO and Lovot precisely because it did not require any new technology to occur. An estimated 11.38 million American households acquired a new pet during the COVID-19 pandemic, with Google search interest in pet adoption rising by as much as 250 percent in 2020 compared with the year before, peaking specifically during the pandemic's early lockdown months. Survey research into the motivations behind that wave found more than half of new pet owners citing companionship directly as a reason for the acquisition, and more than a third specifically citing a desire to avoid or alleviate depression, with the surge concentrated disproportionately among younger adults: 18 percent of Generation Z respondents and 15 percent of millennials reported acquiring a new pet as a direct result of the pandemic, compared with just 10 percent of Generation X and 2 percent of Baby Boomers — a generational skew that lines up precisely with the loneliness data this book has traced across Cigna's index, Gallup's workplace research, and the Holt-Lunstad mortality findings, all of which identified younger adults as bearing a larger share of this book's subject than the popular image of loneliness as primarily an old-age problem would predict. Pets differ from every other companion technology examined in this chapter in one structurally important respect: unlike PARO, Lovot, or any AI chatbot, a living animal cannot be reprogrammed, updated, or discontinued by a corporate parent, and the relationship it offers, however asymmetric compared to human friendship, involves a genuinely independent living creature with its own needs rather than a product engineered specifically to be pleasing. Whether that distinction translates into a meaningfully different psychological or health outcome compared with the technological companions examined throughout this chapter is a genuinely open research question this book does not have the evidence to resolve, though the sheer scale of the pandemic-era pet boom — more households, by a wide margin, than any single AI companion app or robot-companion program examined in this chapter has reached — suggests it deserves at least as much research attention as the more heavily covered technological alternatives.

A newer entrant, marketed less as a clinical therapeutic device and more as a consumer companion product, is Lovot, developed by the Japanese robotics company GrooveX and launched around 2018 to 2019. Lovot is deliberately designed without a specific practical function — it cannot clean a house, answer questions, or perform tasks — built instead purely around eliciting an emotional, caretaking response from its owner through simulated warmth, movement, and expressive behavior, a design philosophy the company has described as building a robot whose entire purpose is to be loved rather than to be useful. Secondary reporting on GrooveX's financials, though not independently verified against the company's own primary filings to the same standard as this book's other financial claims, describes a business that had sold fewer than 20,000 units globally as of the mid-2020s while posting a net loss of roughly ¥2.7 billion — about $17 million — in a recent fiscal year, alongside cumulative losses in the range of ¥8.5 billion, or roughly $54 million, even as the company expanded into the Chinese market with a flagship retail presence in Shanghai. Lovot's numbers illustrate something this book has already observed in the American context with WeWork and Peloton: a product built around a genuine, well-documented emotional need can still struggle badly as a business, because manufacturing and marketing a substitute for connection turns out to be a harder, more expensive, and more uncertain undertaking than the underlying demand alone would predict.

The most ambitious American deployment of this category, notably, has come not from a technology startup pursuing consumer sales but from a state government pursuing a public-health intervention. New York State's Office for the Aging began a partnership with Intuition Robotics, maker of the ElliQ companion device, in May 2022, ultimately acquiring roughly 900 units for distribution to isolated older residents across the state as part of a subsidized pilot program. ElliQ, unlike PARO's zoomorphic seal form or Lovot's deliberately non-functional cuteness, presents as a small, expressive tabletop device that initiates conversation, suggests activities, reminds users to take medication, and checks in proactively rather than waiting passively to be addressed. In an August 2023 press release, the state agency reported what it described as a 95 percent reduction in loneliness among program participants, alongside data showing users averaging roughly thirty interactions a day, six days a week, with more than three-quarters of those interactions tied to goals the program had defined around social, physical, and mental wellbeing. That figure deserves the same treatment this book has applied to every other striking statistic in its earlier chapters: it is a state agency's own self-reported measure of its own pilot program's success, not an independently conducted, peer-reviewed clinical trial of the kind that produced the PARO findings discussed above, and readers should weight it accordingly — as promising, program-level evidence worth taking seriously, not as an independently validated clinical result on the same evidentiary footing as PARO's more rigorously studied outcomes.

China, the country whose own Xiaoice chatbot was examined in chapter eight as one of the earliest mass-market companion products anywhere in the world, faces a structurally distinct but related version of the demographic pressure this chapter has traced through Japan and South Korea, shaped by the specific legacy of its one-child policy, in effect from 1979 until it was formally relaxed to a two-child and later three-child policy beginning in 2016. Demographers describe the resulting household arrangement using a shorthand known as the "4-2-1" structure: a single adult child, together with a spouse, potentially responsible for the care of four grandparents and two parents, an inversion of the extended, multi-child family support networks that had traditionally absorbed elder care in Chinese society. As that single generation of only children has itself aged into adulthood and, increasingly, into the role of caregiver, researchers have documented a related but distinct isolation pattern among elderly Chinese parents specifically: those who relocate, often at their adult child's request, to unfamiliar cities to help with grandchildren or simply to be near their only child, frequently experience what Chinese researchers studying this migrant-elderly population describe as a significant loneliness and mental-health toll, driven by the loss of the established local social network, friendships, and neighborhood familiarity these elderly migrants leave behind in their home regions, arriving in a new city with only a single family relationship to rely on where they previously had an entire community. China's version of this book's central pattern, in other words, runs through a different demographic mechanism than Japan's aging-and-shrinking-household story or South Korea's rapid honjok-driven household transformation, but arrives at a structurally similar destination: an elderly population increasingly dependent on a thinner, more concentrated web of family support than the one earlier generations could rely on, in a country whose earliest and largest AI companion product, Xiaoice, was already serving hundreds of millions of users years before this same demographic pressure had fully worked its way through the population it now affects most acutely.

What connects Japan's hikikomori population, its herbivore-men discourse, and its pioneering robot-companion industry to the rest of this book's argument is not that Japan is uniquely broken, a framing this book rejected explicitly in its opening chapter and rejects again here. It is that Japan, for demographic and historical reasons specific to its postwar economic trajectory and its unusually early and severe population aging, has been living through an intensified, accelerated version of the same underlying dynamic this book has traced across the wealthy world at large: eroding social infrastructure, a labor market with fewer soft landings for people who fall out of it, and, arriving in response, a wave of purpose-built technology attempting to substitute for what that infrastructure used to supply. The next chapter turns from the specific human experience of isolation — in Japan, in the American workplace, on the dating apps — to what that isolation actually costs, in dollars, to the healthcare systems now absorbing its consequences.
