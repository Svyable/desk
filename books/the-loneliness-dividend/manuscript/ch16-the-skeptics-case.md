# The Skeptics' Case

Daniel Cox directs the Survey Center on American Life at the American Enterprise Institute, a think tank generally associated with center-right policy positions, and his own research puts him in an unusual position relative to the argument this book has been building for fifteen chapters: he has looked carefully at much of the same underlying survey terrain this book relies on, and concluded that the popular framing of a generalized "loneliness epidemic" oversells what the data actually shows. In a report titled "A Loneliness Epidemic? How Marriage, Religion, and Mobility Explain the Generation Gap in Loneliness," drawing on his center's own Survey on Community and Society, Cox found that only about 8 percent of Millennials reported being "often" lonely — a figure considerably lower than the more dramatic percentages this book has cited from Cigna's index in chapter ten, which used a different instrument, a different threshold, and a different question design entirely. Cox's larger argument is not that younger generations report no elevated loneliness relative to older ones — his own data shows a real generational gap — but that the gap is better explained by specific, identifiable life-course variables that happen to correlate with generation rather than by any generalized, ambient decline in social connection across society as a whole: lower rates of marriage, lower rates of religious participation, and higher geographic mobility among younger adults, each of which independently predicts lower reported connection regardless of which generation a person happens to belong to. Cox has also argued, in work this book finds genuinely useful for sharpening its own claims, that the data more strongly supports an epidemic of *aloneness* — more hours spent physically alone — than an epidemic of *loneliness* as a subjective emotional state, a distinction with real consequences for what kind of intervention would actually help, since more hours alone and more felt loneliness are related but empirically separable phenomena that do not move in perfect lockstep.

The disagreement about scale runs even deeper once the question turns international, and this is where this book's own framing throughout — treating loneliness as a shared, cross-national trend running through the United States, Britain, and Japan alike — needs its most serious stress test. Buecker, Mund, Chwastek, Sostmann, and Luhmann's 2021 preregistered cross-temporal meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin*, examining loneliness specifically among emerging adults from 1976 to 2019, did find a real rise — on the order of half a standard deviation over that period, a meaningful and statistically significant shift by the standards psychological research generally applies to trend data of this kind. But the rise the researchers found was concentrated heavily in North American samples and considerably weaker, in some cases barely detectable, in the European and Asian samples included in the same analysis. That finding complicates any simple claim that this book's opening chapter's kodokushi statistics and this book's closing institutional chapters' UK and Japanese ministries all reflect the same underlying, universal phenomenon operating at a similar pace and intensity everywhere. It is entirely possible, on the Buecker et al. evidence, that the United States is experiencing something closer to a genuine, sharp generational rise in loneliness among young adults specifically, while Japan's severe and well-documented isolation crisis, examined in chapters one and nine, is being driven by substantially different mechanisms — extreme population aging, a labor market offering few soft landings for people who fall out of it, a historically distinctive employment culture under strain — that happen to produce superficially similar-looking outcomes (solitary deaths, socially withdrawn young men) through a genuinely different causal pathway than whatever is driving the American trend Cox and Buecker's data describe. This book has tried, throughout, to avoid collapsing these into a single undifferentiated global story, but it is worth stating the risk explicitly here: a reader could reasonably come away from this book's earlier chapters with a more unified impression of a single global phenomenon than the country-by-country evidence, read carefully, actually supports.

The most direct challenge to this book's American evidence base concerns the specific data source underlying much of the Surgeon General's 2023 advisory: Kannan and Veazie's 2023 study in *SSM – Population Health*, using the American Time Use Survey to track social isolation, engagement, and companionship from 2003 through 2020, which found isolation rising and companionship declining even before the COVID-19 pandemic — a finding this book has treated, up to this point, as solid underlying evidence for the broader decline narrative. In the course of preparing this chapter, this book's research encountered a claim, circulating in science-journalism coverage rather than in a peer-reviewed publication this book was able to independently locate and verify, that several independent research teams had attempted to reproduce the Kannan and Veazie findings after being unable to obtain the original reproduction code, and had found the actual measured increase in time spent alone across the study period amounted to only about twenty-four minutes per day — roughly an 8 percent relative increase, a small effect by conventional standards — with the original study's data visualizations, using a non-zero-based axis, visually exaggerating a real but modest shift into something that looked more dramatic on the page than the underlying numbers, read plainly, actually support. This book is being deliberately explicit about the limits of its own verification here: it could not trace this reanalysis to a published, peer-reviewed source, only to a blog-format science-journalism piece summarizing what it described as an informal, unpublished reproduction effort. That is a meaningfully weaker evidentiary basis than nearly everything else cited in this book, and this book is choosing to name that weakness directly, in the body of the text rather than in a footnote a reader might skip, because the alternative — quietly including the claim without its shaky sourcing, because it happens to support this book's own argument that the popular framing oversells the underlying data — would be a worse failure than acknowledging an interesting but unverified claim honestly. If the reanalysis holds up under future scrutiny, it would meaningfully deflate one of the more dramatic-sounding pieces of evidence behind the Surgeon General's advisory. If it does not hold up, or turns out to have its own methodological problems, this book's use of it here should not be read as an endorsement, only as a transparent account of an unresolved question this book encountered and could not close.

The question of whether men or women experience more loneliness, a question this book has touched on repeatedly without addressing directly — Japan and South Korea's isolated middle-aged men in chapter nine, the gray-divorce gender gap in chapter four, the "friendship recession" data specific to American men in chapter two — turns out to be a near-perfect illustration of the measurement problem this chapter is building toward. When researchers ask survey respondents directly whether they feel lonely, women consistently report somewhat higher rates than men; one long-running dataset found women reporting loneliness at least three days a week at nearly double the rate of men in some age brackets. But when researchers instead measure broader social isolation — objective network size, frequency of contact, rather than the subjective feeling of loneliness — the pattern reverses: men show slightly higher isolation than women at nearly every age, with the gap widest among men who have never married, a group this book's earlier chapters on hikikomori, gray divorce, and the friendship recession have each separately flagged as particularly vulnerable. Researchers studying this discrepancy have offered a specific, well-supported explanation: men remain more reluctant than women to admit feeling lonely to a survey interviewer, and a man who does report loneliness faces more social stigma for the admission than a woman reporting the identical feeling, meaning the self-reported gender gap may substantially reflect a reporting gap rather than a true experience gap. Meta-analyses using loneliness measures that avoid directly asking respondents to label their own feeling — inferring loneliness instead from indirect behavioral and network indicators — find the gender difference shrinks close to zero, suggesting the seemingly consistent finding that women are lonelier is itself, to a significant degree, an artifact of which specific instrument a given study happened to use, layered on top of the general instrument-inconsistency problem this chapter has already documented.

It is worth laying the specific prevalence figures this book has cited across its earlier chapters side by side, because the spread between them, once assembled in one place, makes the measurement-inconsistency problem concrete in a way abstract discussion of survey instruments cannot. Cigna's 2018 index found 54 percent of American adults meeting its UCLA-scale threshold for loneliness, rising to 61 percent in its 2020 update. The Jo Cox Commission's British figures, examined in chapter twelve, found roughly 14 percent of the UK population reporting feeling lonely often or always. Japan's 2022 government survey found about 40 percent of respondents reporting some degree of loneliness. The WHO's global commission estimated roughly one in six people worldwide, or about 16 percent, experiencing loneliness. These figures span a range from 14 percent to 61 percent — a more than fourfold difference — ostensibly describing overlapping populations in the same rough time period, and no single explanation fully accounts for a gap that large: different countries genuinely do have different underlying rates, different survey instruments genuinely do set different thresholds, and different framings of the underlying question genuinely do produce different answers from the same respondent. A reader who has absorbed this book's earlier chapters chapter by chapter, each citing its own specific, individually well-sourced percentage, could reasonably come away with the impression that these numbers are more directly comparable to each other than they actually are. This book is stating that limitation explicitly, here, rather than letting a reader discover the inconsistency independently and conclude the book's sourcing was sloppier than it actually was: each individual figure this book has cited is accurately reported from its own named source using its own named methodology; none of them should be read as directly comparable to any other figure in this book bearing a different source, a different country, or a different survey instrument, however similar the resulting percentages might look side by side on the page.

A deeper theoretical tension, worth naming before this chapter turns to its measurement-instrument critique, runs through this book's own evidence base without this book having flagged it directly until now. John Cacioppo, the University of Chicago psychologist who cofounded the field of social neuroscience and whose 2008 book *Loneliness: Human Nature and the Need for Social Connection* laid much of the theoretical groundwork underneath Holt-Lunstad's later meta-analyses, conceptualized loneliness as an evolved biological alarm signal — functionally similar to hunger or thirst, evolved to motivate reconnection with a group whose protection early humans depended on for survival. Cacioppo's research consistently emphasized that it is the *subjective experience* of loneliness, not the raw, objectively countable number of a person's social contacts, that drives the biological and health effects his lab documented: a person surrounded by people but feeling disconnected from them, in Cacioppo's framework, suffers the same physiological toll as a person objectively isolated with few contacts at all. This creates a direct, underexamined tension with the AARP-Stanford Medicare research examined in chapter ten, which found that objective social isolation, not subjective loneliness, predicted the excess healthcare spending in that specific dataset — a finding this book leaned on for its "practical noticing" framework running through several later chapters. Both findings are independently well-supported within their own research traditions; neither appears to be simply wrong. The likeliest reconciliation, though this book cannot resolve the tension definitively, is that objective isolation and subjective loneliness are correlated but imperfectly overlapping conditions, each carrying its own partially independent health and cost consequences through partially different mechanisms — which would mean this book's own repeated emphasis on the practical, other-person-noticing mechanism, useful as an organizing framework, has likely been overstating how completely it captures the full picture Cacioppo's foundational research first opened up, and understating how much the subjective experience itself, independent of objective network size, continues to matter on its own terms.

Underneath all of these specific disputes sits a more basic measurement problem this book has been quietly navigating around since its opening chapters without naming directly: different studies measure loneliness using genuinely different instruments, and those instruments do not always agree with each other even when applied to the same population at the same time. The UCLA Loneliness Scale, the twenty-item instrument behind both Cigna's index in chapter ten and much of the academic literature this book has cited, asks respondents to rate their agreement with a series of indirect statements about their social experience — "I feel left out," "I lack companionship" — rather than asking directly whether they consider themselves lonely, a design choice intended to reduce the social stigma respondents might feel admitting loneliness outright, but one that also means the instrument is measuring a constructed composite score against a researcher-set threshold rather than a person's own self-assessment. Simpler, single-item survey questions — "How often do you feel lonely?" — tend to produce different prevalence estimates than the multi-item UCLA scale applied to the same population, sometimes substantially different ones, because a person can score above a clinical threshold on an indirect composite measure while readily telling a pollster, when asked directly, that they do not consider themselves lonely, and the reverse can happen too. This is not a flaw unique to loneliness research; measurement inconsistency across instruments is a familiar problem throughout social science, and it is one reason this book has tried, wherever possible, to name which specific instrument produced which specific number rather than treating "loneliness," as measured, as a single stable quantity comparable cleanly across every study this book has cited. A reader encountering two studies with sharply different loneliness prevalence figures for what looks like the same population should ask, before assuming either study got the measurement wrong, whether they were actually measuring the same thing in the first place.

There is a broader conceptual critique, separate from any specific dataset, that several researchers in the public-health and gerontology literature have raised about the "epidemic" framing itself, one worth taking seriously on its own terms rather than as a mere rhetorical quibble. An epidemic, in its original and still-technical public-health sense, describes a communicable condition with a relatively sudden onset, a period of accelerating spread, and — implicitly — the possibility of a discrete intervention, like a vaccine or a quarantine, capable of stopping the spread outright. Loneliness is not communicable in that sense, does not have a clean onset date, and does not respond to any single, discrete intervention the way an infectious outbreak might. Applying epidemic language to a chronic, unevenly distributed, subjectively experienced condition risks importing assumptions from infectious-disease response that do not actually fit the problem — encouraging policymakers and the public to look for a single dramatic fix, or to treat loneliness as an aberrant departure from a healthy baseline rather than as a condition that has always been part of human social experience, ebbing and flowing with life circumstances, and that becomes a genuine public-health concern specifically in its more severe and prolonged forms rather than in its ordinary, transient, universally experienced ones. This critique does not require disputing any of the specific statistics this book has cited throughout; it is a challenge to the rhetorical frame the statistics have been placed inside, and it is a challenge this book takes seriously enough to name explicitly, here, rather than continuing to use "epidemic" language uncritically for its remaining chapters.

A related methodological question worth raising directly, because it applies to nearly every large attributable-death figure this book has cited, concerns how cleanly loneliness's mortality association can actually be separated from the other risk factors it tends to travel alongside. The same UK Biobank research this book's chapter on healthcare costs drew on for its underwriting discussion also examined, more specifically, how much of social isolation and loneliness's apparent contribution to excess mortality could be explained away once researchers adjusted for the biological, behavioral, socioeconomic, and psychological risk factors that isolated and lonely people also tend to carry at higher rates — worse average diet, higher smoking rates, lower income, more untreated chronic illness, less access to healthcare — factors that are themselves independently associated with earlier death, and that are not randomly distributed across isolated and non-isolated populations. The Biobank analysis found that a meaningful share, though not all, of the raw mortality gap between isolated and non-isolated individuals could be statistically attributed to these confounding factors rather than to isolation itself as an independent causal force, a pattern this book's earlier report of Holt-Lunstad's 2015 findings — odds ratios that shrink but do not disappear after adjustment — already reflected without naming the underlying mechanism this explicitly. This does not mean loneliness's mortality association is spurious; a robust, independent effect remains even in the most rigorously adjusted analyses this book reviewed. It does mean that headline figures like the WHO's estimate of roughly 871,000 loneliness-attributable deaths annually, discussed in chapter three, necessarily rest on a modeling choice about how much of the raw mortality gap to attribute to loneliness itself versus to the confounding conditions it correlates with — a choice reasonable researchers can and do make differently, producing genuinely different headline totals depending on which adjustment approach a given study or commission selects, without any of them being simply wrong.

None of this adds up to a case that this book's underlying subject is illusory, and this chapter is not trying to build one. Even Cox's more skeptical framing accepts a real generational gap in reported aloneness and connection, explained by identifiable and largely undisputed structural causes — declining marriage, declining religious participation, rising mobility — that are themselves the very institutional trends this book has spent its earlier chapters documenting in detail; disputing the "epidemic" label is not the same as disputing that marriage, religious membership, and stable community ties have genuinely declined, which is the actual load-bearing claim underneath this book's argument. Buecker and colleagues' cross-national complication narrows the geographic scope of the sharpest rise to North America specifically rather than the wealthy world uniformly, but it does not erase a real, statistically significant, preregistered finding of rising loneliness among young adults in the country this book's American evidence has focused on most heavily. And even the contested Kannan and Veazie reanalysis, in its most skeptical reading, describes a real, if smaller than commonly portrayed, increase in time spent alone — a modest effect, not a null one. None of the skeptics examined in this chapter — Cox, Fischer before him in chapter two, Odgers and Przybylski in chapter fourteen, Cacioppo's own subjective-versus-objective distinction — argue that nothing has changed. Every one of them, read carefully rather than reduced to a dismissive soundbite, is making a narrower methodological or definitional point about how much has changed, how evenly, and by which measure, not a claim that the underlying institutions this book has spent its length documenting are actually intact. That distinction matters enormously for how a reader should carry this chapter forward into the rest of the book: treating the skeptics as having refuted this book's subject would misread every one of them, while ignoring their specific, well-evidenced qualifications would repeat exactly the overconfident, oversimplified statistical citation this book has criticized throughout its own earlier chapters.

What the skeptics' case most usefully does, for a book trying to make its argument honestly rather than persuasively, is force a downgrade in confidence from "a proven, universal, accelerating global crisis" to something more defensible and, this book would argue, still serious enough to justify everything examined in the chapters ahead: a real, moderately sized, unevenly distributed, and imperfectly measured decline in several specific, identifiable forms of social connection, concentrated most severely in certain countries, certain generations, and certain communities, compounding with genuine and substantial costs in health, in dollars, and in the kind of solitary deaths this book's opening chapter described — real enough to deserve the businesses, the ministries, and the sustained attention examined throughout this book, without needing the inflated language of a runaway epidemic to justify that attention.
