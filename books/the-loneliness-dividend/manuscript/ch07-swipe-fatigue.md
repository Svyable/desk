# Swipe Fatigue

Match Group, the parent company of Tinder, Hinge, Match.com, and a portfolio of smaller dating properties, spent much of the 2010s as one of the more reliable growth stories in consumer technology, built on a premise that seemed, for a while, close to unassailable: courtship, an activity every generation before this one had conducted through some combination of family introduction, workplace proximity, religious congregation, and neighborhood familiarity, could be moved almost entirely onto a smartphone screen, monetized through subscriptions and in-app purchases, and made more efficient in the process. Tinder, launched in 2012 and acquired into the Match Group portfolio, became the signature product of that premise — a swipe-based interface simple enough to use in idle moments, gamified enough to encourage habitual return visits, and, for a period, genuinely novel enough to reshape how an entire generation of young adults expected to meet romantic partners.

The premise has not held up as cleanly as its early growth curve suggested it would. Match Group's own financial filings tell the story in numbers rather than sentiment: Tinder's paying subscriber base, which the company reports as "payers" in its earnings releases, fell by roughly 7 to 8 percent year over year across several recent reporting periods, part of a decline that has persisted for multiple consecutive years rather than representing a single bad quarter. The company's own public statements about the decline point to several overlapping causes rather than a single culprit: a deliberate strategic effort to reposition Tinder away from its long-standing reputation as a casual "hookup app" toward features meant to support more relationship-oriented use, increased competitive pressure from a growing field of rival apps each targeting narrower demographic or interest-based niches, and reduced discretionary spending among the Gen Z users who had once been the platform's most reliable growth engine. Whatever the precise mix of causes, the direction is unambiguous, and it runs directly counter to the assumption, embedded in a decade of tech-industry optimism, that digital matchmaking would simply keep growing as more of courtship moved online.

Tinder's own marketing has responded to the fatigue this chapter has documented with a striking, almost self-critical shift in public messaging. Facing the specific revenue decline this chapter's financial data has already traced — direct revenue falling from roughly $509 million in the third quarter of 2023 to about $482 million by the first quarter of 2024, alongside a 9 percent year-over-year decline in paying subscribers — the company launched "It Starts with a Swipe," its first-ever global brand campaign, explicitly built around research the company itself commissioned into what it called "green flags," reframing the platform's public identity away from its long-standing reputation as a casual hookup app and toward a claimed focus on genuine, Gen Z-defined connection. The shift stands in some tension with the app's own product mechanics, which chapter seven has already argued encourage exactly the rapid, low-commitment evaluation pattern that produces the fatigue Tinder's marketing now claims to be moving past — a company attempting to rebrand its way out of a structural problem embedded in its core interface design, rather than substantially redesigning the interface itself. Hinge's own rebrand tells a more internally consistent story, precisely because its underlying product design changed alongside its messaging: the "Designed to be Deleted" campaign, built around an animated mascot named Hingie that is destroyed each time a matched couple successfully forms a relationship and leaves the app, treats user departure as the product's explicit goal rather than an unfortunate churn statistic to be minimized, aligning the company's marketing, its underlying product incentives, and its measurable outcomes in a way Tinder's messaging shift, applied on top of an unchanged swipe-and-scroll interface, has not yet managed to replicate.

Not every app in Match Group's portfolio has followed Tinder's trajectory, and the divergence within a single company's own product lineup is itself instructive. Hinge, also owned by Match Group and marketed explicitly around its "designed to be deleted" positioning — built, at least in its stated design philosophy, around helping users find a relationship and leave the platform rather than around maximizing ongoing engagement — has continued growing even as Tinder's core numbers have declined, with the company's own investor materials citing Hinge's growth as a partial offset to Tinder's losses in recent reporting periods. The contrast is not proof that one design philosophy is simply superior to the other in some abstract sense; Hinge is younger, was built for a different, somewhat older and more relationship-oriented demographic from the outset, and has not been operating at Tinder's scale or cultural saturation for nearly as long, all of which complicates any simple causal story about design choices alone explaining the diverging trajectories. But the divergence is at least suggestive of something worth naming: a product explicitly optimized to end a user's need for the product, rather than to maximize the time a user spends inside it, appears to be faring better in the current market than the product built on the earlier generation's engagement-maximizing assumptions — a pattern this book will note recurring, in a different form, when it examines AI companion products in the next chapter, several of which face a structurally similar tension between designing for a user's long-term wellbeing and designing for the engagement metrics that a subscription or advertising-based business model rewards.

Pew Research Center's most detailed recent survey of American online-dating experience, published in February 2023, offers a useful window into why the growth might be stalling from the user side rather than only the business side. Roughly half of past or current online daters described their overall experience in broadly positive terms — a meaningful share, and evidence against any simple narrative that online dating has been a uniform failure for the people who try it. But among users who had dated online within the past year, more described the experience using words like "frustrating" than words like "hopeful," and women reported negative experiences at a distinctly higher rate than men across several specific measures the survey asked about, including unwanted contact and feeling like matches misrepresented themselves. The gap between "half of all users describe the overall experience positively" and "a majority of recent, active users report frustration" is not a contradiction; it likely reflects a familiar dynamic in any product with declining marginal returns — early or occasional users, including many who found a long-term partner and stopped using the apps, remember the experience more fondly than users still actively swiping through a pool that has, by most informal and some formal accounts, become saturated with fatigue, bots, and mismatched intentions after more than a decade of mainstream use.

Part of the mechanism behind that fatigue likely connects to a broader psychological pattern documented well outside the dating context, one psychologist Barry Schwartz examined at length in his 2004 book *The Paradox of Choice*: beyond a certain point, additional options do not reliably make a decision easier or its eventual outcome more satisfying, and can instead produce a kind of decision paralysis alongside persistent, nagging regret about the options not chosen, even when the option a person did select was objectively reasonable. A dating app's core promise is volume — access to a far larger pool of potential partners than any pre-digital courtship mechanism could plausibly assemble in one place — and that promise, taken to its logical extreme through infinite scrolling and an effectively unlimited daily supply of new profiles, may be delivering exactly the kind of choice overload Schwartz's broader research describes, translated into the specific, emotionally loaded domain of romantic partnership. A person choosing among three plausible partners introduced through family and neighborhood networks faces a fundamentally different decision architecture than a person facing an app interface implicitly suggesting an inexhaustible supply of alternatives just one more swipe away; the second condition, on Schwartz's broader theory, would be expected to produce exactly the kind of fatigue, second-guessing, and diminished satisfaction with any given match that Pew's 2023 survey data found among recent, active daters. This book cannot claim Schwartz's general theory has been rigorously tested within the dating-app context specifically to the same evidentiary standard this book has applied to its more directly sourced claims, and flags that gap honestly; it is offered here as a plausible, well-grounded explanatory mechanism rather than as an independently confirmed finding.

"Dating app fatigue" is now common enough as a phrase, in both casual conversation and industry trade coverage, that it risks becoming a cliché rather than a diagnosis, and this chapter wants to take it seriously as an actual mechanism rather than repeat it as a vibe. Part of what fatigue seems to describe is a straightforward numbers problem: an interface optimized for rapid, low-commitment evaluation of a large number of potential matches trains its users toward exactly the kind of rapid, low-commitment evaluation that makes any single match feel more disposable, which in turn makes the accumulated experience of many disposable interactions feel exhausting in a way that a smaller number of higher-stakes, socially introduced meetings — the kind courtship infrastructure used to supply by default — did not. This is not a claim unique to dating; it echoes a broader critique, made across contexts from social media feeds to online shopping, that interfaces designed to maximize engagement and choice can produce worse subjective outcomes than interfaces that constrain choice more deliberately, even when the abundance of options is, on paper, an unambiguous improvement over scarcity. Whether that broader critique holds up as rigorously in the dating context as its proponents claim is a genuinely contested empirical question this book does not have space to adjudicate fully; what is not contested is that a large share of the platforms' own active users are, by their own report to Pew, more likely to describe their recent experience as frustrating than hopeful, and that the largest company in the industry is now managing a shrinking core product rather than a growing one.

The dating-app market's overall decline also obscures real growth at its margins, among apps built around specific identities and relationship structures rather than the broad, general-audience model Tinder pioneered. Feeld, an app built around open relationship structures, ethical non-monogamy, and a deliberately expansive set of gender and orientation categories — more than twenty of each, alongside relationship-structure labels distinguishing open, polyamorous, and "partnered and curious" users from conventional single daters — reported membership growth of 368 percent between 2021 and 2025, reaching more than 2 million members worldwide and roughly $65 million in 2024 revenue, up 26 percent year over year, even as Tinder's own core numbers moved in the opposite direction across the same period. Feeld's own growing pains are instructive in their own right: company and press coverage has described a phenomenon insiders call "normie creep," in which the app's rising mainstream popularity draws in increasing numbers of conventionally monogamous daters seeking novelty, diluting the curated, self-selected community of genuinely non-traditional relationship-seekers that had made the app distinctive in the first place — a specific version of the broader tension this book has already examined in chapter five's account of Oldenburg's third places, where growth and popularity can, paradoxically, erode the very qualities of intimacy and shared identity that attracted an original community to a space before it became popular. Feeld's trajectory, alongside a broader landscape of dozens of smaller apps built around narrower fandoms, spiritual practices, fitness interests, and micro-communities that had emerged by 2025, suggests the dating-app market's overall stagnation documented earlier in this chapter is less a story of digital matchmaking failing broadly than one of a single, general-purpose model losing ground to a more fragmented ecosystem of identity- and interest-specific alternatives — a shift with a clear parallel to this book's own broader argument that the specific, generic institutions of the mid-twentieth century have given way to a thinner, more scattered, and often more self-selecting landscape of replacements.

It is worth pausing here to complicate the nostalgic implication that could otherwise creep into this chapter's framing — that the older, pre-app courtship infrastructure this book has described eroding was, for everyone, a straightforward good that dating apps merely degraded. For a meaningful share of potential users, particularly LGBTQ people in communities where family, workplace, and religious introduction networks were actively hostile rather than merely absent, the older infrastructure this book has praised in earlier chapters for its connective function was also frequently exclusionary or outright unsafe as a courtship mechanism, offering little practical path to partners who shared both a specific orientation and a specific willingness to be discreet about it. Apps built for exactly this purpose — Grindr, which built an early and durable business specifically serving gay and bisexual men, alongside a range of smaller platforms serving other underserved dating populations — represent a genuine expansion of courtship access that the third places, congregations, and family networks of earlier chapters frequently failed to provide, or provided only at real personal risk. This book's broader argument about a valuable social infrastructure eroding should not be read as implying every replacement mechanism is worse for every population it serves; for some users, the market-based matching this chapter has otherwise treated skeptically solved a real access problem that the informal, community-based infrastructure this book has praised elsewhere never solved well, or safely, in the first place.

Taken together, the picture this chapter has assembled of the online-dating industry is one of genuine fragmentation rather than uniform collapse: Match Group's flagship product losing ground while its own sister app pursues a structurally different, currently more successful strategy; a niche, identity-specific competitor growing rapidly by serving exactly the population the general-purpose model underserved; and, on a different continent entirely, a family-mediated hybrid model expanding by leaning back into precisely the older courtship infrastructure the American apps were built to replace. No single story — dating apps have failed, dating apps have succeeded, technology has ruined courtship, technology has saved it — survives contact with the full range of evidence this chapter has assembled. What does survive is this book's now-familiar, narrower claim: courtship infrastructure, like every other institution this book has examined, is being actively rebuilt in real time, unevenly, by a wide range of actors pursuing genuinely different theories of what was actually broken about the previous model, with no single actor yet in possession of a version reliably good enough to have settled the question.

What makes this chapter's subject matter directly relevant to this book's broader argument, rather than simply a business-press story about one industry's growth slowdown, is what the apps were substituting for in the first place, and what happens when the substitute itself starts to fail. For most of the twentieth century, romantic pairing was substantially mediated by the same social infrastructure this book has already documented eroding on every other front: family and neighborhood introduction, workplace proximity, religious congregation, and the third places Ray Oldenburg described, all of which functioned, among their other purposes, as informal courtship infrastructure, introducing people to potential partners already vetted by a shared social context. As those institutions thinned, dating apps did not appear as an unrelated technological novelty; they appeared, in significant part, as a direct commercial response to the same gap this book has been tracing throughout — a way to manufacture, through a marketplace mechanism, the introductions that a denser social fabric used to generate for free, in roughly the same relationship WeWork bore to the traditional office and Peloton bore to the group fitness class. And now, more than a decade into that experiment, a meaningful share of the people relying on it report the substitute itself feeling worn out, at the exact moment the underlying social infrastructure it was substituting for has not meaningfully recovered.

India offers a genuinely different, and in this book's view genuinely instructive, response to the same underlying fatigue driving Match Group's American decline — not a rejection of digital matchmaking, but a digitally mediated return toward the family-involved courtship model this book's earlier chapters described the American infrastructure eroding away from decades ago. India's online matrimony sector, distinct from Western-style swipe dating and built around platforms like Shaadi.com that digitize the country's long-established tradition of family-brokered arranged marriage, was valued at roughly $5.5 billion in 2024 and is projected to grow to over $13 billion by 2033, a considerably faster growth trajectory than the Western dating-app market this chapter has otherwise described stalling. Industry analysts covering the sector specifically cite dating-app fatigue as a driver of the shift, describing Indian users, particularly younger ones who have already tried Western-style apps, choosing what one report characterized as the safer, less individually taxing "arranged" route over unmediated swipe-based matching. What makes the Indian case analytically interesting rather than merely a market curiosity is how the role of family has evolved rather than disappeared within it: platform operators and researchers studying the sector describe parents shifting from being the sole decision-makers in traditional arranged matchmaking to acting more as "co-pilots," reviewing and suggesting profiles a platform's algorithm surfaces while leaving the younger user considerably more agency over the final choice than earlier generations of purely family-arranged marriage typically allowed. This is, in effect, a hybrid model directly combining the two courtship mechanisms this book's earlier chapters treated as historical opposites — the family and community network Putnam's and Oldenburg's frameworks describe eroding in the American context, and the market-based algorithmic matching this chapter has otherwise examined critically — suggesting that where the underlying family and community infrastructure this book has spent its early chapters documenting as eroded in America remains comparatively intact, as it still does across much of India, digital tools can extend and modernize that infrastructure rather than being forced to substitute for its absence entirely.

Bumble's own history within this same industry offers a smaller-scale but instructive coda. The company, whose flagship dating product built its early differentiation on requiring women to send the first message, had included a "BFF" mode for platonic friend-finding within its main app since 2016, treating friendship discovery as a minor complement to its primary romantic-matching business. In 2023, facing the same industry-wide dating fatigue affecting its larger rival, Bumble spun the friendship feature out into its own standalone app, Bumble For Friends, betting explicitly that a market exhausted by romantic swiping might still have appetite for a lower-stakes version of the same underlying mechanism, aimed at platonic connection rather than romance. The company's own data, released as part of its marketing for the standalone product, found that a majority of Gen Z respondents reported having met friends online at a rate roughly double the overall population average, evidence of real appetite for the underlying category. But the business results have so far been modest by any measure: Morgan Stanley's 2023 revenue estimate for the broader Bumble company put total company revenue at just over $1 billion, with Bumble BFF and its adjacent business-networking product, Bizz, together contributing roughly $1 million of that total — a rounding error next to the flagship dating business, and a useful reminder that "people clearly want this" and "people will pay enough for this to sustain a standalone business" are two different claims, with the gap between them turning out, in this case, to be quite large.

That gap — between demonstrated appetite for connection and demonstrated willingness to pay a company to supply it — recurs throughout the businesses this book examines, and it is worth naming explicitly here before the chapters ahead move into technologies that raise the stakes considerably. A run club costs almost nothing to join and monetizes, if at all, through incidental merchandise or gym partnerships rather than a subscription fee extracted directly from the desire for company. A dating app or a friendship app asks users to pay, directly or through advertising exposure, for access to the connection itself, which changes the economics in a way that has proven, so far, harder to sustain than either the venture capital backing these companies or the underlying human need would suggest it should be. The next chapter turns to a category of company that has found a more durable, if more ethically complicated, way to monetize exactly this gap: not by matching people to other people at all, but by selling something that behaves like companionship without requiring another human being on the other end of the conversation.
