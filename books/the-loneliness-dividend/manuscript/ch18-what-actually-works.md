# What Actually Works

The scale of the underlying problem social prescribing was designed to address is itself worth stating plainly before describing the program's mechanics, because it explains why NHS England judged the intervention worth building at national scale in the first place. Separate analysis cited by the Campaign to End Loneliness, drawing on the same body of research this book's chapter ten examined, has found that as many as one in ten GP appointments in Britain are taken up by a patient whose presenting concern is loneliness itself, with no other identifiable medical condition driving the visit — a volume of appointments consuming physician time, NHS resources, and appointment-slot capacity that could otherwise go toward patients with conditions a doctor's medical training and prescribing authority can actually address directly. A doctor cannot prescribe a pill for loneliness, but a fifteen-minute appointment slot consumed by a patient whose real need is company still costs the health system the same fifteen minutes, the same overhead, and the same opportunity cost as a slot spent on a condition medicine can treat. Social prescribing's core logic follows directly from that mismatch: rather than a GP absorbing an unaddressable need inside a medical consultation format never designed to meet it, formalize a referral pathway that routes the patient toward an intervention actually suited to what they need, freeing the GP's own limited time for the conditions general practice is actually equipped to treat.

England's National Health Service has, since 2019, been running one of the largest deliberate experiments anywhere in the world in treating social connection as something a doctor can formally prescribe. Social prescribing, as the program is called, works through a specific institutional mechanism: general practitioners, who NHS England's own estimates suggest spend a substantial share of appointment time — roughly one in five GP visits, by the agency's accounting — addressing patient needs that are not primarily medical at all, refer patients to trained "link workers" rather than, or in addition to, prescribing medication or specialist referral. The link worker's job is to connect the patient with community groups, activities, volunteering opportunities, or other non-clinical resources suited to what is actually driving their visit: a widowed patient whose repeated complaints of vague physical symptoms turn out to trace substantially to isolation following a spouse's death; a new parent struggling with the disorientation of a life reorganized entirely around a newborn, with no nearby family and no established peer network; an older adult whose mobility limits have gradually cut off the informal contact that used to come from an easier daily walk to the shops.

The program's scale, by the most recent tracking available, is substantial and still growing. A study from University College London, working with the National Academy for Social Prescribing, found 1.3 million referrals made in 2023 alone, and 5.5 million cumulative referrals or consultations since the program's national rollout began in 2019 — a scale that puts social prescribing among the largest deliberately organized attempts anywhere in the world to treat the specific gap this book has spent seventeen chapters documenting as a formal, addressable feature of a national healthcare system, rather than leaving it to the market, to informal community organizing, or to individual patients to solve on their own initiative. One frequently cited program-level evaluation, covering 1,751 patients in the Tameside and Glossop areas of England, found a 42.2 percent reduction in GP appointment demand among patients referred through social prescribing over a twelve-month follow-up period, compared with just a 5.6 percent reduction in a comparison group — a genuinely striking result, if it holds up under closer scrutiny, suggesting the intervention did not merely make patients feel better in some diffuse sense but measurably reduced their reliance on the healthcare system's most basic point of contact.

This chapter wants to hold that striking result up against a harder, more skeptical standard than the program's own promotional materials apply to it, in keeping with the approach this book has tried to maintain toward every intervention examined across its earlier chapters. A systematic review published in the *British Journal of General Practice*, along with a separate systematic review this book located during its research, both concluded that the overall evidence base for social prescribing's effectiveness remains, by the standards of rigorous clinical and public-health evaluation, genuinely weak — built substantially on small-scale studies, many lacking proper control groups or randomization, more often measuring process outcomes, such as whether patients attended their referred activity, than measuring the harder, more meaningful outcomes of whether their health, wellbeing, or healthcare utilization actually improved as a result. A separate review specifically examining the program's economic evaluation evidence — whether social prescribing actually saves the healthcare system money in the way advocates often claim, offsetting its own delivery costs through reduced downstream medical utilization — found that evidence weak as well, unable to establish with confidence whether the program pays for itself even where it does produce genuine health benefits.

American health insurers have begun building something structurally similar to the UK's social prescribing model directly into their own Medicare Advantage plan offerings, in a policy shift the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services has actively encouraged rather than merely tolerated. CVS Health's Aetna, one of the largest Medicare Advantage insurers in the country, launched programs specifically designed to combat social isolation among its members, and by 2026 CMS was explicitly encouraging plans nationally to offer more targeted supplemental benefits addressing social determinants of health — social isolation prominent among them, alongside food insecurity and housing instability — as part of a broader push toward benefit designs the agency believes can reduce costly downstream hospital readmissions, directly echoing this book's chapter ten argument that isolation functions as a genuine, quantifiable driver of healthcare spending. Some plans have gone as far as covering a paid companion for eligible members, providing several hours a month of company and light household assistance at no direct cost, a benefit design that sits closer to a direct market response than to the UK's more centrally coordinated social-prescribing referral system, reflecting the more fragmented, plan-by-plan structure of American healthcare more broadly. Coverage of these benefits varies considerably by plan, insurer, and county, and this chapter cannot offer a single national assessment of the practice's evidence base, since the benefits are new enough, and structured differently enough across hundreds of competing plans, that no equivalent to the UK's centralized referral-tracking data examined earlier in this chapter yet exists. What the shift does show is that American insurers, operating under the same general body of evidence this book has surveyed throughout, have reached broadly the same conclusion British and Japanese policymakers reached through more centralized government action: that addressing social isolation directly, rather than only treating its downstream medical consequences after they occur, has become a strategy worth investing in on its own economic merits, independent of any government mandate requiring them to do so.

This is not a case for abandoning social prescribing, and this chapter does not read it that way; the honest reading, consistent with how this book has tried to handle every piece of genuinely mixed evidence throughout, is that a program can be simultaneously large, rapidly scaling, intuitively sensible, supported by some genuinely promising individual evaluations like the Tameside and Glossop study, and not yet rigorously proven at the standard a skeptical clinical researcher would require before calling it a demonstrated success. The distinction matters enormously for how governments considering similar programs elsewhere — the ministries examined in chapter twelve, several of which have expressed interest in versions of social prescribing as one policy tool among several — should think about scaling it further: as a promising, worth-expanding intervention that still needs considerably more rigorous evaluation running alongside its expansion, not as a proven solution ready for wholesale replication without further study.

What social prescribing does get structurally right, in a way worth connecting back to the analytical framework chapter ten established, is its targeting mechanism. Recall the distinction that chapter drew from the AARP-Stanford Medicare spending research: what predicted higher healthcare costs was not subjective loneliness on its own, but objectively measured social isolation — small networks, infrequent contact, the practical absence of people positioned to notice a problem early. Social prescribing, unlike an app or a device a person can passively use without ever leaving their home, requires an actual referral into an actual community activity, physically connecting a patient to other physically present people on a recurring basis — which means, structurally, it is targeting exactly the practical, network-density variable the Medicare data found to matter, rather than only the subjective feeling the Medicare data found did not independently predict cost. That structural alignment does not, on its own, prove social prescribing works; the BJGP review's caution about weak underlying evidence still applies regardless of how sensible the program's targeting logic sounds in principle. But it does suggest social prescribing is at least aimed at the right variable, in a way several of the technology-driven interventions examined earlier in this book — particularly the AI companion apps of chapter eight, valuable as they may be for some users along other dimensions — are not obviously aimed at in the same way, since a conversation with a chatbot, however genuinely comforting, does not by itself increase the number of actual humans positioned to notice if something goes wrong.

A structurally distinct intervention worth examining alongside social prescribing and the Silver Line is the growth of certified peer support specialists within the American mental-health and substance-use treatment system — workers whose qualification is not a clinical degree but their own lived experience of recovery from a mental-health condition, a substance-use disorder, or both. The certified peer-support workforce has grown from roughly 30,000 to about 82,000 nationally over the past decade, with nearly every state now maintaining its own training and certification standards for the role. Peer support workers occupy an unusual position relative to every other intervention this chapter has examined: they are neither a purely informal, uncredentialed community response like a run club, nor a conventional clinical intervention delivered by a professionally trained but personally uninvolved provider; they are a formally credentialed workforce whose core qualification is precisely the kind of practical noticing this book's chapter ten has argued matters most, grounded in a shared, first-hand understanding of what the person they are supporting is actually going through. The model has shown documented success in reducing stigma, hospitalization rates, and healthcare costs among the populations it serves, addressing in part the severe capacity shortage in the broader American mental-health system — more than 150 million Americans live in a federally designated mental-health professional shortage area, and 55 percent of U.S. counties have no practicing psychiatrist at all — by creating a credentialed, scalable role that does not require the years of clinical training a psychiatrist or licensed therapist needs, while still offering more structured, sustained support than an informal peer relationship could reliably provide. The model is not without real challenges of its own: peer support specialists themselves report meaningful job dissatisfaction and high turnover, a workforce-sustainability problem this book flags rather than resolves, consistent with its treatment of every other genuinely promising but imperfectly evidenced intervention examined in this chapter.

A cluster of structured intergenerational programs, pairing teenagers with isolated older adults through formal, trained, screened volunteer relationships, offers a version of the practical-noticing intervention this book has emphasized throughout aimed specifically at the age gap chapter thirteen's decline in cross-generational congregational contact left behind. DOROT, a New York-based organization running structured volunteer visiting programs, and newer entrants including Herralink — founded by a nineteen-year-old in 2023 and acquired by the senior-living operator Goodwin Living in May 2024 — and GenerationsConnect, which carefully screens and trains teen volunteers before matching them with older "buddies," each formalize what an extended family or a dense neighborhood used to provide informally: a young person's regular, recurring visit to an older person who otherwise receives few or no visitors. Herralink's own reported retention data, self-reported by the organization rather than independently verified to this book's preferred standard, describes some volunteers maintaining weekly visiting commitments across an entire year with minimal missed sessions, a level of consistency that, if representative rather than a best-case anecdote, would place the model closer to the recurring, dependable contact chapter ten's Medicare research identified as protective than many of the more one-off, event-based interventions this chapter has otherwise examined. These programs also report a bidirectional benefit worth noting explicitly, since this book has focused mostly on outcomes for older isolated adults: the teenage volunteers themselves are described as gaining practice in active listening, empathy, and conversational confidence, skills with their own independent value for a generation chapter fourteen has already shown facing measurable declines in unsupervised, in-person social practice.

One of the simplest interventions this book examines, in terms of the technology it requires, is also among the most directly diagnostic of the underlying problem this book has spent nineteen chapters describing. The Silver Line, a free, confidential telephone helpline for older people launched in the UK in 2013 by broadcaster and campaigner Esther Rantzen, operates every night of the year specifically because, as its founders recognized, isolation does not observe conventional office hours and often intensifies precisely when other support services are closed. The service now receives roughly 10,000 calls a week, has answered more than 1.4 million calls since its national launch, and — the single statistic from this chapter that this book finds hardest to read past without pausing — 53 percent of callers report that the Silver Line volunteer on the other end of the line is literally the only person they have to talk to. More than two-thirds of the service's calls arrive overnight or on weekends, when no comparable alternative exists for an isolated older person who has nowhere else to turn. Beyond the crisis-response hotline itself, the Silver Line runs a structured befriending program, pairing callers who want ongoing contact with volunteer "Silver Line Friends" who commit to regular, recurring weekly calls with the same specific person — a deliberate design choice that converts a one-off crisis contact into exactly the kind of practical, recurring, human-noticing relationship this book's chapter ten identified as the actual mechanism reducing isolation's measurable costs, built on nothing more technologically sophisticated than a telephone and a roster of volunteers willing to show up for the same call, at the same time, week after week.

A narrower, more targeted intervention worth setting alongside social prescribing's broad, general-purpose model is the Men's Sheds movement, which began in Australia in the 1990s around a specific, well-documented gap this book's earlier chapters have already surfaced from different angles: older men, who research across several countries has consistently found to be at particular risk of isolation following retirement or widowhood, and who, as chapter nine's discussion of Japan's herbivore-men discourse and hikikomori population suggested, are often reluctant to seek out conventional social or emotional support in forms explicitly framed around talking about feelings. Men's Sheds sidestep that reluctance by design: they are community workshop spaces built around shared practical activity — woodworking, small-engine repair, metalwork — where conversation and social bonding happen as a byproduct of working alongside other people rather than as the explicit, stated purpose of the gathering, a structure that echoes this book's earlier observation, drawn from Oldenburg's original third-place framework, that the strongest connective institutions often generate community incidentally, around some other shared activity, rather than by asking participants to show up and socialize as an end in itself. The movement has since spread to Canada, Denmark, New Zealand, Ireland, Kenya, and the United Kingdom, and Australia alone now reportedly hosts more Men's Sheds than McDonald's locations, a comparison that, whatever its rhetorical flourish, conveys something real about how deeply the model has embedded itself in ordinary community infrastructure there. The research base, while researchers themselves describe it as still limited, particularly in the UK, has been consistently positive where it exists: a Manchester Metropolitan University study found community shed participation associated with reduced loneliness and improved emotional and physical health among attendees, with roughly 79 percent of participants in one survey reporting improved mental health as a result of attending. Men's Sheds will never reach the referral scale of a national health system's formal prescribing program, and this book is not proposing it as social prescribing's rival so much as its complement — further evidence for this chapter's broader argument that the interventions showing the most consistent promise share a structural feature: they generate connection as the byproduct of a shared, concrete, unrelated activity, rather than asking isolated people to seek out connection directly as its own explicit goal.

The United States has its own homegrown, member-led parallel to Britain's institutionally driven social-prescribing model, grown from a single Boston neighborhood rather than a national health service, and its trajectory illustrates a different set of tradeoffs from the government-run programs this chapter has examined so far. Beacon Hill Village, founded in Boston in 2001, pioneered what has become known as the "Village" model of aging in place: a member-led, dues-supported organization that connects older residents with vetted, affordable service providers for practical needs — transportation, home repairs, technology help — while simultaneously functioning as a deliberately organized peer network, running social events, volunteer-staffed check-ins, and interest-based groups intended to keep members embedded in ongoing, recurring human contact rather than isolated in their own homes as their mobility and independence gradually narrow. The model spread by direct replication rather than top-down mandate: independent Villages, each locally governed and locally funded, had grown to roughly 300 operating organizations across the United States by 2024, with a further fifty in active development, coordinated loosely through a national Village-to-Village Network that shares organizing know-how without centrally controlling any individual Village's operations. Survey data collected by the network itself consistently shows members reporting real, positive impact on their wellbeing and quality of life, and AARP has endorsed the broader model as a person-centered approach capable of delaying or preventing the need for institutional care. But independent researchers examining the movement have reached the same conclusion this chapter has reached about social prescribing: individual members clearly like belonging to a Village, and the popular and philanthropic press has covered the movement warmly, but the rigorous, controlled, outcome-focused research needed to confirm the model's effect on health, isolation, or healthcare costs at the standard this book has applied throughout simply does not yet exist in sufficient quantity, leaving the Village model, like social prescribing, in the same promising-but-unproven category this chapter keeps returning to rather than a demonstrated success story.

Beyond social prescribing's specific and still-developing evidence base, a smaller number of other interventions examined across this book's earlier chapters carry evidence this chapter judges, on balance, more solid, even if none of them amount to a complete solution on their own. PARO's peer-reviewed evidence base, examined in chapter nine, includes genuine randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews, even accounting for their own noted methodological limitations, placing it on firmer evidentiary ground than most of the technology-driven interventions this book has examined. The run-club phenomenon documented in chapter eleven, while lacking anything approaching a rigorous controlled evaluation of its health effects, has the structural advantage social prescribing shares — recurring, in-person, physically anchored gathering with built-in noticing — combined with essentially no direct cost to participants and no company with a commercial incentive to overstate its benefits, a combination this book finds genuinely reassuring even in the absence of formal clinical trial data specifically evaluating it. What this book cannot honestly offer, and does not want to pretend to offer, is a single validated intervention proven, at the standard of a well-designed randomized controlled trial, to reliably reduce loneliness and its downstream costs at the population scale this book's earlier chapters have shown the problem operating at. What exists instead, across the full range of institutions, technologies, and community movements this book has examined, is a portfolio of partial, imperfectly evidenced, structurally varied responses — some more promising than others, none conclusively proven, together representing the best the current evidence actually supports rather than the tidier, more confident answer a book like this one might be tempted to manufacture for the sake of a satisfying conclusion.

The final chapter does not try to manufacture that tidier answer either. It tries instead to draw together what fifteen chapters of evidence, genuine uncertainty, and honestly preserved disagreement actually add up to, for a reader trying to understand not just what has happened to human connection across the wealthy world, but what, realistically, follows from having understood it.
